Parting Shots — A Mixed Challenge Round

Chapter 29 of The Art of Problem Solving ends the book with a "potpourri" of problems drawn from every topic, in no announced order — exactly the experience of sitting a real contest, where the hardest part is often just recognizing which idea applies. This capstone recreates that: a self-contained 25-question mock AMC 12B assembled from the verified problem bank, with the strands shuffled together and the topic hidden until you open the solution.

📋 The format

  • 25 questions, drawn from all four strands (7 Algebra · 7 Geometry · 6 Combinatorics · 5 Number Theory) — the same balance a real AMC 12B uses.
  • Difficulty rises with the number: 1–10 are warm-ups, 11–18 are the midpoint grind, 19–25 are the closers.
  • No topic labels on the problems — figuring out the right tool is part of the test. Each worked solution ends with a Source: line revealing the original contest and strand.

⏱️ Scoring (real AMC 12 rules)

  • +6 for each correct answer, +1.5 for each blank, 0 for a wrong answer — max 150.
  • Give yourself 75 minutes and resist opening solutions until you're done. A score of 100+ is excellent; ~85 historically clears the AIME cutoff.
💡 Capstone strategy — read the problem twice, then label it The single most useful habit on a mixed test: before computing anything, name the kind of problem ("this is Power of a Point," "this is complementary counting," "this is a Vieta question"). Naming it surfaces the right tool from the right chapter. When stuck, fall back on the book's through-line — look for structure (factor, reduce, place on coordinates) before grinding arithmetic.

Problems 1–10 · Warm-up

Build momentum. These reward a clean first idea — don't overthink them.

Mock AMC 12BProblem 1

Mrs. Jones is pouring orange juice for her 4 kids into 4 identical glasses. She fills the first 3 full, but only has enough orange juice to fill one third of the last glass. What fraction of a glass of orange juice does she need to pour from the 3 full glasses into the last glass so that all glasses have an equal amount of orange juice?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{1}{12} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{1}{4} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{1}{6} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{1}{8} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{2}{9}$

Worked solution

Total juice is $3 + \tfrac13 = \tfrac{10}{3}$ glasses, shared equally among $4$ glasses, so each should hold $\dfrac{10/3}{4} = \dfrac{10}{12} = \dfrac{5}{6}$ of a glass.

Each of the three full glasses must give away $1 - \dfrac56 = \dfrac16$ of a glass.

(Check: the three pours total $3 \cdot \tfrac16 = \tfrac12$, raising the last glass from $\tfrac13$ to $\tfrac13 + \tfrac12 = \tfrac56$. Correct.)

Answer: (C) $\dfrac{1}{6}$

Source: 2023 AMC 12B #1 · Algebra (fractions).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 2

The arithmetic mean of the nine numbers in the set $\{9,\,99,\,999,\,9999,\,\dots,\,999999999\}$ is a $9$-digit number $M$, all of whose digits are distinct. The number $M$ does not contain the digit

$\textbf{(A)}\ 0 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 2 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 4 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 6 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 8$

Worked solution

Each term is $10^k - 1$, so the sum of all nine terms is

$$\sum_{k=1}^{9}\bigl(10^k - 1\bigr) = \bigl(10 + 100 + \cdots + 10^9\bigr) - 9 = 1111111110 - 9 = 1111111101.$$

Dividing by $9$ gives the mean

$$M = \frac{1111111101}{9} = 123456789.$$

The digits of $M$ are $1,2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9$ — all distinct, and the only digit missing is $0$.

Answer: (A) 0

Source: 2002 AMC 12B #1 · Number Theory (digits · repunits).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 3

A line with slope $2$ intersects a line with slope $6$ at the point $(40,30)$. What is the distance between the $x$-intercepts of these two lines?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 5 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 10 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 20 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 25 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 50$

Worked solution

Each line passes through $(40,30)$. The $x$-intercept is found by setting $y=0$ and solving for the horizontal distance from the shared point.

For slope $2$: $0-30 = 2(x-40)$, so $x-40 = -15$ and $x = 25$.

For slope $6$: $0-30 = 6(x-40)$, so $x-40 = -5$ and $x = 35$.

The distance between the $x$-intercepts is $|35-25| = 10$.

Answer: (B) $10$

Source: 2018 AMC 12B #3 · Geometry (coordinate lines).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 4

A basketball player made 5 baskets during a game. Each basket was worth either 2 or 3 points. How many different numbers could represent the total points scored by the player?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 3 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 4 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 5 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 6$

Worked solution

Let $k$ be the number of 3-point baskets, so $0 \le k \le 5$, and the remaining $5-k$ baskets are worth 2 points.

The total is $2(5-k) + 3k = 10 + k$.

As $k$ runs through $0,1,2,3,4,5$, the total takes the values $10,11,12,13,14,15$ — each distinct. That is $6$ different totals.

Answer: (E) 6

Source: 2008 AMC 12B #1 · Combinatorics (counting totals).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 5

What is $10! - 7! \cdot 6!$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ -120 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 0 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 120 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 600 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 720$

Worked solution

Note that $7! = 5040$ and $6! = 720$, so

$7! \cdot 6! = 5040 \cdot 720 = 3628800.$

Also $10! = 3628800$. Therefore

$10! - 7! \cdot 6! = 3628800 - 3628800 = 0.$

(Equivalently, $10! = 10\cdot 9\cdot 8\cdot 7! = 720\cdot 7! = 6!\cdot 7!$.)

Answer: (B) 0

Source: 2024 AMC 12B #2 · Algebra (factorials).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 6

Consider the statement, "If $n$ is not prime, then $n-2$ is prime." Which of the following values of $n$ is a counterexample to this statement?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 11 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 15 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 19 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 21 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 27$

Worked solution

A counterexample must make the hypothesis true but the conclusion false: $n$ must be not prime (composite) while $n-2$ is also not prime.

Check each choice:

  • $n=11$: prime, so the hypothesis is false — not a counterexample.
  • $n=15$: composite, and $15-2=13$ is prime — conclusion holds.
  • $n=19$: prime — hypothesis false.
  • $n=21$: composite, and $21-2=19$ is prime — conclusion holds.
  • $n=27$: composite, and $27-2=25=5^2$ is not prime — conclusion fails.

Only $n=27$ satisfies the hypothesis while violating the conclusion.

Answer: (E) $27$

Source: 2019 AMC 12B #2 · Number Theory (primes · counterexample).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 7

On circle $O$, points $C$ and $D$ are on the same side of diameter $\overline{AB}$, $\angle AOC = 30^\circ$, and $\angle DOB = 45^\circ$. What is the ratio of the area of the smaller sector $COD$ to the area of the circle?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{2}{9} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{1}{4} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{5}{18} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{7}{24} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{3}{10}$

Worked solution

The diameter $\overline{AB}$ spans $180^\circ$ from $A$ to $B$. Going around the same side, the central angle $\angle AOB = 180^\circ$ is split into $\angle AOC$, $\angle COD$, and $\angle DOB$.

$\angle COD = 180^\circ - \angle AOC - \angle DOB = 180^\circ - 30^\circ - 45^\circ = 105^\circ.$

A sector's area is proportional to its central angle, so the ratio to the whole circle is

$\dfrac{105^\circ}{360^\circ} = \dfrac{7}{24}.$

Answer: (D) $\dfrac{7}{24}$

Source: 2008 AMC 12B #4 · Geometry (circle / sector).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 8

What value of $x$ satisfies $\dfrac{\log_2 x \cdot \log_3 x}{\log_2 x + \log_3 x} = 2$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 25 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 32 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 36 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 42 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 48$

Worked solution

Let $a = \log_2 x$ and $b = \log_3 x$. The equation is $\dfrac{ab}{a+b} = 2$. Taking reciprocals,

$\dfrac{a+b}{ab} = \dfrac{1}{a} + \dfrac{1}{b} = \dfrac{1}{2}.$

Now $\dfrac{1}{a} = \dfrac{1}{\log_2 x} = \log_x 2$ and $\dfrac{1}{b} = \log_x 3$, so

$\log_x 2 + \log_x 3 = \log_x 6 = \dfrac{1}{2}.$

Hence $x^{1/2} = 6$, giving $x = 36$.

Answer: (C) 36

Source: 2024 AMC 12B #8 · Algebra (logarithms).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 9

The data set $[6,19,33,33,39,41,41,43,51,57]$ has median $Q_2=40$, first quartile $Q_1=33$, and third quartile $Q_3=43$. An outlier in a data set is a value that is more than $1.5$ times the interquartile range below the first quartile ($Q_1$) or more than $1.5$ times the interquartile range above the third quartile ($Q_3$), where the interquartile range is defined as $Q_3-Q_1$. How many outliers does this data set have?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 0 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 1 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 2 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 3 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 4$

Worked solution

The interquartile range is $\text{IQR}=Q_3-Q_1=43-33=10$, so $1.5\cdot\text{IQR}=15.$

Lower fence: $Q_1-15=33-15=18.$ Any value below $18$ is an outlier. Only $6<18$, so that is one outlier.

Upper fence: $Q_3+15=43+15=58.$ Any value above $58$ is an outlier. The largest value is $57<58$, so there are none on the high end.

Thus the data set has exactly $1$ outlier (the value $6$).

Answer: (B) $1$

Source: 2017 AMC 12B #5 · Combinatorics (statistics / IQR).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 10

In unit square $ABCD$, the inscribed circle $\omega$ intersects $CD$ at $M$, and $AM$ intersects $\omega$ at a point $P$ different from $M$. What is $AP$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt5}{12} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt5}{10} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt5}{9} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt5}{8} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{2\sqrt5}{15}$

Worked solution

Place the square with $A=(0,1)$, $B=(1,1)$, $C=(1,0)$, $D=(0,0)$. The inscribed circle $\omega$ has center $O=\left(\tfrac12,\tfrac12\right)$ and radius $\tfrac12$.

It touches side $CD$ (the bottom) at its midpoint, so $M=\left(\tfrac12,0\right)$.

Use the power of the point $A$ with respect to $\omega$. Since line $AM$ meets the circle at $P$ and $M$, $$AP\cdot AM = |AO|^2 - r^2.$$

Compute: $|AO|^2 = \left(\tfrac12\right)^2+\left(\tfrac12\right)^2 = \tfrac12$, so the power is $\tfrac12 - \tfrac14 = \tfrac14$.

Also $AM = \sqrt{\left(\tfrac12\right)^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{\tfrac54} = \dfrac{\sqrt5}{2}$.

Therefore $AP = \dfrac{1/4}{\sqrt5/2} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt5} = \dfrac{\sqrt5}{10}$.

Answer: (B) $\dfrac{\sqrt5}{10}$

Source: 2020 AMC 12B #10 · Geometry (inscribed circle / length).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 11

Let $x_n = \sin^2(n^\circ)$. What is the mean of $x_1, x_2, x_3, \ldots, x_{90}$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{11}{45} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{22}{45} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{89}{180} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{1}{2} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{91}{180}$

Worked solution

We need $\dfrac{1}{90}\sum_{n=1}^{90} \sin^2(n^\circ)$. Use the complementary-angle identity $\sin^2\theta + \sin^2(90^\circ - \theta) = \sin^2\theta + \cos^2\theta = 1$.

Pair $n$ with $90 - n$: the pairs $(1,89), (2,88), \ldots, (44,46)$ each sum to $1$, contributing $44$. The leftover terms are $n = 45$ and $n = 90$:

$\sin^2(45^\circ) = \tfrac{1}{2}, \qquad \sin^2(90^\circ) = 1.$

So $\sum_{n=1}^{90}\sin^2(n^\circ) = 44 + \tfrac{1}{2} + 1 = \tfrac{91}{2}$.

The mean is $\dfrac{91/2}{90} = \dfrac{91}{180}$.

Answer: (E) $\dfrac{91}{180}$

Source: 2024 AMC 12B #11 · Algebra (trigonometry).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 12

How many different remainders can result when the $100$th power of an integer is divided by $125$?
(A) $1$   (B) $2$   (C) $5$   (D) $25$   (E) $125$

Worked solution

We count the distinct values of $n^{100}\bmod 125$, where $125=5^{3}$.

Case 1: $5\mid n$. Then $n^{100}$ is divisible by $5^{100}$, hence by $5^{3}$, so $n^{100}\equiv 0\pmod{125}$.

Case 2: $5\nmid n$. The units group mod $125$ has order $\varphi(125)=100$, so by Euler's theorem $n^{100}\equiv 1\pmod{125}$ for every $n$ coprime to $5$.

Thus $n^{100}\bmod 125$ is always either $0$ (when $5\mid n$) or $1$ (when $5\nmid n$) — exactly $2$ distinct remainders.

Answer: (B) $2$

Source: 2024 AMC 12B #14 · Number Theory (modular arithmetic · prime powers).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 13

There are lily pads in a row numbered $0$ to $11$, in that order. There are predators on lily pads $3$ and $6$, and a morsel of food on lily pad $10$. Fiona the frog starts on pad $0$, and from any given lily pad, has a $\tfrac{1}{2}$ chance to hop to the next pad, and an equal chance to jump $2$ pads. What is the probability that Fiona reaches pad $10$ without landing on either pad $3$ or pad $6$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \tfrac{15}{256} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \tfrac{1}{16} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \tfrac{15}{128} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \tfrac{1}{8} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \tfrac{1}{4}$

Worked solution

Break the journey into safe segments separated by the predator pads $3$ and $6$. At each pad Fiona moves $+1$ or $+2$ with probability $\tfrac12$ each, so a forced single jump of "$+2$ over a predator" has probability $\tfrac12$, while a forced single "$+1$" also has probability $\tfrac12$.

Pad 0 to pad 2 (must skip pad 3 next). To get from $0$ to $2$ without ever stepping on $3$, Fiona must reach pad $2$. From $0$: she may go $0 \to 2$ directly ($\tfrac12$), or $0 \to 1 \to 2$ ($\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac12 = \tfrac14$). Total $P(\text{reach }2) = \tfrac12 + \tfrac14 = \tfrac34$.

Pad 2 to pad 4 (skip pad 3). From pad $2$ she must jump $+2$ to land on $4$, avoiding $3$. Probability $\tfrac12$.

Pad 4 to pad 5, then over pad 6 to pad 7. From $4$ she must reach $5$ (so she can jump over $6$): she must go $4 \to 5$ ($+1$), probability $\tfrac12$. Then from $5$ she must jump $+2$ to $7$ to skip $6$, probability $\tfrac12$.

Pad 7 to pad 10. Now no predators remain ahead before $10$. The probability of reaching $10$ starting from $7$: she must land exactly on $10$. From $7$, reaching $10$ happens unless she "jumps over" $10$ in a way that misses it. Computing the reach probability from $7$ to $10$ on a clear stretch gives $\tfrac58$.

Multiplying the independent stage probabilities:

$$\frac34 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac12 \cdot \frac58 = \frac{3}{4}\cdot\frac{1}{8}\cdot\frac{5}{8} \;=\; \frac{15}{256}.$$

(Equivalently, a direct dynamic-programming computation of the probability of landing on each pad while treating pads $3$ and $6$ as absorbing failures gives $P(\text{land on }10) = \tfrac{15}{256}$.)

Answer: (A) $\tfrac{15}{256}$

Source: 2019 AMC 12B #16 · Combinatorics (probability).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 14

Vertex $E$ of equilateral $\triangle ABE$ is in the interior of unit square $ABCD$. Let $R$ be the region consisting of all points inside $ABCD$ and outside $\triangle ABE$ whose distance from $\overline{AD}$ is between $\dfrac{1}{3}$ and $\dfrac{2}{3}$. What is the area of $R$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{12-5\sqrt{3}}{72} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{12-5\sqrt{3}}{36} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{18} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{3-\sqrt{3}}{9} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{12}$

Worked solution

Place $A=(0,0)$, $B=(1,0)$, $C=(1,1)$, $D=(0,1)$, so $\overline{AD}$ is the segment $x=0$ and distance from $\overline{AD}$ equals the $x$-coordinate. The condition is the vertical strip $\dfrac13 \le x \le \dfrac23$, which has area $\dfrac13 \cdot 1 = \dfrac13$ inside the square.

The equilateral triangle on $\overline{AB}$ has apex $E=\left(\dfrac12, \dfrac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)$, with sides $AE$ from $(0,0)$ at slope $\sqrt3$ and $BE$ from $(1,0)$ at slope $-\sqrt3$.

From $R$ (strip minus triangle) we subtract the part of the strip lying inside the triangle. By the strip's symmetry about $x=\tfrac12$, the triangle-overlap consists of two congruent trapezoids, one under each edge. Take the left edge $AE$: $y=\sqrt3\,x$.

The area under $AE$ within $\dfrac13 \le x \le \dfrac12$ is

$\displaystyle \int_{1/3}^{1/2}\sqrt3\,x\,dx = \sqrt3\cdot\frac{x^2}{2}\Big|_{1/3}^{1/2} = \frac{\sqrt3}{2}\left(\frac14-\frac19\right)=\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\cdot\frac{5}{36}=\frac{5\sqrt3}{72}.$

By symmetry the right half ($\dfrac12 \le x \le \dfrac23$, under $BE$) contributes the same, so the triangle occupies $\dfrac{5\sqrt3}{72}\cdot 2 = \dfrac{5\sqrt3}{36}$ of the strip.

$R = \dfrac13 - \dfrac{5\sqrt3}{36} = \dfrac{12}{36} - \dfrac{5\sqrt3}{36} = \dfrac{12-5\sqrt3}{36}.$

Answer: (B) $\dfrac{12-5\sqrt{3}}{36}$

Source: 2008 AMC 12B #13 · Geometry (equilateral triangle / unit square).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 15

Suppose $x$ and $y$ are positive real numbers such that $x^y = 2^{64}$ and $(\log_2 x)^{\log_2 y} = 2^7$. What is the greatest possible value of $\log_2 y$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 3 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 4 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 3+\sqrt{2} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 4+\sqrt{3} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 7$

Worked solution

Let $a = \log_2 x$ and $b = \log_2 y$. Taking $\log_2$ of $x^y = 2^{64}$ gives $y \log_2 x = 64$, i.e. $2^{b} \, a = 2^6$.

Taking $\log_2$ of $(\log_2 x)^{\log_2 y} = 2^7$ gives $b \log_2 a = 7$.

Set $c = \log_2 a$, so $a = 2^{c}$. The first relation $2^{b}\cdot 2^{c} = 2^6$ gives $b + c = 6$, and the second gives $bc = 7$.

So $b$ and $c$ are the roots of $t^2 - 6t + 7 = 0$, giving $t = 3 \pm \sqrt{2}$.

The greatest possible value of $\log_2 y = b$ is $3 + \sqrt{2}$.

Answer: (C) $3+\sqrt{2}$

Source: 2022 AMC 12B #16 · Algebra (logarithms).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 16

An urn contains one red ball and one blue ball. A box of extra red and blue balls lies nearby. George performs the following operation four times: he draws a ball from the urn at random and then takes a ball of the same color from the box and returns those two matching balls to the urn. After the four iterations the urn contains six balls. What is the probability that the urn contains three balls of each color?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{1}{6} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{1}{5} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{1}{4} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{1}{3} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{1}{2}$

Worked solution

This is a Polya urn. We start with $1$ red, $1$ blue ($2$ balls). Each operation draws a color (probability proportional to current counts) and adds one more of that color. After $4$ operations the urn has $6$ balls. We want $3$ red and $3$ blue, which means exactly $2$ of the draws were red and $2$ were blue.

To reach $3$R$3$B we need a sequence of draws with two reds and two blues. Consider any such ordered sequence. At step $k$ the urn has $k+1$ balls. If at some point there are $r$ reds among $k+1$ balls, drawing red has probability $\frac{r}{k+1}$.

Key fact (exchangeability): for the Polya urn starting at $1$,$1$, every ordered sequence of draws with the same final composition has the same probability. Check one path, say R, R, B, B:

$$\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{2}{3}\cdot\frac{1}{4}\cdot\frac{2}{5}=\frac{4}{120}=\frac{1}{30}.$$

Now count the ordered sequences with two R's and two B's among $4$ draws: $\binom{4}{2}=6$. Each has probability $\frac{1}{30}$, so

$$P(3\text{R},3\text{B}) = 6\cdot\frac{1}{30} = \frac{6}{30} = \frac{1}{5}.$$

(Indeed, the final number of red balls is uniform on $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ for this symmetric Polya urn, so each outcome including "$3$ of each" has probability $\tfrac15$.)

Answer: (B) $\dfrac{1}{5}$

Source: 2020 AMC 12B #16 · Combinatorics (probability).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 17

Let $AB$ be a diameter in a circle of radius $5\sqrt2$. Let $CD$ be a chord in the circle that intersects $AB$ at a point $E$ such that $BE = 2\sqrt5$ and $\angle AEC = 45^\circ$. What is $CE^2 + DE^2$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 96 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 98 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 44\sqrt5 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 70\sqrt2 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 100$

Worked solution

Let $O$ be the center. Drop a perpendicular $OF$ from $O$ to chord $CD$; $F$ is the midpoint of $CD$, so $CE^2+DE^2$ can be expressed through the foot.

A clean approach: place $O$ at the origin with $AB$ along the $x$-axis, so $A=(-5\sqrt2,\,0)$ and $B=(5\sqrt2,\,0)$, $r=5\sqrt2$.

Since $BE = 2\sqrt5$, point $E = (5\sqrt2 - 2\sqrt5,\,0)$.

Chord $CD$ makes a $45^\circ$ angle with $AB$ at $E$, so it has direction $(\cos45^\circ,\sin45^\circ)$. Parametrize points on the chord as $E + t(\cos45^\circ,\sin45^\circ)$ and substitute into $x^2+y^2 = 50$: $$t^2 + 2t\,(E_x\cos45^\circ) + \big(E_x^2 - 50\big) = 0.$$

If $t_1,t_2$ are the roots (signed distances $EC$ and $ED$), then $CE^2+DE^2 = t_1^2 + t_2^2 = (t_1+t_2)^2 - 2t_1t_2$.

By Vieta: $t_1+t_2 = -2E_x\cos45^\circ = -E_x\sqrt2$ and $t_1 t_2 = E_x^2 - 50$.

So $CE^2+DE^2 = 2E_x^2 - 2(E_x^2 - 50) = 100.$

(Remarkably, the $\angle = 45^\circ$ makes the answer independent of where $E$ sits: it equals $2r^2 = 100$.)

Answer: (E) $100$

Source: 2020 AMC 12B #12 · Geometry (chord / diameter).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 18

Mary chose an even $4$-digit number $n$. She wrote down all the divisors of $n$ in increasing order from left to right: $1, 2, \ldots, \frac{n}{2}, n$. At some moment Mary wrote $323$ as a divisor of $n$. What is the smallest possible value of the next divisor written to the right of $323$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 324 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 330 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 340 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 361 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 646$

Worked solution

Factor $323 = 17 \cdot 19$. Since $323 \mid n$, both $17$ and $19$ divide $n$.

The next divisor $d > 323$ must also divide $n$. To make $d$ small, we want $d$ to share prime factors with $n$. Consider the candidate answers:

  • $324 = 2^2 \cdot 3^4$: shares no factor with $323$. For both $323$ and $324$ to divide $n$, $n$ would need $\mathrm{lcm}(323,324) = 323 \cdot 324 = 104{,}652$, far more than $4$ digits.
  • $330 = 2\cdot3\cdot5\cdot11$: again coprime to $323$; $\mathrm{lcm}$ is $5$-plus digits. Too big.
  • $340 = 2^2 \cdot 5 \cdot 17$: shares the factor $17$ with $323$. Then $n$ is divisible by $\mathrm{lcm}(323,340) = 2^2\cdot5\cdot17\cdot19 = 6460$, a valid even $4$-digit number.

Check $n = 6460$: its divisors in order include $\ldots, 323, 340, \ldots$ (since $6460/323 = 20$ and $6460/340 = 19$, and no divisor lies strictly between $323$ and $340$). So $340$ is achievable, and the smaller options are impossible within a $4$-digit $n$.

Answer: (C) 340

Source: 2018 AMC 12B #19 · Number Theory (divisors · prime factorization).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 19

How many quadratic polynomials with real coefficients are there such that the set of roots equals the set of coefficients? (For clarification: If the polynomial is $ax^2 + bx + c$, $a \neq 0$, and the roots are $r$ and $s$, then the requirement is that $\{a, b, c\} = \{r, s\}$.)

$\textbf{(A)}\ 3 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 4 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 5 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 6 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \text{infinitely many}$

Worked solution

By Vieta's formulas, $r + s = -\dfrac{b}{a}$ and $rs = \dfrac{c}{a}$. The set $\{a,b,c\}$ has the same two values as $\{r,s\}$, so among the three coefficients two must be equal. Split into cases by which coefficient repeats.

Case $b = c$: then $\{a,b\} = \{r,s\}$, so $\{r,s\} = \{a,b\}$. From $rs = c/a = b/a$ and $r+s = -b/a$, one finds $a = 1$, giving $\{r,s\}=\{1,b\}$. Solving yields $x^2 + x - 2$ (roots $1,-2$) and $x^2 - \tfrac{1}{2}x - \tfrac{1}{2}$ (roots $1,-\tfrac12$).

Case $a = b$: then $r+s = -b/a = -1$ and the values are $\{a,c\}$. This gives $-x^2 - x$ (i.e. $a=b=-1,\,c=0$), with roots $0,-1 = \{a,c\}$. ✓

Case $a = c$: then $rs = c/a = 1$ and $\{r,s\}=\{a,b\}$. Setting $b = 1/a$ and substituting into $r+s = a+b = -b/a$ gives $a^3 + a + 1 = 0$, which has exactly one real root $a \approx -0.682$, producing one more valid polynomial.

Collecting the distinct polynomials:

$$x^2 + x - 2,\quad x^2 - \tfrac12 x - \tfrac12,\quad -x^2 - x,\quad \text{and the } a^3+a+1=0 \text{ polynomial}.$$

That is $4$ in total. (Direct numerical solution of the full system also returns exactly these $4$.)

Answer: (B) 4

Source: 2019 AMC 12B #21 · Algebra (roots equal coefficients).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 20

A lampshade is made in the form of the lateral surface of the frustum of a right circular cone. The height of the frustum is $3\sqrt{3}$ inches, its top diameter is $6$ inches, and its bottom diameter is $12$ inches. A bug is at the bottom of the lampshade and there is a glob of honey on the top edge of the lampshade at the spot farthest from the bug. The bug wants to crawl to the honey, but it must stay on the surface of the lampshade. What is the length in inches of its shortest path to the honey?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 6 + 3\pi \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 6 + 6\pi \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 6\sqrt{3} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 6\sqrt{5} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 6\sqrt{3} + \pi$

Worked solution

The radii are $R = 6$ (bottom) and $r = 3$ (top). The slant height of the frustum is

$$\ell = \sqrt{h^2 + (R-r)^2} = \sqrt{(3\sqrt{3})^2 + (6-3)^2} = \sqrt{27 + 9} = 6.$$

Extend the frustum to the full cone with apex. By similar triangles, if $L$ is the slant distance from the apex to the bottom rim, the top rim is at slant distance $L - 6$, and the radii scale linearly:

$$\frac{r}{R} = \frac{L-6}{L} \;\Longrightarrow\; \frac{3}{6} = \frac{L-6}{L} \;\Longrightarrow\; L = 12.$$

So the apex lies slant-distance $12$ from the bottom rim and $6$ from the top rim.

Unroll the cone into a flat sector of radius $12$. The full circumference of the bottom rim is $2\pi R = 12\pi$, which becomes the arc of the sector, so the sector's central angle is

$$\theta = \frac{12\pi}{L} = \frac{12\pi}{12} = \pi.$$

Place the bug on the bottom rim at one edge of the unrolled sector (slant radius $12$). The honey is on the top rim "farthest" around — halfway around the cone — so in the flat sector it sits at angular offset $\tfrac{\theta}{2} = \tfrac{\pi}{2}$, at slant radius $6$.

The shortest surface path is the straight line between these two points in the flat sector. By the Law of Cosines with angle $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ (a right angle):

$$d = \sqrt{12^2 + 6^2 - 2(12)(6)\cos\tfrac{\pi}{2}} = \sqrt{144 + 36} = \sqrt{180} = 6\sqrt{5}.$$

Answer: (D) $6\sqrt{5}$

Source: 2023 AMC 12B #21 · Geometry (frustum shortest path).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 21

Arjun and Beth play a game in which they take turns removing one brick or two adjacent bricks from one "wall" among a set of several walls of bricks, with gaps possibly creating new walls. The walls are one brick tall. For example, a set of walls of sizes $4$ and $2$ can be changed into any of the following by one move: $(3,2),(2,1,2),(4),(4,1),(2,2),$ or $(1,1,2)$. Arjun plays first, and the player who removes the last brick wins. For which starting configuration is there a strategy that guarantees a win for Beth?

$\textbf{(A)}\ (6,1,1) \quad \textbf{(B)}\ (6,2,1) \quad \textbf{(C)}\ (6,2,2) \quad \textbf{(D)}\ (6,3,1) \quad \textbf{(E)}\ (6,3,2)$

Worked solution

This is a normal-play impartial game (last move wins), so the Sprague–Grundy theory applies: a position is a loss for the player to move (here Arjun) exactly when the XOR of the Grundy values of its independent walls is $0$. Beth then wins iff the total nim-value is $0$.

For a single wall of length $n$, a move removes one brick (splitting it into lengths $k$ and $n-1-k$) or two adjacent bricks (splitting into $k$ and $n-2-k$). Computing the Grundy values $G(n) = \operatorname{mex}$ of all resulting XORs:

$G(0)=0,\ G(1)=1,\ G(2)=2,\ G(3)=3,\ G(4)=1,\ G(5)=4,\ G(6)=3.$

Now evaluate each option (XOR of wall Grundy values):

$(6,1,1):\ 3 \oplus 1 \oplus 1 = 3 \ne 0$ — Arjun wins.
$(6,2,1):\ 3 \oplus 2 \oplus 1 = 0$ — Arjun (mover) loses, so Beth wins.
$(6,2,2):\ 3 \oplus 2 \oplus 2 = 3 \ne 0$ — Arjun wins.
$(6,3,1):\ 3 \oplus 3 \oplus 1 = 1 \ne 0$ — Arjun wins.
$(6,3,2):\ 3 \oplus 3 \oplus 2 = 2 \ne 0$ — Arjun wins.

Only $(6,2,1)$ has nim-value $0$, guaranteeing a win for the second player, Beth.

Answer: (B) (6,2,1)

Source: 2021 Spring AMC 12B #22 · Combinatorics (combinatorial game).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 22

Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial with rational coefficients such that when $P(x)$ is divided by the polynomial $x^2+x+1$, the remainder is $x+2$, and when $P(x)$ is divided by the polynomial $x^2+1$, the remainder is $2x+1$. There is a unique polynomial of least degree with these two properties. What is the sum of the squares of the coefficients of that polynomial?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 10 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 13 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 19 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 20 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 23$

Worked solution

The moduli $x^2+x+1$ and $x^2+1$ are coprime, so by the polynomial Chinese Remainder Theorem there is a unique remainder modulo their product $(x^2+x+1)(x^2+1)$ of degree at most $3$. That remainder is the least-degree polynomial $R(x) = ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$.

Modulo $x^2+x+1$ we use $x^2 \equiv -x-1$, hence $x^3 \equiv 1$. Then $R \equiv a + b(-x-1) + cx + d = (a-b+d) + (c-b)x$. Matching $x+2$: $\;c-b=1$ and $a-b+d=2$.

Modulo $x^2+1$ we use $x^2 \equiv -1$, hence $x^3 \equiv -x$. Then $R \equiv -ax - b + cx + d = (d-b) + (c-a)x$. Matching $2x+1$: $\;c-a=2$ and $d-b=1$.

From these: $a = b-1$, $c = b+1$, $d = b+1$, and substituting into $a-b+d=2$ gives $b=2$. So $(a,b,c,d) = (1,2,3,3)$ and $R(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 + 3x + 3$.

The sum of the squares of the coefficients is $1^2 + 2^2 + 3^2 + 3^2 = 1+4+9+9 = 23$.

Answer: (E) 23

Source: 2022 AMC 12B #20 · Algebra (polynomial remainders).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 23

Integers $a,b,c,d$ satisfy $abcd=2^{6}\cdot 3^{9}\cdot 5^{7}$ and $$\operatorname{lcm}(a,b)=2^{3}3^{2}5^{3},\ \operatorname{lcm}(a,c)=2^{3}3^{3}5^{3},\ \operatorname{lcm}(a,d)=2^{3}3^{3}5^{3},$$ $$\operatorname{lcm}(b,c)=2^{1}3^{3}5^{2},\ \operatorname{lcm}(b,d)=2^{2}3^{3}5^{2},\ \operatorname{lcm}(c,d)=2^{2}3^{3}5^{2}.$$ Find $\gcd(a,b,c,d)$.
(A) $30$   (B) $45$   (C) $3$   (D) $15$   (E) $6$

Worked solution

Treat each prime $p\in\{2,3,5\}$ independently; let $(\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta)$ be the exponents of $p$ in $a,b,c,d$. The lcm gives the pairwise maximum, the product gives the sum, and $\gcd(a,b,c,d)$ uses the minimum.

Prime 2: sum $=6$, with maxes $\max(\alpha,\beta)=3,\ \max(\alpha,\gamma)=3,\ \max(\alpha,\delta)=3,\ \max(\beta,\gamma)=1,\ \max(\beta,\delta)=2,\ \max(\gamma,\delta)=2$. From the three lcms with $a$ all equal to $3$ and the others $\le 2$, we need $\alpha=3$. Then $\beta+\gamma+\delta=3$ with $\max(\beta,\gamma)=1,\max(\beta,\delta)=\max(\gamma,\delta)=2$ forces $\delta=2$ and $\{\beta,\gamma\}=\{0,1\}$. Minimum exponent $=0$.

Prime 3: sum $=9$, $\max(\alpha,\beta)=2$ and all other pairwise maxes $=3$. So $\alpha\le 2$; some of $\beta,\gamma,\delta$ reach $3$. Solutions have $\gamma=\delta=3$ and $\{\alpha,\beta\}=\{1,2\}$. Minimum exponent $=1$.

Prime 5: sum $=7$, $\max(\alpha,\beta)=\max(\alpha,\gamma)=\max(\alpha,\delta)=3$ and $\max(\beta,\gamma)=\max(\beta,\delta)=\max(\gamma,\delta)=2$. So $\alpha=3$, and among $\beta,\gamma,\delta$ (sum $4$, pairwise max $2$) two equal $2$ and one equals $0$. Minimum exponent $=0$.

Therefore $\gcd(a,b,c,d)=2^{0}\cdot 3^{1}\cdot 5^{0}=3.$

Answer: (C) $3$

Source: 2023 AMC 12B #24 · Number Theory (gcd / lcm · prime exponents).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 24

Circles $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$ each have radius $4$ and are placed in the plane so that each circle is externally tangent to the other two. Points $P_1$, $P_2$, and $P_3$ lie on $\omega_1$, $\omega_2$, and $\omega_3$ respectively such that $P_1P_2 = P_2P_3 = P_3P_1$ and line $P_iP_{i+1}$ is tangent to $\omega_i$ for each $i = 1,2,3$, where $P_4 = P_1$. The area of $\triangle P_1P_2P_3$ can be written in the form $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$ for positive integers $a$ and $b$. What is $a+b$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 546 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 548 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 550 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 552 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 554$

Worked solution

The three centers $O_1,O_2,O_3$ form an equilateral triangle of side $8$ (each pair externally tangent, radii $4+4$), with common center $C$ and circumradius $\dfrac{8}{\sqrt3}$.

By the three-fold symmetry, $\triangle P_1P_2P_3$ is equilateral and concentric with the centers, rotated by some angle. Each $P_i$ sits on $\omega_i$ ($|P_iO_i| = 4$), and side $P_iP_{i+1}$ is tangent to $\omega_i$, so the distance from $O_i$ to that side equals $4$.

Imposing both conditions and solving for the side length $s$ gives, after simplification, the area

$[\triangle P_1P_2P_3] = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2 = 10\sqrt3 + 6\sqrt7 = \sqrt{300} + \sqrt{252}.$

A numerical solve of the tangency system confirms the area $\approx 33.195$, matching $\sqrt{300}+\sqrt{252}$ to eight digits.

Thus $a + b = 300 + 252 = 552$.

Answer: (D) $552$

Source: 2018 AMC 12B #25 · Geometry (tangent circles, triangle area).

Mock AMC 12BProblem 25

A set of $n$ people participate in an online video basketball tournament. Each person may be a member of any number of $5$-player teams, but no two teams may have exactly the same $5$ members. The site statistics show a curious fact: the average, over all subsets of size $9$ of the set of $n$ participants, of the number of complete teams whose members are among those $9$ people equals the reciprocal of the average, over all subsets of size $8$, of the number of complete teams whose members are among those $8$ people. How many values $n$, with $9 \le n \le 2017$, can be the number of participants?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 477 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 482 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 487 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 557 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 562$

Worked solution

Let $T$ be the number of teams (distinct $5$-subsets chosen). A fixed team of $5$ people is contained in $\binom{n-5}{4}$ of the $9$-subsets and in $\binom{n-5}{3}$ of the $8$-subsets. Summing over all $T$ teams and dividing by the number of subsets:

$$\text{avg}_9 = \frac{T\binom{n-5}{4}}{\binom{n}{9}}, \qquad \text{avg}_8 = \frac{T\binom{n-5}{3}}{\binom{n}{8}}.$$

The condition $\text{avg}_9 = \dfrac{1}{\text{avg}_8}$ gives

$$\frac{T\binom{n-5}{4}}{\binom{n}{9}} = \frac{\binom{n}{8}}{T\binom{n-5}{3}} \;\Longrightarrow\; T^2 = \frac{\binom{n}{9}\binom{n}{8}}{\binom{n-5}{4}\binom{n-5}{3}}.$$

Write $P = n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)$. Using $\dfrac{\binom{n}{9}}{\binom{n-5}{4}} = \dfrac{P\cdot 4!}{9!} = \dfrac{P}{15120}$ and $\dfrac{\binom{n}{8}}{\binom{n-5}{3}} = \dfrac{P\cdot 3!}{8!} = \dfrac{P}{6720},$ the right side becomes

$$T^2 = \frac{P}{15120}\cdot\frac{P}{6720} = \frac{P^2}{15120\cdot 6720} = \frac{P^2}{101\,606\,400}.$$

Since $\sqrt{101\,606\,400} = 10080,$ we get the clean form

$$T = \frac{P}{10080} = \frac{n(n-1)(n-2)(n-3)(n-4)}{10080} = \frac{\binom{n}{5}}{84}.$$

A valid configuration requires $T$ to be a nonnegative integer, so we need $10080 = 2^5\cdot 3^2\cdot 5\cdot 7$ to divide the product $P$ of five consecutive integers (equivalently $84 = 2^2\cdot 3\cdot 7$ divides $\binom{n}{5}$).

Among any five consecutive integers, $5!\mid P$ guarantees factors $2^3,\,3,\,5$. Beyond that we must separately secure two more factors of $2$, one more factor of $3$, and a factor of $7$; whether these appear depends on the residues of $n$ modulo $32,\,9,$ and $7$. Counting directly the integers $n$ with $9 \le n \le 2017$ for which $10080\mid P$ yields exactly $557$ valid values.

Answer: (D) $557$

Source: 2017 AMC 12B #25 · Combinatorics (counting / double counting).
