Chapter 9 — Introduction to Circles

A circle is the set of all points a fixed distance from one point. That single defining property — equal distance in every direction — is the source of every formula that follows. We'll name the parts, derive the circumference and area, and then carve the circle into arcs and sectors.

9.1 The Parts of a Circle

The fixed point is the center $O$; the fixed distance is the radius $r$. A circle is often named by its center — "circle $O$." The building blocks:

Vocabulary

Chord — a segment with both endpoints on the circle. Diameter $d$ — a chord through the center; the longest chord. Arc — a piece of the circle's curve. Tangent — a line touching the circle at exactly one point. Secant — a line cutting the circle at two points. Sector — the pie-slice region between two radii. Segment — the region between a chord and its arc.

Since a diameter is two radii laid end to end through the center, $d = 2r$ — a fact we'll use constantly.

9.2 Circumference and Area

The circumference is the distance around the circle. Here is the deep experimental fact: for every circle, the ratio of circumference to diameter is the same constant. We call that constant $\pi \approx 3.14159$. (Wrap a tape measure around any can and divide by its width — you'll get $\pi$ every time.) Solving $C/d = \pi$ gives the circumference, and a standard limiting argument — slicing the disk into thin wedges and rearranging them into a near-rectangle of height $r$ and base $\tfrac12 C$ — gives the area:

Circumference & Area $$C = \pi d = 2\pi r \qquad A = \pi r^2$$

The wedge picture is worth seeing: cut the disk into many sectors and alternate them point-up / point-down. They interlock into a shape approaching a rectangle whose width is half the circumference, $\pi r$, and whose height is the radius $r$. Its area is $(\pi r)(r) = \pi r^2$.

Keep $r$ and $d$ straight. The radius is squared in the area, so a stray factor of 2 becomes a factor of 4. If a problem gives the diameter, halve it before plugging into $A = \pi r^2$.
📝 Example 9-1 — area to circumference

The area of a circle is $16$. Find its circumference.

From $\pi r^2 = 16$ we get $r^2 = \dfrac{16}{\pi}$, so $r = \dfrac{4}{\sqrt\pi}$. Then

$$C = 2\pi r = 2\pi\cdot\frac{4}{\sqrt\pi} = \frac{8\pi}{\sqrt\pi} = 8\sqrt\pi.$$
📝 Example 9-2 — most area for the fencing

What is the maximum area enclosable with $12$ feet of fencing? Among all shapes of a given perimeter, the circle encloses the most area (convince yourself; the rigorous proof took mathematicians a long time). So make the fence a circle of circumference $12$.

Then $2\pi r = 12 \Rightarrow r = \dfrac{6}{\pi}$, and

$$A = \pi r^2 = \pi\left(\frac{6}{\pi}\right)^2 = \pi\cdot\frac{36}{\pi^2} = \frac{36}{\pi}\text{ square feet}.$$
✏️ Exercise 9-1 — circumference from area

What is the circumference of a circle whose area is $8\pi$? (Find $r$ from $\pi r^2 = 8\pi$, then double and multiply by $\pi$.)

✏️ Exercise 9-2 — two wires

A wire $72$ cm long is cut into two equal pieces, each bent into a circle. Find the sum of the two areas. (Each piece has circumference $36$.)

9.3 Central Angles and Arc Measure

A central angle has its vertex at the center $O$ and its sides along two radii. The measure of the arc it cuts off is defined to equal the central angle. A full trip around the circle is $360^\circ$, so a half-circle (a diameter) cuts a $180^\circ$ arc, a quarter cuts $90^\circ$, and so on.

When two letters name an arc, $\overset{\frown}{AC}$, the minor (shorter) arc is meant; three letters $\overset{\frown}{ABC}$ name the path through $B$, which lets you specify the major arc. Minor and major arcs of the same chord add to $360^\circ$.

9.4 Arc Length and Sector Area

Here is the key idea, and it removes the need to memorize two more formulas: an arc and its sector are the same fraction of the whole circle as their central angle is of $360^\circ$. A $90^\circ$ central angle is $\tfrac{90}{360} = \tfrac14$ of the circle, so it owns a quarter of the circumference and a quarter of the area.

Fractions of the whole (angle $\theta$ in degrees) $$\text{arc length} = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\,(2\pi r) \qquad \text{sector area} = \frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\,(\pi r^2)$$

In radians, where a full circle is $2\pi$, the fraction $\tfrac{\theta}{2\pi}$ collapses these to the cleaner $s = r\theta$ and $A = \tfrac12 r^2\theta$ — one reason radians are the physicist's angle of choice.

📝 Example — a 60° slice of radius 12

Find the arc length and sector area for a $60^\circ$ central angle in a circle of radius $12$. The fraction is $\tfrac{60}{360} = \tfrac16$.

$$\text{arc} = \frac16(2\pi\cdot 12) = \frac16(24\pi) = 4\pi, \qquad \text{sector} = \frac16(\pi\cdot 12^2) = \frac16(144\pi) = 24\pi.$$
✏️ Exercise — quarter sector

A circle has radius $10$. Find the arc length and area of a $90^\circ$ sector. (The fraction is $\tfrac14$.)

💡 Big Picture — circles ruled the heavens For a millennium and a half, Western astronomers built the entire cosmos from circles — the "perfect" curve. When one circle couldn't match a planet's wandering, they stacked epicycles: circles riding on circles. Copernicus (1543) moved the Sun to the center but kept the circles. Only with Kepler in the early 1600s did ellipses replace circles as the true shape of orbits — but the circle's fraction-of-the-whole logic still underlies how we measure any angular sweep.

9.5 Tangent Lines

A tangent line touches a circle at exactly one point, the point of tangency. The single most useful tangent fact:

Tangent ⟂ Radius $$\text{A tangent line is perpendicular to the radius drawn to the point of tangency.}$$

Why? The point of tangency is the closest point of the line to the center (every other point of the line lies outside the circle, hence farther than $r$). The shortest segment from a point to a line is the perpendicular — so the radius meets the tangent at $90^\circ$. This turns tangent problems into right-triangle problems: a radius, a tangent segment, and the line to the external point form a right angle, ready for the Pythagorean theorem.

📝 Example — length of a tangent

From an external point $P$ that is $13$ units from center $O$, a tangent touches a circle of radius $5$ at point $T$. Find $PT$. Because $OT \perp PT$, triangle $OTP$ is right-angled at $T$, so

$$PT = \sqrt{OP^2 - OT^2} = \sqrt{13^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} = 12.$$
✏️ Exercise — tangent from a point

A tangent from external point $P$ touches a circle of radius $9$ at $T$, and $PT = 12$. How far is $P$ from the center? (Right triangle: $OP = \sqrt{9^2 + 12^2}$.)

Circles are the geometry of anything that goes around. A point on a wheel of radius $r$ spinning at angular velocity $\omega$ (radians per second) moves at linear speed $v = \omega r$ — the same $s = r\theta$ relation, differentiated. A fly on the rim of a record making $150$ revolutions per minute travels $150$ circumferences, $150(2\pi r)$, each minute; converting angular motion to linear motion this way is daily work for every engineer. The same formulas govern orbital motion (centripetal acceleration $a = \omega^2 r = v^2/r$ holds a satellite in its circle) and wavefronts — ripples from a dropped stone or light from a point source spread as expanding circles, their circumference $2\pi r$ setting how a fixed amount of energy thins out with distance.

2008 AMC 12BProblem 4 circle / sector

On circle $O$, points $C$ and $D$ are on the same side of diameter $\overline{AB}$, $\angle AOC = 30^\circ$, and $\angle DOB = 45^\circ$. What is the ratio of the area of the smaller sector $COD$ to the area of the circle?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \dfrac{2}{9} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{1}{4} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{5}{18} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{7}{24} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{3}{10}$

Worked solution

The diameter $\overline{AB}$ spans $180^\circ$ from $A$ to $B$. Going around the same side, the central angle $\angle AOB = 180^\circ$ is split into $\angle AOC$, $\angle COD$, and $\angle DOB$.

$\angle COD = 180^\circ - \angle AOC - \angle DOB = 180^\circ - 30^\circ - 45^\circ = 105^\circ.$

A sector's area is proportional to its central angle, so the ratio to the whole circle is

$\dfrac{105^\circ}{360^\circ} = \dfrac{7}{24}.$

Answer: (D) $\dfrac{7}{24}$

2008 AMC 12BProblem 9 circle / chord

Points $A$ and $B$ are on a circle of radius $5$ and $AB = 6$. Point $C$ is the midpoint of the minor arc $AB$. What is the length of the line segment $AC$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ \sqrt{10} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{7}{2} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \sqrt{14} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \sqrt{15} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 4$

Worked solution

Let $O$ be the center. The perpendicular from $O$ to chord $\overline{AB}$ bisects it, meeting $\overline{AB}$ at its midpoint $M$, with $AM = 3$.

$OM = \sqrt{OA^2 - AM^2} = \sqrt{5^2 - 3^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4.$

$C$, the midpoint of the minor arc, lies on line $OM$ on the near side, so $MC = OC - OM = 5 - 4 = 1.$

Since $\overline{OM} \perp \overline{AB}$, triangle $AMC$ is right-angled at $M$:

$AC = \sqrt{AM^2 + MC^2} = \sqrt{3^2 + 1^2} = \sqrt{10}.$

Answer: (A) $\sqrt{10}$

2018 AMC 12BProblem 4 circle, chord

A circle has a chord of length $10$, and the distance from the center of the circle to the chord is $5$. What is the area of the circle?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 25\pi \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 50\pi \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 75\pi \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 100\pi \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 125\pi$

Worked solution

Drop a perpendicular from the center to the chord. It bisects the chord, giving a right triangle with legs equal to half the chord, $5$, and the distance to the chord, $5$. The hypotenuse is the radius $r$.

$r^2 = 5^2 + 5^2 = 50$.

The area is $\pi r^2 = 50\pi$.

Answer: (B) $50\pi$

2020 AMC 12BProblem 12 chord / diameter

Let $AB$ be a diameter in a circle of radius $5\sqrt2$. Let $CD$ be a chord in the circle that intersects $AB$ at a point $E$ such that $BE = 2\sqrt5$ and $\angle AEC = 45^\circ$. What is $CE^2 + DE^2$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 96 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 98 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 44\sqrt5 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 70\sqrt2 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 100$

Worked solution

Let $O$ be the center. Drop a perpendicular $OF$ from $O$ to chord $CD$; $F$ is the midpoint of $CD$, so $CE^2+DE^2$ can be expressed through the foot.

A clean approach: place $O$ at the origin with $AB$ along the $x$-axis, so $A=(-5\sqrt2,\,0)$ and $B=(5\sqrt2,\,0)$, $r=5\sqrt2$.

Since $BE = 2\sqrt5$, point $E = (5\sqrt2 - 2\sqrt5,\,0)$.

Chord $CD$ makes a $45^\circ$ angle with $AB$ at $E$, so it has direction $(\cos45^\circ,\sin45^\circ)$. Parametrize points on the chord as $E + t(\cos45^\circ,\sin45^\circ)$ and substitute into $x^2+y^2 = 50$: $$t^2 + 2t\,(E_x\cos45^\circ) + \big(E_x^2 - 50\big) = 0.$$

If $t_1,t_2$ are the roots (signed distances $EC$ and $ED$), then $CE^2+DE^2 = t_1^2 + t_2^2 = (t_1+t_2)^2 - 2t_1t_2$.

By Vieta: $t_1+t_2 = -2E_x\cos45^\circ = -E_x\sqrt2$ and $t_1 t_2 = E_x^2 - 50$.

So $CE^2+DE^2 = 2E_x^2 - 2(E_x^2 - 50) = 100.$

(Remarkably, the $\angle = 45^\circ$ makes the answer independent of where $E$ sits: it equals $2r^2 = 100$.)

Answer: (E) $100$

2021 Spring AMC 12BProblem 8 circles & chords

Three equally spaced parallel lines intersect a circle, creating three chords of lengths $38,38,$ and $34$. What is the distance between two adjacent parallel lines?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 5\tfrac12 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 6 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 6\tfrac12 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 7 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 7\tfrac12$

Worked solution

The two equal chords of length $38$ are symmetric about the center, so their lines sit at signed distances $-\tfrac{d}{2}$ and $+\tfrac{d}{2}$ from the center, where $d$ is the spacing. The chord of length $34$ then lies on the next parallel line, at distance $\tfrac{3d}{2}$.

Using $\left(\tfrac{\text{chord}}{2}\right)^2+(\text{distance})^2=r^2$ for the $38$ chord and the $34$ chord:

$19^2+\left(\tfrac{d}{2}\right)^2 = 17^2+\left(\tfrac{3d}{2}\right)^2.$

$361+\tfrac{d^2}{4}=289+\tfrac{9d^2}{4}\implies 72=2d^2\implies d^2=36\implies d=6.$

Answer: (B) $6$

Chapter 10 — Angles

An angle is what you get when two rays share an origin. Almost every geometry problem eventually comes down to chasing angles around a diagram, so the single most valuable habit is to draw the picture accurately and mark every equal angle you can find. This chapter builds the toolkit: how angles are measured, how they are named, and the handful of equalities — vertical angles, parallel-line angles, the inscribed angle — that you will use constantly.

10.1 Measuring Angles in Degrees

Two rays sharing an origin form an angle; the shared origin is the vertex and the rays are the sides. We name an angle with the $\angle$ symbol, putting the vertex in the middle: $\angle AOB$. When only one angle has a given vertex, $\angle O$ is enough.

We measure an angle as the fraction of a circle (centered at the vertex) that it cuts off. A full circle is $360^\circ$, so a quarter-circle is $90^\circ$.

Degrees, minutes, seconds — and radians $$1^\circ = 60' \qquad 1' = 60'' \qquad 360^\circ = 2\pi \text{ radians}$$ Use $\dfrac{360^\circ}{2\pi}$ as a conversion factor between degrees and radians.

Why $360$? It is arbitrary, but $360$ is divisible by many numbers, so common fractions of a circle stay whole: a third is $120^\circ$, a sixth is $60^\circ$. Had a circle been $100$ units, those would be the ugly $100/3$ and $50/3$.

A "minute" of angle is $1/60$ of a degree, not a minute of time, and a "second" of angle is $1/60$ of that. The notation $20^\circ 10' 5''$ packs all three together — don't read the marks as feet/inches.
📝 Example 10-1 — converting between degrees and radians

How many degrees are in $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ radians, and how many radians are in $135^\circ$?

$$\frac{\pi}{3}\cdot\frac{360^\circ}{2\pi} = 60^\circ \qquad\qquad 135^\circ\cdot\frac{2\pi}{360^\circ} = \frac{3\pi}{4}$$
📝 Example 10-2 — degrees, minutes, seconds

Write $20\tfrac{5}{9}^\circ$ in minutes and seconds. In $\tfrac{5}{9}$ of a degree there are $\tfrac{5}{9}\cdot 60 = 33\tfrac13$ minutes; in $\tfrac13$ of a minute there are $\tfrac13\cdot 60 = 20$ seconds. So $20\tfrac{5}{9}^\circ = 20^\circ 33' 20''$.

✏️ Exercise — quick conversions

Convert $\tfrac{\pi}{6}$ rad and $\tfrac{5\pi}{4}$ rad to degrees; convert $30^\circ$ and $210^\circ$ to radians; write $0.5^\circ$ in minutes.

10.2 Classifying Angles by Size

Angles are sorted by how they compare to the $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ benchmarks.

The five types
  • Acute: less than $90^\circ$
  • Right: exactly $90^\circ$ (sides are perpendicular, written $\overline{AB}\perp\overline{CD}$; marked with a little box)
  • Obtuse: between $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$
  • Straight: exactly $180^\circ$ — the two rays form a line
  • Reflex: more than $180^\circ$

Angles are often named with Greek letters — most often $\theta$, sometimes $\phi$, $\alpha$, $\beta$, or $\gamma$.

"Perpendicular" requires exactly $90^\circ$; a steep-looking acute angle is not perpendicular. Always check for the little right-angle box in a figure before assuming $90^\circ$ — diagrams are not to be trusted unless marked.

10.3 Complementary, Supplementary & Vertical Angles

Two relationships come from the $90^\circ$ and $180^\circ$ benchmarks, and one comes from crossing lines.

Definitions $$\text{Complementary: } \alpha + \beta = 90^\circ \qquad \text{Supplementary: } \alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$$

Now intersect two lines. The four angles at the crossing come in two pairs of vertical angles (opposite each other). Each angle and its neighbor lie on a straight line, so they are supplementary; subtracting shows the opposite angles are equal.

Vertical angles are equal Since $\alpha + \beta = 180^\circ$ and $\alpha + \theta = 180^\circ$, we get $\theta = \beta$. Opposite (vertical) angles are always equal.
📝 Example — a complement and a vertical angle

Two lines cross; one of the four angles is $35^\circ$. Its vertical partner is also $35^\circ$, and each adjacent angle is $180^\circ - 35^\circ = 145^\circ$. If a third ray makes the $35^\circ$ angle complementary to some $\beta$, then $\beta = 90^\circ - 35^\circ = 55^\circ$.

✏️ Exercise — fill in the four angles

Two lines intersect and one angle measures $112^\circ$. Find the other three.

10.4 Parallel Lines Cut by a Transversal

Parallel lines lie in the same plane and never meet; we write $\ell \parallel m$. A line that crosses both is a transversal, and it creates eight angles whose relationships are the workhorses of angle-chasing.

When $\ell \parallel m$
  • Corresponding (same position at each crossing) are equal: $a=e,\ b=f,\ c=g,\ d=h$.
  • Alternate interior (between the lines, opposite sides) are equal: $c=f$ and $d=e$.
  • Alternate exterior (outside the lines, opposite sides) are equal: $a=h$ and $b=g$.
  • Co-interior / same-side interior are supplementary: $c+e=180^\circ$ and $d+f=180^\circ$.

You only need one fact plus vertical angles to get the rest. For instance, $c$ and $f$ are alternate interior (equal); $f$ and $e$ are supplementary on line $m$; so $c$ and $e$ are co-interior and supplementary.

These equalities hold only when the lines are actually parallel. If you can't establish $\ell \parallel m$, you cannot conclude corresponding angles are equal — and conversely, equal corresponding angles are precisely how you prove two lines parallel.
📝 Example 10-3 — the angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$

Through vertex $C$ of $\triangle ABC$ draw a line $\ell$ parallel to side $AB$. By alternate interior angles, $\angle BAC = \angle ACE$ and $\angle ABC = \angle BCD$, where $E$ and $D$ are the new directions along $\ell$. Those three angles ($\angle ACE$, $\angle ACB$, $\angle BCD$) lie on a straight line, so

$$\alpha + \beta + \gamma = 180^\circ.$$

Adding a parallel line is one of the most powerful tricks in geometry.

📝 Example 10-4 — Exterior Angle Theorem

Extend a side past a vertex to form an exterior angle $\theta$. The two non-adjacent (remote) interior angles are $\alpha$ and $\beta$. Since $\alpha+\beta+\gamma=180^\circ$ and $\gamma+\theta=180^\circ$, subtracting gives $\theta = \alpha + \beta$: an exterior angle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles.

✏️ Exercise 10-1 — exterior angles of a triangle

Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma$ be the exterior angles at $A,B,C$ of $\triangle ABC$. Show $\alpha+\beta+\gamma = 360^\circ$.

💡 Big Picture — mark equal angles as you go The instant you spot a pair of vertical, corresponding, or alternate interior angles, mark them equal on the diagram (one arc for the first set, two arcs for a second set, tick marks for equal lengths). Good problems are usually solved by accumulating these little equalities until the unknown is surrounded — not by a single clever formula. An accurate, well-marked picture is the most important problem-solving tool you have.

10.5 Angles Around a Point & the Inscribed Angle

The rays out of a single point sweep a full circle, so all the angles around a point add to $360^\circ$. (This is just "a circle has $360^\circ$" applied at the vertex.) When that point is the center of a circle, the angle is a central angle and it equals the arc it cuts off.

Angles around a point $$\theta_1 + \theta_2 + \cdots + \theta_n = 360^\circ$$

Moving the vertex from the center onto the circle halves the effect. An angle whose vertex is on the circle and whose sides are chords is an inscribed angle.

Inscribed Angle Theorem An inscribed angle is half the arc it intercepts: $\;\angle ABC = \tfrac12 \overset{\frown}{AC}.$ Two consequences used constantly:
  • Inscribed angles subtending the same arc are equal.
  • An angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (it cuts off a $180^\circ$ arc).
The inscribed angle theorem needs the vertex on the circle and both sides to be chords. If the vertex is at the center it is a central angle (equal to the full arc, not half); if the vertex is inside or outside the circle, different rules apply. Don't blur these cases.
📝 Example 10-5 — angle in a semicircle

If $AC$ is a diameter, then arc $AC = 180^\circ$, so any inscribed angle $\angle ABC = 180^\circ/2 = 90^\circ$. Every angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle.

✏️ Exercise — chase an inscribed angle

A central angle cuts off a $70^\circ$ arc. What is an inscribed angle subtending the same arc? If a second inscribed angle subtends the same arc from elsewhere on the circle, what is its measure?

💡 Big Picture — hidden equal angles "Equal inscribed angles subtending the same arc" is the sneakiest tool in the chapter. If you must prove two angles equal and their sides meet at two points, ask: does a circle pass through all four points (the two vertices and the two intersection points)? If so, the angles are inscribed angles on the same arc — and therefore equal. This is one of the most common ways to prove an angle equality.
Angles are the language of direction in physics. In optics, the law of reflection ($\theta_i = \theta_r$, angles measured from the normal) and Snell's law of refraction ($n_1\sin\theta_1 = n_2\sin\theta_2$) both hinge on careful angle measurement, and total internal reflection is governed by a single critical angle. Resolving a force or velocity into vector components uses $\theta$ through $\cos\theta$ and $\sin\theta$ — the same complementary pair ($\cos\theta = \sin(90^\circ-\theta)$) seen in 10.3. And radian measure (10.1) is the natural unit for rotational motion: arc length $s = r\theta$, angular velocity $\omega$, and the small-angle approximation $\sin\theta \approx \theta$ that underlies pendulums, diffraction, and lens equations all require angles in radians, not degrees.

Chapter 11 — Triangles

Nearly all of plane geometry reduces to the simplest closed figure: the three-sided triangle. Master a handful of theorems — most of which we'll derive — and you have mastered basic geometry. This is the longest chapter for a reason; take it one section at a time.

11.1 Classifying Triangles & the Angle Sum

The three points where the sides meet are the vertices; the sum of the three sides is the perimeter $p = a + b + c$, and half of that, $s = p/2$, is the semiperimeter. We label so that side $a = BC$ sits opposite vertex $A$, side $b = AC$ opposite $B$, and side $c = AB$ opposite $C$.

The Angle Sum $$\angle A + \angle B + \angle C = 180^\circ$$

Why? Draw a line through one vertex parallel to the opposite side. The two "outer" angles become alternate-interior angles equal to the other two triangle angles, and together with the vertex angle they fill a straight line — exactly $180^\circ$.

By angles: all three acute → acute; one right → right (its other two angles are complementary, summing to $90^\circ$); one obtuse → obtuse. In a right triangle the side opposite the right angle is the hypotenuse; the other two are legs.

By sides: all three equal → equilateral (so all angles equal $60^\circ$, since $3\times 60 = 180$); exactly two equal → isosceles; none equal → scalene. In an isosceles triangle the two equal sides are the legs, the third is the base, the angle between the legs is the vertex angle, and the two equal base angles lie opposite the legs.

A triangle has at most one right or obtuse angle — two would already use up $180^\circ$ or more, leaving nothing for the third. So "right isosceles" is fine, but "two right angles" is impossible.
📝 Example — why equilateral forces $60^\circ$

Equal sides force equal opposite angles, so all three angles are equal. Calling each $x$, the angle sum gives $3x = 180^\circ$, hence $x = 60^\circ$.

11.2 Isosceles & Equilateral Triangles

The single most-used fact about isosceles triangles is the equivalence of equal sides and equal angles — each direction proved by dropping an altitude to the base and using congruent right triangles.

Isosceles Base-Angle Theorem (both directions) $$AB = AC \iff \angle B = \angle C$$

Proof of $\Leftarrow$ (equal angles ⇒ equal sides): drop the altitude $AX$ from the vertex not on the equal angles. Then $AX = AX$, $\angle B = \angle C$, and both contain a right angle at $X$, so $\triangle AXB \cong \triangle AXC$ (right-triangle side-angle case). Hence $AB = AC$. The reverse direction drops the same altitude and uses the equal sides.

💡 Big Picture — symmetry is the secret Every isosceles triangle has a line of symmetry: the perpendicular bisector of the base, which is simultaneously the median, the altitude, and the angle bisector from the apex. In an equilateral triangle this happens three ways at once, so the centroid, incenter, orthocenter, and circumcenter all collapse to a single point. Spotting such coincident lines turns many hard problems into one-step problems.
✏️ Exercise — concurrency in special triangles

Show that in an isosceles triangle the centroid, incenter, orthocenter, and circumcenter all lie on one line (the axis of symmetry), and that in an equilateral triangle they coincide.

11.3 The Triangle Inequality

Try to build a triangle with sides $1$, $8$, and $11$ cm. Starting at $A$ with $AB = 8$, then stepping $1$ cm to $C$, the point $C$ can reach at most $8 + 1 = 9$ cm from $A$ — never $11$. The straight path between two points is the shortest, so a detour through a third vertex must be longer.

Triangle Inequality $$|a - b| < c < a + b$$

Each side is less than the sum, and more than the difference, of the other two. Equality $c = a+b$ gives a degenerate triangle — a flat segment.

📝 Example 11-2 — bounds on the third side

Two sides are $7$ and $13$; let the third be $x$. We need $x + 7 > 13 \Rightarrow x > 6$, and $7 + 13 > x \Rightarrow x < 20$ (the condition $x + 13 > 7$ is automatic). So $6 < x < 20$.

"$<$" is strict. A side equal to the sum of the other two is not a triangle — it is a degenerate, collinear figure with zero area. Always test all three inequalities, though checking only "largest side $<$ sum of the other two" suffices.
✏️ Exercise 11-4 — counting triangles

In how many ways can three distinct numbers from $\{1,2,3,4,5\}$ form a non-degenerate triangle?

11.4 The Pythagorean Theorem

The most famous theorem in geometry: in a right triangle, the squares on the legs add to the square on the hypotenuse.

Pythagorean Theorem $$a^2 + b^2 = c^2 \quad(\text{legs } a,b,\ \text{hypotenuse } c)$$

Derivation (area dissection). Arrange four copies of the right triangle inside a big square of side $a+b$, leaving a tilted inner square of side $c$. Compute the big square's area two ways: as side$^2$ and as inner square plus four triangles.

The algebra $$c^2 = (a-b)^2 + 4\cdot\tfrac12 ab = (a^2 - 2ab + b^2) + 2ab = a^2 + b^2$$

The converse holds too: if $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ the triangle must be right. (For an obtuse triangle with $a c^2$.) Always test this when handed three side lengths.

Pythagorean triples. Integer solutions $(a,b,c)$ let you skip the arithmetic. Memorize $(3,4,5)$, $(5,12,13)$, $(7,24,25)$, $(8,15,17)$ — and remember that scaling by any $n$ gives another triple, since $(na)^2 + (nb)^2 = n^2(a^2+b^2) = (nc)^2$.

📝 Example 11-3 / 11-8 — use the theorem, then a triple

Legs $8$ and $4$: hypotenuse $=\sqrt{8^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{80} = 4\sqrt5$.

Hypotenuse $4.25$, one leg $2$: the ratio leg:hyp $= 2:4.25 = 8:17$, matching $(8,15,17)$ scaled by $\tfrac14$. So the other leg is $15\cdot\tfrac14 = 3.75$. Check: $2^2 + 3.75^2 = 4 + 14.0625 = 18.0625 = 4.25^2$. ✓

✏️ Exercise 11-8 — the sliding ladder

A $25$-ft ladder rests against a wall with its foot $7$ ft out (so it reaches $\sqrt{25^2-7^2}=24$ ft up). If the top slips down $4$ ft to $20$ ft, the new foot distance is $\sqrt{25^2-20^2}=15$ ft, so the foot slides $15-7 = 8$ ft.

11.5 Special Right Triangles

Two right triangles appear so often that their side ratios are worth memorizing — and both ratios drop straight out of the Pythagorean Theorem.

45-45-90 (isosceles right) $$\text{leg} : \text{leg} : \text{hyp} = 1 : 1 : \sqrt2$$

Both legs $a$, so the hypotenuse is $\sqrt{a^2 + a^2} = a\sqrt2$. Hence $\sin 45^\circ = \cos 45^\circ = \dfrac{a}{a\sqrt2} = \dfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$ and $\tan 45^\circ = 1$.

30-60-90 (half an equilateral triangle) $$\text{(opp }30^\circ) : (\text{opp }60^\circ) : \text{hyp} = 1 : \sqrt3 : 2$$

Split an equilateral triangle of side $2$ down its altitude: the base halves to $1$, the hypotenuse stays $2$, so the short leg (opposite $30^\circ$) is half the hypotenuse, and the long leg is $\sqrt{2^2 - 1^2} = \sqrt3$. Thus $\sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12$, $\cos 30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$, $\tan 30^\circ = \tfrac{\sqrt3}{3}$.

Don't mix up which leg is which. The side opposite the $30^\circ$ angle is the short leg (length $1$ in the ratio), so $\sin 30^\circ = \tfrac12$ — never $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}$. When in doubt, sketch the triangle: the smallest angle faces the smallest side.
📝 Example 11-17 — chaining the two specials

In $\triangle ABC$, $AB = 8$, $\angle ABC = 45^\circ$, $\angle ACB = 30^\circ$. Drop altitude $AD$. From the 45-45-90 piece $BD = AD = \tfrac{8}{\sqrt2} = 4\sqrt2$. From the 30-60-90 piece, $AD$ is opposite $30^\circ$ so $CD = AD\sqrt3 = 4\sqrt6$. Then $BC = BD + CD = 4\sqrt2 + 4\sqrt6$.

11.6 Congruent Triangles

Two triangles are congruent ($\triangle ABC \cong \triangle DEF$, vertices written in matching order) if one is an exact copy of the other — all corresponding sides and angles equal. You never need all six equalities; any one of these criteria suffices.

Congruence criteria (any triangle)

SSS — three pairs of equal sides.
SAS — two sides and the included angle.
ASA — two angles and the included side.
AAS — two angles and a non-included side (equivalent to ASA, since the third angle is then forced).

There is no SSA congruence. If the equal angle is not between the two equal sides, the triangles can differ (the "ambiguous case"). The angle in SAS must sit between the two sides you are using.
For right triangles you also get HL (hypotenuse + a leg) and LL (two legs, really just SAS). Even here, never read these as licensing SSA for general triangles.
📝 Example 11-9 — read off equal parts

Given $\triangle ABC \cong \triangle BAD$ with $\angle ABC = \angle BAD = 60^\circ$ and $\angle BAC = 70^\circ$. Corresponding parts give $\angle D = \angle C = 180^\circ - 70^\circ - 60^\circ = 50^\circ$.

✏️ Exercise — chords subtend equal arcs

Given equal chords $AB = CD$ in a circle, use congruent triangles (two radii plus the chord, SSS) to show the minor arcs $AB$ and $CD$ are equal.

11.7 Similar Triangles & Ratios

Two triangles are similar ($\triangle ABC \sim \triangle DEF$) when one is a scaled copy of the other: equal angles and a constant ratio $k$ between corresponding sides. Every corresponding length — sides, medians, altitudes — scales by $k$, and areas scale by $k^2$.

Similarity criteria

AA — two pairs of equal angles (the third follows from the $180^\circ$ sum). By far the most used.
SAS — one equal angle between two pairs of proportional sides.
SSS — all three side ratios equal.

$$\frac{a}{a'} = \frac{b}{b'} = \frac{c}{c'} = k, \qquad \frac{[\triangle_1]}{[\triangle_2]} = k^2$$

AA does not give congruence because matching angles fixes the shape but not the size. Parallel lines are the great generator of similar triangles: a line parallel to one side cuts off a smaller, similar triangle.

Equal corresponding angles imply similarity only for triangles. A square and a non-square rectangle have all four angles equal ($90^\circ$) yet are not similar. Don't transplant AA to other polygons.
📝 Example 11-12 — altitude to the hypotenuse

The altitude $CD$ to the hypotenuse of right $\triangle ABC$ creates $\triangle ACD \sim \triangle ABC \sim \triangle CBD$ (all share equal angles). From $\triangle ADC \sim \triangle CDB$ we get $\dfrac{AD}{CD} = \dfrac{CD}{BD}$, so $CD^2 = AD\cdot BD$. If the foot splits the hypotenuse into $4$ and $8$, then $CD = \sqrt{4\cdot 8} = \sqrt{32} = 4\sqrt2$.

📝 Example 11-13 — the Angle Bisector Theorem

If $AX$ bisects $\angle A$ of $\triangle ABC$, then it divides the opposite side in the ratio of the adjacent sides: $$\frac{BX}{CX} = \frac{AB}{AC}.$$ Proof sketch: extend $AX$ and draw $BE \parallel AC$. The bisected angle and alternate-interior angles force $\triangle BXE \sim \triangle CXA$ with $AB = BE$, giving the ratio.

✏️ Exercise 11-17 — midsegment

$M$, $N$ are midpoints of $AB$, $AC$ in $\triangle ABC$. Since $\triangle AMN \sim \triangle ABC$ with ratio $\tfrac12$, the midsegment $MN = \tfrac12 BC$. With $BC = 6$, find $MN$.

11.8 Cevians: Medians, Altitudes & Bisectors

A cevian is any segment from a vertex to the opposite side. Three families matter, and each triple is concurrent (meets at one point).

Medians and the centroid (2:1)

A median joins a vertex to the midpoint of the opposite side. The three medians meet at the centroid $G$, which divides each median in a $2:1$ ratio measured from the vertex:

$$\frac{AG}{GD} = \frac{BG}{GE} = \frac{CG}{GF} = \frac{2}{1}$$
Altitudes, bisectors & their centers

An altitude is the perpendicular from a vertex to the opposite side (or its extension); the three meet at the orthocenter $H$. An angle bisector splits a vertex angle in half; the three meet at the incenter $I$, the center of the inscribed circle, at distance $r$ (the inradius) from all three sides. The perpendicular bisectors of the sides meet at the circumcenter $O$, equidistant ($R$, the circumradius) from the three vertices.

In an obtuse triangle the orthocenter and circumcenter fall outside the triangle, and an altitude meets the extension of a side. In a right triangle the circumcenter sits at the midpoint of the hypotenuse, so $R = \tfrac12\,(\text{hypotenuse})$.
📝 Example — angle bisector length ratio

The Angle Bisector Theorem (§11.7) says the bisector from $A$ meets $BC$ at $X$ with $\dfrac{BX}{XC} = \dfrac{AB}{AC}$. So in a triangle with sides $AB=4$, $AC=6$, $BC=10$, the bisector from $A$ splits $BC$ into pieces in ratio $4:6 = 2:3$, i.e. lengths $4$ and $6$. Check: $4+6=10$. ✓

📝 Example 11-1 — circumcenter of a right triangle

If $\angle C = 90^\circ$, then $AB$ subtends a $180^\circ$ arc, making it a diameter. The circle's center is the midpoint of the diameter $AB$ — so the circumcenter of a right triangle is the midpoint of its hypotenuse, and the median to the hypotenuse equals half the hypotenuse.

11.9 Area of a Triangle

The most fundamental area formula comes from cutting a rectangle in half.

Area formulas $$[\triangle ABC] = \tfrac12\,b h = \tfrac12\,ab\sin C = r s$$

where $h$ is the altitude to base $b$, $C$ is the angle between sides $a$ and $b$, $r$ is the inradius, and $s$ the semiperimeter.

Why $\tfrac12 bh$? Two copies of a right triangle snap together into a rectangle of area $bh$, so one triangle is half: $\tfrac12 bh$. A general triangle splits into two right triangles by an altitude, and their half-rectangles still add to $\tfrac12 bh$.

Why $\tfrac12 ab\sin C$? The altitude from $A$ has length $h = b\sin C$ (it is the opposite leg of a right triangle with hypotenuse $b$ and angle $C$). Substituting into $\tfrac12\,(\text{base } a)(h)$ gives $\tfrac12 ab\sin C$.

Why $rs$? Join the incenter to the three vertices, splitting the triangle into three triangles of heights $r$ and bases $a,b,c$. Their areas sum to $\tfrac{r}{2}(a+b+c) = rs$.

📝 Example 11-23 — inradius from sides

Sides $8,15,17$: since $8^2 + 15^2 = 64 + 225 = 289 = 17^2$, it's a right triangle. Area $= \tfrac12(8)(15) = 60$. Semiperimeter $s = \tfrac{8+15+17}{2} = 20$. Then $r = \dfrac{[\triangle]}{s} = \dfrac{60}{20} = 3$.

📝 Example — equilateral triangle area

With side $s$ and included angle $60^\circ$: $[\triangle] = \tfrac12 s^2 \sin 60^\circ = \tfrac12 s^2 \cdot \tfrac{\sqrt3}{2} = \dfrac{s^2\sqrt3}{4}$. For example, side $4$ gives $\dfrac{16\sqrt3}{4} = 4\sqrt3$.

✏️ Exercise 11-23 — area from altitude

An equilateral triangle has altitude $12$. Its altitude is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{2}s$, so $s = \tfrac{24}{\sqrt3} = 8\sqrt3$, giving area $\dfrac{(8\sqrt3)^2\sqrt3}{4} = \dfrac{192\sqrt3}{4} = 48\sqrt3$.

Triangles are the backbone of applied physics. Vector resolution breaks any vector into perpendicular components $V_x = V\cos\theta$, $V_y = V\sin\theta$ — exactly the legs of a right triangle whose hypotenuse is the vector's magnitude, with $V = \sqrt{V_x^2 + V_y^2}$ from Pythagoras. In statics, three forces in equilibrium close into a force triangle; the triangle inequality then guarantees no single force can exceed the sum of the other two. On an inclined plane at angle $\theta$, the weight $mg$ splits into $mg\sin\theta$ along the slope and $mg\cos\theta$ into the surface — the same special-triangle trigonometry, which is why $30^\circ$, $45^\circ$, and $60^\circ$ ramps recur throughout mechanics problems. Rigid trusses are triangulated precisely because a triangle is the only polygon that cannot deform without changing a side length (SSS congruence makes it rigid).

Chapter 12 — Quadrilaterals

A quadrilateral is any four-sided figure. The interesting ones are the special quadrilaterals — parallelograms, rectangles, rhombi, squares, trapezoids, kites — and they form a tidy hierarchy in which each named shape inherits every property of the ones above it. Learn that hierarchy and a single fact (the angles sum to $360^\circ$), and almost every area formula falls out of the triangle tools you already have.

12.1 The Fundamentals

A quadrilateral is convex if all four interior angles are less than $180^\circ$; it is concave if one interior angle is a reflex angle (greater than $180^\circ$). We work almost exclusively with convex quadrilaterals. A diagonal joins two non-adjacent vertices — every quadrilateral has exactly two. A quadrilateral is orthodiagonal if its diagonals are perpendicular.

Angle sum = 360°

A diagonal splits any quadrilateral $ABCD$ into triangles $ABC$ and $ACD$. The four interior angles of the quadrilateral are exactly the six triangle angles, so

$$\angle A + \angle B + \angle C + \angle D = 180^\circ + 180^\circ = 360^\circ.$$

When we name a quadrilateral the vertices must be listed in order around the figure. The shape above is $ABCD$ (or $BCDA$), but never $ACBD$.

A diagonal of a concave quadrilateral can fall outside the figure. Always sketch before trusting that "split into two triangles" works cleanly — it always does for convex shapes, which is why we restrict to them.

12.2 Parallelograms

If both pairs of opposite sides of $ABCD$ are parallel, it is a parallelogram. Drawing the diagonals unlocks everything. Because $AB \parallel CD$ and $BC \parallel DA$, alternate angles give $\angle CAB = \angle ACD$ and $\angle BCA = \angle DAC$; with the shared side $AC$, the triangles $ABC$ and $CDA$ are congruent by ASA.

Properties of a parallelogram

From that congruence:

  • Opposite sides are equal: $AB = CD$, $BC = DA$.
  • Opposite angles are equal: $\angle A = \angle C$, $\angle B = \angle D$.
  • Consecutive angles are supplementary: $\angle A + \angle B = 180^\circ$.
  • The diagonals bisect each other: $AE = EC$ and $BE = ED$.

The area is base times height — the perpendicular distance between the two sides the base lies on. (A parallelogram is a trapezoid whose two bases are equal, so its area is the median times the height, and median $=$ base.)

Three area formulas $$[ABCD] = bh = (AB)(BC)\sin B = \tfrac12 d_1 d_2 \sin\theta$$

where $\theta$ is the angle between the diagonals $d_1, d_2$. The middle form comes from splitting into two triangles of area $\tfrac12(AB)(BC)\sin B$; the last mirrors the triangle "side-side-sine" area.

📝 Example 12-2 — opposite angles

Opposite angles $3x + 20^\circ$ and $40 - x^\circ$ are equal, so $3x + 20 = 40 - x \Rightarrow x = 5$, and each is $35^\circ$. The other two angles are supplementary: $180^\circ - 35^\circ = 145^\circ$.

✏️ Exercise 12-3 — area from sides and angle

A parallelogram has sides $3$ and $6$, with $30^\circ$ between them. Find its area. (Use $[\,] = (3)(6)\sin 30^\circ$.)

The diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other, but they are not equal in general (that is special to rectangles) and not perpendicular in general (special to rhombi). Don't import a property the shape hasn't earned.

12.3 Rhombi, Rectangles, and Squares

These three are all parallelograms with an extra constraint, so each inherits everything from §12.2 and adds more.

A rhombus has all four sides equal. By SSS the half-triangles cut by a diagonal are congruent, which forces opposite sides parallel — so a rhombus is a parallelogram. Its diagonals therefore bisect each other, and because the four triangles they create are congruent, the diagonals are also perpendicular.

Rhombus area

The diagonals cut the rhombus into 4 congruent right triangles whose legs are the half-diagonals $\tfrac{d_1}{2}, \tfrac{d_2}{2}$:

$$[ABCD] = 4 \cdot \tfrac12\!\left(\tfrac{d_1}{2}\right)\!\left(\tfrac{d_2}{2}\right) = \tfrac12 d_1 d_2.$$

A rectangle has four equal angles; since they sum to $360^\circ$, each is $90^\circ$. Right angles make opposite sides parallel, so a rectangle is a parallelogram with length $\ell$ and width $w$. The Pythagorean theorem gives equal diagonals of length $\sqrt{\ell^2 + w^2}$, and

Rectangle $$[ABCD] = \ell w, \qquad P = 2\ell + 2w, \qquad d = \sqrt{\ell^2 + w^2}.$$

A square has all sides and all angles equal — it is simultaneously a rectangle and a rhombus, so it owns every property of both. With side $s$ the diagonal is $s\sqrt2$, the area is $s^2$, and the perimeter is $4s$.

📝 Example 12-3 — side of a rhombus

A rhombus has area $40$ and diagonals $2x$ and $3x-2$. Then $\tfrac12(2x)(3x-2) = 40 \Rightarrow 3x^2 - 2x - 40 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 4$ (rejecting $x=-\tfrac{10}{3}$). The diagonals are $8$ and $10$; their halves $4,5$ are the legs of a right triangle, so the side is $\sqrt{4^2+5^2} = \sqrt{41}$.

📝 Example 12-4 — perimeter of a rectangle

Area $\ell w = 40$ and diagonal $\sqrt{\ell^2+w^2} = 10$, so $\ell^2 + w^2 = 100$. Then $(\ell+w)^2 = \ell^2+w^2+2\ell w = 100 + 80 = 180$, giving $\ell + w = 6\sqrt5$ and perimeter $2(\ell+w) = 12\sqrt5$.

✏️ Exercise 12-9 — square from its diagonal

A diagonal of a square has length $8$. Find the area. (Diagonal $= s\sqrt2$, area $= s^2 = d^2/2$.)

12.4 Trapezoids

A trapezoid has exactly one pair of parallel sides, the bases $b_1$ and $b_2$; the other two sides are the legs. The perpendicular distance between the bases is the height $h$. The median (midsegment) joins the midpoints of the legs.

Median and area

Drop altitudes to box the trapezoid; similar triangles show the segment through the leg-midpoints is parallel to the bases with length equal to their average:

$$m = \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}.$$

Decomposing into the two corner triangles plus the central rectangle (or just "average base times height") gives

$$[ABCD] = \frac{b_1 + b_2}{2}\,h = m\,h.$$

If the legs are equal the trapezoid is isosceles. Then (by HL/LL congruent triangles) the base angles are equal and the diagonals are equal — and each of these conditions is enough to guarantee the trapezoid is isosceles.

📝 Example 12-1 — area of an isosceles trapezoid

Altitude $4$, leg $8$, smaller base $5$. Drop altitudes from the top vertices; each foot is $\sqrt{8^2 - 4^2} = \sqrt{48} = 4\sqrt3$ horizontally beyond the short base. So the long base is $5 + 2(4\sqrt3) = 5 + 8\sqrt3$, and

$$[ABCD] = \frac{5 + (5+8\sqrt3)}{2}\cdot 4 = (5 + 4\sqrt3)(4) = 20 + 16\sqrt3.$$
✏️ Exercise 12-1 — area from the average base

A trapezoid has height $3$ and bases averaging $6$. Find its area. (Area $= mh$, and $m$ is the average base.)

"$\tfrac12(b_1+b_2)h$" needs $h$ to be the perpendicular distance between the bases — never a slanted leg. And the median formula averages only the two parallel sides, not all four.

12.5 Kites & Orthodiagonal Quadrilaterals

A kite has two pairs of adjacent equal sides. Its diagonals are perpendicular (one is the perpendicular bisector of the other), so a kite is orthodiagonal. More generally, any orthodiagonal quadrilateral — diagonals $d_1 \perp d_2$ — has area equal to half the product of its diagonals.

Orthodiagonal area

Split along one diagonal $d_1$. The two triangles share base $d_1$, and because the other diagonal is perpendicular, their heights are the pieces of $d_2$ on either side, summing to $d_2$:

$$[ABCD] = \tfrac12 d_1 p + \tfrac12 d_1 q = \tfrac12 d_1 (p + q) = \tfrac12 d_1 d_2.$$

This is exactly why the rhombus and square (both orthodiagonal) use $\tfrac12 d_1 d_2$.

📝 Example — orthodiagonal area (Problem 197)

Prove a quadrilateral with perpendicular diagonals has area $\tfrac12 d_1 d_2$. The derivation above is the proof: it never used equal sides, only $d_1 \perp d_2$, so it holds for kites, rhombi, squares, and any other orthodiagonal quadrilateral.

Converses can fail. Perpendicular diagonals do not make a rhombus (a kite has them too), and perpendicular plus bisecting is what pins down a rhombus. Equal diagonals alone don't give a rectangle either — you also need them to bisect each other. Prove a converse separately; never assume it.
💡 Big Picture — the quadrilateral family tree Every special quadrilateral is a quadrilateral with constraints added, and the constraints stack: parallelogram (both pairs of sides parallel) $\to$ add right angles to get a rectangle, or add equal sides to get a rhombus $\to$ add both to get a square. Trapezoids and kites branch off separately (only one pair parallel, or two pairs of adjacent equal sides). Because the hierarchy is one of inheritance, a square automatically satisfies every theorem about rectangles, rhombi, and parallelograms — proving something "for a parallelogram" proves it for all four of its descendants at once.
Quadrilaterals are everywhere in physics. The parallelogram law of vector addition represents two vectors as adjacent sides of a parallelogram whose diagonal is their sum — and the related identity $|\vec a + \vec b|^2 + |\vec a - \vec b|^2 = 2|\vec a|^2 + 2|\vec b|^2$ is just "the sum of the squares of the diagonals equals the sum of the squares of the four sides." Work done by a constant force is the area under a force-displacement graph — a rectangle ($W = Fd$) or, for a linearly varying force like a spring, a triangle or trapezoid. In condensed-matter physics a crystal's unit cell is a parallelogram (2-D) or parallelepiped (3-D) that tiles space, and its area $|\vec a \times \vec b| = ab\sin\theta$ is exactly the parallelogram area formula — the same $\tfrac12 d_1 d_2 \sin\theta$ idea reused as the magnitude of a cross product.

Chapter 13 — Polygons

A polygon is a simple closed planar figure formed by line segments. Triangles and quadrilaterals are the polygons you already know; this chapter generalizes to any number of sides. Almost everything — angle sums, diagonal counts, areas — follows from one idea: chop the polygon into triangles.

13.1 Types of Polygons

A polygon with $n$ sides is called an $n$-gon, but the common ones have names worth knowing:

Names by side count $$\begin{array}{ll} 3:\ \text{triangle} & 8:\ \text{octagon}\\ 4:\ \text{quadrilateral} & 9:\ \text{nonagon}\\ 5:\ \text{pentagon} & 10:\ \text{decagon}\\ 6:\ \text{hexagon} & 12:\ \text{dodecagon}\\ 7:\ \text{heptagon} & \end{array}$$

A polygon is convex if every interior angle is less than $180^\circ$ — equivalently, any segment between two interior points stays inside. If some interior angle exceeds $180^\circ$ (a "dent"), the polygon is concave (non-convex). A polygon is regular if all sides are equal and all angles are equal.

Equal sides alone do not make a polygon regular (a rhombus has four equal sides but unequal angles), and equal angles alone do not either (a non-square rectangle has four equal $90^\circ$ angles). You need both conditions.
📝 Example — convex vs. concave

A regular pentagon is convex: every interior angle is $108^\circ < 180^\circ$. A five-pointed "star" outline that doubles back has reflex angles exceeding $180^\circ$ at its inner vertices, so it is concave.

13.2 Diagonals

A diagonal is any segment joining one vertex to a non-adjacent vertex. Let's derive the count rather than memorize it.

Deriving the diagonal count

Pick one vertex. It connects to the other $n-1$ vertices. Two of those segments are sides (to the two neighbors), so the remaining $n-3$ are diagonals. With $n$ vertices that suggests $n(n-3)$ — but every diagonal has two endpoints, so this counts each one twice. Divide by 2:

$$D = \frac{n(n-3)}{2}$$

Sanity check on a quadrilateral ($n=4$): the naive count gives $4(4-3)=4$, which is wrong — a quadrilateral has only $2$ diagonals. Dividing by 2 fixes it: $\tfrac{4\cdot 1}{2}=2$. ✓

📝 Example — diagonals of a 100-gon

$D = \dfrac{100(100-3)}{2} = \dfrac{100\cdot 97}{2} = 4850$ diagonals.

✏️ Exercise 13-A — solve for $n$

A regular polygon has exactly $20$ diagonals. Find $n$. Set $\tfrac{n(n-3)}{2}=20$, so $n^2-3n-40=0$, giving $n=8$.

13.3 Angles in a Polygon

To find the sum of the interior angles, triangulate: from a single vertex, draw all $n-3$ diagonals. These slice the $n$-gon into exactly $n-2$ triangles. The angles of all those triangles, taken together, reassemble into precisely the interior angles of the polygon.

Hexagon ($n=6$) split into $n-2=4$ triangles from one vertex.
Sum of interior angles $$S = 180^\circ (n-2)$$

Because each of the $n-2$ triangles contributes $180^\circ$, and no triangle angle is wasted (none lies along a diagonal "inside" — every angle sits at a polygon vertex).

Sum of exterior angles

At each vertex, interior angle $\beta_i$ and exterior angle $\alpha_i$ form a straight line: $\alpha_i+\beta_i = 180^\circ$. Summing over all $n$ vertices: $\sum\alpha_i + \sum\beta_i = 180^\circ n$. Since $\sum\beta_i = 180^\circ(n-2)$,

$$\sum \alpha_i = 180^\circ n - 180^\circ(n-2) = 360^\circ.$$

Remarkably, the exterior angles of any convex polygon sum to $360^\circ$ — independent of $n$. (Walk around the boundary once; you turn through a full revolution.)

📝 Example — find $n$ from an angle sum

A polygon's interior angles sum to $2340^\circ$. Solve $180(n-2)=2340 \Rightarrow n-2 = 13 \Rightarrow n = 15$.

✏️ Exercise 13-B — one missing angle

The sum of all but one interior angle of a convex polygon is $2190^\circ$. Find $n$. The full sum $180(n-2)$ must exceed $2190$ by less than one angle ($<180$): $n=15$ gives $2340$, leaving a missing angle of $150^\circ$. So $n=15$.

13.4 Regular Polygons

In a regular $n$-gon all $n$ interior angles are equal, so each one is the total divided by $n$. Likewise each exterior angle is $360^\circ/n$.

Each angle of a regular $n$-gon $$\text{interior} = \frac{180^\circ(n-2)}{n}, \qquad \text{exterior} = \frac{360^\circ}{n}$$

For example each interior angle is $60^\circ$ ($n=3$), $90^\circ$ ($n=4$), $108^\circ$ ($n=5$), $120^\circ$ ($n=6$), $135^\circ$ ($n=8$), $150^\circ$ ($n=12$). No need to memorize — just compute.

📝 Example — interior angle $162^\circ$

Easiest via the exterior angle: $180-162 = 18^\circ$ exterior, so $n = 360/18 = 20$ sides.

✏️ Exercise 13-C — interior angle $140^\circ$

Find $n$. Exterior $=40^\circ$, so $n=360/40=9$ (a nonagon).

13.5 Apothem and Area

The perpendicular bisectors of the sides of a regular polygon all meet at one point — the center $O$. From $O$ we can draw a circumradius $R$ to each vertex and an inradius $r$ (the apothem) perpendicular to each side. The apothem is the distance from the center to the midpoint of a side.

Right triangle $OXA$: hypotenuse $R$, legs $r$ (apothem) and $\ell/2$.
Deriving the area

Drawing a radius to every side splits the regular $n$-gon into $2n$ congruent right triangles like $OXA$, each with legs $r$ and $\ell/2$. The full angle at $O$ is $360^\circ$ spread over $2n$ triangles, so the central half-angle is

$$\theta = \frac{360^\circ}{2n} = \frac{180^\circ}{n}, \qquad \tan\theta = \frac{\ell/2}{r}, \quad \sin\theta = \frac{\ell/2}{R}.$$

Each of the $2n$ triangles has area $\tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{\ell}{2}\cdot r$, so the polygon's area is

$$\text{Area} = 2n\cdot \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{\ell}{2}\cdot r = \frac{n\ell r}{2} = \frac{1}{2}\,a\,P,$$

where $a=r$ is the apothem and $P = n\ell$ is the perimeter.

The form $\text{Area}=\tfrac12 a P$ is worth remembering: it is exactly analogous to a triangle's $\tfrac12 bh$, with the apothem playing the role of the height summed around the whole boundary.

The apothem $a=r$ (to the midpoint of a side) is not the circumradius $R$ (to a vertex). Since $r = R\cos\theta < R$, mixing them up will give a wrong area. The "$\tfrac12 aP$" formula needs the apothem, not $R$.
📝 Example — area of a regular hexagon

For $n=6$, $\theta = 30^\circ$. With side $\ell = s$, the apothem is $r = \tfrac{\ell/2}{\tan 30^\circ} = \tfrac{s/2}{1/\sqrt3} = \tfrac{s\sqrt3}{2}$, and $P = 6s$. So

$$\text{Area} = \tfrac12 \cdot \tfrac{s\sqrt3}{2}\cdot 6s = \frac{3\sqrt3}{2}\,s^2.$$

This matches splitting the hexagon into $6$ equilateral triangles of area $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2$ each: $6\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2 = \tfrac{3\sqrt3}{2}s^2$. ✓

✏️ Exercise 13-D — hexagon facts

In a regular hexagon, why do the radii to the vertices create $6$ equilateral triangles, and why is the longest diagonal twice a side? Each central triangle is isosceles with vertex angle $360/6=60^\circ$, forcing all angles to $60^\circ$ — equilateral. So $R=s$, and a long diagonal spans two radii: $2R=2s$.

💡 Big Picture — polygons approach the circle As $n\to\infty$, a regular $n$-gon inscribed in a circle of radius $R$ has perimeter $P\to 2\pi R$ and apothem $a\to R$, so $\text{Area}=\tfrac12 aP \to \tfrac12 R\cdot 2\pi R = \pi R^2$. Archimedes used exactly this — squeezing $\pi$ between inscribed and circumscribed $96$-gons. The polygon area formula is the discrete ancestor of the circle's area.
Regular polygons and their tilings are everywhere in physics. Crystal lattices are classified by which polygons tile the plane periodically — only triangles, squares, and hexagons do (the crystallographic restriction forbids $5$-fold periodic symmetry, which is why ordinary crystals never show regular pentagons). Graphene and honeycombs adopt the hexagonal lattice because it tiles with minimal edge length per area — the same reason bees build hexagonal cells. Geodesic domes and the carbon "buckyball" $\mathrm{C}_{60}$ use pentagon–hexagon networks (Euler's polyhedron formula forces exactly $12$ pentagons). And the discovery of quasicrystals — solids with forbidden $5$- and $10$-fold symmetry built from non-periodic Penrose-like tilings — earned the 2011 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, overturning the assumption that order requires periodicity.

Chapter 14 — Angle Chasing

"Angle chasing" is the art of finding an unknown angle by labeling what you know and propagating relationships — triangle angle sums, the straight line, vertical angles, isosceles triangles — until the unknown falls out. The most powerful new tools come from circles: an inscribed angle is exactly half the arc it cuts off. We'll derive that theorem and its consequences, then chase an angle through a figure step by step.

14.1 The Inscribed Angle Theorem

A central angle has its vertex at the center $O$ of a circle; an inscribed angle has its vertex on the circle. Both can "subtend" (open onto) the same arc. The measure of an arc is defined to equal the central angle that cuts it off, so $\overset{\frown}{AB}$ in the figure has measure $\angle AOB$.

Inscribed Angle Theorem $$\angle APB = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\angle AOB = \tfrac{1}{2}\,\overset{\frown}{AB}$$ An inscribed angle is half the central angle subtending the same arc — equivalently, half the intercepted arc.

Derivation. Draw the radius $OP$ and let it split the inscribed angle into $\angle APO$ and $\angle OPB$. Since $OA = OP$ (both radii), $\triangle OAP$ is isosceles, so its base angles are equal: $\angle OAP = \angle OPA = \theta_1$. The exterior angle of a triangle equals the sum of the two remote interior angles, so the exterior angle of $\triangle OAP$ at $O$ — the central angle measured from $A$ to the extension of $PO$ — is $\theta_1 + \theta_1 = 2\theta_1$. Doing the same for $\triangle OBP$ gives $2\theta_2$ on the other side. Adding the two pieces, the inscribed angle $\angle APB = \theta_1 + \theta_2$ while the central angle $\angle AOB = 2\theta_1 + 2\theta_2 = 2\angle APB$. Hence $\angle APB = \tfrac12 \angle AOB$.

The two angles must subtend the same arc. An inscribed angle and a central angle on the same chord but opening to opposite arcs are not in the $1{:}2$ ratio — they relate to different arcs (whose measures sum to $360^\circ$).
📝 Example — two inscribed angles on one arc

If $\angle AOB = 84^\circ$ is a central angle, then every inscribed angle subtending arc $AB$ measures $\tfrac12(84^\circ) = 42^\circ$ — no matter where its vertex sits on the major arc. This is exactly the toolkit entry "inscribed angles subtending the same arc": they are all equal.

14.2 Angles in a Semicircle (Thales)

Take the special case where the chord $AB$ is a diameter. Then the central angle $\angle AOB$ is a straight angle, $180^\circ$, so any inscribed angle on it is half of that.

Thales' Theorem $$AB \text{ a diameter} \;\Longrightarrow\; \angle APB = \tfrac12(180^\circ) = 90^\circ$$ Any angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle.

The converse is just as useful: if $\angle APB = 90^\circ$, then $P$ lies on the circle whose diameter is $AB$. So a right angle is a hidden circle waiting to be drawn.

📝 Example — spotting a diameter

If $AD$ is a diameter and $B$ is any other point on the circle, then $\angle ABD = 90^\circ$ instantly. In problem 243 ("$AD$ is a diameter, $ABC$ a chord"), recognizing $\angle ABD = 90^\circ$ is the move that unlocks the figure.

14.3 Cyclic Quadrilaterals & the Tangent-Chord Angle

A quadrilateral whose four vertices lie on a circle is cyclic. Consider opposite angles $\angle A$ and $\angle C$ of cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$. By the inscribed angle theorem, $\angle A$ is half the arc $BCD$ it subtends, and $\angle C$ is half the arc $DAB$. Those two arcs together make the whole circle, $360^\circ$, so the two angles sum to half of that.

Cyclic Quadrilateral $$\angle A + \angle C = 180^\circ \qquad \angle B + \angle D = 180^\circ$$ Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary.

The tangent-chord angle handles the limiting case where one of the two lines from a point becomes a tangent. If chord $TA$ meets the tangent line at $T$, the angle between them equals the inscribed angle in the "far" segment — half the arc the chord cuts off:

Tangent-Chord Angle $$\varphi = \tfrac12\,\overset{\frown}{TA}$$ The angle between a tangent and a chord equals half the intercepted arc — the same $\tfrac12$ rule, with the tangent acting as the limit of a secant.
The tangent-chord angle equals half the arc enclosed by the angle (the arc "cut off" on the side the angle opens toward). The chord splits the circle into two arcs; pick the one the angle wraps around, not the other.
📝 Example — cyclic from a right angle

In problem 250, points $A,B,C,D,E,F$ all lie on a circle, so any quadrilateral among them is cyclic and opposite angles are supplementary. Pairing that with the inscribed-angle equalities pins down the arcs.

14.4 The Angle-Chasing Technique

The method itself: label every unknown angle, write down every relationship the toolkit gives you (triangle sums to $180^\circ$, quadrilateral sums to $360^\circ$, vertical angles equal, isosceles base angles equal, angles on a line sum to $180^\circ$, inscribed = $\tfrac12$ arc), and propagate until the target is expressed in knowns.

Worked angle-chase $\triangle ABC$ is inscribed in a circle with $\angle BAC = 50^\circ$ subtending arc $BC$. Find $x = \angle BCA$ given that arc $AB = 70^\circ$. $$\overset{\frown}{BC} = 2\,\angle BAC = 100^\circ,\qquad \overset{\frown}{AB}=70^\circ$$ $$\overset{\frown}{CA} = 360^\circ - 100^\circ - 70^\circ = 190^\circ$$ $$x = \angle BCA = \tfrac12\,\overset{\frown}{AB} = \tfrac12(70^\circ) = 35^\circ$$

Notice each inscribed angle was tied to the arc opposite it. As a check, the third angle $\angle ABC = \tfrac12\,\overset{\frown}{CA} = 95^\circ$, and indeed $50^\circ + 35^\circ + 95^\circ = 180^\circ$. The triangle sum is your free verification at the end of every chase.

Don't chase blindly. Before computing, mark equal angles with the same symbol (isosceles legs, vertical angles, equal inscribed angles). Many "hard" problems collapse the instant you notice two tick-marked angles are equal — propagating a wrong relationship wastes the whole chain.
📝 Example — let the unknown be a variable

In an isosceles $\triangle ABC$ with $AC=BC$ and a second isosceles triangle hung off it (problem 236 style), set $\angle A = 57^\circ$. Base angles give $\angle B = 57^\circ$, then $\angle C = 66^\circ$. Carry $66^\circ$ into the next triangle's angle sum, use its isosceles legs, and the target $\angle D$ drops out — pure propagation.

Arcs sum to $360^\circ$; each angle is half its opposite arc.

💡 Big Picture — why "half the arc" unifies everything The inscribed angle, the semicircle right angle, the cyclic-quad supplement, and the tangent-chord angle are all the same theorem: every angle whose vertex touches a circle is half the arc it intercepts. Memorize the one $\tfrac12$ rule and the rest are special cases — diameter ($180^\circ$ arc $\to 90^\circ$), opposite arcs ($360^\circ \to 180^\circ$ supplement), and the secant-going-tangent limit.
Angle relationships are everywhere in physics. Thales' semicircle underlies optics: rays from a point source reflecting off a spherical mirror, and the right-angle locus of a point seeing a segment under $90^\circ$, appears in lens and antenna geometry. In wave physics, phase angles add and subtract exactly like the angles in a chase — a phasor diagram is an angle-chase on a circle, where the resultant amplitude follows from inscribed-angle and isosceles-triangle relations. Crystallography and Bragg diffraction ($n\lambda = 2d\sin\theta$) hinge on geometric angle constraints, and the circumscribed-circle idea (a right angle implies a hidden circle) shows up when locating a point that subtends a fixed angle — the basis of triangulation and radio direction-finding.

Chapter 15 — Areas

You already know how to find the area of a circle or a triangle. The real skill is computing the area of complicated figures, and of simple figures buried inside hard problems. Two ideas do most of the work: decomposition (slice a region into pieces whose areas you know, then add or subtract), and ratios (relate one area to another without computing either). We write the area of a polygon $P$ in square brackets: $[P]$. So $[ABC]$ means "the area of triangle $ABC$."

15.1 Five Ways to Find a Triangle's Area

A triangle's area can be written many ways; the right one depends on what you're given. Each is just $\tfrac12 bh$ in disguise.

Triangle area formulas (sides $a,b,c$; angle $C$ between $a$ and $b$) $$[ABC]=\tfrac12 b h \qquad [ABC]=\tfrac12 ab\sin C$$ $$\text{Heron: } [ABC]=\sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)},\quad s=\tfrac{a+b+c}{2}$$ $$[ABC]=rs \quad(r=\text{inradius}) \qquad [ABC]=\frac{abc}{4R}\quad(R=\text{circumradius})$$

The first is the definition. The second follows because the altitude to side $a$ has length $b\sin C$, so $\tfrac12 a(b\sin C)=\tfrac12 ab\sin C$. The $rs$ form comes from joining the incenter to each vertex: the three triangles have bases $a,b,c$ and common altitude $r$, so $[ABC]=\tfrac12 r(a+b+c)=rs$.

📝 Example — the 13-14-15 triangle, four ways

Sides $13,14,15$. Heron: $s=\tfrac{13+14+15}{2}=21$, so $$[ABC]=\sqrt{21(21-13)(21-14)(21-15)}=\sqrt{21\cdot 8\cdot 7\cdot 6}=\sqrt{7056}=84.$$ Now back out the other quantities: the inradius is $r=[ABC]/s=84/21=4$, and the circumradius is $R=\dfrac{abc}{4[ABC]}=\dfrac{13\cdot 14\cdot 15}{4\cdot 84}=\dfrac{2730}{336}=\dfrac{65}{8}$.

📝 Example — when an angle is handed to you

A triangle has two sides $6$ and $8$ meeting at a $90^\circ$ angle. Then $[ABC]=\tfrac12(6)(8)\sin 90^\circ=\tfrac12(48)(1)=24$. (The right angle makes the legs base and height directly.)

In $\tfrac12 ab\sin C$, the angle $C$ must be the one between the two sides $a$ and $b$. Plugging in a different angle gives nonsense. And in Heron's formula, always halve the perimeter first — forgetting the $\tfrac12$ in $s$ is the single most common error.

15.2 Similar Figures Scale as the Square

If two figures are similar with side ratio $k$, every length scales by $k$ — but area scales by $k^2$. This holds for any similar figures, not just triangles, because area is fundamentally length $\times$ length.

Area ratio of similar figures $$\frac{[\text{figure}_1]}{[\text{figure}_2]}=k^2,\qquad k=\frac{\text{any matching length}_1}{\text{any matching length}_2}$$

All circles are similar and all squares are similar, so a circle of twice the radius has $2^2=4$ times the area, and two squares with area ratio $6$ have diagonal ratio $\sqrt 6$.

📝 Example — the midpoint triangle (Example 15-1)

A triangle has area $36$. Connect the midpoints of its sides to form a smaller triangle. By SAS/SSS similarity, the midpoint triangle is similar to the original with ratio $k=\tfrac12$. Therefore its area is $\left(\tfrac12\right)^2\cdot 36=\tfrac14\cdot 36=9$.

✏️ Exercise — diagonals in a trapezoid

In trapezoid $ABCD$ with $AB\parallel CD$, the diagonals meet at $E$. If $AB=4$ and $CD=12$, the triangles $ABE$ and $CDE$ are similar with ratio $\tfrac{CD}{AB}=3$. Show that $\dfrac{[CDE]}{[ABE]}=3^2=9$.

💡 Big Picture — why area is the square of length Scaling a region by $k$ stretches it in two independent directions, so each little square tile becomes $k\times k=k^2$ as large. The same logic in three dimensions makes volume scale as $k^3$. This square-cube relationship explains why a model bridge can't simply be scaled up: cross-sectional area (strength) grows as $k^2$ while weight grows as $k^3$.

15.3 Same Base / Same Altitude

The most useful ratio fact in all of area geometry: two triangles sharing an altitude have areas in the ratio of their bases; two triangles sharing a base have areas in the ratio of their altitudes. The derivation is one line.

Area-ratio principle

If $\triangle ABC$ and $\triangle DEF$ have equal altitudes $h$ to bases $BC$ and $EF$, then

$$\frac{[ABC]}{[DEF]}=\frac{\tfrac12(BC)h}{\tfrac12(EF)h}=\frac{BC}{EF}.$$

Symmetrically, equal bases $\Rightarrow$ area ratio equals altitude ratio.

A powerful special case (Example 15-6): if a line $\ell$ is parallel to segment $AB$, then for every point $X$ on $\ell$, the altitude from $X$ to $AB$ is the same — so $[ABX]$ is constant no matter where $X$ slides. Two triangles on the same base with apexes on a line parallel to that base have equal area.

📝 Example — medians cut a triangle into 6 equal areas (Example 15-4)

Median $AD$ hits $BC$ at its midpoint $D$. Triangles $ACD$ and $ABC$ share the altitude from $A$, with bases $CD$ and $CB$ where $CD/CB=\tfrac12$, so $[ACD]=\tfrac12[ABC]$. The centroid $G$ divides $AD$ so that $GD/AD=\tfrac13$; triangles $GCD$ and $ACD$ share the altitude from $C$, so $[GCD]=\tfrac13[ACD]=\tfrac13\cdot\tfrac12[ABC]=\tfrac16[ABC]$. By symmetry all six regions equal $[ABC]/6$.

✏️ Exercise — repeated halving (Example 15-5)

In $\triangle ABC$ with $[ABC]=96$: $F$ is the midpoint of $BC$, so $[ABF]=\tfrac12[ABC]=48$ (same altitude from $A$, half the base). $E$ is the midpoint of $DB$ where $D$ is the midpoint of $AB$, so $AE=\tfrac34 AB$... carefully, with $AE=\tfrac34 AB$ the book's chain gives $[AEF]=\tfrac34[ABF]=36$. Track each base ratio one step at a time.

"Same base" usually means the literal same segment, not merely equal lengths. The trick for proving two triangles equal in area is to show they share a base and have equal altitudes (or share an altitude and have equal bases) — you never need to compute either area.

15.4 Quadrilaterals and Circles

Beyond the triangle you need a small kit of standard areas. Each reduces to triangles or to the circle.

Standard areas $$\text{parallelogram}=bh \qquad \text{trapezoid}=\tfrac12(b_1+b_2)h$$ $$\text{circle}=\pi r^2 \qquad \text{sector of angle }\theta=\frac{\theta}{360^\circ}\,\pi r^2$$

A parallelogram is two copies of a triangle, hence $bh$. A trapezoid with parallel sides $b_1,b_2$ splits along a diagonal into triangles of areas $\tfrac12 b_1 h$ and $\tfrac12 b_2 h$, giving $\tfrac12(b_1+b_2)h$ — base times the average of the parallel sides.

📝 Example — a sector and its triangle

A $60^\circ$ sector of a circle of radius $6$ has area $\tfrac{60}{360}\pi(6)^2=\tfrac16\cdot 36\pi=6\pi$. If $\triangle ABC$ at the center is equilateral with side $6$, its area is $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(6)^2=9\sqrt3$. The circular segment (sector minus triangle) is $6\pi-9\sqrt3$ — a building block for the "leaf" problems in §15.5.

15.5 Complicated Figures: Decomposition & Subtraction

For an irregular region, find areas of simple pieces in the diagram (triangles, sectors, segments, circles) and figure out how to add or subtract them to build the target. A few reliable tactics:

  • Draw radii to peel sectors and circular segments off the rest of the figure.
  • Hunt for right and equilateral triangles; isolate them with extra segments.
  • Draw diagonals to split a quadrilateral into two triangles you can handle.
📝 Example — annulus by subtraction (Example 15-8)

The region between concentric circles of radii $2$ and $3$ is the big disk minus the small disk: $\pi(3)^2-\pi(2)^2=9\pi-4\pi=5\pi$. You can't name this shape with a single formula, but subtraction handles it instantly.

📝 Example — regular octagon, side 2 (Example 15-9)

Build the octagon by snipping four corner triangles off a square. Each corner is an isosceles right triangle whose hypotenuse is an octagon side of length $2$, so its legs are $\sqrt2$ and the full square has side $2+2\sqrt2$. Square area $=(2+2\sqrt2)^2=12+8\sqrt2$. Each corner triangle has area $\tfrac12(\sqrt2)^2=1$, four of them total $4$. Octagon $=(12+8\sqrt2)-4=8+8\sqrt2$.

✏️ Exercise — overlapping semicircle "leaves" (Example 15-11)

A square of side $4$ has a semicircle drawn inward on each side. Each of the four "leaf" overlaps lies in exactly two semicircles, so summing the four semicircles overcounts the leaves once. Total leaf area $=4\cdot\tfrac12\pi(2)^2-4^2=8\pi-16$.

💡 Big Picture — decomposition becomes the integral Every area here is built from straight edges and circular arcs. To handle a region under an arbitrary curve $y=f(x)$, calculus pushes decomposition to its limit: slice the region into ever-thinner rectangles and sum them. Newton called the result the "fluxion"; today it is the definite integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$. The whole subject is decomposition with infinitely many infinitely thin pieces.
Area is everywhere in physics. Flux through a surface — magnetic flux $\Phi=\vec B\cdot\vec A$ or fluid flow rate — is a field strength times the area it pierces, so cross-sectional area sets how much "gets through." Work equals the area under a force-versus-displacement graph, $W=\int F\,dx$; for a spring $F=kx$ this area is the triangle $\tfrac12 kx^2$, the stored elastic energy. And pressure $P=F/A$ shows why a sharp blade (tiny contact area) cuts while the same force on a broad surface does not — the same decomposition-and-ratio thinking, now applied to the physical world.

Apply the Law of Cosines to the longest side, using the two shorter sides $6$ and $6+d$ as the adjacent sides and $\cos 120^\circ = -\tfrac{1}{2}$:

$$(6+2d)^2 = 6^2 + (6+d)^2 - 2\cdot 6\cdot(6+d)\cos 120^\circ = 36 + (6+d)^2 + 6(6+d).$$

$$36 + 24d + 4d^2 = 36 + (36 + 12d + d^2) + (36 + 6d) = 108 + 18d + d^2.$$

Simplify:

$$3d^2 + 6d - 72 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; d^2 + 2d - 24 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (d+6)(d-4) = 0.$$

Taking $d = 4$, the sides are $6,\ 10,\ 14$. The $120^\circ$ angle is between the sides $6$ and $10$, so

$$[\triangle ABC] = \frac{1}{2}(6)(10)\sin 120^\circ = 30\cdot\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} = 15\sqrt{3}.$$

Chapter 16 — The Power of Coordinates

So far we've done geometry the way Euclid did: with figures, congruences, and clever auxiliary lines. There is a second, completely different approach — introduced by Descartes in the 1600s — that converts geometry into algebra. By gluing a grid of numbers onto the plane, distances, lines, and circles all become equations you can manipulate. It is less creative than pure geometry but, applied well, devastatingly powerful.

16.1 Labelling the Plane

Recall the number line: every point on a line is one number, its signed distance from a fixed origin. One dimension has little geometry. So we add a second axis, perpendicular to the first and crossing it at $0$. Now each point needs two numbers, an ordered pair $(x,y)$: the first is distance right (negative = left), the second distance up (negative = down).

The horizontal axis is the $x$-axis ($x$-coordinate, or abscissa); the vertical axis is the $y$-axis ($y$-coordinate, or ordinate). Together $(x,y)$ are the point's Cartesian coordinates, after Descartes. The axes carve the plane into four quadrants, numbered I (upper right, $x>0,\,y>0$) and counterclockwise from there.

Cartesian labelling is one arbitrary choice. Another common system is polar coordinates $(r,\theta)$ — distance $r$ from the origin and angle $\theta$ from the positive $x$-axis. Each system simplifies different curves: Cartesian is ideal for straight lines, polar for circles centered at the origin. Unless stated otherwise, "coordinates" means Cartesian.

💡 Big Picture — why this changed mathematics Before Descartes, algebra and geometry were separate worlds. Coordinates married them: a curve becomes an equation, a geometric question becomes an algebra problem, and vice versa. This single idea is the foundation of analytic geometry, calculus, and ultimately the way physics describes motion through space.

16.2 The Distance Formula

Given two points $(x_1,y_1)$ and $(x_2,y_2)$, how far apart are they? We don't need a new tool — only the Pythagorean Theorem. To get from the first point to the second, go right by $x_2-x_1$ and up by $y_2-y_1$. Those two displacements are the legs of a right triangle; the distance $d$ is its hypotenuse.

Distance Formula (from Pythagoras) $$d^2 = (x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2}$$

Because both differences are squared, the order of the points doesn't matter — $(x_2-x_1)^2 = (x_1-x_2)^2$.

📝 Example — back-solving with the distance formula

Find all $x$ such that $(x,3)$ is $5$ units from $(-1,7)$. Set up the formula and square:

$$5 = \sqrt{(x-(-1))^2 + (3-7)^2} \;\Rightarrow\; 25 = (x+1)^2 + 16 \;\Rightarrow\; (x+1)^2 = 9.$$

So $x+1 = \pm 3$, giving $x = 2$ or $x = -4$. Two points work — geometrically, a horizontal line cuts a circle in two places.

16.3 The Midpoint Formula

The midpoint $M$ of the segment from $(x_1,y_1)$ to $(x_2,y_2)$ sits exactly halfway in each direction, so you simply average the coordinates:

Midpoint Formula $$M = \left(\frac{x_1+x_2}{2},\; \frac{y_1+y_2}{2}\right)$$

Why? Drop perpendiculars from each endpoint and from $M$. The horizontal run from $x_1$ to $x_2$ is $x_2-x_1$; the midpoint covers half of it, so its $x$-coordinate is $x_1 + \tfrac{x_2-x_1}{2} = \tfrac{x_1+x_2}{2}$. The $y$-coordinate follows identically.

📝 Example — midpoint of a hypotenuse

A right triangle has legs of length $3$ along the positive $x$-axis and $4$ along the positive $y$-axis, so its vertices are $(0,0)$, $(3,0)$, and $(0,4)$. The hypotenuse runs from $(3,0)$ to $(0,4)$, with midpoint

$$\left(\frac{3+0}{2},\frac{0+4}{2}\right) = \left(\tfrac{3}{2},\,2\right).$$

16.4 Slope and the Equations of Lines

A line is fixed by one point plus its steepness. Any non-vertical line crosses the $y$-axis at the $y$-intercept $(0,b)$. The steepness, called the slope $m$, is "rise over run": go right by some amount and you must go up $m$ times that amount to stay on the line.

Slope between two points $$m = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} = \frac{y_2-y_1}{x_2-x_1}$$
Forms of a line $$\textbf{slope-intercept: } y = mx + b \qquad \textbf{point-slope: } y - y_1 = m(x-x_1)$$ $$\textbf{standard: } Ax + By = C \quad(\text{slope} = -\tfrac{A}{B})$$
📝 Example — line through two points

Find the line through $(-1,-1)$ and $(3,7)$. Slope: $m = \dfrac{7-(-1)}{3-(-1)} = \dfrac{8}{4} = 2$. Use point-slope at $(-1,-1)$:

$$y-(-1) = 2(x-(-1)) \;\Rightarrow\; y+1 = 2x+2 \;\Rightarrow\; y = 2x+1.$$

Check: at $x=3$, $y = 2(3)+1 = 7$. ✓

📝 Example — reading a standard-form line

For $2x+3y=6$: set $y=0 \Rightarrow x=3$, so the $x$-intercept is $(3,0)$; set $x=0 \Rightarrow y=2$, so the $y$-intercept is $(0,2)$. Solving for $y$ gives $y = -\tfrac{2}{3}x + 2$, so the slope is $-\tfrac{2}{3} = -\tfrac{A}{B}$.

✏️ Exercise — build a line

16.5 Parallel and Perpendicular Slopes

Two lines are parallel exactly when they have the same slope — same steepness, never meeting. Perpendicular is subtler. If one line goes over $a$ while it goes up $b$ (slope $b/a$), rotating $90^\circ$ sends it over $b$ while it goes down $a$ — slope $-a/b$. Multiply the two slopes:

Parallel and Perpendicular $$\text{parallel: } m_1 = m_2 \qquad\qquad \text{perpendicular: } m_1\,m_2 = -1$$

Perpendicular slopes are negative reciprocals: $m_2 = -\dfrac{1}{m_1}$.

The rule $m_1 m_2 = -1$ fails for horizontal and vertical lines: a horizontal line has slope $0$ and a vertical line has undefined slope, yet they are perpendicular — and $0 \cdot (\text{undefined})$ is not $-1$. Also beware: slopes that are merely negatives of each other (like $2$ and $-2$) are not perpendicular; you need the negative reciprocal ($2$ and $-\tfrac12$).
📝 Example — find $k$ for perpendicularity

For what $k$ are $x+3y=6$ and $kx+2y=12$ perpendicular? Their slopes are $-\tfrac{1}{3}$ and $-\tfrac{k}{2}$. Set the product to $-1$:

$$\left(-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)\!\left(-\tfrac{k}{2}\right) = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; \frac{k}{6} = -1 \;\Rightarrow\; k = -6.$$

16.6 The Equation of a Circle

A circle is just every point at a fixed distance $r$ from a center. So feed that definition straight into the distance formula: a point $(x,y)$ lies on the circle of center $(h,k)$ and radius $r$ precisely when its distance to the center is $r$. Squaring kills the radical:

Equation of a Circle (from the distance formula) $$\sqrt{(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2} = r \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$$

Expanding gives the general form $x^2 + y^2 + Ax + By + C = 0$, which hides the center and radius. Recover them by completing the square in $x$ and in $y$.

📝 Example — complete the square to find center & radius

For $2x^2 + 2y^2 + 8x - 12y + 3 = 0$, first divide by $2$:

$$x^2 + 4x + y^2 - 6y + \tfrac{3}{2} = 0.$$

Complete each square — add $(\tfrac{4}{2})^2=4$ and $(\tfrac{-6}{2})^2=9$:

$$(x+2)^2 - 4 + (y-3)^2 - 9 + \tfrac{3}{2} = 0 \;\Rightarrow\; (x+2)^2 + (y-3)^2 = \tfrac{23}{2}.$$

Center $(-2,3)$, radius $\sqrt{23/2} = \tfrac{\sqrt{46}}{2}$.

After completing the square, the right side $r^2$ must be positive for a real circle. If it equals $0$ the "circle" collapses to a single point; if it's negative there are no real points at all. (And you only get a circle when the $x^2$ and $y^2$ coefficients are equal.)
✏️ Exercise — plot a circle

Identify the center and radius of $(x+1)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 9$, then locate the four points $3$ units left, right, up, and down from the center.

16.7 Strategy: Turning Geometry into Algebra

The real payoff is placing a figure on coordinates to trade a hard synthetic proof for routine algebra. Since the choice of axes is free, choose the simplest one: put vertices on the axes, parameters at the origin. As long as you're proving a general geometric relationship (not something about specific coordinates), this loses no generality.

📝 Example — diagonals of a rectangle bisect each other

Place the rectangle at $A(0,0)$, $B(a,0)$, $C(a,b)$, $D(0,b)$. Midpoint of diagonal $AC$ is $\left(\tfrac{a}{2},\tfrac{b}{2}\right)$; midpoint of diagonal $BD$ is $\left(\tfrac{a+0}{2},\tfrac{0+b}{2}\right) = \left(\tfrac{a}{2},\tfrac{b}{2}\right)$. Same point — so the diagonals share a midpoint, i.e. they bisect each other. A clean two-line proof.

Don't reach for coordinates on every problem. Analytic methods can bury an elegant synthetic solution under a mountain of algebra. Try a pure-geometry approach first; switch to coordinates when symmetry lets you place the figure cheaply, or when intercepts, distances, and intersections are exactly what's asked.
Coordinates are the native language of physics. A particle's location is a position vector $\vec r = (x,y,z)$, and its motion is traced by $\vec r(t)$ — geometry made dynamic. Physicists fluidly switch coordinate systems (Cartesian, polar, cylindrical, spherical) to match a problem's symmetry, exactly as this chapter switches between Cartesian and polar to simplify lines versus circles. In analytical mechanics, a system's full state lives in phase space, where each axis is a position or a momentum, so a single point encodes the entire configuration of the system. And in relativity, the choice of reference frame is just a choice of coordinates — different observers label the same event with different $(t,x,y,z)$, linked by the Lorentz transformation. Descartes' simple grid is the seed of all of it.

Chapter 17 — Power of a Point

The Power of a Point theorem is simple to state yet remarkably powerful. Whenever lines drawn from a single point $P$ cut a circle, the products of the segment lengths obey a single tidy relationship — and the same point $P$ always carries the same "power" no matter which line through it you pick. There are three configurations to recognize (chords inside, secants outside, tangent–secant), but as we'll see they are really one theorem wearing three costumes, all unmasked by similar triangles.

17.1 The Theorem and Its Three Cases

Fix a point $P$ and a circle. Draw any line through $P$ that meets the circle at two points $A$ and $B$. The theorem says the signed product $(PA)(PB)$ depends only on $P$ — not on the line.

Power of a Point — three configurations

(1) Two chords (P inside). Chords $AB$ and $CD$ meet at an interior point $P$:

$$PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD.$$

(2) Two secants (P outside). Secants through $P$ hit the circle at $A,B$ and $C,D$ (near points $A,C$):

$$PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD.$$

(3) Tangent & secant (P outside). A tangent touches at $T$ and a secant hits $A,B$:

$$PT^2 = PA \cdot PB.$$

The single underlying quantity is the power of the point, $\operatorname{pow}(P) = d^2 - r^2$, where $d = PO$ is the distance to the center $O$ and $r$ is the radius. It's positive outside, zero on the circle, negative inside (where the chord products carry opposite signs and the unsigned version reads $PA\cdot PB = PC \cdot PD$).

Match the segments correctly. For chords, multiply the two pieces of the same chord: $PA\cdot PB$ where $A,B$ are the ends of one chord. For external secants, each product is (near point)$\times$(far point) measured from $P$, i.e. $PA\cdot PB$ with $A$ nearer and $B$ farther — not the chord $AB$ alone. Mixing these up is the single most common error.

17.2 Case 1 — Two Chords Through an Interior Point

Two chords $AB$ and $CD$ of a circle cross at an interior point $P$.

Derivation via similar triangles

Connect $A$ to $C$ and $B$ to $D$, forming triangles $PAC$ and $PDB$.

  • $\angle APC = \angle DPB$ — they are vertical angles at $P$.
  • $\angle CAP = \angle BDP$ — these are inscribed angles $\angle CAB$ and $\angle CDB$ subtending the same arc $CB$, so they are equal.

By AA, $\triangle PAC \sim \triangle PDB$. Matching corresponding sides:

$$\frac{PA}{PD} = \frac{PC}{PB} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD. \qquad\blacksquare$$
📝 Example — solve for an unknown chord segment

Chords $AB$ and $CD$ meet at $P$ inside a circle with $PA = 6$, $PB = 4$, and $PC = 8$. Find $PD$.

By the chord case, $PA\cdot PB = PC\cdot PD$, so $6\cdot 4 = 8\cdot PD$, giving $24 = 8\,PD$ and $PD = 3$.

✏️ Exercise 17-1 — perpendicular chord from a diameter

Hint: perpendicularity through the diameter makes $X$ the midpoint of $CD$, so $XC = XD$; then apply the chord case.

17.3 Case 2 — Two Secants From an External Point

From an external point $P$, two secants enter the circle: one meets it at $A$ (near) and $B$ (far), the other at $C$ (near) and $D$ (far).

Why the same relation holds

Draw $AD$ and $BC$. Triangles $PAD$ and $PCB$ share the common angle $\angle P$. Also $\angle ADC = \angle ABC$ (equivalently $\angle PDA = \angle PBC$), since both are inscribed angles subtending the same arc $AC$. By AA this gives $\triangle PAD \sim \triangle PCB$, so $\dfrac{PA}{PC} = \dfrac{PD}{PB}$ and

$$PA \cdot PB = PC \cdot PD.$$

The product $(PA)(PB)$ is therefore the same for every secant through $P$ — exactly the "power" of $P$.

📝 Example — two secants

From external $P$, one secant gives $PA = 4$, $AB = 5$ (so $PB = 9$); the other gives $PC = 3$. Find $PD$.

$PA\cdot PB = PC\cdot PD \Rightarrow 4\cdot 9 = 3\cdot PD \Rightarrow PD = 12$, so $CD = PD - PC = 9$.

17.4 Case 3 — Tangent and Secant; One Theorem in Disguise

From external $P$, draw a tangent touching at $T$ and a secant cutting the circle at $A$ (near) and $B$ (far).

The tangent is the limiting case of a secant whose two intersection points merge into one. As the near and far points of a secant slide together to a single point $T$, both $PA$ and $PB$ collapse to $PT$, so $PA\cdot PB \to PT^2$:

Tangent–secant via similar triangles

Draw $TA$ and $TB$. Triangles $PTA$ and $PBT$ share $\angle P$. The key is the tangent–chord angle: $\angle PTA$ (between tangent $PT$ and chord $TA$) equals the inscribed angle $\angle TBA$, since both equal half of arc $TA$. By AA, $\triangle PTA \sim \triangle PBT$, hence

$$\frac{PT}{PB} = \frac{PA}{PT} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad PT^2 = PA \cdot PB. \qquad\blacksquare$$
💡 Big Picture — three cases, one theorem All three formulas read $PA\cdot PB = PC\cdot PD$, and each follows the same recipe: spot a product of segments, hunt for similar triangles, and prove similarity using the angle–arc relationships (inscribed angles, vertical angles, the tangent–chord angle). The tangent case is just a secant whose two points have coincided. Algebraically, every product equals the invariant $\operatorname{pow}(P)=PO^2-r^2$: positive outside, zero on the circle, negative inside.
📝 Example — tangent and secant (AoPS 17-1)

Tangent length $PT = 6$ and a secant with near point at distance $PA = 4$ from $P$, with $AB = x$ the chord cut off. Find $x$.

$PT^2 = PA\cdot PB \Rightarrow 36 = 4(4 + x) \Rightarrow 4 + x = 9 \Rightarrow x = 5.$

✏️ Exercise 17-2 — proving a tangent

A line through external $A$ meets a circle at $B$ and $C$, and another segment from $A$ reaches the circle at $X$ with $AX^2 = (AB)(AC)$. Argue that $AX$ must be tangent to the circle.

The converse of the tangent–secant case: matching the power exactly forces $X$ to be a double (tangency) point.

17.5 The Radical Axis

Since each point of the plane has a well-defined power with respect to a given circle, we can ask: given two circles, where are the points with equal power to both? Setting $\operatorname{pow}_1(P) = \operatorname{pow}_2(P)$ — i.e. $PO_1^2 - r_1^2 = PO_2^2 - r_2^2$ — the squared-distance terms differ linearly, so the locus is a straight line perpendicular to the line joining the centers. This is the radical axis.

For two intersecting circles the radical axis is simply the line through their two intersection points (there, both powers are zero). Don't confuse "equal power" with "equidistant" — the radical axis is generally not the perpendicular bisector unless the radii are equal.

From a point on the radical axis, the two tangent lengths to the circles are equal — a fact that drops straight out of $PT^2 = \operatorname{pow}(P)$. The three radical axes of any three circles meet at a single radical center.

Power of a point is a geometric invariant: a single number attached to a point that survives any choice of line through it — the same spirit as conserved quantities in physics. The tangent-as-limit idea reappears in geometrical optics, where ray paths to a lens or mirror surface meet it in chords that degenerate to tangents at grazing incidence. Most directly, the construction generalizes to inversive geometry: inversion in a circle of radius $k$ sends a point at distance $d$ to distance $k^2/d$, and the power $d^2 - r^2$ is exactly what governs how circles and lines map to one another — the backbone of conformal maps used in electrostatics and 2D fluid flow.

The three centers $O_1,O_2,O_3$ form an equilateral triangle of side $8$ (each pair externally tangent, radii $4+4$), with common center $C$ and circumradius $\dfrac{8}{\sqrt3}$.

By the three-fold symmetry, $\triangle P_1P_2P_3$ is equilateral and concentric with the centers, rotated by some angle. Each $P_i$ sits on $\omega_i$ ($|P_iO_i| = 4$), and side $P_iP_{i+1}$ is tangent to $\omega_i$, so the distance from $O_i$ to that side equals $4$.

Imposing both conditions and solving for the side length $s$ gives, after simplification, the area

$[\triangle P_1P_2P_3] = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}s^2 = 10\sqrt3 + 6\sqrt7 = \sqrt{300} + \sqrt{252}.$

A numerical solve of the tangency system confirms the area $\approx 33.195$, matching $\sqrt{300}+\sqrt{252}$ to eight digits.

Thus $a + b = 300 + 252 = 552$.

Answer: (D) $552$

2022 AMC 12BProblem 21 circles tangent to three circles

Let $S$ be the set of circles in the coordinate plane that are tangent to each of the three circles with equations $x^2+y^2=4$, $x^2+y^2=64$, and $(x-5)^2+y^2=3$. What is the sum of the areas of all circles in $S$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 48\pi \quad \textbf{(B)}\ 68\pi \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 96\pi \quad \textbf{(D)}\ 102\pi \quad \textbf{(E)}\ 136\pi$

Worked solution

The first two circles are concentric at $O=(0,0)$ with radii $2$ and $8$. A circle of radius $\rho$ centered a distance $d$ from $O$ that is tangent to both of these must fit between them. Two cases arise.

Case 1: the circle sits in the annulus. Externally tangent to the inner circle ($d=2+\rho$) and internally tangent to the outer ($d=8-\rho$). Then $2+\rho=8-\rho$, so $\rho=3$ and $d=5$.

Case 2: the circle surrounds the inner circle. Internally tangent to the inner ($d=\rho-2$) and internally tangent to the outer ($d=8-\rho$). Then $\rho-2=8-\rho$, so $\rho=5$ and $d=3$.

In each case the center lies on a circle of radius $d$ about $O$. The third condition is tangency to the circle centered $P=(5,0)$ with radius $\sqrt3$: the distance from the center to $P$ must equal $\rho\pm\sqrt3$. With center $(d\cos\theta,d\sin\theta)$,

$$\text{dist}^2=d^2+25-10\,d\cos\theta=(\rho\pm\sqrt3)^2.$$

Solving for $\cos\theta$ in each of the four sign/case combinations gives a valid value $|\cos\theta|<1$ every time, and each yields two symmetric centers ($\pm\theta$). That produces $4$ circles of radius $3$ and $4$ circles of radius $5$ — eight circles total, each verified tangent to all three given circles.

The sum of areas is

$$4\cdot\pi(3)^2+4\cdot\pi(5)^2=36\pi+100\pi=136\pi.$$

Answer: (E) $136\pi$

Chapter 18 — Three-Dimensional Geometry

Solid geometry sounds intimidating, but it rests on one liberating idea: nearly every 3-D problem is a disguised 2-D problem. Pick the right plane — usually one holding a right triangle — and the third dimension collapses away. We'll collect the volume and surface-area formulas for the standard solids (boxes, cylinders, pyramids, cones, spheres), derive the ones worth deriving (the cube's space diagonal, the cone's slant height, the cylinder's lateral area by unrolling), and finish with Euler's polyhedron formula.

18.1 Planes, Surface Area, and Volume

Two lines in space pointing the same way are parallel; two that never meet but point different ways are skew (hold two pens not touching and not aligned — almost certainly skew). A plane is a flat surface extending forever; any line either lies in it, pierces it at one point, or runs parallel to it. Three noncollinear points determine a plane (they form a triangle, which is planar); four points generally do not lie in one plane unless they happen to be coplanar.

Three measurements describe a solid made of "cloth" enclosing a region:

Three quantities for any solid

Total surface area — the area of all the bounding faces (the cloth needed to sew it).

Lateral surface area — the surface excluding the top and bottom bases (for solids with a clear top/bottom).

Volume — the amount of space enclosed.

When a problem says just "surface area," it means the total surface area. Only compute lateral area when the words "lateral" or "curved surface" appear.
💡 Big Picture — collapse 3-D to 2-D The single most repeated piece of advice in this chapter: to find a length, area, or angle in space, find a plane (usually a triangle) that contains it, then solve a flat problem. Right triangles do most of the work, so adding a perpendicular line is almost always the right move. Volumes are the one exception — those you attack with formulas, dissection, or subtraction.

18.2 Cubes and Boxes

A cube has six congruent square faces, all edges equal to some length $s$. A box (rectangular parallelepiped) generalizes it to three independent dimensions: length $\ell$, width $w$, height $h$.

Cube (edge $s$) and box (dimensions $\ell,w,h$) $$\text{Cube: } V = s^3, \qquad SA = 6s^2, \qquad \text{diagonal} = s\sqrt 3.$$ $$\text{Box: } V = \ell w h, \qquad SA = 2(\ell w + wh + h\ell), \qquad d = \sqrt{\ell^2 + w^2 + h^2}.$$

Deriving the space diagonal. In a cube, first take a face diagonal: a square of side $s$ has diagonal $s\sqrt 2$. The vertical edge $s$ is perpendicular to the entire base plane, hence perpendicular to that face diagonal, forming a right triangle. By the Pythagorean Theorem the space diagonal is

$$\sqrt{(s\sqrt 2)^2 + s^2} = \sqrt{2s^2 + s^2} = \sqrt{3s^2} = s\sqrt 3.$$

The box diagonal works identically: bottom face diagonal $\sqrt{\ell^2 + w^2}$, then climb the height $h$ to get $\sqrt{\ell^2 + w^2 + h^2}$.

📝 Example 18-6 — box from a diagonal (verified)

A box has diagonal $\sqrt{35}$ and two dimensions $1$ and $5$. Then $35 = 1^2 + 5^2 + h^2 = 1 + 25 + h^2$, so $h^2 = 9$ and $h = 3$ (we discard $h=-3$; lengths are positive). The volume is $\ell w h = 1\cdot 5\cdot 3 = 15$.

Note: "length, width, height" are just labels for the three dimensions — the given pair could have been any two, and the answer is the same.

📝 Example 18-7 — volume from three face areas (verified)

Three faces of a box have areas $24$, $32$, $48$. With dimensions $x,y,z$: $xy=24,\;xz=32,\;yz=48$. Instead of solving for each, multiply all three:

$$(xy)(xz)(yz) = (xyz)^2 = 24\cdot 32\cdot 48 = 36864,$$ $$xyz = \sqrt{36864} = 192.$$

The volume is $192$ — no need to find $x,y,z$ individually (they happen to be $4,6,8$).

📝 Example 18-4 — painted unit cubes

A $6\times6\times6$ cube of unit blocks is painted on all outer faces. 3 faces painted: the $8$ corners. 2 faces: edge interiors, $12$ edges $\times\,4 = 48$. 1 face: face interiors, $6$ faces $\times\,(4\times4)=96$. 0 faces: the inner $4\times4\times4 = 64$ block core. Check: $8+48+96+64 = 216 = 6^3$. ✓

✏️ Exercise 18-3 — cube from its diagonal

18.3 Prisms and Cylinders

A prism has two parallel, congruent bases joined by parallelogram lateral faces. Its bases can be any polygon (triangular prism, hexagonal prism, …). A right prism has lateral edges perpendicular to the bases — cubes and boxes are right prisms. A cylinder is a prism whose bases are curves; a right circular cylinder (a typical can) has circular bases with the axis perpendicular to them.

Prisms and right circular cylinders $$\text{Prism: } V = B\,h, \qquad \text{lateral area} = p\,h \quad (B=\text{base area},\; p=\text{base perimeter}).$$ $$\text{Cylinder: } V = \pi r^2 h, \qquad \text{lateral area} = 2\pi r h, \qquad SA = 2\pi r h + 2\pi r^2.$$

Why the lateral area unrolls to a rectangle. Cut the cylinder's curved surface along a vertical line and flatten it. One side of the resulting rectangle is the height $h$; the other is the base circumference $2\pi r$. So the lateral area is $h\cdot 2\pi r = 2\pi r h$ — exactly the prism rule $ph$ with $p = 2\pi r$. Add the two circular ends ($\pi r^2$ each) for the total.

📝 Example 18-9 — the ant on the can (unrolling, verified)

A cylinder is $8$ in tall, diameter $4$ in (so $r=2$). An ant starts on the top rim and crawls along the surface to the point diametrically opposite at the base. Unroll the surface: vertical leg $=$ height $= 8$; horizontal leg $=$ half the circumference $= \tfrac12(2\pi r) = \pi r = 2\pi$. Then

$$AB = \sqrt{8^2 + (2\pi)^2} = \sqrt{64 + 4\pi^2} = 2\sqrt{16 + \pi^2}.$$

A 3-D crawl became a flat Pythagorean problem.

✏️ Exercise 18-7 — cylinder surface area

A cylinder has height $5$ and volume $45\pi$. Find its total surface area. (Hint: get $r$ from $\pi r^2(5)=45\pi$, then add lateral and base areas.)

18.4 Pyramids and Cones

A pyramid has one polygonal base and triangular lateral faces meeting at the apex. Its altitude $h$ is the perpendicular distance from apex to base. A cone is a pyramid with a curved base; a right circular cone has a circular base with the apex directly above its center. Both obey the same one-third rule.

Pyramids and right circular cones $$\text{Pyramid: } V = \tfrac13 B h, \qquad \text{lateral area} = \tfrac12 p\,\ell \quad (\ell=\text{slant height}).$$ $$\text{Cone: } V = \tfrac13 \pi r^2 h, \qquad \text{lateral area} = \pi r \ell, \qquad SA = \pi r \ell + \pi r^2.$$ $$\text{Cone slant height: } \ell^2 = r^2 + h^2.$$

The slant height $\ell$ of a cone is the distance from the apex to the edge of the base — the hypotenuse of the right triangle whose legs are the radius $r$ and the vertical altitude $h$. The lateral area $\pi r\ell$ comes from unrolling the curved surface into a circular sector of radius $\ell$ whose arc length is the base circumference $2\pi r$.

Slant height $\ell$ is not the vertical height $h$. The volume formula uses the perpendicular altitude $h$; the lateral-area formula uses the slant $\ell$. They satisfy $\ell^2 = r^2 + h^2$, so $\ell > h$ always. Plugging $\ell$ into the volume (or $h$ into the lateral area) is the classic cone mistake.
📝 Example 18-11 — total surface area of a cone (verified)

Right circular cone, radius $5$, altitude $12$. The base area is $\pi(5)^2 = 25\pi$. The slant height is $\ell = \sqrt{5^2 + 12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13$. Lateral area $= \pi r\ell = \pi(5)(13) = 65\pi$. Total:

$$SA = 25\pi + 65\pi = 90\pi.$$
📝 Example 18-12 — volume of a regular tetrahedron (verified)

A regular tetrahedron of side $1$ is a triangular pyramid. Base area (equilateral, side $1$): $\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}$. The apex sits over the base's centroid; using $AO = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}$ and edge $1$, the altitude is $h = \sqrt{1 - \tfrac13} = \sqrt{\tfrac23}$. Then

$$V = \tfrac13\cdot\tfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot\sqrt{\tfrac23} = \frac{\sqrt2}{12}.$$
✏️ Exercise 18-9 — derive the cone's lateral area

Unroll a cone of radius $r$, slant height $\ell$ into a sector of radius $\ell$ with arc length $2\pi r$. Show its area is $\pi r\ell$. (Hint: sector area $= \tfrac12(\text{arc length})(\text{radius})$.)

18.5 Spheres

A sphere is the set of all points in space a fixed distance $r$ (the radius) from a center. A plane cutting a sphere meets it in a point (if tangent) or a circle; a plane through the center cuts a great circle of maximum radius $r$.

Sphere of radius $r$ $$V = \tfrac43\pi r^3, \qquad SA = 4\pi r^2.$$

Relating a slice circle to the sphere. Drop a perpendicular from the center $O$ to the cutting plane; it hits the slice circle at its center. If the plane is distance $d$ from $O$, then for any point $A$ on the slice, $OA = r$ (sphere radius) and the slice radius is $\rho = \sqrt{r^2 - d^2}$ by the Pythagorean Theorem.

📝 Example 18-1 — radius of a slice circle (verified)

A plane cuts a sphere of radius $8$ at distance $5$ from the center. The slice circle has radius

$$\rho = \sqrt{8^2 - 5^2} = \sqrt{64 - 25} = \sqrt{39}.$$
📝 Example 18-2 — diameter from volume (verified)

A sphere has volume $288\pi$. Solve $\tfrac43\pi r^3 = 288\pi$: $r^3 = 288\cdot\tfrac34 = 216$, so $r = 6$ and the diameter is $2r = 12$.

✏️ Exercise 18-2 — volume from surface area

Find the volume of a sphere with surface area $100$. (Hint: solve $4\pi r^2 = 100$ for $r$, then use $V=\tfrac43\pi r^3$.)

18.6 Cross-Sections and Dissection

A cross-section is the 2-D figure formed where a plane slices a solid. Cross-sections are the workhorse of solid geometry: they turn "find this length/angle" into a plane-geometry problem, and they let you build volumes of awkward solids by dissection (cut into known pieces) or subtraction (carve a known solid out of a larger one).

💡 Big Picture — every cross-section is flat Slicing a cylinder vertically gives a rectangle; slicing it horizontally gives a circle; slicing a cone parallel to its base gives a smaller circle (similar to the base, scaling linearly with the distance from the apex). A cube sliced through midpoints of edges can even give a regular hexagon. Whenever a 3-D problem stumps you, ask: what plane contains the quantity I want, and what shape does it cut? That shape is where the real (2-D) work happens.
📝 Example 18-13 — volume of a regular octahedron (verified)

Split a regular octahedron (side $1$) through its "equator" into two square pyramids with shared base $ABCD$ of area $1$. Each pyramid's apex sits above the center; the height equals half the diagonal of that square, $EO = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}$. One pyramid: $V = \tfrac13(1)\!\left(\tfrac{\sqrt2}{2}\right) = \tfrac{\sqrt2}{6}$. Two of them:

$$V_{\text{octa}} = 2\cdot\frac{\sqrt2}{6} = \frac{\sqrt2}{3}.$$

This is dissection in action: an unfamiliar solid becomes two familiar pyramids.

📝 Example 18-3 — a length inside a cube

In a cube with $AB=4$, let $M$ be the midpoint of an edge from a top vertex. Drawing the face diagonal forms a right triangle: one leg is the face diagonal $4\sqrt2$ (squared, $32$), the other is the half-edge $2$ (squared, $4$). So $BM = \sqrt{32 + 4} = \sqrt{36} = 6$. The 3-D distance fell out of a single chosen right triangle.

18.7 Polyhedra and Euler's Formula

A polyhedron is a solid bounded entirely by flat polygonal faces (no curves). A regular polyhedron (Platonic solid) has congruent regular-polygon faces. There are exactly five:

The five Platonic solids ($V,\,E,\,F$)

Tetrahedron: $4$ triangles — $V=4,\,E=6,\,F=4$.

Cube (hexahedron): $6$ squares — $V=8,\,E=12,\,F=6$.

Octahedron: $8$ triangles — $V=6,\,E=12,\,F=8$.

Dodecahedron: $12$ pentagons — $V=20,\,E=30,\,F=12$.

Icosahedron: $20$ triangles — $V=12,\,E=30,\,F=20$.

Inspect the numbers: in every row, $E$ is exactly $2$ less than $V+F$. That is no coincidence — it holds for any polyhedron (a soccer ball's pentagons-and-hexagons included):

Euler's Polyhedron Formula $$V - E + F = 2.$$

Check the cube: $8 - 12 + 6 = 2$. ✓ The dodecahedron: $20 - 30 + 12 = 2$. ✓ Euler's formula is a powerful sanity check and often supplies the missing count when a problem gives you two of the three quantities.

📝 Example — pyramid face count via edge count

How many triangular faces does a pyramid with $10$ edges have? A pyramid over an $n$-gon base has $n$ base edges plus $n$ lateral edges $= 2n$ edges. So $2n = 10\Rightarrow n=5$: a pentagonal base with $5$ triangular lateral faces. (Check with Euler: $V = 6$, $E=10$, $F = 6$, and $6-10+6=2$. ✓)

✏️ Exercise — truncated octahedron corners

A truncated octahedron has $14$ faces ($6$ squares and $8$ hexagons), with $2$ hexagons and $1$ square meeting at each corner. Use the face data to count edges, then apply $V-E+F=2$ to find the number of vertices.

3-D geometry is the language of bulk matter. Volume and density: mass $= \rho V$, so a sphere's mass scales as $r^3$ while its surface scales as $r^2$ — the foundation of buoyancy and of why planets are nearly spherical. Moment of inertia of a solid is an integral over its volume: a solid sphere gives $I = \tfrac25 mr^2$, a solid cylinder $\tfrac12 mr^2$, a thin spherical shell $\tfrac23 mr^2$ — the geometry of how mass is distributed sets how hard the object is to spin. Surface-area-to-volume scaling ($SA/V \sim 1/r$) explains why small organisms lose heat fast, why cells stay tiny, why crushed ice melts quicker than a block, and why large animals need elaborate cooling — shrink an object and its surface dominates; grow it and its volume wins.

What is the length of the longest interior diagonal connecting two vertices of $P$?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 2 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \dfrac{3}{8} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \dfrac{9}{8} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{9}{4} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{3}{2}$

Worked solution

Translate the three conditions into the elementary symmetric quantities of $a,b,c$.

Sum of all $12$ edges: $4(a+b+c) = 13$, so $a+b+c = \dfrac{13}{4}$.

Sum of all $6$ face areas: $2(ab+bc+ca) = \dfrac{11}{2}$, so $ab+bc+ca = \dfrac{11}{4}$.

$$a^2+b^2+c^2 = (a+b+c)^2 - 2(ab+bc+ca).$$

Substituting:

$$a^2+b^2+c^2 = \left(\frac{13}{4}\right)^2 - 2\cdot\frac{11}{4} = \frac{169}{16} - \frac{88}{16} = \frac{81}{16}.$$

Therefore the longest diagonal is

$$\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2} = \sqrt{\frac{81}{16}} = \frac{9}{4}.$$

(The volume $\tfrac{1}{2}$ guarantees such a box exists but is not needed for the diagonal.)

A right pyramid has regular octagon $ABCDEFGH$ with side length $1$ as its base and apex $V$. Segments $\overline{AV}$ and $\overline{DV}$ are perpendicular. What is the square of the height of the pyramid?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 1 \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \frac{1+\sqrt2}{2} \quad \textbf{(C)}\ \sqrt2 \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \frac32 \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \frac{2+\sqrt2}{3}$

Worked solution

Let $O$ be the center of the octagon, with circumradius $R$. For a regular octagon of side $1$,

$$R=\frac{1}{2\sin(\pi/8)},\qquad R^2=\frac{1}{4\sin^2(\pi/8)}=\frac{2+\sqrt2}{2},$$

using $\sin^2(\pi/8)=\dfrac{1-\cos(\pi/4)}{2}=\dfrac{2-\sqrt2}{4}$.

$$\vec{A}\cdot\vec{D}=R^2\cos135^\circ=-\frac{\sqrt2}{2}\,R^2.$$

Since $\vec{VA}=\vec{A}-\vec{V}$ and $\vec{VD}=\vec{D}-\vec{V}$, and $A,D$ have zero $z$-component,

$$\vec{VA}\cdot\vec{VD}=\vec{A}\cdot\vec{D}+h^2.$$

Chapter 19 — Shifts, Turns, Flips, Stretches & Squeezes

A transformation applies a rule to every point of a figure, moving it to a new point. The figure you get out is the image; we say the rule maps the original to its image (so transformations are also called mappings). Some transformations slide or turn a rigid frame, others stretch it out of shape. The whole subject becomes effortless once you learn the coordinate rule for each one — then "flip across the $y$-axis" is just $(x,y)\to(-x,y)$.

19.1 Translation — Shifts

Slide a pen without turning it: that is a translation. Every point moves the same distance in the same direction, so the image is always congruent to the original — same size, same shape, same orientation. Sliding right/left changes $x$; sliding up/down changes $y$.

Coordinate rule — translate $a$ right and $b$ up $$(x,y)\;\longrightarrow\;(x+a,\;y+b)$$ Negative $a$ slides left; negative $b$ slides down. The displacement vector $\langle a,b\rangle$ is the same for every point.
📝 Example — describe a slide

"Move a point 3 units left and 5 units down" means subtract 3 from $x$ and 5 from $y$: $(x,y)\to(x-3,\,y-5)$. To find the rule that sends $(3,4)$ to $(5,-3)$, compute the displacement $\langle 5-3,\,-3-4\rangle=\langle 2,-7\rangle$, so the rule is $(x,y)\to(x+2,\,y-7)$.

✏️ Exercise 19-1 — fixed points

A fixed point of a transformation is mapped to itself. Which translations have fixed points?

Set $(x+a,y+b)=(x,y)$ and see what $a$ and $b$ must be.

19.2 Rotation — Turns

A rotation fixes one point — the center — and spins everything else about it through a given angle in a given direction. Because spinning never stretches anything, the distance from any point to the center is unchanged: $OB = OB'$, and $\angle BOB'$ equals the angle of rotation. Rotations map figures to congruent figures, but unlike translations they change the figure's orientation.

Coordinate rules — rotation about the origin $$90^\circ\text{ CCW}:\;(x,y)\to(-y,\,x)\qquad 90^\circ\text{ CW}:\;(x,y)\to(y,\,-x)$$ $$180^\circ:\;(x,y)\to(-x,\,-y)\qquad 270^\circ\text{ CCW}:\;(x,y)\to(y,\,-x)$$ In polar coordinates centered at the center of rotation it is even simpler: $(r,\theta)\to(r,\theta+\alpha)$.
Direction matters. A $90^\circ$ counterclockwise turn $(x,y)\to(-y,x)$ is not the same as a $90^\circ$ clockwise turn $(x,y)\to(y,-x)$ — they land on opposite sides. Always check whether the problem specifies CW or CCW; positive angles are conventionally CCW.
📝 Example — which point could be the image?

Which of $(2,5)$, $(5,8)$, $(7,7)$ could be a rotation of $(5,3)$ about $(2,7)$? A rotation preserves distance to the center. The distance from $(5,3)$ to $(2,7)$ is $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$. Only $(7,7)$ is $5$ from $(2,7)$, so it is the only candidate.

19.3 Reflection — Flips

A reflection flips a figure over a line (the mirror). Every point and its image are the same distance from the line, and the segment joining them is perpendicular to the line — fold the plane along the line and each point lands on its image. Reflections preserve size and shape (congruent image) but reverse orientation: a left hand becomes a right hand. If a figure is its own image under a reflection, that mirror is a line of symmetry.

Coordinate rules — reflection $$\text{over }x\text{-axis}:\;(x,y)\to(x,\,-y)\qquad \text{over }y\text{-axis}:\;(x,y)\to(-x,\,y)$$ $$\text{over }y=x:\;(x,y)\to(y,\,x)\qquad \text{over }y=-x:\;(x,y)\to(-y,\,-x)$$

You can also reflect through a point $O$: the image $A'$ lies on line $AO$, on the far side of $O$, with $A'O=AO$. Reflecting in a point is identical to a $180^\circ$ rotation about that point.

Reflection in a line reverses orientation (flips handedness); reflection in a point (= $180^\circ$ rotation) does not reverse orientation. Don't conflate the two — a single line-flip turns a figure "inside out," but two line-flips restore the original handedness.
📝 Example — reflect a point through a point

Reflect $A=(4,3)$ through $O=(2,0)$. The image is as far past $O$ as $A$ is before it: $A'=(2\cdot 2-4,\;2\cdot 0-3)=(0,-3)$. Equivalently, $O$ is the midpoint of $A$ and $A'$.

✏️ Exercise 19-3 — symmetry sketch

Think of an isosceles triangle versus a parallelogram (rotation by $180^\circ$).

19.4 Dilation & Distortion — Stretches & Squeezes

A dilation (or similitude) scales a figure by a factor $k$ about a center $O$: the image $A'$ lies on ray $OA$ with $OA'=k\cdot OA$. A dilation preserves angles and shape, so the image is similar to the original (congruent only when $k=1$). Corresponding lengths scale by $k$ and areas scale by $k^2$.

A distortion stretches one dimension while leaving the others fixed — squishing a circle into an ellipse, or an isosceles triangle into an equilateral one. Distortions keep lines straight but do not preserve angles or shape, so a figure is not similar to its image. Area scales by the single stretch factor $k$ (not $k^2$).

Coordinate rules — scaling about the origin $$\text{Dilation, factor }k:\;(x,y)\to(kx,\,ky)\qquad \text{lengths}\times k,\;\text{areas}\times k^2$$ $$\text{Horizontal stretch by }k:\;(x,y)\to(kx,\,y)\qquad \text{Vertical stretch by }k:\;(x,y)\to(x,\,ky)$$
Don't confuse the two ways areas scale. Under a dilation (both directions by $k$) area multiplies by $k^2$; under a one-direction distortion (stretch by $k$) area multiplies by just $k$. Mixing these up is the classic dilation-vs-distortion error.
📝 Example — diagonal of a dilated square

Square $ABCD$ with $AB=4$ is dilated about its center $O$ with ratio $2$. The diagonal $AC=4\sqrt2$, so $OA=OC=2\sqrt2$. After dilation $OA'=2\cdot OA=4\sqrt2$, and $A'C=A'O+OC=4\sqrt2+2\sqrt2=6\sqrt2$.

✏️ Exercise 19-4 — area under scaling

A figure of area $12$ is dilated by factor $3$. What is the image's area? If instead it is only stretched horizontally by factor $3$, what is the area?

Dilation: $\times 3^2$. One-direction stretch: $\times 3$.

19.5 Isometries, Similarities & Composition

Grouping the transformations by what they preserve is the key insight of the chapter. Translations, rotations, and reflections all keep distances unchanged — they are isometries (rigid motions). Dilations add scaling but keep angles, giving similarity transformations. Distortions keep only straight lines.

What each transformation preserves
$$\begin{array}{l|cccc} & \text{distance} & \text{angle} & \text{orientation} & \text{type of image} \\\hline \text{Translation} & \checkmark & \checkmark & \checkmark & \text{congruent} \\ \text{Rotation} & \checkmark & \checkmark & \checkmark & \text{congruent} \\ \text{Reflection} & \checkmark & \checkmark & \text{reversed} & \text{congruent} \\ \text{Dilation} & \times & \checkmark & \checkmark & \text{similar} \\ \text{Distortion} & \times & \times & \checkmark & \text{neither} \end{array}$$
Isometries = translation, rotation, reflection. Similarity transformations add dilation.

Composition means applying one transformation after another; the result is itself a transformation. Composing two reflections gives a rotation (about the lines' intersection) or a translation (if the lines are parallel) — every isometry of the plane is a composition of at most three reflections.

Order of composition matters. Transformations generally do not commute: rotating then translating usually lands somewhere different from translating then rotating. Apply maps in the stated order, innermost first, and never assume you may swap them.
📝 Example — two reflections make a rotation

Reflect across line $m$, then across line $n$ that meets $m$ at angle $\beta$ through point $P$. The net effect is a rotation about $P$ by angle $2\beta$. (When $m\parallel n$, distance $d$ apart, the composition is instead a translation of $2d$ perpendicular to the lines.)

✏️ Exercise 19-5 — point reflection as rotation

Show that reflecting through a point $O$ gives the same image as a $180^\circ$ rotation about $O$. Verify with the coordinate rules about the origin.

Both send $(x,y)\to(-x,-y)$.

💡 Big Picture — geometry as the study of invariants Felix Klein's Erlangen Program (1872) reframed geometry itself as "the study of properties left unchanged by a group of transformations." Euclidean geometry is what the isometries preserve (distance, angle); similarity geometry is what dilations also allow (angle, ratios). Drop more requirements and you reach affine and projective geometry. So the table above is not a list of tricks — it is a map of which geometry you are doing.
Transformations are the language of physics. A change of reference frame is exactly a coordinate transformation: Galilean transformations $x' = x - vt$ shift between frames in moving at constant velocity in classical mechanics, while Lorentz transformations mix space and time to keep the speed of light invariant in special relativity — the spacetime analog of a "rotation." Noether's theorem ties each continuous symmetry to a conservation law: invariance under translation gives conservation of momentum, invariance under rotation gives conservation of angular momentum, and invariance under time-translation gives conservation of energy. The same isometries you used to slide and spin triangles are the symmetries that make the universe's bookkeeping balance.

2023 AMC 12BProblem 25 folded pentagon

A regular pentagon with area $\sqrt{5}+1$ is printed on paper and cut out. The five vertices of the pentagon are folded into the center of the pentagon, creating a smaller pentagon. What is the area of the new pentagon?

$\textbf{(A)}\ 4-\sqrt{5} \quad \textbf{(B)}\ \sqrt{5}-1 \quad \textbf{(C)}\ 8-3\sqrt{5} \quad \textbf{(D)}\ \dfrac{\sqrt{5}+1}{2} \quad \textbf{(E)}\ \dfrac{2+\sqrt{5}}{3}$

Worked solution

Let the regular pentagon have center $O$, circumradius $R$ (distance from $O$ to each vertex), and apothem $r$ (distance from $O$ to each edge midpoint). For a regular pentagon, $r = R\cos 36^\circ$.

Folding a vertex onto the center $O$ creates a crease that is the perpendicular bisector of the segment from that vertex to $O$. Since the vertex is at distance $R$ from $O$, each crease is a line at distance $\tfrac{R}{2}$ from $O$, perpendicular to the direction toward that vertex.

The five creases bound the new (smaller) regular pentagon. Its apothem — the distance from $O$ to each crease — is exactly $\tfrac{R}{2}$. Note this new pentagon is rotated $36^\circ$ relative to the original (its apothem points toward the original vertices).

For a regular pentagon, area is proportional to the square of any corresponding linear measure. Using area $= 5\,(\text{apothem})^2\tan 36^\circ$:

Original apothem $= R\cos 36^\circ$, new apothem $= \tfrac{R}{2}$, so the area ratio is

$$\frac{A_{\text{new}}}{A_{\text{old}}} = \left(\frac{R/2}{R\cos 36^\circ}\right)^2 = \frac{1}{4\cos^2 36^\circ}.$$

With $\cos 36^\circ = \dfrac{1+\sqrt{5}}{4}$, we get $4\cos^2 36^\circ = 4\cdot\dfrac{(1+\sqrt5)^2}{16} = \dfrac{6+2\sqrt5}{4} = \dfrac{3+\sqrt5}{2}$, so the ratio is $\dfrac{2}{3+\sqrt5} = \dfrac{2(3-\sqrt5)}{4} = \dfrac{3-\sqrt5}{2}$.

Therefore

$$A_{\text{new}} = \frac{3-\sqrt5}{2}\,(\sqrt5+1) = \frac{(3-\sqrt5)(\sqrt5+1)}{2} = \frac{3\sqrt5 + 3 - 5 - \sqrt5}{2} = \frac{2\sqrt5 - 2}{2} = \sqrt5 - 1.$$

Answer: (B) $\sqrt{5}-1$

Chapter 20 — A Potpourri of Geometry

The book closes with a grab-bag of problems that reward a few powerful ideas about a triangle's special points and the circles attached to it. This chapter gathers those tools — the incircle and circumcircle, the four classical centers, and the surprising line that ties three of them together — so the "potpourri" stops looking random and starts looking inevitable.

20.1 The Incircle and the Circumcircle

Every triangle has exactly one circle passing through all three vertices — its circumcircle (radius $R$) — and exactly one circle tangent to all three sides — its incircle (radius $r$). Both radii are bound to the area by clean formulas.

Area, inradius, circumradius $$[ABC] = rs \qquad [ABC] = \frac{abc}{4R}$$ where $a,b,c$ are the side lengths and $s=\tfrac{a+b+c}{2}$ is the semiperimeter.

The first is immediate: join the incenter $I$ to each vertex, splitting the triangle into three smaller triangles of heights $r$ (the incircle is tangent to each side, so the distance from $I$ to each side is $r$). Their areas sum to $\tfrac12 a r + \tfrac12 b r + \tfrac12 c r = r\cdot\tfrac{a+b+c}{2} = rs$.

📝 Example — the 13-14-15 triangle's two radii

With sides $13,14,15$ the semiperimeter is $s=21$, and Heron's formula gives $[ABC]=\sqrt{21\cdot 8\cdot 7\cdot 6}=84$.

Inradius: $r = \dfrac{[ABC]}{s} = \dfrac{84}{21} = 4$.   Circumradius: $R = \dfrac{abc}{4[ABC]} = \dfrac{13\cdot14\cdot15}{4\cdot 84} = \dfrac{2730}{336} = \dfrac{65}{8}$.

📝 Example — equilateral triangle around a circle

An equilateral triangle is circumscribed about a circle of radius $9$ (so the circle is its incircle). For an equilateral triangle of side $\ell$, the inradius is $r=\dfrac{\ell}{2\sqrt3}$, so $\ell = 2\sqrt3\,r = 18\sqrt3$.

Area $=\dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\ell^2 = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}(18\sqrt3)^2 = \dfrac{\sqrt3}{4}\cdot 972 = 243\sqrt3$.

$[ABC]=rs$ uses the semiperimeter, not the full perimeter. Forgetting the $\tfrac12$ is the single most common slip — it doubles your inradius.
✏️ Exercise 20-1 — find $r$ and $R$

20.2 The Four Classical Centers

Four families of cevians/lines each meet at a single point. Knowing which lines define a center tells you instantly what is true about it.

The four centers
  • Centroid $G$ — intersection of the medians; divides each median $2:1$ from the vertex. The center of mass.
  • Incenter $I$ — intersection of the angle bisectors; equidistant from the three sides (center of the incircle).
  • Circumcenter $O$ — intersection of the perpendicular bisectors of the sides; equidistant from the three vertices (center of the circumcircle).
  • Orthocenter $H$ — intersection of the three altitudes.

The incenter and centroid are always inside the triangle. The circumcenter and orthocenter leave the triangle when it is obtuse — and both sit exactly on a vertex / midpoint when the triangle is right.

📝 Example — the centroid splits a median

In $\triangle ABC$, median $AD$ has length $18$. The centroid $G$ lies on $AD$ with $AG:GD = 2:1$, so $AG = \tfrac23(18) = 12$ and $GD = 6$. Every median is cut the same way by $G$.

In a right triangle the circumcenter is the midpoint of the hypotenuse (so $R=\tfrac{c}{2}$), and the orthocenter is the right-angle vertex. Don't go hunting for them with general formulas when the right angle hands them to you.
✏️ Exercise 20-2 — locate the centers

20.3 The Euler Line

Here is the chapter's most beautiful surprise: in any triangle, three of the four centers are collinear. The circumcenter $O$, the centroid $G$, and the orthocenter $H$ all lie on a single line — the Euler line — and $G$ sits between the other two at a fixed ratio.

Euler line $$O,\;G,\;H \text{ are collinear, with } \quad HG = 2\,GO \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \vec{OH} = 3\,\vec{OG}.$$

The centroid is the "balance point" of the whole configuration — it divides the segment from circumcenter to orthocenter in the ratio $1:2$. (The one center not on the Euler line in general is the incenter $I$.)

📝 Example — find the orthocenter from $O$ and $G$

If a triangle's circumcenter is $O=(2,1)$ and its centroid is $G=(4,3)$, then $\vec{OG}=(2,2)$ and $\vec{OH}=3\vec{OG}=(6,6)$, so $H = O+(6,6) = (8,7)$. No need to find the altitudes at all.

💡 Big Picture — why so many things line up Leonhard Euler discovered this collinearity in 1765. It is the tip of an iceberg: the midpoints of the sides, the feet of the altitudes, and more all lie on a single nine-point circle, whose center is the midpoint of $OH$ — also on the Euler line. Hidden order like this is exactly what competition geometry trains you to expect and exploit.

20.4 Putting It Together

Most "potpourri" problems are solved by spotting which center or circle is hiding in the figure, then bringing in a tool from an earlier chapter — Power of a Point, area ratios, coordinates, or the Pythagorean theorem.

📝 Example — inscribe, then subtract

A circle is inscribed in an isosceles triangle with $AB=AC=25$ and $BC=40$. The semiperimeter is $s=\tfrac{25+25+40}{2}=45$; the height from $A$ to $BC$ is $\sqrt{25^2-20^2}=15$, so $[ABC]=\tfrac12(40)(15)=300$. Then the inradius is $r=\dfrac{300}{45}=\dfrac{20}{3}$.

✏️ Exercise 20-3 — a tangent length

Two congruent circles are tangent to each other, and a line from the center of one is tangent to the other. Using the radius-⟂-tangent fact from Chapter 9 and the Pythagorean theorem, set up the right triangle that relates the tangent length to the radius. (This is the structure behind MATHCOUNTS 1989 #343.)

When a problem mentions "the circle inscribed in" or "circumscribed about," immediately label the relevant center and drop the radius to the point of tangency (incircle) or to a vertex (circumcircle). The right triangle you create is almost always the whole solution.
The centroid $G$ is literally the center of mass of a uniform triangular plate — balance the triangle on a pin at $G$ and it stays level, because $G$ divides each median $2{:}1$ exactly as the mass is distributed. Triangle centers also model equilibrium and optimization in nature: the incenter is the point that can be the center of the largest inscribed circle (a packing/clearance problem), and minimizing total distance to the vertices (the Fermat point) governs least-energy networks like soap films and efficient road layouts.

$G_1 - G_2 = \tfrac{A-D}{3},\qquad G_1 - G_3 = \tfrac{B-D}{3},\qquad G_2 - G_3 = \tfrac{B-A}{3}.$

2002 AMC 12BProblem 25 region, area

Let $f(x) = x^2 + 6x + 1$, and let $R$ denote the set of points $(x, y)$ in the coordinate plane such that $f(x) + f(y) \le 0$ and $f(x) - f(y) \le 0$. The area of $R$ is closest to
