Test Format & Scoring
⏱️ 50 questions · 35 minutes · passage-based

That's about 42 seconds per question — the ACT English section rewards speed and pattern recognition over deep reading. Questions are spread across several short passages (usually 350–500 words each), with most asking you to fix or improve an underlined portion. (This is the enhanced ACT introduced in 2025; older paper forms ran 75 questions in 45 minutes. The skills tested are the same.)

🎯 The Three Reporting Categories

Your English score (1–36) breaks down into three reporting categories. The approximate weights below tell you where to spend your prep time (ACT may vary the exact percentages slightly from form to form):

Reporting Category Weight What It Tests
Conventions of Standard English 51–56% Punctuation, grammar & usage, sentence structure (commas, semicolons, fragments, run-ons, agreement, verbs, modifiers)
Production of Writing 29–32% Topic development (purpose, adding/deleting info) and organization (transitions, ordering sentences, intros/conclusions)
Knowledge of Language 13–19% Word choice, concision, style, tone, and register
✓ The takeaway

Over half the test is pure grammar and punctuation — a finite, learnable rule set. Master commas, semicolons, sentence boundaries, and agreement, and you've locked in the majority of available points. The "rhetorical" questions (transitions, add/delete, word choice) are the other big chunk and reward a few clear strategies.

How questions look

Two formats dominate:

  • Underlined-portion questions: A portion of the passage is underlined. The answer choices offer alternatives. A. NO CHANGE is almost always the first option — choose it when the original is already correct (it's right about 1 in 4 times, so don't fear it).
  • Boxed-number / stand-alone questions: A question is asked in words (e.g., "Which choice best introduces the paragraph?" or "Should the writer add the following sentence?"). These are the Production-of-Writing questions.
📐 Scoring snapshot

English is one of the ACT's multiple-choice sections; each section is scored 1–36, and the Composite is the average of English, Math, and Reading (rounded). On the enhanced ACT, Science is optional and reported separately, not folded into the core Composite. There is no penalty for wrong answers — always fill in every bubble. Raw-to-scaled conversions vary from form to form, so treat any conversion table as an estimate: missing only a couple of questions still lands near 36, and the curve falls off from there.

🧠
First Principles: How to Think on ACT English

ACT English is not a vocabulary test or a "do you have an ear for English" test. It is a test of a small, fixed set of grammar rules plus a handful of rhetorical judgment skills. Internalize these five principles and most questions answer themselves.

1. Shorter is usually better

When meaning is preserved, ACT prefers the most concise option. Redundancy and wordiness are wrong answers by design. If two choices say the same thing and one is shorter, pick the shorter one.

2. Every choice must be grammatical AND make sense

A choice can be perfectly grammatical and still wrong because it changes the meaning or breaks the logic of the sentence. Read the whole sentence, not just the underlined part.

3. The answer is in the question type

Vertical answer choices reveal what's being tested. If the options differ only in punctuation, it's a punctuation question. If they differ in verb tense, it's a tense question. Compare the choices to diagnose.

4. "NO CHANGE" is a real answer

It's correct ~25% of the time. Don't change something just because it's underlined. Only "fix" it if you can name the rule that's broken.

5. Context decides rhetorical questions

For transitions, add/delete, and ordering questions, the right answer is the one that fits the surrounding sentences and the passage's stated purpose — not the fanciest or longest one.

6. Punctuation pairs must match

Commas and dashes that set off a phrase come in pairs of the same type. You can't open with a comma and close with a dash. Mismatched pairs are an instant elimination.

🧭 The Universal 3-Step Approach
  1. Read the full sentence (and a bit before/after for context).
  2. Scan the answer choices vertically to identify what's being tested (punctuation? tense? word choice? transition?).
  3. Apply the rule or the context test and eliminate. When in doubt, pick the shortest grammatical option that keeps the meaning.

Conventions of Standard English

This is the majority of the test. We'll cover the three pillars: Punctuation, Sentence Structure, and Usage & Agreement. These rules repeat on every ACT — learn them once and harvest points forever.

✒️ Punctuation

Commas — the most-tested punctuation

The ACT tests four comma jobs. If a comma isn't doing one of these jobs, it's probably wrong (the ACT loves to test unnecessary commas).

Comma JobRuleExample
FANBOYS join Comma + coordinating conjunction (For, And, Nor, But, Or, Yet, So) joins two independent clauses "The lab closed early, so we finished the report at home."
Introductory element Comma after an introductory word, phrase, or dependent clause "After the storm passed, the team resumed testing."
Lists Separate three or more items (ACT accepts the Oxford comma) "We measured mass, velocity, and charge."
Non-essential info A pair of commas brackets info you could delete without breaking the sentence "Dr. Lee, our advisor, reviewed the data."
🚫 Comma traps the ACT loves
  • Never put a comma between a subject and its verb: "The new microscope, arrived Tuesday." ✗
  • Never separate a verb from its object: "She measured, the temperature." ✗
  • Don't put a comma before "that": "He argued, that gravity..." ✗
  • Never put a comma directly after a preposition, and rarely right before one that links to its object: "Ana enjoys traveling, to Hawaii." ✗ (A comma can come before a preposition when it closes a non-essential element — "Cade, with whom I studied, ...")
  • If you can't name the comma's job, it probably shouldn't be there. When unsure, leave it out.

Essential vs. non-essential (the comma's hardest case)

Test: can you delete it and keep the core meaning?

Non-essential (use commas): "My older brother, who studies physics, helped me." → The clause adds info but isn't needed to identify the brother.

Essential (no commas): "The student who scored highest won the prize." → Remove "who scored highest" and you don't know which student. It's needed, so no commas.

The "Big Three" sentence connectors

This single table answers a huge share of punctuation questions. Memorize what can go between two independent clauses (complete sentences):

MarkWhat it doesRule
Period / Semicolon Separate two complete sentences Independent clause . Independent clause — A semicolon is interchangeable with a period. Both need a complete sentence on each side.
Comma + FANBOYS Join two complete sentences Independent clause, and independent clause — comma alone is NOT enough (that's a comma splice).
Colon Introduce a list, explanation, or example The part before the colon must be a complete sentence: "We needed one thing: more time."
Dash Set off an interruption or add emphasis A pair of dashes works like a pair of commas; a single dash works like a colon (less formal).
⭐ The Semicolon = Period shortcut

If a choice uses a semicolon, mentally replace it with a period. If both sides are complete sentences, the semicolon is valid. If either side is a fragment, eliminate it. This one trick resolves most semicolon questions instantly.

🏗️ Sentence Structure

Roughly a quarter of conventions questions test sentence boundaries. You must instantly tell independent clauses (can stand alone) from dependent clauses and fragments.

Run-on / Fused

Two complete sentences jammed together with no punctuation.

"We ran the test it failed." ✗

Comma Splice

Two complete sentences joined by only a comma.

"We ran the test, it failed." ✗

Fragment

Missing a subject or verb, or starts with a subordinator and never resolves.

"Because we ran the test." ✗

✓ Four legal ways to fix a comma splice / run-on
  1. Period: "We ran the test. It failed."
  2. Semicolon: "We ran the test; it failed."
  3. Comma + FANBOYS: "We ran the test, but it failed."
  4. Subordinate one clause: "When we ran the test, it failed."

On the ACT, often only one answer choice does one of these correctly — the rest are splices or run-ons. Spotting the boundary is the whole game.

Modifiers — keep the description next to what it describes

A modifying phrase at the start of a sentence must describe the subject that immediately follows the comma. Mismatches = dangling/misplaced modifiers.

✗ "Running across the field, the ball was caught." (The ball wasn't running.)

✓ "Running across the field, the receiver caught the ball."

ACT tactic: after an opening -ing or -ed phrase, the word right after the comma must be the thing doing the action.

🔗 Usage & Agreement

Subject–Verb Agreement

The verb must match the subject in number. The ACT hides the subject by inserting phrases between it and the verb.

Cross out the middle: "The box of samples (was / were) labeled." → Subject is "box" (singular) → was. Ignore "of samples."

Watch for: prepositional phrases ("of the...", "along with..."), and tricky subjects. Each, everyone, neither, either are singular. Collective nouns (team, committee) are usually singular.

Pronoun Agreement & Case

  • Agreement: a pronoun matches its antecedent in number. "Each student brought their..." is tested — singular antecedent ("each") → use "his or her" (ACT tends to want singular agreement).
  • Vague pronouns: "It" or "they" with no clear noun to point to is wrong. The ACT often replaces a vague "it/they/this" with the actual noun.
  • Who vs. whom: who = subject (he/she), whom = object (him/her). "Who ran the test?" / "To whom did you send it?"
  • Subject vs. object case: "between you and me" (not "I"); "she and I went" (not "her and me").

Verb Tense & Consistency

Keep tense consistent with the surrounding sentences unless the meaning requires a shift. The ACT tests needless tense changes — match the paragraph's established timeframe.

Parallel Structure

Items in a list or comparison must share the same grammatical form. When the ACT underlines one item in a series, check that it matches the others.

✗ "The intern was responsible for logging data, calibration of sensors, and to file reports."

✓ "The intern was responsible for logging data, calibrating sensors, and filing reports." (all -ing)

Also keep paired structures parallel: "not only X but also Y," "either X or Y," "more X than Y" — X and Y must match in form.

Frequently confused words (idiom & diction)

Don't confuseQuick rule
then / thanthen = time; than = comparison
affect / effectaffect = verb (to influence); effect = noun (the result)
fewer / lessfewer = countable; less = uncountable amount
among / betweenbetween = two; among = three or more
that / whichthat = essential (no comma); which = non-essential (comma). On the ACT, a which clause is almost always non-essential → set it off with a comma — the exception is when which follows a preposition ("the rate at which it cooled"). that is essential → no comma.

Comparisons: comparative vs. superlative

Comparing two things uses the comparative (-er / more / less). Comparing three or more uses the superlative (-est / most / least). The ACT tests this by mismatching them.

✗ "Between the two prototypes, the second performed the best." / "Helium is the lighter of all the noble gases."

✓ "Between the two prototypes, the second performed better." / "Helium is the lightest of all the noble gases."

Who vs. that · Adjective vs. adverb

  • who refers to people; that/which refers to things. "The student who ran the test" (not "that").
  • Adverbs modify verbs/adjectives; adjectives modify nouns. "The reaction proceeded quickly" (adverb), not "proceeded quick."

Idioms — the one place to trust your ear

Idioms don't follow a rule you can memorize; you rely on familiarity with the phrase. The ACT tests two kinds:

  • Prepositional idioms — the correct preposition after a word: "responsible for" (not "of"), "worried about" (not "of"), "refrain from," "capable of."
  • Gerund vs. infinitive — "decided to read" (infinitive, not "decided reading"); "enjoy reading" (gerund, not "enjoy to read").

Strategic exception: Everywhere else on the test, "what sounds right" is unreliable — but idioms are the one question type where your ear is the best tool you have. If a preposition or verb form sounds off, it probably is.

Grammar & Punctuation — 30 Practice Questions

Thirty ACT-format questions on Conventions of Standard English. Click a choice to reveal the answer and explanation.

1 · Comma splice
The robotics team finished wiring the motor controller, the sensors still needed calibration before the first test run.
NO CHANGE
the sensors, still needing calibration before the first test run.
but the sensors still needed calibration before the first test run.
the sensors still needing calibration, before the first test run.
Answer: C — but the sensors still needed calibration before the first test run.
Two independent clauses joined by only a comma form a comma splice; the coordinating conjunction "but" fixes it. The other options leave the splice or create awkward fragments.
2 · Comma (intro element)
After the telescope cooled to ambient temperature, the astronomers began recording images of the distant nebula.
NO CHANGE
After the telescope cooled to ambient temperature
After the telescope, cooled to ambient temperature,
After the telescope cooled, to ambient temperature
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
An introductory dependent clause should be followed by a single comma, exactly as written. The other options omit that comma or insert a comma that wrongly separates the subject from its verb.
3 · Apostrophe (its/it's)
The chemistry club repainted the storage cabinet because its' surface had begun to rust along the seams.
NO CHANGE
it's surface
its surface's
its surface
Answer: D — its surface
The possessive of "it" is "its" with no apostrophe; "its'" is never a word and "it's" means "it is." "Surface's" adds a needless possessive.
4 · Subject–verb agreement
The box of glass slides for the microscopes are stored in the locked cabinet near the door.
NO CHANGE
is stored
were being stored
have been stored
Answer: B — is stored
The subject is "box" (singular); "of glass slides for the microscopes" is a prepositional phrase that does not change the verb. The plural verbs incorrectly agree with the nearby nouns rather than the true subject.
5 · Semicolon (period rule)
The first launch attempt was scrubbed for high winds; the team rescheduled the test for the following morning.
NO CHANGE
the team, rescheduled
the team rescheduling
and the team, rescheduled
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
A semicolon correctly joins two independent clauses, and "the team rescheduled" is a complete clause needing no comma. The other options add a stray comma or turn the clause into a fragment.
6 · Comma (no comma subject–verb)
The students who finished the lab report early volunteered, to help clean the glassware at the back bench.
NO CHANGE
volunteered to help,
volunteered; to help
volunteered to help
Answer: D — volunteered to help
No punctuation belongs between the verb "volunteered" and its infinitive "to help." The comma, misplaced comma, and semicolon all interrupt a tight verb phrase.
7 · Fragment
The marching band rehearsed for three hours every afternoon that week. Which is why their championship performance was flawless.
NO CHANGE
week, which is why their
week; which is why their
week, which is why, their
Answer: B — week, which is why their
"Which is why their championship performance was flawless" is a fragment when it stands alone; joining it to the prior clause with a comma fixes it. A semicolon needs an independent clause after it, and the stray comma after "why" is wrong.
8 · Apostrophe (their/there/they're)
The debate coaches told the freshmen that there going to need note cards for the regional tournament.
NO CHANGE
their going
they're going
theyre going
Answer: C — they're going
The sentence needs the contraction "they're" (they are) before "going." "There" is a place, "their" is possessive, and "theyre" omits the apostrophe.
9 · Colon usage
The field kit contained everything we needed for the survey, a tape measure, a clipboard, and a soil-sample bag.
NO CHANGE
; a tape measure, a clipboard, and a soil-sample bag.
a tape measure, a clipboard, and a soil-sample bag.
: a tape measure, a clipboard, and a soil-sample bag.
Answer: D — : a tape measure, a clipboard, and a soil-sample bag.
The clause before the punctuation ("The field kit contained everything we needed for the survey") is complete, so a colon correctly introduces the list. A bare comma (NO CHANGE) can't introduce a list here, a semicolon needs an independent clause after it, and dropping the punctuation creates a run-on.
10 · Comma (FANBOYS joining)
The pendulum swung steadily for nearly a minute, so the team logged each period in the data table.
NO CHANGE
so, the team logged each period in the data table.
so the team logged each period, in the data table.
the team logged each period in the data table.
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
A comma plus the coordinating conjunction "so" correctly joins two independent clauses. The other options add a stray comma or drop the conjunction, creating a comma splice.
11 · Pronoun case
The lab partners agreed that the final analysis would be divided evenly between she and I.
NO CHANGE
she and me.
her and me.
her and I.
Answer: C — her and me.
After the preposition "between," both pronouns must be in the objective case: "her and me." Subjective pronouns like "she" and "I" cannot be objects of a preposition.
12 · Fused run-on
The history class visited the archive downtown they examined original letters from the Revolutionary War.
NO CHANGE
downtown, where they examined
downtown they, examined
downtown examining,
Answer: B — downtown, where they examined
The original fuses two clauses with no punctuation; adding ", where" subordinates the second clause and fixes the run-on. The other choices misplace the comma or create a fragment.
13 · Comma (non-essential)
Dr. Reyes, who designed the spectrometer, will present the findings at the conference next week.
NO CHANGE
who designed the spectrometer will
who designed the spectrometer; will
who designed the spectrometer—will,
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
The clause "who designed the spectrometer" is non-essential and must be set off by a pair of commas. Dropping the second comma, using a semicolon, or mixing a dash with a comma all break the matched pair.
14 · Apostrophe (plural possessive)
The judges inspected all of the students projects before announcing the winners of the science fair.
NO CHANGE
student's projects
students' projects
students's projects
Answer: C — students' projects
Multiple students own the projects, so the plural possessive "students'" takes an apostrophe after the s. "Students" lacks the possessive, "student's" is singular, and "students's" is incorrect form.
15 · Dangling modifier
Peering through the eyepiece, the rings of Saturn were finally visible to Mara.
NO CHANGE
Peering through the eyepiece, the rings of Saturn finally became visible to Mara.
The rings of Saturn, peering through the eyepiece, were finally visible to Mara.
Peering through the eyepiece, Mara could finally see the rings of Saturn.
Answer: D — Peering through the eyepiece, Mara could finally see the rings of Saturn.
The opening modifier must describe the noun that follows it; only Mara can peer through the eyepiece, not the rings. The other options leave the modifier dangling on "the rings."
16 · Subject–verb agreement (each/neither)
Neither of the two prototypes were able to complete the obstacle course without stalling.
NO CHANGE
was able
have been able
are able
Answer: B — was able
"Neither" is singular and takes a singular verb, "was," regardless of the plural "prototypes" in the prepositional phrase. The plural verbs wrongly agree with "prototypes."
17 · Dash pairs
The new alloy—lighter than aluminum but far stronger, proved ideal for the drone's frame.
NO CHANGE
, lighter than aluminum but far stronger—
—lighter than aluminum but far stronger—
—lighter than aluminum but far stronger
Answer: C — —lighter than aluminum but far stronger—
A parenthetical phrase set off with a dash must close with a matching dash, not a comma. Mixing a dash with a comma, or omitting the closing punctuation, breaks the pair.
18 · Comma (items in a list)
For the experiment we measured temperature, pressure, and volume at one-minute intervals.
NO CHANGE
temperature pressure, and volume
temperature, pressure and, volume
temperature, pressure, and, volume
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
Three items in a series are separated by commas, including before the conjunction. The other options drop a needed comma or add a stray comma after "and."
19 · Pronoun (who vs whom)
The coach praised the swimmer who she had trained since middle school.
NO CHANGE
whom she had trained
who she had trained,
whom, she had trained
Answer: B — whom she had trained
The pronoun is the object of "had trained" (she trained whom), so the objective "whom" is correct. "Who" is subjective, and the comma versions add unneeded punctuation.
20 · Verb tense consistency
Last spring the orchestra rehearsed every weekend and performs three concerts before the school year ended.
NO CHANGE
will perform
performing
performed
Answer: D — performed
The sentence describes past events ("Last spring," "rehearsed," "ended"), so the verb must be the past-tense "performed." The present and future tenses break consistency, and "performing" is not a finite verb.
21 · Comma (FANBOYS joining)
The voltage reading kept fluctuating, and the technician suspected a loose ground wire.
NO CHANGE
the technician suspected a loose ground wire.
and, the technician suspected a loose ground wire.
and the technician suspected, a loose ground wire.
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
A comma plus "and" correctly links two independent clauses. Dropping "and" creates a comma splice, and the other options insert commas that interrupt the clause.
22 · Comparatives vs superlatives
Of the three telescopes in the observatory, the refractor produces the sharper images of the moon.
NO CHANGE
more sharp
sharpest
most sharpest
Answer: C — sharpest
Comparing three or more items requires the superlative "sharpest." "Sharper" and "more sharp" are comparatives for two items, and "most sharpest" is a double superlative.
23 · Apostrophe (whose/who's)
The engineer who's blueprint won the competition will lead the bridge project.
NO CHANGE
whose blueprint
whos blueprint
who's blueprints
Answer: B — whose blueprint
The possessive form is "whose"; "who's" means "who is" and makes no sense here. "Whos" is not a word, and changing to plural does not fix the wrong contraction.
24 · Comma splice
The coding club met every Thursday, they built a small weather app over the semester.
NO CHANGE
they built a small weather app, over the semester.
building a small weather app over the semester, too.
and they built a small weather app over the semester.
Answer: D — and they built a small weather app over the semester.
Two independent clauses linked only by a comma form a comma splice; adding "and" supplies the needed conjunction. The other options leave the splice or create an awkward tacked-on phrase.
25 · Parallel structure
A good lab report requires recording data carefully, analyzing results honestly, and to present conclusions clearly.
NO CHANGE
the presentation of
presenting
to be presenting
Answer: C — presenting
The series uses "-ing" gerunds ("recording," "analyzing"), so the third item must be "presenting" to stay parallel. The infinitive and noun-phrase forms break the parallel pattern.
26 · Comma (no comma before preposition)
The rover transmitted its first images from the surface of Mars early Tuesday morning.
NO CHANGE
from the surface, of Mars
from, the surface of Mars
from the surface of Mars,
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
The prepositional phrase flows without internal punctuation, so no comma belongs before or inside it. Each alternative inserts a comma that interrupts the phrase.
27 · Subject–verb agreement (collective noun)
The research team were presenting its annual report to the department on Friday.
NO CHANGE
are presenting
have presented
is presenting
Answer: D — is presenting
"Team" acts as a single unit here, signaled by the singular "its," so it takes the singular "is presenting." The plural verbs disagree with the collective noun treated as one body.
28 · Pronoun (vague reference)
When Maria poured the solution into the beaker, it bubbled over and ruined the reading.
NO CHANGE
the solution bubbled over and ruined the reading.
they bubbled over and ruined the reading.
it bubbled over, ruining the reading they took.
Answer: B — the solution bubbled over and ruined the reading.
The pronoun "it" could refer to the catalyst or the beaker, so naming "the solution" removes the ambiguity. "They" has no plural antecedent, and the last option keeps the vague "it" while adding another unclear pronoun.
29 · Semicolon (period rule)
The first trial produced inconsistent data; the second trial, however, confirmed our hypothesis.
NO CHANGE
, the second trial
: the second trial
the second trial
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
A semicolon correctly separates two independent clauses, and the commas around "however" are appropriate. A comma alone would splice the clauses, a colon does not fit, and no punctuation fuses them.
30 · Idiom (preposition)
The committee's decision to fund the lab was based off of the strength of last year's results.
NO CHANGE
off from
on
around
Answer: C — on
The standard idiom is "based on," so "on" is correct. "Off of," "off from," and "around" are nonstandard or change the intended meaning.
🎨
Rhetorical Skills (Production of Writing + Knowledge of Language)

About 45% of the test asks you to make writing judgments rather than apply grammar rules. These reward reading in context, not memorization.

📌 Transitions

Transition questions test the logical relationship between sentences. Read the sentence before AND after, decide the relationship, then pick the matching word.

RelationshipTransition words
Contrasthowever, but, nevertheless, on the other hand, yet, although
Cause/Effecttherefore, thus, consequently, as a result, because, so
Additionfurthermore, moreover, in addition, also, additionally
Examplefor instance, for example, specifically, such as
Sequence/Timefirst, then, next, meanwhile, finally, subsequently
⚠️ Transition trap

Don't pick a transition because it "sounds smart." "However" between two sentences that agree is wrong, even though it's a fancy word. The logic must match. If the two sentences simply continue the same idea, often no transition (or "and") is correct.

➕➖ Add / Delete / Revise Questions

These give a yes/no choice plus a reason. The reason matters more than the yes/no — eliminate choices with wrong reasons even if the yes/no seems right.

"Should the writer add/keep this sentence?" Ask: Does it support the paragraph's main point and the essay's purpose?

  • Add/Keep if it provides relevant detail, support, or a needed transition.
  • Delete if it's off-topic, redundant, or interrupts the flow — even if it's "interesting." Interesting ≠ relevant.

🧩 Organization & Ordering

  • Sentence/paragraph order: Look for chronology and for pronouns/transitions that depend on a prior sentence ("this," "such," "the latter"). A sentence introducing a noun must come before sentences that refer back to it.
  • Where to place a sentence: Find the logical anchor — the sentence it elaborates on or transitions from.
  • Intro/conclusion questions: The best opener previews the paragraph; the best closer wraps the stated topic without adding new tangents.

✂️ Knowledge of Language: Concision, Word Choice, Tone

Concision (redundancy)

Cut repeated ideas. The ACT plants pairs like "the final end result," "past history," "combine together," "each and every." The shortest grammatical option usually wins.

✗ "returned back again"

✓ "returned"

Word choice & tone

Match the passage's register. A formal essay shouldn't suddenly use slang. Pick the word whose precise meaning and tone fit — not the biggest word.

✓ The "OMIT" option

When "OMIT the underlined portion" or "DELETE" is offered, it is correct surprisingly often. If the underlined text is redundant or adds nothing, deleting it is usually right. Always seriously consider OMIT.

Rhetorical Skills — 30 Practice Questions

Thirty ACT-format questions on transitions, add/delete, organization, concision, word choice, and tone. Click a choice to reveal the answer and explanation.

1 · Transitions
The thermometer showed no change after five minutes. Therefore, the students concluded that the reaction was endothermic only after they added more catalyst and waited an additional ten minutes.
NO CHANGE
However,
Likewise,
For example,
Answer: B — However,
The sentence contrasts the lack of an immediate result with the eventual conclusion, so a contrast word is needed; "Therefore" and "Likewise" signal consequence or similarity, and "For example" signals illustration.
2 · Concision
The lab manual instructed us to record our measurements in a written log that we wrote down by hand after each trial.
NO CHANGE
in a log that was handwritten and written down
by writing them down in a handwritten log
in a handwritten log
Answer: D — in a handwritten log
"Handwritten" already conveys that the log was written by hand, so all the other versions repeat the same idea redundantly.
3 · Add/Delete with reason
The paragraph explains how chlorophyll absorbs light during photosynthesis. The writer is considering adding the following sentence: "Chlorophyll is what gives most plant leaves their green color." Should the writer make this addition?
Yes, because it relates relevant information about chlorophyll that fits the paragraph's focus on the pigment.
Yes, because it explains why photosynthesis occurs only in sunlight.
No, because it contradicts the claim that chlorophyll absorbs light.
No, because the color of leaves has nothing to do with any plant process.
Answer: A — Yes, relevant to the paragraph's focus
The paragraph is about chlorophyll, and the sentence adds relevant detail about that pigment; the other reasons misstate the science or wrongly call the detail irrelevant.
4 · Which choice best accomplishes the goal
The writer wants to emphasize the suddenness of the temperature spike. Which choice best accomplishes this goal?
The temperature rose over the course of the afternoon.
The temperature eventually reached a higher reading.

The modifier should limit the number, not the verb, and only one such word is needed, giving "recorded nearly forty."
47 · Sentence structure (run-on)
The titration reached its endpoint the indicator turned a pale pink.
NO CHANGE
endpoint, and the indicator turned
endpoint the indicator, turning
endpoint, the indicator turned
Answer: B — endpoint, and the indicator turned
Two independent clauses run together; joining them with a comma plus "and" correctly separates them.
48 · Idioms
The findings differ from those reported in the earlier study.
NO CHANGE
than
with
to
Answer: A — NO CHANGE
The idiom "differ from" is standard when comparing things that are unlike.
49 · Concision (wordiness)
The reason the trial failed is due to the fact that the sample was contaminated.
NO CHANGE
is on account of the fact that
was because of the reason that
is that
Answer: D — is that
"Is that" expresses the idea concisely and avoids the redundant "the reason...is due to the fact that."
50 · Transitions

Even so,
In fact,
Answer: C — Even so,
Despite the grant covering most costs, the team still needed more money, so a contrast transition like "Even so" is required.

Top Misconceptions & How to Fix Them

Misconception #1: "Pick the answer that sounds best."

❌ The Problem
Casual speech is full of comma splices and run-ons that "sound" fine. Your ear was trained on conversation, not on Standard Written English.
✓ The Fix

Diagnose the question type from the answer choices, then apply the rule. "Sounds right" is a tiebreaker, never the method — especially for punctuation and agreement.

Misconception #2: "Longer, more detailed answers are better."

❌ The Problem
Students assume the verbose, descriptive choice shows sophistication. On the ACT it usually signals redundancy.
✓ The Fix

When meaning is equal, choose the shortest grammatical option. Take "OMIT/DELETE" seriously — it's right more often than students expect.

Misconception #3: "Never pick NO CHANGE."

❌ The Problem
Some students assume an underlined portion must be wrong, so they always change it.
✓ The Fix

NO CHANGE is correct ~25% of the time. Only change something if you can name the broken rule. No nameable error → keep it.

Misconception #4: "A comma goes wherever I'd pause."

❌ The Problem
The "pause" myth produces commas between subjects and verbs, before "that," and inside essential phrases.
✓ The Fix

A comma needs one of four jobs (FANBOYS, intro, list, non-essential). No job → no comma. When stuck between adding or removing a comma, removing is the higher-percentage bet.

Misconception #5: "Transition questions are about vocabulary."

❌ The Problem
Students pick "however" or "moreover" for sophistication without checking the logical relationship.
✓ The Fix

Read the sentences before and after, name the relationship (contrast? cause? addition?), then match. If the ideas just continue, the simplest connector — or none — is right.

The Hardest Parts (And How to Master Them)
Challenge 1: Telling independent from dependent clauses under time pressure

Nearly every punctuation question hinges on whether each side of a mark is a complete sentence. The fast test:

  1. Does it have a subject and a verb?
  2. Does it start with a subordinator (because, although, when, if, since, while, that, which)? If yes, it's dependent even with a subject/verb.

Drill: cover the rest of the sentence and ask "could this stand alone as a text I'd send a friend?" If yes → independent; semicolon/period are legal. If no → fragment; it can't stand alone.

Challenge 2: Commas vs. dashes vs. colons (the punctuation cluster)

When all four choices differ only in punctuation, run this decision tree:

  • Is a list or explanation coming after a complete sentence? → colon.
  • Are two complete sentences being joined? → period, semicolon, or comma+FANBOYS.
  • Is non-essential info being set off? → matching pair (comma…comma OR dash…dash — never mixed).
  • None of these apply? → no punctuation.

Killer shortcut: If two answer choices are grammatically identical in function (e.g., a comma and a dash both used as a single mid-sentence pause that isn't a pair), they can't both be right, so the answer is usually neither — look for the structural option.

Challenge 3: Hidden subject–verb and pronoun agreement

The ACT buries the subject under prepositional phrases and interrupters. Always find and isolate the true subject, then mentally delete everything between it and the verb.

Tricky singulars to memorize: each, every, either, neither, anyone, everyone, nobody, the number of → singular. A number of → plural. Collective nouns (team, jury, family) → usually singular on the ACT.

Challenge 4: "Which choice best accomplishes [goal]?" questions

These specify a goal ("emphasize the speed of the reaction," "provide a specific example," "maintain the essay's lighthearted tone"). The trap: a choice can be well-written but accomplish the wrong goal.

Method: underline the goal in the question stem. Then test each choice against ONLY that goal. The grammatically nicest answer that ignores the goal is a distractor.

Challenge 5: Pacing — about 42 seconds per question

Time is the real enemy. Grammar/punctuation questions should take ~20 seconds; banking that time funds the slower rhetorical and "best accomplishes" questions.

  • Don't reread the whole passage for each question — read around the underline.
  • For boxed/ordering questions, you may need broader context; do those second within a passage.
  • If a question stalls you past ~45 seconds, mark a guess, flag it, and move — never leave it blank (no wrong-answer penalty).

Memory Anchors & Quick Reference
FANBOYS — coordinating conjunctions

For • And • Nor • But • Or • Yet • So

Comma + one of these = legal way to join two complete sentences.

Semicolon = Period

If a semicolon could be swapped for a period and both sides still stand alone, it's correct. Otherwise eliminate it.

"When in doubt, take it out"

Unsure whether a comma belongs? The ACT plants far more extra commas than missing ones. Leaning toward removal beats the coin flip.

Shorter is smarter

Equal meaning → fewest words. Always weigh the OMIT/DELETE option.

Cross-out test (agreement)

Delete the phrase between subject and verb to expose the true subject, then match number.

Extension: Grammar as a Tool for Science Writing

The same skills the ACT tests are exactly what make a lab report or research paper clear. Strong scientific writing is concise, unambiguous, and grammatically tight.

Concision = clarity in a methods section

✗ "In order to be able to measure the resulting final temperature, we made use of a thermal camera."

✓ "We measured the final temperature with a thermal camera."

Same ACT instinct — cut redundancy ("in order to be able to," "resulting final") — produces a cleaner methods sentence a reviewer can parse instantly.

Modifiers and ambiguity in data description

A dangling modifier in a paper can change a claim: "Measured at 300 K, we recorded a voltage of 1.2 V" wrongly says you were measured at 300 K. Fix: "Measured at 300 K, the sample produced 1.2 V." Precision in what modifies what is precision in what you're claiming.

Semicolons in complex results

Semicolons cleanly separate parallel results: "Run 1 produced no deflection; Run 2 produced a 3° deflection; Run 3 failed." The ACT semicolon rule (complete sentence on each side) keeps such lists readable.

The discipline ACT English rewards — say it correctly, say it once, make every word earn its place — is the same discipline that gets a paper past peer review and a grant proposal funded. Grammar isn't busywork; it's the interface between your idea and the reader's understanding.

Test-Day Strategy

⏱️ Pacing Blueprint

  • 50 questions in 35 minutes → about 42 seconds each. Check the clock at the halfway mark — you should be near question 25 by ~17 minutes in. If passages look roughly equal, aim to finish each in 6–7 minutes.
  • Move fast on grammar/punctuation (≈25 sec) to bank time for the slower "best accomplishes," ordering, and add/delete questions.
  • Never leave a blank. No wrong-answer penalty — guess and flag anything that stalls you past ~45 seconds.
  • Read around the underline, not the whole passage, for most questions. For organization/ordering questions, read the fuller context.

📝 Question-Attack Tactics

  • Diagnose from the choices. Vertical comparison tells you the topic (punctuation, tense, word choice, transition). Test the relevant rule, not your ear.
  • Default short. Equal-meaning choices → fewest words. Genuinely consider OMIT/DELETE.
  • Match the goal. For "which choice best…" questions, underline the goal and judge each option against only that goal.
  • Both halves must be right on yes/no + reason questions — eliminate any choice with a false reason.
  • Trust the rule over the fancy word. A wrong-logic "however" beats no one.

🧠 Week-Before Game Plan

  • 7 days out: Take one full timed English section daily (see Practice Tests below). Log every miss by category.
  • 5 days out: Drill your two weakest categories (often commas and transitions). Re-derive the rule for every miss.
  • 3 days out: Full timed section; focus on pacing — finishing with 1–2 minutes to review flags.
  • 1 day before: Skim the one-glance rule sheet and memory anchors. Light review only — rest.
  • Test morning: Re-read FANBOYS, "semicolon = period," and "when in doubt, take it out." Bring a watch.

🏆 Common Pitfalls to Avoid

  • Don't over-comma. The #1 error source. Remove commas with no job.
  • Don't fear NO CHANGE / OMIT. Each is right far more often than students believe.
  • Don't pick by sound. Comma splices "sound" fine — apply the boundary rule.
  • Don't ignore the reason on add/delete questions — the reason is half the answer.
  • Don't burn time. A flagged guess on one hard question protects three easy ones at the end.
