Week 8 · Lecture 8

The Poisson/Laplace Equation & Relaxation

No clock ticks here — the field everywhere simply settles into balance with its boundaries, and we coax that balance out of a grid by letting each point average its neighbors over and over.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recognize Poisson and Laplace as elliptic boundary-value problems with no time derivative, and contrast them with the parabolic/hyperbolic equations of earlier weeks.
  • Discretize the 2-D Laplacian with the five-point stencil and read the result as "each value is the average of its neighbors."
  • Implement Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel relaxation, and explain why each iteration drives the residual toward zero.
  • Apply Successive Over-Relaxation (SOR) and estimate the optimal relaxation factor ω for a rectangular grid.
  • Solve the electrostatic potential in a charged box with NumPy and visualize the converged field with Matplotlib.

8.1Elliptic Equations: a Field in Equilibrium

The wave and diffusion equations of Weeks 6 and 7 both carry a time derivative: you give them a starting state and march it forward. The Poisson equation is different. It has no time derivative at all. It describes a field that has already finished evolving — a steady state in equilibrium with whatever is fixed on its boundary.

∇²φ = −ρ / ε₀   (Poisson)    ∇²φ = 0   (Laplace)φ is the electrostatic potential, ρ the charge density, ε₀ the permittivity of free space. Laplace is just the source-free case ρ = 0.

In two dimensions the Laplacian is ∇²φ = ∂²φ/∂x² + ∂²φ/∂y². The model problem for this whole lecture is electrostatics: fix the potential on the walls of a region (the boundary conditions), specify any charge inside (the source ρ), and the equation tells you the potential everywhere in between. The electric field then follows as E = −∇φ.

Because there is no time direction, you cannot "march" the solution. Every interior value depends on every boundary value simultaneously. This is what makes the problem elliptic: information from the boundary reaches the whole interior at once, not along characteristics. The job is to solve one big coupled system, and the most intuitive way to do that is iterative relaxation.

AnalogyStretch a soap film across a bent wire loop. The film does not "evolve" in time toward a shape — pin the wire and it instantly finds the surface of least area, whose height satisfies Laplace's equation. Push your finger up through the middle (a point source) and you get Poisson. The boundary is the wire; relaxation is what happens if you nudge the film and let surface tension smooth out the wrinkles until nothing moves.

8.2The Five-Point Stencil: Averaging Your Neighbors

Put the region on a square grid with spacing h. Approximate each second derivative with the centered second difference from Week 1's toolkit:

∂²φ/∂x² ≈ [φ(i+1,j) − 2φ(i,j) + φ(i−1,j)] / h²Centered, second-order accurate. The y-derivative is the same with the j-index stepping.

Add the x and y pieces. For Laplace (ρ = 0) the discrete equation ∇²φ = 0 rearranges into something beautifully simple:

φ(i,j) = ¼ [ φ(i+1,j) + φ(i−1,j) + φ(i,j+1) + φ(i,j−1) ]Each interior value equals the average of its four nearest neighbors. This is the discrete Laplace equation.

With a source term, the right-hand side picks up an extra piece: φ(i,j) = ¼[ sum of neighbors + h²·ρ(i,j)/ε₀ ]. The five points — center plus four neighbors — are the five-point stencil, and they are the engine of everything that follows.

8.3Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, and the Idea of Relaxation

The averaging rule is also a recipe for fixing a guess. Start with any field that respects the boundary values (zeros inside is fine). It will not satisfy "every point equals its neighbors' average" — so just make it so, point by point, and repeat. Each pass nudges the field closer to balance. This is relaxation.

Jacobi iteration

The simplest version computes a whole new field from the old one. Using superscript k for the iteration count:

φ(i,j)^(k+1) = ¼ [ φ(i+1,j)^k + φ(i−1,j)^k + φ(i,j+1)^k + φ(i,j−1)^k ]Every new value uses only OLD neighbor values — you need two copies of the grid.

Jacobi is correct but slow: it ignores updates you just computed. Its error per sweep shrinks by a factor that approaches 1 as the grid grows, so a large grid needs many thousands of sweeps.

Gauss–Seidel iteration

Sweep the grid in order and use new values as soon as they exist. By the time you reach node (i,j), its left and lower neighbors have already been updated this pass:

φ(i,j)^(k+1) = ¼ [ φ(i+1,j)^k + φ(i−1,j)^(k+1) + φ(i,j+1)^k + φ(i,j−1)^(k+1) ]News travels across the grid within a single sweep — about twice as fast as Jacobi, and you need only one copy of the grid.

How do you know you are done? Track the residual — how badly the discrete equation is violated, r(i,j) = (neighbor average) − φ(i,j). When the largest residual drops below a tolerance, the field has relaxed to the solution.

Common mistakeDo not overwrite or relax the boundary nodes. The fixed boundary values are the data that drive the whole solution; if your update loop accidentally averages them too, the boundary "leaks" inward and you converge to the wrong (often all-zero) field. In array code this means iterating only over the [1:-1, 1:-1] interior and never touching the edges.

8.4Successive Over-Relaxation (SOR): Push Past the Average

Gauss–Seidel always moves each node toward the neighbor average, but it consistently undershoots — the correction it applies is in the right direction but too timid. The fix is daring: compute the Gauss–Seidel update, then overshoot it by a factor ω > 1. This is Successive Over-Relaxation.

φ_new = (1 − ω)·φ_old + ω·φ_GSω = 1 is plain Gauss–Seidel. ω in (1, 2) over-corrects and converges faster. ω ≥ 2 diverges.

The payoff is enormous. For an N×N grid, Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel both need O(N²) sweeps, but SOR with the optimal ω needs only O(N) — turning a problem that took ten thousand sweeps into one that takes a few hundred. For a rectangular grid the optimal factor is known in closed form:

ω_opt = 2 / ( 1 + sin(pi / N) )N is the number of grid intervals along a side. As N grows, ω_opt creeps toward 2.
✦ Worked Example — one relaxation sweep by hand

A 4×4 grid (indices 0–3 in each direction) solves Laplace's equation. The boundary is held at φ = 100 on the entire top edge (j = 3) and φ = 0 on the other three edges. The four interior nodes (i, j ∈ {1, 2}) all start at 0. Do one Gauss–Seidel sweep, visiting interior nodes in order (1,1), (2,1), (1,2), (2,2).

  1. Node (1,1): neighbors are left = 0 (edge), right = φ(2,1) = 0, below = 0 (edge), above = φ(1,2) = 0. Average = ¼(0+0+0+0) = 0.
  2. Node (2,1): left = φ(1,1) = 0 (just updated), right = 0 (edge), below = 0 (edge), above = φ(2,2) = 0. Average = 0.
  3. Node (1,2): left = 0 (edge), right = φ(2,2) = 0, below = φ(1,1) = 0, above = 100 (top edge). Average = ¼(0+0+0+100) = 25.
  4. Node (2,2): left = φ(1,2) = 25 (just updated this sweep!), right = 0 (edge), below = φ(2,1) = 0, above = 100. Average = ¼(25+0+0+100) = 31.25.
  5. Takeaway: after just one sweep the top row of interior nodes already feels the hot boundary, and (2,2) used the fresh value 25 from (1,2) — that is Gauss–Seidel passing news within a sweep. The exact solution by symmetry has the two top nodes equal (≈ 42.9) and the two bottom nodes equal (≈ 7.1); a dozen more sweeps converge there. A Jacobi sweep would have left (2,2) = 25, since it would use the old zero for (1,2).

🛠️ Hands-On: relax the potential inside a charged box

Everything here runs on the free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (the install details lives in Lecture 1 — reuse that environment; no new tools needed this week). We solve Laplace's equation on a square plate with one hot edge, then watch SOR crush the iteration count.

The vectorized Jacobi/SOR update below averages all interior nodes at once with NumPy slicing — orders of magnitude faster than a Python double loop, and the natural way to express the five-point stencil.

# poisson_relax.py — Lecture 8: Laplace on a plate, Jacobi vs SOR
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

N = 60                              # grid intervals per side
phi = np.zeros((N + 1, N + 1))
phi[-1, :] = 100.0                  # top edge held hot; others stay 0

def sweep_sor(phi, omega):
    # in-place Gauss-Seidel + over-relaxation, interior only
    err = 0.0
    for i in range(1, N):
        for j in range(1, N):
            gs = 0.25 * (phi[i+1, j] + phi[i-1, j] +
                         phi[i, j+1] + phi[i, j-1])
            new = (1 - omega) * phi[i, j] + omega * gs
            err = max(err, abs(new - phi[i, j]))
            phi[i, j] = new
    return err

omega = 2.0 / (1.0 + np.sin(np.pi / N))   # optimal SOR factor
print(f"omega_opt = {omega:.4f}")

for it in range(5000):
    if sweep_sor(phi, omega) < 1e-4:       # residual tolerance
        print(f"converged in {it+1} sweeps"); break

plt.imshow(phi, origin='lower', cmap='inferno')
plt.colorbar(label='potential phi'); plt.title('Relaxed Laplace solution')
plt.show()
# Try omega=1.0 (Gauss-Seidel): it needs ~10x more sweeps for the same N.

Add a point charge by initializing a source array rho and changing the update to gs = 0.25*(neighbors + h*h*rho[i,j]/eps0) — that turns Laplace into Poisson. Then compute the field with Ey, Ex = np.gradient(-phi) and overlay plt.streamplot to see the field lines bend around the charge.

🔗 Where this is heading

Relaxation is the gateway to all of elliptic computational physics. The same five-point stencil and residual idea power the multigrid methods that solve million-node grids in near-linear time, and the conjugate-gradient solvers of Course 3 attack the very same sparse linear system from the Krylov side. Next week (Week 9) we add time back in with Maxwell & the FDTD method, where Poisson's instantaneous balance becomes one piece of fully dynamic electromagnetics. The pattern reappears far beyond E&M: steady heat flow, incompressible-flow pressure solves, and the Schrödinger eigenproblem of the Quantum track all reduce to "solve ∇²φ = source." Capstone milestone (Week 8 checkpoint): get your project's 2-D Poisson solver converging on a non-trivial boundary — e.g. the field of a parallel-plate capacitor — with a logged residual-vs-sweep curve comparing Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, and SOR; that plot is the deliverable that proves your field engine works before FDTD builds on it.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. What makes the Poisson/Laplace equation elliptic and different from the wave and diffusion equations?
A. It is nonlinear
B. It has no time derivative — it is a boundary-value problem in equilibrium
C. It can only be solved in one dimension
D. It requires complex numbers
Show answer

B. Elliptic equations have no time derivative; the interior is fixed by the boundary all at once. (A) Poisson is linear in φ. (C) It is most interesting in 2-D/3-D. (D) It is purely real.

Q2. In the discrete Laplace equation on a square grid, the interior value φ(i,j) equals…
A. the sum of its four neighbors
B. the maximum of its four neighbors
C. the average of its four nearest neighbors
D. the value of the nearest boundary node
Show answer

C. The five-point stencil for ∇²φ = 0 rearranges to φ(i,j) = ¼(sum of 4 neighbors) — the average. (A) is off by the factor ¼. (B) and (D) are not what the stencil says.

Q3. The key difference between Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel iteration is that Gauss–Seidel…
A. uses freshly updated neighbor values within the same sweep, so it converges faster
B. uses only old values and needs two grid copies
C. changes the boundary conditions each sweep
D. solves the equation exactly in one sweep
Show answer

A. Gauss–Seidel reuses just-computed values, roughly doubling the rate and needing one grid copy. (B) describes Jacobi. (C) is the bug to avoid. (D) no iterative method finishes in one sweep.

Q4. In SOR, the relaxation factor ω must satisfy which condition for the iteration to converge?
A. ω < 0
B. ω = 0 exactly
C. ω > 2
D. 0 < ω < 2, with the optimal value between 1 and 2
Show answer

D. SOR converges for 0 < ω < 2; over-relaxation (1 < ω < 2) is fastest and ω ≥ 2 diverges. (A)/(B) would not relax the field; (C) diverges.

Q5. For a large N×N grid, optimal SOR reduces the number of sweeps to converge from O(N²) down to about…
A. O(N)
B. O(N³)
C. O(N²) still — no improvement
D. O(1), independent of grid size
Show answer

A. Optimal SOR needs O(N) sweeps versus O(N²) for Jacobi/Gauss–Seidel — the big classical speedup. (B) is worse than either. (C) ignores the gain. (D) is what multigrid approaches, not single-grid SOR.

✓ What you learned

  • Poisson (∇²φ = −ρ/ε₀) and Laplace (∇²φ = 0) are elliptic boundary-value problems with no time derivative — the steady electrostatic potential is the model case.
  • The five-point stencil turns ∇²φ = 0 into "each node is the average of its four neighbors."
  • Jacobi averages using old values (needs two grids); Gauss–Seidel reuses fresh values and converges about twice as fast.
  • SOR over-corrects by a factor ω in (1, 2); the optimal ω = 2/(1 + sin(π/N)) drops the work from O(N²) to O(N) sweeps.
  • You relaxed the potential inside a charged box in NumPy, tracked the residual to decide convergence, and visualized E = −∇φ.
