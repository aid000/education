Week 2 · Lecture 2

Finite Differences on a Grid

A continuous field lives at infinitely many points; a computer keeps only a lattice of samples — and a clever bit of algebra turns calculus on that lattice into arithmetic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Discretize a continuous domain in space (and time) onto a uniform grid and name its spacings h and Δt.
  • Derive forward, backward, and central finite-difference stencils for the first derivative from Taylor series, and state their orders.
  • Build the standard second-derivative stencil and explain why it is second-order accurate.
  • Assemble the five-point Laplacian in 2D and read it as a stencil acting on grid neighbors.
  • Turn a PDE into a system of algebraic update rules on grid points and implement the Laplacian in NumPy.

2.1Discretizing Space and Time

Last week we met the three families of PDEs — elliptic, parabolic, and hyperbolic — written for a continuous field u(x, t) defined at every point of space and time. A computer cannot hold a continuum. The first move of every finite-difference method is therefore to replace the continuum with a grid: a finite set of equally spaced sample points.

In one spatial dimension we chop the interval [0, L] into N equal cells, giving grid points

xi = i · h,   i = 0, 1, …, N,   h = L / Nh is the grid spacing. We store only the samples ui ≈ u(xi); everything between the dots is unknown to the machine.

If the problem also evolves in time we discretize that axis too, with a time step Δt, writing uin ≈ u(xi, tn) where tn = n·Δt. The superscript is a time index, never a power — a convention you will see everywhere in this course. The whole field becomes a 1D array (or, in higher dimensions, a 2D/3D array) of numbers, and a PDE becomes a recipe for filling that array.

AnalogyA grid is like the pixels of a digital photo. The real scene is continuous, but the camera keeps a finite array of samples. Make the pixels smaller (shrink h) and the picture sharpens toward the true field — at the cost of more storage and more arithmetic. Choosing h is choosing your resolution.

2.2Stencils for the First Derivative

How do we recover a derivative from samples? Taylor's theorem connects a neighbor to the center. Expanding u about xi:

ui+1 = ui + h·u'i + (h²/2)·u''i + (h³/6)·u'''i + …The value one cell to the right, written in terms of the value and derivatives at the center.

Solve for u'i and you get the forward difference. Doing the same with ui−1 gives the backward difference. Each drops a term of order h, so each is first-order accurate — the leading error is proportional to h.

u'i ≈ (ui+1 − ui) / h  (forward),   u'i ≈ (ui − ui−1) / h  (backward)Both have error O(h): halving h roughly halves the error.

Now subtract the expansion of ui−1 from that of ui+1. The even-order terms (including u'') cancel, the leading error becomes O(h²), and we get the prized central difference:

u'i ≈ (ui+1 − ui−1) / (2h)   with error O(h²)Symmetry buys an extra order of accuracy for the same number of points.

The list of weights applied to neighbors — here {−1/2h, 0, +1/2h} on {ui−1, ui, ui+1} — is called a stencil. A stencil is the entire idea of finite differences distilled into a small pattern of multipliers slid across the grid.

2.3The Second Derivative and the 2D Laplacian

Most physics PDEs feature the second derivative — diffusion, waves, and electrostatics all carry u''. To build its stencil, add the Taylor expansions of ui+1 and ui−1. The odd terms cancel, the first derivative drops out, and solving for u'' gives:

u''i ≈ (ui+1 − 2ui + ui−1) / h²   with error O(h²)The three-point second-derivative stencil: weights {1, −2, 1} / h². Second-order accurate.

This {1, −2, 1} pattern is the most-used stencil in computational physics. It reads as "how much does the center stick out above the average of its neighbors?" — exactly the curvature.

In two dimensions the Laplacian is ∇²u = uxx + uyy. We apply the second-derivative stencil once along x and once along y on a grid with the same spacing h in both directions, indexing points as ui,j. Adding the two gives the celebrated five-point Laplacian:

∇²ui,j ≈ (ui+1,j + ui−1,j + ui,j+1 + ui,j−1 − 4ui,j) / h²Four neighbors with weight +1, the center with weight −4, all over h². Error O(h²).
Common mistakeForgetting the 1/h². The pattern {1, 1, 1, 1, −4} gives the correct shape, but it is dimensionally wrong and numerically off by a factor of h². If your simulation blows up or freezes the moment you change the grid spacing, the missing (or mis-placed) h² is the first suspect. A second classic slip: applying the interior stencil at a boundary point, where neighbor ui−1 or ui+1 does not exist — boundaries need their own rule (next section).

2.4From PDE to Algebraic Update Rules

Here is the payoff. A PDE relates derivatives of a field. Replace every derivative by its stencil and the differential equation collapses into algebra on grid values — one equation per interior grid point. Two flavors arise depending on the PDE type.

Time-dependent (parabolic/hyperbolic). Take the 1D heat equation ut = α·uxx. Use a forward difference in time and the second-derivative stencil in space:

uin+1 = uin + (α·Δt / h²)·(ui+1n − 2uin + ui−1n)An explicit update rule: the new value at a point is computed directly from the three old neighbors.

This is a literal recipe — loop over i, compute the right side from the current array, store it as the next time level. We "march" the field forward step by step. (Next week we'll discover this only works when Δt is small enough — the stability condition.)

Steady-state (elliptic). For Poisson's equation ∇²u = f the time index is gone. The five-point stencil set equal to f at every interior point yields one linear equation per point, i.e. a large sparse linear system A·u = b — solved all at once by the linear-algebra tools of Course 3.

AnalogyTurning a PDE into stencil updates is like replacing a smooth conversation with a game of telephone on a grid of people. Each person only listens to immediate neighbors and updates their own number by a fixed rule. Run enough rounds and the local whispering reproduces the global behavior of the continuous field — diffusion, equilibrium, or a traveling wave.
✦ Worked Example — Laplacian of a known field

On a grid with spacing h = 0.5, a field has these values near point (i, j): center ui,j = 4.0, and neighbors ui+1,j = 5.0, ui−1,j = 3.0, ui,j+1 = 6.0, ui,j−1 = 2.0. Estimate ∇²u at the center with the five-point stencil.

  1. Write the stencil: ∇²u ≈ (ui+1,j + ui−1,j + ui,j+1 + ui,j−1 − 4ui,j) / h².
  2. Sum the four neighbors: 5.0 + 3.0 + 6.0 + 2.0 = 16.0.
  3. Subtract 4× the center: 16.0 − 4(4.0) = 16.0 − 16.0 = 0.0.
  4. Divide by h²: h² = 0.25, so ∇²u ≈ 0.0 / 0.25 = 0.0.
  5. Interpret: the center exactly equals the average of its four neighbors (16/4 = 4), so the discrete Laplacian is zero — locally harmonic, as it would be for a steady-state solution of Laplace's equation. Bump ui,j to 3.5 and you'd get (16 − 14)/0.25 = +8.0: the field now curves upward away from the center.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a Laplacian without a single loop

The free Python + NumPy / Matplotlib stack runs everything in this specialization (Numba is optional, added later only for speed). Week 2 setup: install it once below, then reuse it for every lecture and the course project.

The key trick: array slicing applies a stencil to every interior point at once — shifting the array by one index is the same as picking the neighbor. We test it on u(x, y) = x² + y², whose exact Laplacian is the constant 4.

# laplacian.py — Lecture 2 (C4)
import numpy as np

N, L = 50, 1.0
h = L / N
x = np.linspace(0, L, N + 1)
X, Y = np.meshgrid(x, x, indexing="ij")
u = X**2 + Y**2                          # exact Laplacian = 4 everywhere

lap = np.zeros_like(u)
# five-point stencil on interior points, via slicing (no Python loop)
lap[1:-1, 1:-1] = (
    u[2:, 1:-1] + u[:-2, 1:-1] +          # i+1 and i-1 neighbors
    u[1:-1, 2:] + u[1:-1, :-2] -          # j+1 and j-1 neighbors
    4 * u[1:-1, 1:-1]                     # center, weight -4
) / h**2

print("interior min/max:", lap[1:-1, 1:-1].min(), lap[1:-1, 1:-1].max())
# both ~ 4.0 (small round-off): the discrete Laplacian recovers the exact value

Try u = sin(pi·X)·sin(pi·Y): its exact Laplacian is −2pi²·u. Compare the slice result to that and watch the error shrink like h² as you double N.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. On a uniform 1D grid, what does the spacing h represent?
A. the total length of the domain
B. the number of grid points
C. the time step of the simulation
D. the distance between adjacent grid points
Show answer

D. h is the gap between neighboring samples, h = L/N. The domain length (A) is L; the count of points (B) is N+1; the time step (C) is the separate Δt.

Q2. Why is the central difference (ui+1 − ui−1)/(2h) more accurate than the forward difference (ui+1 − ui)/h?
A. it uses fewer grid points
B. it avoids all round-off error
C. its symmetry cancels the leading h term, giving O(h²) instead of O(h)
D. it does not require a Taylor series
Show answer

C. Subtracting the two symmetric expansions cancels the even-order terms, raising the order from O(h) to O(h²). It actually uses the same number of points (A is false), still has round-off (B), and is derived from Taylor series (D).

Q3. The second-derivative stencil (ui+1 − 2ui + ui−1)/h² has what order of accuracy?
A. first order, O(h)
B. second order, O(h²)
C. zeroth order (no convergence)
D. fourth order, O(h⁴)
Show answer

B. Adding the symmetric Taylor expansions cancels the odd terms, leaving a leading error proportional to h², so it is second-order. Wider stencils can reach O(h⁴) (D), but not this three-point one.

Q4. The five-point Laplacian assigns which weights to the center and its four edge-neighbors (before dividing by h²)?
A. center +4, neighbors −1 each
B. center −2, neighbors +1 each
C. center −4, neighbors +1 each
D. center 0, neighbors +1 each
Show answer

C. Summing the 1D stencils along x and y gives center −4 and +1 on each of the four neighbors, all over h². (A) has the wrong signs; (B) is the 1D second-derivative center; (D) would compute a neighbor sum, not a Laplacian.

Q5. Turning the heat equation ut = α·uxx into uin+1 = uin + (αΔt/h²)(ui+1n − 2uin + ui−1n) is an example of…
A. replacing derivatives by stencils to get an explicit algebraic update rule
B. solving the PDE exactly with no approximation
C. a method that needs no grid
D. integrating the PDE analytically over the domain
Show answer

A. Both the time and space derivatives are swapped for finite-difference stencils, yielding an explicit recipe for the next time level from the current one. It is an approximation (B false), defined on a grid (C false), and purely algebraic rather than analytic (D false).

✓ What you learned

  • A continuous field is discretized onto a uniform grid with spacing h (and time step Δt), storing only samples ui (or ui,jn).
  • Forward and backward first-derivative differences are O(h); the symmetric central difference is O(h²).
  • The three-point second-derivative stencil {1, −2, 1}/h² measures curvature and is second-order accurate.
  • The five-point Laplacian (center −4, neighbors +1, over h²) estimates ∇²u and drives heat, Poisson, and wave solvers.
  • Replacing derivatives by stencils turns a PDE into algebraic update rules — explicit marching for time problems, a sparse linear system for steady states.
