Maxwell's Equations & the FDTD Method

Two coupled curl equations, a clever staggered grid, and a leapfrog in time — enough to watch a pulse of light march across your screen.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write Maxwell's two curl equations as a coupled first-order time-marching system for E and H.
  • Explain why the Yee grid staggers E and H in both space and time, and what that buys you.
  • Derive the leapfrog update equations for a 1D FDTD scheme from centered differences.
  • State and apply the Courant (CFL) stability condition for FDTD and predict what happens when it is violated.
  • Implement a 1D FDTD pulse in Python + NumPy and watch it propagate at the speed of light.

9.1Maxwell's Curl Equations as a Time-Marching System

Electromagnetics in source-free, non-magnetic, linear media is governed by just two of Maxwell's equations — the two curl laws. Faraday's law says a changing magnetic field drives a curling electric field; Ampère's law (with Maxwell's displacement-current term) says a changing electric field drives a curling magnetic field. The two divergence laws are automatically preserved if we start from divergence-free fields, so the curl pair is all we need to step forward in time:

∂H/∂t = −(1/μ) ∇ × E     ∂E/∂t = (1/ε) ∇ × HFaraday (left) and Ampère (right). Each field's time derivative is set by the spatial curl of the other — a coupled, self-propagating system.

The structure is the key insight. E feeds H, and H feeds E. Neither field evolves alone; each is the engine for the other. This mutual feedback is exactly what a propagating electromagnetic wave is — a disturbance in E creates H one instant later, which recreates E a little further along, and the pattern walks through space at the speed of light c = 1/√(με).

To keep the algebra honest we will work in 1D: a wave traveling along x, with the field polarized so only Ez and Hy are nonzero. The curls collapse to single spatial derivatives:

∂Ez/∂t = (1/ε) ∂Hy/∂x     ∂Hy/∂t = (1/μ) ∂Ez/∂xThe 1D transverse-EM mode. Two fields, two first-order PDEs, coupled through a single spatial derivative each.
AnalogyThink of two children on a seesaw who can only push, never pull. When E rises it gives H a shove; the shoved H then shoves E a step further down the board. Light is that endless, self-sustaining exchange of pushes — neither child moves on their own, but together they send the motion traveling all the way down the plank.

9.2The Yee Grid: Staggering E and H in Space and Time

Both update equations are built from a spatial derivative of the other field. The most accurate, cheapest way to approximate a first derivative is a centered difference, which is second-order accurate. But a centered difference of a quantity defined at the grid points lands between the points. K. S. Yee's 1966 insight was to embrace that: don't fight the half-step — put the two fields on grids offset by half a cell, so each derivative naturally lands where the other field lives.

So we store Ez at integer spatial nodes i and Hy at half-integer nodes i + 1/2. We do the same in time: E is known at integer time levels n, and H at half-integer levels n + 1/2. Every derivative we ever need is then a clean, centered difference of values that already exist on the grid — no interpolation, no averaging.

In 2D and 3D the same idea places each field component on its own offset sub-grid, so that a discrete curl of one field always evaluates exactly where the other field is stored. The 3D version forms the famous interlocking "Yee cell," but the 1D picture above already contains the whole idea.

9.3The Leapfrog Update

Now turn the two PDEs into update formulas. Replace each derivative with a centered difference about the staggered point. Start with Faraday's law, evaluated at the half-integer node i + 1/2 and the integer time level n — exactly where the time derivative of H is centered:

Hy[i+1/2]n+1/2 = Hy[i+1/2]n−1/2 + (Δt / μΔx) (Ez[i+1]n − Ez[i]n)Update H half a step using the spatial difference of the current E. The time difference is centered on level n; the space difference is centered on node i+1/2.

Then Ampère's law, evaluated at the integer node i and the half-integer time level n + 1/2, advances E using the H values we just computed:

Ez[i]n+1 = Ez[i]n + (Δt / εΔx) (Hy[i+1/2]n+1/2 − Hy[i−1/2]n+1/2)Update E a full step using the freshly-updated H. Again both differences are centered, hence second-order accurate.

The pattern is a leapfrog: H jumps from n−1/2 to n+1/2 over the standing E at level n; then E jumps from n to n+1 over the standing H at level n+1/2. The two fields perpetually hop over one another, each using the other's most recent value. Because every difference is centered, the whole scheme is second-order accurate in both space and time — remarkable for something so cheap. It is also explicit: each new value is an instant formula, no linear system to solve.

Common mistakeDo not update all of E and then all of H using the same old arrays as if it were one big simultaneous step. The leapfrog only works — and only stays second-order — if you update H first, then use those new H values immediately when updating E (or vice versa, consistently). Mixing in stale values, or trying to advance both fields to the same time level at once, breaks the staggering and degrades or destabilizes the scheme.

9.4The Courant Stability Condition

FDTD is explicit, and like every explicit hyperbolic scheme it is only conditionally stable: the time step must not be too large relative to the cell size. The governing rule is the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition. The physical idea is a numerical "speed limit": in one time step, information on the grid must not be asked to travel farther than the scheme can carry it. The fastest physical signal is light at speed c, so we require that c Δt not exceed the cell size Δx. In 1D:

S = c Δt / Δx ≤ 1    (in d dimensions: c Δt ≤ Δx / √d)S is the Courant number. Stability requires S ≤ 1 in 1D; in 3D with equal cells the bound tightens to 1/√3.

If S > 1, a numerical wave would have to outrun light on the grid — physically impossible — and the scheme responds by blowing up: high-frequency modes grow exponentially and the fields overflow within a few dozen steps. If S is far below 1 the run is stable but wastefully slow and slightly more dispersive. The sweet spot in 1D is the magic time step S = 1, where c Δt = Δx and the discrete scheme propagates the pulse with essentially zero numerical dispersion.

✦ Worked Example — sizing Δx and Δt for a 1D simulation

You want to simulate a vacuum pulse with wavelength around λ = 600 nm (visible light) on a 1D grid. Choose a cell size, then the largest stable time step, and find how many steps it takes the pulse to cross a 30-µm domain.

  1. Resolve the wavelength: a standard rule of thumb is ≥ 10 cells per wavelength. Take Δx = λ/20 = 600 nm / 20 = 30 nm for a comfortable margin.
  2. Apply the CFL bound: in 1D, Δt ≤ Δx / c. With c = 3.0×108 m/s, Δt ≤ (30×10−9) / (3.0×108) = 1.0×10−16 s (0.1 fs).
  3. Pick the magic step: set S = 1 exactly, so Δt = Δx / c = 1.0×10−16 s. This both maximizes speed and minimizes numerical dispersion in 1D.
  4. Domain crossing time: light crosses 30 µm in t = (30×10−6) / (3.0×108) = 1.0×10−13 s.
  5. Step count: number of steps = t / Δt = (1.0×10−13) / (1.0×10−16) = 1000 steps. Equivalently, with S = 1 the pulse advances exactly one cell per step, and 30 µm / 30 nm = 1000 cells — the two ways of counting agree, a nice sanity check.

🛠️ Hands-On: a 1D FDTD pulse in NumPy

We use the same free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (see the install panel in the early lectures). No new packages are needed — vectorized NumPy slicing is all it takes to implement the leapfrog. We work in dimensionless "natural units" where c = 1, Δx = 1, so the magic step is simply Δt = 1.

The whole solver is the two leapfrog lines inside the loop. Each is a single vectorized difference of neighboring array cells — the discrete curl.

# fdtd1d.py - Lecture 9: 1D FDTD pulse in vacuum (c = dx = 1, magic step dt = 1)
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

N      = 400                 # number of spatial cells
steps  = 350                 # time steps to run
S      = 1.0                 # Courant number c*dt/dx (1.0 = magic step)

Ez = np.zeros(N)             # E at integer nodes 0..N-1
Hy = np.zeros(N)             # H at half-integer nodes (stored index = i+1/2)

def source(n):               # a soft Gaussian pulse injected at the left
    t0, spread = 40.0, 12.0
    return np.exp(-((n - t0) / spread) ** 2)

for n in range(steps):
    # update H from spatial difference of E  (Faraday)
    Hy[:-1] += S * (Ez[1:] - Ez[:-1])
    # update E from spatial difference of H  (Ampere), using the NEW H
    Ez[1:]  += S * (Hy[1:] - Hy[:-1])
    Ez[5]   += source(n)     # inject the pulse near the left boundary

plt.plot(Ez, color="#818cf8")
plt.title("Ez after %d steps - the pulse has marched right" % steps)
plt.xlabel("cell index i"); plt.ylabel("Ez")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Run it and you will see a clean Gaussian bump sitting to the right of where it was launched — the pulse propagated at one cell per step, exactly c. Now experiment: set S = 1.05 and rerun. Within a few dozen steps the field explodes to inf — that is the CFL condition biting. Drop to S = 0.5 and the pulse survives but you will see it broaden slightly as numerical dispersion smears the sharp edges. Reflections off the hard Ez[0] and Ez[N-1] boundaries are the motivation for the absorbing boundary conditions you would add next.

🔗 Where this is heading

This 1D leapfrog is the seed of a workhorse used across optics, antenna design, radar cross-section prediction, photonics, and biomedical microwave imaging. Next week's capstone (Week 10) puts FDTD side-by-side with the implicit heat-equation solver you built earlier, contrasting explicit-but-conditionally-stable wave marching against unconditionally-stable diffusion. To make FDTD genuinely useful you would add absorbing boundary conditions (the perfectly matched layer) so waves leave the domain without reflecting, dielectric and dispersive materials via spatially varying ε and μ, and the full 3D Yee cell. The same Courant-number thinking governs the acoustic and seismic wave solvers of geophysics and the lattice schemes of computational plasma physics in the Plasma track.

Q1. Which two of Maxwell's equations does FDTD time-march, and why are the other two not stepped?
A. The two divergence (Gauss) laws; the curl laws are redundant.
B. The two curl laws (Faraday and Ampère); the divergence laws are preserved automatically if the initial fields satisfy them.
C. Only Faraday's law; Ampère's law is solved separately as a static problem each step.
D. All four, simultaneously, as one coupled linear system.
Show answer

B. The curl laws give ∂E/∂t and ∂H/∂t, the time-stepping engine. The divergence laws contain no time derivative; they are constraints that stay satisfied if the start is divergence-free. A is backwards (the divergence laws cannot march time), C drops half the physics, and D mischaracterizes FDTD as implicit when it is explicit.

Q2. On the Yee grid, why are E and H stored at points offset by half a cell in both space and time?
A. To halve the memory needed for the field arrays.
B. So that a centered (second-order) difference of one field lands exactly where the other field is stored, needing no interpolation.
C. To make the scheme implicit and therefore unconditionally stable.
D. Because Maxwell's equations require E and H to be measured at different physical locations.
Show answer

B. The half-cell offset makes every needed derivative a clean centered difference of existing samples, giving second-order accuracy for free. A is false (staggering does not save memory), C is false (FDTD stays explicit and only conditionally stable), and D confuses a numerical convenience with a physical requirement — E and H coexist everywhere.

Q3. The leapfrog update advances the fields by…
A. solving a large linear system for E and H together at each step.
B. updating E and H to the same time level simultaneously using only old values.
C. alternately stepping H over the current E, then E over the just-updated H, each a half-step apart.
D. integrating each field independently, ignoring the other.
Show answer

C. H hops from n−1/2 to n+1/2 over E at level n, then E hops from n to n+1 over the new H at n+1/2 — the defining leapfrog interleaving. A describes an implicit solver (FDTD is explicit), B breaks the staggering and degrades the scheme, and D ignores the coupling that makes a wave propagate at all.

Q4. In 1D, the Courant number is S = cΔt/Δx. What happens if you choose Δt so that S = 1.2?
A. The simulation is unstable: errors grow exponentially and the fields overflow within a few dozen steps.
B. The simulation runs fine but 20% slower.
C. The pulse travels at 1.2c, which is physically correct on a grid.
D. Nothing changes; the Courant number only affects accuracy, never stability.
Show answer

A. S > 1 violates the CFL condition: the physical signal would have to outrun the numerical domain of dependence, so high-frequency modes amplify each step and the run diverges. B and D wrongly treat the bound as cosmetic, and C asserts faster-than-light propagation, which the grid cannot support.

Q5. A 1D FDTD scheme uses centered differences in both space and time. What is its order of accuracy, and how does it relate to the "magic" step S = 1?
A. First-order in space, first-order in time; S = 1 has no special property.
B. Second-order in space, first-order in time; S = 1 maximizes numerical dispersion.
C. Fourth-order overall; S = 1 makes it unstable.
D. Second-order in both space and time; at S = 1 the 1D scheme propagates with essentially zero numerical dispersion.
Show answer

D. Centered differences are second-order, so the scheme is second-order in space and time. At the 1D magic step S = 1 the numerical and physical light cones coincide, eliminating dispersion. A understates the order, B inverts the dispersion behavior, and C both overstates the order and wrongly calls S = 1 unstable (S = 1 is exactly the 1D stability boundary and the optimum).

✓ What you learned

  • Maxwell's two curl laws form a coupled first-order system where E feeds H and H feeds E — the engine of wave propagation.
  • The Yee grid staggers E and H by half a cell in space and time so every derivative is a clean centered difference with no interpolation.
  • The leapfrog update alternately hops H over E and E over H, giving an explicit, second-order scheme in space and time.
  • The Courant (CFL) condition S = cΔt/Δx ≤ 1 (1/√d in d dimensions) sets the stability speed limit; S > 1 blows up.
  • A 1D FDTD pulse in NumPy is just two vectorized difference lines, and at S = 1 it marches one cell per step at exactly c.

Model response

Maxwell's two curl equations are intrinsically mutual: ∂H/∂t is set by the spatial curl of E, and ∂E/∂t is set by the spatial curl of H — each field's time derivative is literally the other field's spatial derivative. A centered (second-order) spatial difference of a quantity defined at integer grid points naturally evaluates between those points, at a half-integer location. Rather than fight that by interpolating E or H onto a point where it isn't naturally defined, the Yee grid embraces it: E lives at integer nodes and integer time levels, H lives at half-integer nodes and half-integer time levels, so every derivative either equation ever needs is a clean centered difference of values that already exist on the grid — no interpolation error is ever introduced.

The "leapfrog dance" then falls out automatically from that staggering rather than being imposed on top of it. Updating H to its next half-integer time level needs E at the surrounding integer nodes at the current time; updating E to its next integer time level needs the freshly-computed H at the surrounding half-integer nodes. So the natural order is: advance H by hopping over the standing E, then advance E by hopping over the just-updated H. Each step is explicit and second-order in both space and time, and the computational order mirrors the physical push-and-pull between E and H exactly — the staggering isn't a numerical trick bolted onto Maxwell's equations, it's the grid finally matching the structure the equations already had.
