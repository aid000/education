The Wave Equation

Pluck a string and a disturbance races outward at a fixed speed — the wave equation is how a computer learns to let ripples travel, reflect, and carry energy without losing a drop.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the second-order wave equation from a vibrating string and read off the meaning of the wave speed c.
  • Build the central-time central-space (leapfrog) update from finite differences and identify it as a three-time-level scheme.
  • State and apply the CFL condition for waves (c·dt ≤ dx) and explain physically why violating it blows up.
  • Predict reflection and transmission of a pulse at fixed, free, and impedance-mismatched boundaries.
  • Track discrete energy as a stability diagnostic and simulate a vibrating string in NumPy.

7.1From a Plucked String to a PDE

Imagine a taut string under tension T with mass per unit length ρ. Let u(x,t) be the small sideways displacement at position x and time t. Pick a tiny segment between x and x+dx. The string is pulled at both ends; because it is slightly curved, the two tension forces do not quite cancel, and the leftover points sideways. The net vertical force is proportional to how much the slope changes across the segment — that is, to the curvature ∂²u/∂x². Newton's second law (mass × acceleration = force) then reads ρ dx · ∂²u/∂t² = T · (∂²u/∂x²) dx. Cancel dx and define c² = T/ρ:

∂²u/∂t² = c² · ∂²u/∂x²The 1-D wave equation. c = sqrt(T/rho) is the wave speed; curvature in space drives acceleration in time.

This is the prototype hyperbolic PDE. Unlike the diffusion equation of Week 5 (which smooths everything out), the wave equation preserves shape: d'Alembert showed any solution is a sum of a right-mover and a left-mover, u(x,t) = f(x − ct) + g(x + ct). Two rigid profiles slide past each other at speed c. Our entire numerical job is to reproduce that sliding faithfully on a grid.

The same equation governs sound in air (c from pressure and density), light in a vacuum (c from Maxwell's equations), seismic P-waves through rock, and ripples on a guitar or piano string. Change what c means and the math is identical — which is exactly why one solver serves so many fields.

AnalogyThink of a long line of children holding hands. If one child lurches, the pull on their neighbors depends on how bent the line is right there — a straight stretch feels no net tug, a sharp kink yanks hard. That "force from local bending" is curvature, and the lurch it triggers ripples down the line at a fixed pace set by how strongly they grip (tension) and how heavy they are (mass). That fixed pace is c.

7.2The Leapfrog Scheme: Central in Time, Central in Space

Lay down a grid: positions xi = i·dx and times tn = n·dt. Write uin for the displacement at node i and step n. We replace both second derivatives with the standard centered second difference. In space:

∂²u/∂x² ≈ (u[i+1] - 2 u[i] + u[i-1]) / dx²Centered second difference in space, evaluated at the current time level n. Error is O(dx^2).

In time, applied to the same node:

∂²u/∂t² ≈ (u[i] at n+1 - 2 u[i] at n + u[i] at n-1) / dt²Centered second difference in time. It links three time levels: past (n-1), present (n), future (n+1).

Set them equal through the PDE and solve for the only unknown, the future value uin+1. Defining the dimensionless Courant number r = c·dt/dx:

u[i] at n+1 = 2 u[i] - u[i] at n-1 + r^2 ( u[i+1] - 2 u[i] + u[i-1] )The leapfrog (CTCS) update. r = c*dt/dx. Every term on the right is already known, so this is fully explicit.

This is gorgeously simple: no matrix to invert, just one weighted stencil per node per step. The name leapfrog comes from needing two past levels (n and n−1) to leap to n+1 — the scheme hops over the present. That raises a wrinkle: at the very first step we only have the initial shape u0. We get the missing u−1 from the initial velocity v(x) = ∂u/∂t using a centered approximation, which gives the special starter step ui1 = ui0 + dt·vi + ½r²(ui+10 − 2ui0 + ui−10). After that, the three-level update runs forever.

7.3The CFL Condition: Don't Outrun the Wave

The explicit scheme is cheap, but it is only conditionally stable. A von Neumann stability analysis — plug a single Fourier mode uin = Gn ei k x into the update and demand the amplification |G| ≤ 1 for every wavenumber k — yields one clean requirement on the Courant number:

r = c·dt/dx ≤ 1   i.e.   c·dt ≤ dxThe Courant-Friedrichs-Lewy (CFL) condition for the explicit wave scheme in 1-D.

The physics is beautifully intuitive. In one time step a real wave travels a distance c·dt. The numerical stencil, meanwhile, only "looks" one grid cell to each side, so information can spread at most dx per step. If c·dt > dx, the true wave outruns what the grid can communicate: the scheme is being asked to know something it has no access to, and the missing information manifests as exponentially growing garbage. The numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical one — that is the whole content of CFL.

Common mistakeTreating CFL like the diffusion limit from Week 5. There the stable step scaled as dt <= dx2/(2D) — quadratic in dx, brutally small on fine grids. The wave condition is linear: dt <= dx/c. Halving dx only halves dt, not quarters it. Don't import the wrong scaling, and don't forget to recompute dt when you change c or dx — the most common cause of a "stable code that suddenly explodes."

Choosing r exactly equal to 1 is a magical sweet spot in 1-D: the leapfrog scheme then reproduces d'Alembert's solution exactly, with zero numerical dispersion, because the update collapses to uin+1 = ui+1n + ui−1n − uin−1. In practice we use r slightly below 1 (say 0.9) for safety margin and to handle variable c.

7.4Boundaries, Reflection & Energy

A wave eventually hits the edge of the domain, and what happens there is set entirely by the boundary condition (Week 6). Two cases dominate:

  • Fixed (Dirichlet) end: clamp u = 0, like a guitar string pinned to the bridge. A pulse reflects inverted — an upward bump returns as a downward one. The clamp must push back, and Newton's third law flips the sign.
  • Free (Neumann) end: set ∂u/∂x = 0, like a string end on a frictionless ring that slides vertically. A pulse reflects upright, same sign. Numerically you enforce it with a ghost node u−1 = u1.

When two media with different wave speeds meet (a light string tied to a heavy one), part of the wave transmits and part reflects. The split is governed by the impedance Z = sqrt(T·ρ). The amplitude reflection coefficient is

R = (Z1 - Z2) / (Z1 + Z2)Amplitude reflection coefficient at an interface between impedances Z1 and Z2. A fixed end is the Z2 -> infinity limit (R = -1, full inverted reflection); a free end is Z2 -> 0 (R = +1).

Because the wave equation conserves energy in the continuous world, a good scheme should very nearly conserve it too. The total discrete energy is kinetic plus potential:

E = sum_i [ (1/2) (du/dt)_i^2 + (c^2/2) ((u[i+1]-u[i])/dx)^2 ] dxDiscrete energy of the string. Kinetic from the velocity, potential from the local stretch (slope). Monitor it: drift signals instability or a boundary leak.

Energy is your free lie-detector. On a closed domain with fixed or free ends and r ≤ 1, the leapfrog scheme conserves a closely related discrete energy to round-off over very long runs. If you watch E climb, your CFL number is too big or a boundary is wrong; if it bleeds away, you have an absorbing or lossy boundary (sometimes deliberately, to fake an infinite domain).

✦ Worked Example — picking dt for a guitar string

A string of length L = 0.65 m has wave speed c = 200 m/s (a low-E-ish note). You discretize with N = 500 intervals and want a safety factor so the Courant number is r = 0.9. Find dx, the largest CFL-stable dt, your chosen dt, and how many steps it takes a pulse to cross the string once.

  1. Grid spacing: dx = L/N = 0.65/500 = 1.3×10⁻³ m = 1.3 mm.
  2. CFL limit: the stability bound is dt ≤ dx/c = 1.3×10⁻³ / 200 = 6.5×10⁻⁶ s. Any larger and the scheme blows up.
  3. Chosen step: with r = 0.9, dt = r·dx/c = 0.9 × 6.5×10⁻⁶ = 5.85×10⁻⁶ s ≈ 5.9 µs.
  4. Crossing time: a pulse travels L at speed c, taking Tcross = L/c = 0.65/200 = 3.25×10⁻³ s.
  5. Steps per crossing: Tcross/dt = 3.25×10⁻³ / 5.85×10⁻⁶ ≈ 556 steps. (Sanity check: with r = 0.9 a wave moves 0.9 cells/step, so crossing 500 cells needs 500/0.9 ≈ 556 steps — consistent.) Simulating one full back-and-forth round trip (a half-period of the fundamental) is just twice that.

🛠️ Hands-On: a vibrating string from one pluck

The free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack runs everything here (set up once in Week 1 — reuse that environment; no new tools needed for this lecture). We start the string with a Gaussian bump at rest, march the leapfrog scheme with fixed ends, and watch the energy stay flat to confirm stability.

# wave_string.py — Lecture 7: leapfrog wave equation, fixed ends
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L, c = 1.0, 1.0                 # length and wave speed
N = 400
dx = L / N
r  = 0.9                        # Courant number <= 1 (CFL)
dt = r * dx / c
x  = np.linspace(0, L, N + 1)

# initial shape: a Gaussian pulse at rest (velocity = 0)
u_prev = np.exp(-((x - 0.5) / 0.05) ** 2)
u      = u_prev.copy()          # starter: zero initial velocity
# special first step (v=0): u^1 = u^0 + 0.5 r^2 (lap)
u[1:-1] = u_prev[1:-1] + 0.5 * r**2 * (u_prev[2:] - 2*u_prev[1:-1] + u_prev[:-2])
u[0] = u[-1] = 0.0              # fixed (Dirichlet) ends

def energy(u, u_old):
    v = (u - u_old) / dt                       # time derivative
    ke = 0.5 * np.sum(v**2) * dx
    pe = 0.5 * c**2 * np.sum(np.diff(u)**2) / dx
    return ke + pe

steps = 2000
for n in range(steps):
    u_new = np.empty_like(u)
    u_new[1:-1] = (2*u[1:-1] - u_prev[1:-1]
                   + r**2 * (u[2:] - 2*u[1:-1] + u[:-2]))
    u_new[0] = u_new[-1] = 0.0                  # clamp both ends
    u_prev, u = u, u_new
    if n % 500 == 0:
        print(f"step {n:5d}   E = {energy(u, u_prev):.6f}")

plt.plot(x, u); plt.title("string after 2000 steps"); plt.show()
# E stays ~constant -> stable. Set r = 1.05 and watch it explode (CFL violated).

Experiments: (1) bump r to 1.05 and watch the energy diverge within a few steps — that is CFL. (2) Switch one end to free with a ghost node (u_new[-1] = u_new[-2] via reflection) and confirm the pulse comes back upright. (3) Give the string an initial velocity instead of a displacement and hear (plot) the difference.

🔗 Where this is heading

The leapfrog stencil and CFL thinking you just met scale straight up. In 2-D and 3-D the same explicit update drives finite-difference time-domain (FDTD) solvers for Maxwell's equations — the workhorse of antenna, photonics, and radar design — where CFL becomes c·dt ≤ dx/sqrt(dimensions). Next week (Week 8) you'll meet the elliptic cousin, the Poisson/Laplace equation, which has no time at all and is solved by relaxation. Later in the specialization, absorbing boundaries (perfectly matched layers) extend today's reflection ideas to fake an infinite world, and the Schrödinger equation in the Quantum track is solved with a close relative of leapfrog. Master the wave equation and you hold the key to acoustics, seismology, electromagnetics, and quantum dynamics alike.

Q1. In the wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c²∂²u/∂x², the quantity c represents…
A. the speed at which disturbances propagate, with c = sqrt(T/ρ) for a string
B. the amplitude of the wave
C. a diffusion coefficient that smooths the profile
D. the machine epsilon of the grid
Show answer

A. c is the wave speed, set by tension and mass density (c = sqrt(T/ρ)). It is not an amplitude (B). It is not a diffusion coefficient (C) — that is the heat equation, which smooths rather than propagates. (D) is unrelated floating-point jargon.

Q2. The CFL condition for the explicit 1-D leapfrog wave scheme is…
A. dt ≤ dx²/(2c)
B. c·dt ≤ dx (Courant number r ≤ 1)
C. dt ≥ dx/c
D. dt can be arbitrarily large because the scheme is implicit
Show answer

B. Stability requires r = c·dt/dx ≤ 1. (A) is the quadratic diffusion limit — wrong equation. (C) reverses the inequality (it would force CFL to be violated). (D) is false: the scheme is explicit and only conditionally stable.

Q3. A right-moving pulse hits a fixed (clamped, u = 0) end of a string. The reflected pulse is…
A. upright and unchanged in sign
B. completely absorbed, with no reflection
C. inverted (flipped to the opposite sign), reflection coefficient R = −1
D. doubled in amplitude
Show answer

C. A fixed end is the Z2 → ∞ limit, giving R = −1: the pulse returns inverted. Upright reflection (A) is the free end (R = +1). A lossless clamp does not absorb (B) or amplify (D) energy.

Q4. Why is the scheme called "leapfrog," and what extra information does it need to start?
A. It uses only the current time level, and needs nothing extra
B. It inverts a large matrix each step, needing an initial guess
C. It uses one-sided differences, needing values from outside the domain
D. It links three time levels (past, present, future), so the first step needs a special starter built from the initial velocity
Show answer

D. The centered time derivative ties n−1, n, n+1 together, hopping over the present like a leapfrog; with only u⁰ known, the first step uses the initial velocity to supply the missing past level. (A) describes a one-level method. (B) describes an implicit solve — leapfrog is explicit. (C) misstates the (centered, in-domain) stencil.

Q5. You run the string solver with fixed ends and r = 0.9, but the total discrete energy slowly climbs each step. The most likely cause is…
A. energy is supposed to grow for a wave on a closed domain
B. a bug — a lossless closed domain should conserve energy, so a boundary or starter step is implemented wrong
C. the grid is too fine
D. floating-point round-off, which always makes energy grow exponentially
Show answer

B. With r ≤ 1 and lossless ends, leapfrog conserves a discrete energy to round-off — steady growth flags a wrong boundary or starter step. Energy should not grow on a closed lossless domain (A). A finer grid (C) does not create energy. Round-off (D) causes tiny random drift, not systematic exponential growth.

✓ What you learned

  • The wave equation ∂²u/∂t² = c²∂²u/∂x² comes from Newton's law on a string; c = sqrt(T/ρ) is the propagation speed and solutions are shape-preserving left- and right-movers.
  • The leapfrog (CTCS) scheme is an explicit three-time-level update built from centered second differences, needing a special starter step from the initial velocity.
  • The CFL condition c·dt ≤ dx is linear in dx (unlike diffusion's dx²) and means the numerical domain of dependence must contain the physical one.
  • Fixed ends invert a pulse (R = −1), free ends keep it upright (R = +1), and partial reflection at an interface is set by impedance Z = sqrt(Tρ).
  • Tracking discrete energy is a free stability diagnostic: it stays flat on a lossless closed domain when the scheme is correct and CFL-stable.
