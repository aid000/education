PDEs in Physics & the Three Types

Almost every law of continuum physics is a partial differential equation — and nearly all of them fall into just three families that behave in wildly different ways.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe a PDE as a rule for how a field changes across space and time, and read the meaning of its partial derivatives.
  • Classify a linear second-order PDE as parabolic, hyperbolic, or elliptic from its discriminant.
  • Match each type to its canonical physics: diffusion (heat), waves, and steady-state potentials (Poisson/Laplace).
  • Explain the qualitatively different behavior of each type — smoothing, propagation at finite speed, and instant global equilibrium.
  • Distinguish an initial-value problem from a boundary-value problem and say which type needs which.

1.1A PDE Is a Field's Rulebook

An ordinary differential equation tracks a quantity that depends on a single variable — usually time. But the physical world is spread out: temperature has a value at every point of a metal bar, the electric potential fills all of space, the displacement of a guitar string is defined along its whole length. A quantity defined over a region of space (and possibly time) is a field, written like u(x, t) or u(x, y, z).

A partial differential equation (PDE) is a rule relating a field's value to its rates of change in different directions — its partial derivatives. The symbol ∂u/∂t means "how fast u changes in time, holding position fixed," and ∂²u/∂x² means "how curved u is along x." PDEs are the native language of continuum physics: heat flow, electromagnetism, fluids, acoustics, quantum mechanics, and general relativity are all PDEs.

u = u(x, t)    u_t = ∂u/∂t    u_xx = ∂²u/∂x²A field u and its partial derivatives. Subscripts are a compact shorthand for partial derivatives.

This course studies linear second-order PDEs, which cover an enormous fraction of physics. In one space dimension plus time they have the general form below, and — remarkably — the single coefficient combination B² − 4AC decides everything about how solutions behave.

A·u_xx + B·u_xt + C·u_tt + (lower-order terms) = 0The principal part (highest derivatives) sets the type; A, B, C are the coefficients of the second derivatives.
AnalogyAn ODE is a recipe for a single dancer's path through time. A PDE is the choreography for an entire crowd: it doesn't just say how each point moves, it ties every point's motion to what its neighbors are doing. The second derivative u_xx is exactly "how do I compare to the average of my neighbors?" — which is why it shows up in nearly every field equation.

1.2The Three Types and What Decides Them

Borrowing the names of the conic sections (B² − 4AC also classifies ellipses, parabolas, and hyperbolas), we sort second-order PDEs by their discriminant D = B² − 4AC:

D = B² − 4AC   D < 0: elliptic  |  D = 0: parabolic  |  D > 0: hyperbolicThe sign of the discriminant of the principal part fixes the PDE's qualitative behavior.

This is not a bookkeeping curiosity. The three classes describe three genuinely different physical worlds:

  • Parabolic (diffusion): information spreads infinitely fast but is heavily damped; sharp features get smoothed out over time. The heat equation u_t = α·u_xx is the archetype.
  • Hyperbolic (waves): information travels at a finite speed c along characteristics; features propagate without smoothing. The wave equation u_tt = c²·u_xx is the archetype.
  • Elliptic (equilibrium): no time variable — the field instantly settles into a steady balance set entirely by its boundaries. Laplace's equation u_xx + u_yy = 0 is the archetype.

Check the archetypes: the heat equation has A = α, B = 0, C = 0, so D = 0 (parabolic). The wave equation rearranges to c²·u_xx − u_tt = 0, giving A = c², C = −1, so D = −4AC = 4c² > 0 (hyperbolic). Laplace's equation u_xx + u_yy has A = 1, C = 1, so D = −4 < 0 (elliptic). The names line up exactly.

1.3Physical Examples of Each Family

These equations are not abstractions — they are the working tools of applied physics and engineering.

Parabolic — the diffusion / heat equation

The same equation governs heat conduction through a turbine blade, the spreading of a dye in still water, the diffusion of dopants into a silicon wafer, and (with an imaginary coefficient) the Schrödinger equation. Its hallmark is irreversible smoothing: solve it backward in time and small errors blow up.

u_t = α·u_xxα is the diffusivity (m²/s). Larger α means faster smoothing.

Hyperbolic — the wave equation

Sound in air, vibrations on a string or drumhead, seismic waves through rock, light in vacuum (Maxwell's equations reduce to it), and the ripples of gravitational radiation all obey the wave equation. Its hallmark is propagation at a finite speed c with no loss of shape.

u_tt = c²·u_xxc is the wave speed (m/s). Disturbances reach a point only after time = distance / c.

Elliptic — Poisson and Laplace

Electrostatic potential around charged conductors, the steady temperature of a heated plate, the gravitational potential of a mass distribution, and incompressible-flow stream functions are all elliptic. There is no time: the field is a global equilibrium fixed by sources and boundaries.

u_xx + u_yy = −ρ/ε₀  (Poisson)   u_xx + u_yy = 0  (Laplace)With a source ρ it is Poisson's equation; with no source it reduces to Laplace's equation.
Common mistakeDo not assume "more time derivatives" means "more dynamic." It is the combination of derivatives that matters. The heat equation (one time derivative, u_t) and the wave equation (two time derivatives, u_tt) look almost identical on paper, yet one smooths features away forever and the other carries them across the room unchanged. Always classify by the discriminant before you reach for a solver — picking a wave-style scheme for a diffusion problem (or vice versa) gives nonsense or instability.

1.4Initial-Value vs Boundary-Value Problems

Knowing the PDE is only half the story; a PDE has infinitely many solutions until you pin it down with side conditions. What kind of conditions you need depends directly on the type.

A PDE with a time variable (parabolic or hyperbolic) is an initial-value problem (IVP): you specify the field everywhere at the starting moment t = 0 and march it forward. Diffusion needs one initial condition u(x, 0); the wave equation, having two time derivatives, also needs the initial velocity u_t(x, 0). Whenever the domain has edges, you also supply boundary conditions in space (the temperature at the ends of the bar, the clamped ends of a string) — so most real problems are mixed initial-boundary-value problems.

An elliptic PDE has no time at all, so there is nothing to "march." It is a pure boundary-value problem (BVP): the solution in the interior is whatever value makes the field consistent with the conditions imposed all around the boundary, solved in one shot for the whole domain.

IVP: data at t = 0, integrate forward   |   BVP: data on the boundary, solve everywhere at onceParabolic and hyperbolic problems march in time; elliptic problems are solved globally.

Two boundary-condition flavors recur all course long: a Dirichlet condition fixes the value of u on the boundary (e.g., the ends of the bar are held at 0 °C); a Neumann condition fixes the derivative ∂u/∂n (e.g., an insulated end lets no heat flux through, so ∂u/∂x = 0). Choosing them correctly is what makes a simulation match the lab.

✦ Worked Example — classify a PDE and choose its side conditions

A vibrating beam model is proposed as 3·u_tt − 12·u_xx + 5·u_x = 0 on 0 ≤ x ≤ L. Classify it, and state what side conditions a well-posed simulation needs.

  1. Identify the principal part: the highest derivatives are u_tt and u_xx. Write it as A·u_xx + B·u_xt + C·u_tt. Here A = −12, B = 0, C = 3. (The 5·u_x term is lower order and does not affect type.)
  2. Compute the discriminant: D = B² − 4AC = 0² − 4·(−12)·(3) = 0 + 144 = 144.
  3. Classify: D = 144 > 0, so the PDE is hyperbolic — a wave-type equation, as expected for a vibrating beam.
  4. Wave speed: rewriting as u_tt = (12/3)·u_xx + … = 4·u_xx + …, we read c² = 4, so the disturbance speed is c = 2 (in the model's units).
  5. Side conditions: being hyperbolic with two time derivatives, it is an initial-value problem needing two initial conditions — the initial shape u(x, 0) and the initial velocity u_t(x, 0) — plus a boundary condition at each end (e.g. clamped ends u(0, t) = u(L, t) = 0). That is exactly enough to make the problem well-posed.

First, a tiny classifier that turns the discriminant into a type. Then a side-by-side comparison: the same initial bump evolved one step by a diffusion rule and a wave rule, so you can see smoothing versus propagation.

# pde_types.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

def classify(A, B, C):
    D = B**2 - 4*A*C
    if D < 0:   return "elliptic  (equilibrium / Poisson-Laplace)"
    if D == 0:  return "parabolic (diffusion / heat)"
    return "hyperbolic (wave)"

print("heat   u_t = a u_xx ->", classify(1, 0, 0))   # parabolic
print("wave   c^2 u_xx - u_tt ->", classify(1, 0, -1)) # hyperbolic
print("Laplace u_xx + u_yy   ->", classify(1, 0, 1))   # elliptic

# --- one step of diffusion vs. wave on the same bump ---
N = 200; dx = 1.0/N
x = np.linspace(0, 1, N)
u = np.exp(-((x-0.5)**2)/0.001)         # a sharp Gaussian bump

lap = np.zeros_like(u)
lap[1:-1] = (u[2:] - 2*u[1:-1] + u[:-2]) / dx**2   # u_xx

dt = 0.2*dx**2                          # small, stable time step
u_diffuse = u + dt*lap                  # heat: u_t = u_xx  -> bump SHRINKS
c = 1.0
u_wave    = u + (c*dt)**2 * lap         # wave (1st half-step): bump KEEPS shape, will split

print("peak start  :", u.max())
print("peak diffuse:", u_diffuse.max())  # lower: smoothing
print("peak wave   :", u_wave.max())      # ~unchanged: propagation

Plot all three arrays with Matplotlib (plt.plot(x, u); plt.plot(x, u_diffuse); plt.plot(x, u_wave)). Predict before you run: which curve loses height? Which keeps it? You are watching the parabolic/hyperbolic distinction in a single line of code each.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The discriminant of a linear second-order PDE is D = B² − 4AC. A PDE with D < 0 is classified as…
A. hyperbolic
B. parabolic
C. elliptic
D. nonlinear
Show answer

C. D < 0 is elliptic (like Laplace's equation). D > 0 is hyperbolic (A) and D = 0 is parabolic (B). The sign of D says nothing about linearity, so (D) is unrelated.

Q2. Which equation is the archetype of a parabolic PDE?
A. u_t = α·u_xx (heat / diffusion)
B. u_tt = c²·u_xx (wave)
C. u_xx + u_yy = 0 (Laplace)
D. u_x + u_t = 0 (linear advection, first order)
Show answer

A. The heat equation has A = α, C = 0, B = 0, so D = 0 — parabolic. The wave equation (B) is hyperbolic, Laplace (C) is elliptic, and advection (D) is first order, so the second-order classification does not even apply.

Q3. A key qualitative feature of hyperbolic PDEs (like the wave equation) is that…
A. sharp features are immediately smoothed away
B. disturbances propagate at a finite speed and keep their shape
C. there is no time dependence at all
D. the solution depends only on the source term, not on initial data
Show answer

B. Hyperbolic equations carry information at finite speed c along characteristics with little or no smoothing. Smoothing (A) is the parabolic/diffusion hallmark; no time dependence (C) describes elliptic equations; (D) describes nothing here — initial data is exactly what hyperbolic problems propagate.

Q4. An elliptic PDE such as Laplace's equation u_xx + u_yy = 0 is most naturally posed as a…
A. initial-value problem marched forward in time
B. problem needing both an initial value and an initial velocity
C. problem with no conditions at all
D. boundary-value problem solved over the whole domain at once
Show answer

D. Elliptic equations have no time variable, so there is nothing to march; the interior is fixed entirely by data on the boundary, solved globally. (A) and (B) describe time-dependent parabolic/hyperbolic problems; (C) is wrong because a unique solution still requires boundary conditions.

Q5. The wave equation needs two initial conditions — u(x,0) and u_t(x,0) — whereas the heat equation needs only one, because…
A. the wave equation has a second-order time derivative (u_tt), while the heat equation has only first order (u_t)
B. the wave equation is nonlinear
C. the heat equation has no boundary conditions
D. waves travel faster than heat diffuses
Show answer

A. The number of initial conditions in time matches the order of the highest time derivative: u_tt (second order) needs two, u_t (first order) needs one. The wave equation is linear, so (B) is false; the heat equation does have boundary conditions, so (C) is false; relative speed (D) is irrelevant to how many initial conditions are required.

✓ What you learned

  • A PDE is a rule relating a field u(x, t) to its partial derivatives — the language of continuum physics.
  • The discriminant D = B² − 4AC sorts linear second-order PDEs into elliptic (D < 0), parabolic (D = 0), and hyperbolic (D > 0).
  • Each type behaves differently: parabolic smooths, hyperbolic propagates at finite speed, elliptic gives a global equilibrium set by boundaries.
  • Physical archetypes: the heat equation, the wave equation, and Poisson/Laplace.
  • Time-dependent types are initial-value problems (march forward); elliptic types are boundary-value problems (solve all at once), with Dirichlet and Neumann conditions.
