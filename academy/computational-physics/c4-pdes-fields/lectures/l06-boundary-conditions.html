Week 6 · Lecture 6

Boundary Conditions

A PDE only knows what happens in the interior — it's the edges of your grid that decide whether the answer is a heat sink, a perfect insulator, or a wraparound universe.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the physical meaning of Dirichlet (fixed value) and Neumann (fixed flux) boundary conditions.
  • Implement Neumann conditions accurately using ghost points without losing the interior scheme's order.
  • Show how boundary conditions modify the rows of the update matrix in a discretized PDE.
  • Implement periodic boundaries and explain when a "wraparound" domain is the right model.
  • Verify boundary treatment in NumPy and recognize how a sloppy edge can destroy an accurate interior.

6.1The Boundary Is Half the Problem

In Weeks 1–5 we learned to discretize the interior of a domain: replace the Laplacian with a finite-difference stencil, march forward in time, control stability with the CFL number. But a partial differential equation by itself is under-determined. The heat equation ut = α uxx has infinitely many solutions; choosing one requires telling the solver what happens at the edges of the grid.

Physically this makes sense. "How does this metal rod cool?" is unanswerable until you say what the ends do: are they clamped to ice water, wrapped in perfect insulation, or joined into a ring? Each answer is a different boundary condition (BC), and each produces a completely different solution from the same interior equation.

The two most common BC types name what you specify on the boundary:

Dirichlet:  u(boundary) = g      Neumann:  du/dn (boundary) = qDirichlet fixes the field value; Neumann fixes the normal derivative (the flux through the boundary).

A Dirichlet condition pins the value: the rod's end is held at 0 deg C. A Neumann condition pins the slope, i.e. the flux: an insulated end lets no heat through, so du/dx = 0 there. (A blend of the two, a·u + b·du/dn = c, is a Robin condition — convective cooling, for example — but we focus on the two pure types here.)

AnalogyThink of the grid as a relay race where each interior point passes information to its neighbors. Dirichlet is a runner whose position is bolted to the ground — it never moves no matter what its neighbors do. Neumann controls the handoff rate at the end of the track: an insulated end (du/dn = 0) is a wall that bounces the baton straight back, sending no flux out of the domain.

6.2Dirichlet Is Easy — Neumann Needs Ghost Points

Imagine a 1-D grid of N+1 nodes, u0, u1, …, uN, with spacing h. A Dirichlet condition is almost trivial: just overwrite the boundary node with the prescribed value, u0 = g, every step. The interior scheme only touches u1…uN-1, and the known u0 feeds into the stencil for u1 as a constant.

Neumann is subtler. We must enforce a derivative at the edge, but at node 0 there is no point to the left to form a centered difference. The naive fix — a one-sided difference (u1 − u0)/h = q — is only first-order accurate, and will quietly drag a beautiful second-order interior down to first order.

The clean trick is a ghost point: invent a fictitious node u-1 just outside the domain. Now we can write a centered (second-order) approximation of the boundary derivative:

(u1 - u-1) / (2h) = q  ==>   u-1 = u1 - 2 h qFor an insulated end q = 0, this gives u(-1) = u(1): the field mirrors across the boundary.

We substitute this expression for the ghost into the interior stencil applied at the boundary node itself. The ghost is never stored as an unknown — it is eliminated algebraically, leaving a modified equation for the real boundary node that is still second-order. For the insulated case (q = 0) the picture is a mirror: whatever sits at u1 is reflected to the phantom u-1.

Common mistakeUsing the one-sided difference (u1 - u0)/h = q for a Neumann condition. It "works" (the code runs) but it is only O(h) accurate. Bolted onto a second-order interior, the global error stalls at first order and your convergence study flatlines. Always use the ghost-point (centered) form, or a second-order one-sided formula, to preserve the interior's order.

6.3How Boundaries Enter the Update Matrix

Most PDE solvers reduce to a matrix equation. An implicit step (Week 5) or a steady-state Poisson solve looks like A u = b, where A encodes the discrete Laplacian. The interior rows of A all look the same — the familiar tridiagonal [1, -2, 1]/h2 stencil. The boundary rows are where the BCs live, and they look different.

For a steady 1-D diffusion problem -uxx = f on N+1 nodes, the interior row i is

(-u(i-1) + 2 u(i) - u(i+1)) / h^2 = f(i)The standard second-difference stencil, valid for interior nodes i = 1 .. N-1.

A Dirichlet node is forced. Its matrix row becomes the identity row [0 … 1 … 0] with right-hand side equal to the prescribed value: row 0 reads u0 = g. A Neumann node folds the ghost into the stencil. Substituting u-1 = u1 - 2hq into the boundary stencil collapses the two u1 terms, giving a modified diagonal and a known term that moves to b:

(2 u(0) - 2 u(1)) / h^2 = f(0) + 2 q / hThe Neumann row: coefficient on u(1) doubles, and the flux q is absorbed into the right-hand side.

This is why the slogan "the boundary is half the problem" is literal at the matrix level: get one boundary row wrong and you have solved a different linear system — often one that is singular (a pure-Neumann Poisson problem has no unique solution until you pin one value or fix the mean).

6.4Periodic Boundaries: a Universe That Wraps Around

Sometimes the right model has no physical edge. A wave on a ring, turbulence in a representative cube of fluid, or electron density in a crystal lattice all want the domain to repeat seamlessly. A periodic boundary condition glues the last node back to the first:

u(N) = u(0)  and   u(N+1) = u(1),  u(-1) = u(N-1)The grid becomes a loop: the neighbor "off the right edge" is the first interior node, and vice versa.

In code this is the cleanest BC of all — np.roll shifts the whole array and wraps automatically, so the same stencil applies everywhere with no special boundary rows. In the matrix picture, periodicity adds two corner entries to A (top-right and bottom-left), turning the tridiagonal matrix into a circulant one. Those corners are exactly why periodic problems are diagonalized by the discrete Fourier transform — the foundation of the spectral methods you will meet later.

AnalogyA periodic domain is the classic arcade game where flying off the right edge brings you back on the left. There is no wall to bounce off and nothing leaks out; the "world" is a loop. Physically it is the honest choice when you simulate a small patch of something effectively infinite and uniform — you don't want artificial walls injecting reflections into your data.

Be careful counting nodes: with periodicity, uN and u0 are the same point, so you store only N unique values, not N+1. Double-counting that shared node is a classic off-by-one bug that shifts your wave speed or leaks energy.

✦ Worked Example — assembling a mixed-BC Poisson system

Solve -uxx = 0 on [0, 1] with 5 nodes (h = 0.25). Left end is Dirichlet u(0) = 100 (a hot clamp); right end is insulated, Neumann du/dx = 0 at x = 1. Find the steady temperature profile by hand.

  1. Set up the grid: nodes at x = 0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 1.0, values u0..u4, spacing h = 0.25.
  2. Left BC (Dirichlet): row 0 is the identity row, u0 = 100.
  3. Interior rows (i = 1,2,3): -u(i-1) + 2u(i) - u(i+1) = 0 (since f = 0 and h2 cancels).
  4. Right BC (Neumann, q = 0): ghost u5 = u3. The stencil at node 4 is -u3 + 2u4 - u5 = 0, and substituting the ghost gives 2u4 - 2u3 = 0, i.e. u4 = u3.
  5. Solve the chain: the interior equations say each value is the average of its neighbors, so u is linear; the insulated end forces zero slope. The only solution with u0 = 100 and zero slope at the right is the flat profile u = 100 everywhere. Physically exact: with one end held hot and the other perfectly insulated and no source, heat fills the rod uniformly to 100 deg.
  6. Sanity check: swap the right BC to Dirichlet u4 = 0 and the same system gives the straight line u(x) = 100(1 - x) — the classic conduction gradient. Same interior, different boundary, completely different field.

🛠️ Hands-On: three boundaries, one solver

Uses the free Python + NumPy / Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the install details block lives in the early lectures) — no new tools needed here. We build the 1-D Poisson matrix and swap only the boundary rows to see Dirichlet, Neumann, and periodic in action.

# boundary_conditions.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np

N = 50                       # interior + boundary nodes
h = 1.0 / (N - 1)
x = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, N)
f = np.zeros(N)              # source term (-u'' = f); zero here

# --- interior second-difference stencil [-1, 2, -1] ---
A = (np.diag(2*np.ones(N)) - np.diag(np.ones(N-1), 1)
     - np.diag(np.ones(N-1), -1)) / h**2
b = f.copy()

# --- LEFT: Dirichlet u(0) = 100 (overwrite row 0) ---
A[0, :] = 0.0; A[0, 0] = 1.0; b[0] = 100.0

# --- RIGHT: Neumann du/dx = 0 via ghost u[N] = u[N-2] ---
A[-1, :] = 0.0
A[-1, -1] = 2.0 / h**2       # 2*u(N-1)
A[-1, -2] = -2.0 / h**2      # -2*u(N-2)  (ghost folded in)
b[-1] = f[-1] + 2.0 * 0.0 / h    # flux q = 0

u = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
print("u[0], u[-1] =", u[0], u[-1])   # 100.0  100.0  (flat: insulated end)

# --- PERIODIC version: circulant matrix, no special rows ---
Ap = (np.diag(2*np.ones(N)) - np.diag(np.ones(N-1), 1)
      - np.diag(np.ones(N-1), -1)) / h**2
Ap[0, -1]  = -1.0 / h**2     # wrap left neighbor of node 0
Ap[-1, 0]  = -1.0 / h**2     # wrap right neighbor of node N-1
# (pure-periodic Poisson is singular -> pin the mean or add a source)

Try it: change the Neumann row to the naive one-sided form A[-1,-1]=1/h; A[-1,-2]=-1/h and run a convergence study against the exact solution. You will watch the global error drop from second order to first — the cost of a sloppy boundary.

🔗 Where this is heading

Boundary conditions only get richer from here. Next week the wave equation needs non-reflecting (absorbing) boundaries so outgoing waves don't bounce back and contaminate the domain — the same idea behind the perfectly matched layers used in electromagnetics and seismic imaging. In 2-D and 3-D field solvers the boundary becomes a curve or surface, and the ghost-point idea generalizes to immersed-boundary and finite-volume flux methods. The circulant structure of periodic BCs is the doorway to spectral methods later in this course, where the discrete Fourier transform turns derivatives into multiplications. And in the Plasma and Quantum tracks, periodic boxes are the standard cell for simulating bulk matter.

💬 Discussion

A colleague is simulating heat in a long metal bar and reports: "My interior scheme is fully second-order — I verified it against an exact solution on an infinite domain — but on my finite bar the convergence study stubbornly shows first-order accuracy, and the temperature near the insulated right end is always a little off." Nothing in the interior loop has changed. What is the most likely cause, how would you confirm it, and what is the fix? More broadly: why can a boundary touched by only one or two grid points dominate the accuracy of a scheme that runs over thousands of interior points?

Show a model response

The symptom — globally first-order convergence despite a verified second-order interior, with the largest error parked at the insulated (Neumann) end — points straight at the boundary discretization. The most likely cause is a one-sided, first-order approximation of the zero-flux condition: (u[1]-u[0])/h = 0. Even though it touches only one node, its O(h) error feeds into the solution every step and propagates inward, so the global error inherits the worst order anywhere in the domain — a scheme is only as accurate as its weakest piece.

To confirm it: run a refinement study and tabulate the error ratio as h halves. A clean second-order method quarters the error (ratio ~4); first order only halves it (ratio ~2). Then plot the spatial error and check whether it is concentrated at the boundary node — if it is, the interior is exonerated. The fix is to enforce the Neumann condition with a centered, ghost-point form, u(-1) = u(1) - 2hq, folded into the boundary stencil (or a second-order one-sided formula). After the fix the ratio should snap back to ~4.

Why a one- or two-node boundary can dominate: order of accuracy is a statement about the maximum error over the domain, and errors do not stay local — they diffuse/advect inward through the same stencil that couples the interior. A first-order leak at the edge therefore caps the whole solution at first order, no matter how fine and elegant the interior is.

❓ Lecture 6 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A perfectly insulated end of a heat-conducting rod is modeled by which boundary condition?
A. Dirichlet, u = 0
B. Neumann, du/dn = 0 (zero flux)
C. Periodic, u(N) = u(0)
D. Robin, u + du/dn = 1
Show answer

B. Insulation means no heat flux crosses the end, so the normal derivative is zero — a homogeneous Neumann condition. A (Dirichlet) fixes a temperature, not a flux; C is a wraparound model with no real edge; D mixes value and flux (convective cooling), which an insulator does not.

Q2. Why is a ghost point preferred over the one-sided difference (u1 - u0)/h for a Neumann condition?
A. It uses less memory
B. It makes the matrix symmetric in every case
C. It gives a second-order (centered) boundary derivative, preserving the interior's order of accuracy
D. It is required for the scheme to be stable
Show answer

C. The one-sided form is only O(h); the ghost lets you write a centered O(h^2) derivative so the global order isn't dragged down. A is false (you eliminate the ghost algebraically — no extra storage); B is not generally true; D confuses accuracy with stability — the one-sided form can still be stable, just less accurate.

Q3. In the matrix A u = b for a steady diffusion problem, a Dirichlet node corresponds to which kind of row?
A. An identity row [0 ... 1 ... 0] with the prescribed value in b
B. A row with a doubled off-diagonal entry
C. A row with extra corner entries far from the diagonal
D. A row of all zeros
Show answer

A. Forcing u = g means the row simply reads u = g, i.e. a 1 on the diagonal and g in b. B describes the Neumann row (ghost folded in); C describes the periodic (circulant) corners; D would make the system singular.

Q4. Periodic boundary conditions turn the tridiagonal Laplacian matrix into a circulant matrix by…
A. setting the first and last rows to identity rows
B. adding nonzero entries in the top-right and bottom-left corners
C. doubling every diagonal entry
D. removing the off-diagonal entries
Show answer

B. Wrapping the grid into a loop means node 0's left neighbor is node N-1 and vice versa, placing entries in the two far corners (circulant structure — diagonalized by the DFT). A is Dirichlet; C and D would change the physics entirely and break the second-difference stencil.

Q5. A steady Poisson problem -uxx = f with Neumann conditions on both ends has what issue?
A. It always has a unique solution
B. It is unconditionally unstable
C. It cannot be written as a matrix system
D. The solution is non-unique (defined only up to an additive constant) unless you pin a value or fix the mean
Show answer

D. Only derivatives are constrained, so adding any constant to a solution gives another solution — the matrix is singular. You must anchor one value or fix the mean. A is the opposite of the truth; B confuses a steady solve with time stability; C is false — it is perfectly expressible as A u = b, just with a singular A.

✓ What you learned

  • A PDE is under-determined; boundary conditions select the unique physical solution from the same interior equation.
  • Dirichlet fixes the value (just overwrite the node); Neumann fixes the flux and needs a ghost point for second-order accuracy.
  • Boundary conditions live in the boundary rows of the update matrix: an identity row (Dirichlet) or a folded-ghost row with a doubled off-diagonal (Neumann).
  • Periodic boundaries wrap the domain into a loop, producing a circulant matrix and modeling effectively infinite, uniform media.
  • A sloppy boundary caps the global order of accuracy — the boundary really is half the problem.
