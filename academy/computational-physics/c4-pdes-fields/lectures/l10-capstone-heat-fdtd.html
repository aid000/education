Week 10 · Lecture 10

Capstone: 2D Heat Diffusion or FDTD EM Pulse

Ten weeks of stencils, stability conditions, and boundary conditions converge here — into one simulation you build, verify, and defend with a conserved quantity that proves it works.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Choose between the two capstone tracks — 2D heat diffusion on a plate, or an FDTD electromagnetic pulse — and lay out the grid and update for either.
  • Apply mixed boundary conditions (Dirichlet, Neumann, absorbing) correctly and explain what each one physically enforces.
  • State and check the explicit stability condition for your chosen scheme before trusting any output.
  • Track a conserved or monotonic diagnostic — total heat / monotone energy decay for diffusion, or field energy for FDTD — as your primary correctness test.
  • Assemble the diagnostics into a defensible deliverable: convergence, stability, and conservation, presented as evidence.

10.1The Capstone: Two Tracks, One Skill Set

Everything in this course was a rehearsal. You learned to classify PDEs (10.1), discretize space on a grid (10.2), march the heat equation explicitly (10.3), respect the CFL limit (10.4), upgrade to implicit Crank–Nicolson (10.5), and impose boundary conditions (10.6), before turning to wave and Maxwell systems and the leapfrog FDTD update. The capstone asks you to stand up one complete, verified field simulation end to end — and to prove it is right rather than merely assert it.

You pick one of two tracks. Both live on a regular grid, both march in time, and both rise or fall on the same three pillars: a correct stencil, a respected stability condition, and a conserved or monotonic diagnostic.

Track A — 2D Heat Diffusion on a Plate

Solve the parabolic heat equation on a square plate with mixed boundaries: hot edge (Dirichlet), insulated edge (Neumann), and so on. The physics is irreversible — heat spreads and smooths. Your monotonic diagnostic is the discrete thermal energy, which can only decay (with insulated walls, total heat is exactly conserved).

∂u/∂t = alpha (∂2u/∂x2 + ∂2u/∂y2)2D heat equation. u(x,y,t) is temperature; alpha is thermal diffusivity. Parabolic: smoothing, irreversible, infinite signal speed.

Track B — FDTD Electromagnetic Pulse (1D or 2D)

Solve Maxwell's curl equations with the Yee leapfrog scheme: E and H staggered in space and time. A launched pulse propagates, reflects off boundaries (or is swallowed by an absorbing boundary), and your diagnostic is the total electromagnetic field energy — conserved in a lossless, perfectly reflecting box.

∂E/∂t = (1/eps0) ∂H/∂x,   ∂H/∂t = (1/mu0) ∂E/∂x1D Maxwell (Ez, Hy). Hyperbolic: a pulse travels at speed c = 1/sqrt(eps0 mu0) with no smoothing — energy sloshes between E and H.
AnalogyTrack A is a drop of ink in still water: it blurs outward, never sharpens, and the total amount of ink is fixed if the cup is sealed. Track B is a clap echoing in a tiled room: the sound keeps its shape as it travels, bounces off walls, and the total acoustic energy stays put until something absorbs it. Diffusion forgets the past; waves remember it — and your diagnostic (decaying heat vs. constant energy) is exactly that difference made measurable.

10.2Mixed Boundary Conditions: What Each One Enforces

A PDE only becomes a well-posed problem once the edges are pinned down. The capstone deliberately mixes boundary types so you must implement each one correctly — a single wrong edge silently poisons the whole interior.

For the heat plate (Track A)

Dirichlet fixes the value: u = Thot on an edge held at a known temperature (a heated rail). You overwrite those boundary nodes every step. Neumann fixes the flux: an insulated wall means no heat crosses it, so the normal derivative is zero. The clean discrete trick is a ghost node mirror — set the value just outside the wall equal to the value just inside, so the gradient across the wall is exactly zero.

insulated wall:  ∂u/∂n = 0  =>  u[ghost] = u[interior]The ghost-node mirror makes the one-sided second difference at the wall reduce to a no-flux condition: heat reflects rather than leaks.

For the FDTD pulse (Track B)

A perfect electric conductor (PEC) wall forces the tangential E field to zero (E = 0) — a perfectly reflecting mirror; energy is conserved in the box. An absorbing boundary condition (ABC) instead lets the pulse leave without reflecting, mimicking open space. The simplest one-way (Mur first-order) update sets the boundary field to the value its inward neighbor had one step earlier, since the pulse travels exactly one cell per step at the Courant limit.

Common mistakeUpdating the boundary nodes inside the same loop that uses the interior stencil. The stencil at an edge reaches for neighbors that do not exist (or are stale). Always update the interior with the full stencil first, then apply boundary conditions as a separate pass that overwrites or mirrors the edges. Mixing the two — or forgetting to apply a Neumann ghost every step — produces a result that looks plausible but quietly violates conservation. Your energy/heat diagnostic is what catches it.

10.3Stability: the Non-Negotiable Check

An explicit scheme is only conditionally stable. Exceed the limit and the solution does not just lose accuracy — it explodes into oscillating infinities within a few steps. You verify the condition before running, and you build it into the code so the time step is derived from the grid, never guessed.

Track A — the 2D diffusion limit

Von Neumann analysis of the explicit (FTCS) heat scheme on a square grid (dx = dy = h) gives the diffusion number limit:

alpha dt (1/dx2 + 1/dy2) <= 1/2  =>   dt <= h2 / (4 alpha)The classic 2D explicit-diffusion bound. Halving the grid spacing forces dt down by 4x — the price of explicit stability.

Track B — the Courant (CFL) limit

For FDTD the signal travels at c, and the Courant condition demands the numerical domain of dependence contain the physical one. In nD with equal spacing:

c dt sqrt( n_dim ) / h <= 1  (1D: c dt <= h;  2D: c dt <= h/sqrt(2))The Courant number S = c dt / h must satisfy S <= 1/sqrt(n_dim). Run exactly at the limit in 1D and the pulse advects one cell per step with near-zero dispersion.
AnalogyThink of the time step as a stride length and information as a walker crossing your grid. The CFL condition says: never step so far that the walker leaps clean over a cell without the stencil "seeing" it. If your stride outruns the physics, the scheme is blind to what it is modeling — and a blind explicit scheme does not stand still, it falls over (blows up).

10.4Diagnostics: Tracking the Conserved Quantity

The single most important habit this course teaches: a simulation is not trusted until a physical invariant confirms it. Pretty color maps lie; conserved quantities don't. For each track there is a natural scalar to log every step and plot against time.

Track A — total heat and monotone energy

The discrete total heat is the sum of temperature over all cells times cell area. With insulated (all-Neumann) walls it must be conserved to round-off. With any heat-removing (Dirichlet) edge, total heat instead decreases monotonically toward steady state, and the "thermal energy" surrogate (sum of u2) decays — never rises. A rising diagnostic is an instant red flag.

Q(t) = sum_(i,j) u_(i,j) dx dy  ->  dQ/dt = (net flux through boundary)Insulated box: dQ/dt = 0 exactly. With a cold/hot Dirichlet edge: Q drifts monotonically toward the steady-state value set by the boundaries.

Track B — electromagnetic energy

The field energy is the integral of the energy density. In a lossless PEC box it is conserved (it oscillates by a tiny bounded amount from the staggered leapfrog, but does not drift); with an ABC it should decay as the pulse exits, asymptoting to zero.

U(t) = (1/2) sum ( eps0 E2 + mu0 H2 ) dxTotal EM energy. PEC box: bounded, no secular drift. ABC: monotone decay to ~0 as the pulse radiates out. Secular growth = stability violated.
✦ Worked Example — sizing and sanity-checking a heat-plate run

You build Track A on a 1 m x 1 m steel plate, alpha = 1.0x10-5 m2/s, with a 100 x 100 grid. Left edge held at 100 C (Dirichlet), the other three insulated (Neumann). Pick a stable dt, estimate the run length, and state the conservation check.

  1. Grid spacing: h = 1 m / (100 - 1) ≈ 1.01x10-2 m, so h2 ≈ 1.02x10-4 m2.
  2. Stability cap: dt <= h2/(4 alpha) = 1.02x10-4 / (4 x 1.0x10-5) ≈ 2.55 s. Use a safety factor 0.9: dt = 2.3 s.
  3. Diffusion time scale: the plate equilibrates on tau ≈ L2/alpha = 1 / 1.0x10-5 = 1.0x105 s. To reach near-steady state you need roughly tau/dt ≈ 1.0x105/2.3 ≈ 4.3x104 steps. (Explicit diffusion is step-hungry — this is exactly why Crank–Nicolson from 10.5 is the production choice.)
  4. Conservation check (insulated subcase): if you temporarily insulate all four edges, total heat Q = sum(u) x h2 must stay constant to ~10-12 relative. Log it every 100 steps; a drift means a boundary is leaking.
  5. Monotone check (mixed BCs): with the 100 C Dirichlet edge active, heat flows in, so Q rises monotonically toward the steady profile (a linear ramp from the hot edge). The energy surrogate relaxes smoothly with no overshoot. If dt is set to 3.0 s (above the 2.55 s cap), the field instead develops a checkerboard that doubles in amplitude each step — the unmistakable explicit-diffusion blow-up.

🛠️ Hands-On: the capstone skeleton (Track A, with Track B drop-in)

This runs on the same free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 of C1 (the one-time install lives in that lecture's setup box). Numba is optional and only worth it if you push to large grids or long FDTD runs. Below is a complete, runnable Track-A core with the stability check and the conservation diagnostic wired in — exactly the structure your deliverable should have.

# capstone_heat.py — 2D explicit heat diffusion, mixed BCs + diagnostics
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

L, N = 1.0, 100
alpha = 1.0e-5
h  = L / (N - 1)
dt = 0.9 * h**2 / (4 * alpha)          # derive dt from the stability cap
assert alpha * dt * 2 / h**2 <= 0.5, "stability violated!"   # verify BEFORE running

u = np.zeros((N, N))                    # initial plate at 0 C
T_hot = 100.0

def step(u):
    un = u.copy()
    # interior: explicit 5-point Laplacian (vectorized)
    un[1:-1,1:-1] = u[1:-1,1:-1] + alpha*dt/h**2 * (
        u[2:,1:-1] + u[:-2,1:-1] + u[1:-1,2:] + u[1:-1,:-2] - 4*u[1:-1,1:-1])
    # boundary conditions, applied as a SEPARATE pass
    un[:, 0]  = T_hot                   # left edge: Dirichlet 100 C
    un[:, -1] = un[:, -2]               # right: Neumann (ghost mirror)
    un[0, :]  = un[1, :]                # top: insulated
    un[-1, :] = un[-2, :]               # bottom: insulated
    return un

heat = []
for n in range(40000):
    u = step(u)
    if n % 100 == 0:
        heat.append(u.sum() * h**2)     # total-heat diagnostic Q(t)

plt.subplot(1,2,1); plt.imshow(u, cmap='inferno'); plt.title('T(x,y)')
plt.subplot(1,2,2); plt.plot(heat);  plt.title('total heat Q(t) -> steady')
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('capstone.png', dpi=120)
print("final Q:", heat[-1], "  monotone increasing:", np.all(np.diff(heat) >= -1e-9))

For Track B, swap the core for the 1D Yee leapfrog: Ez[1:] -= (dt/(eps0*h))*(Hy[1:]-Hy[:-1]) then Hy[:-1] -= (dt/(mu0*h))*(Ez[1:]-Ez[:-1]), set dt = 0.999*h/c (Courant), log U = 0.5*(eps0*Ez**2 + mu0*Hy**2).sum()*h, and try a PEC wall (Ez[0]=Ez[-1]=0) versus a Mur ABC. Predict whether U is flat or decaying before you plot it.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. On your 2D heat plate with grid spacing dx = dy = h, the explicit (FTCS) scheme is stable provided…
A. dt <= h2 / (4 alpha)
B. dt <= h / c
C. dt <= h2 / alpha (no factor)
D. any dt — explicit diffusion is unconditionally stable
Show answer

A. The 2D von Neumann bound is alpha dt (1/dx2+1/dy2) <= 1/2, which with dx=dy=h gives dt <= h2/(4 alpha). B is the CFL (wave) condition — wrong physics. C drops the factor 4 and would blow up. D is false; only implicit schemes (Crank–Nicolson) are unconditionally stable.

Q2. You insulate all four edges of the heat plate. Which diagnostic confirms the simulation is correct?
A. the temperature at the center rises forever
B. total heat Q = sum(u)·h2 stays constant to round-off
C. the maximum temperature keeps increasing each step
D. total heat decays monotonically to zero
Show answer

B. A fully insulated (all-Neumann) box exchanges no heat with its surroundings, so total heat is conserved exactly (to round-off). A and C describe growth that should never happen with no source. D is the behavior for a heat-removing Dirichlet edge, not an insulated box.

Q3. In your 1D FDTD pulse, what is the correct order of operations within a single time step?
A. update E and H simultaneously from the same old arrays
B. update interior with the stencil and boundaries in the same expression
C. update the interior fields first, then apply boundary conditions as a separate pass
D. apply boundary conditions first, then never touch the interior
Show answer

C. The interior stencil must run first, then boundaries are imposed as a separate overwrite/mirror pass — otherwise the edge stencil reaches for nonexistent or stale neighbors. A is wrong: the Yee scheme is a staggered leapfrog (update E, then H using the just-updated E). B is exactly the common mistake from this lecture. D never advances the physics.

Q4. A perfect-electric-conductor (PEC) wall in FDTD enforces the tangential E field to zero. Physically, what does it model and what should your energy diagnostic do?
A. open space; energy decays to zero
B. a perfect mirror; energy stays bounded with no secular drift
C. a heat sink; temperature relaxes to steady state
D. a source; energy grows linearly in time
Show answer

B. Forcing tangential E = 0 is a perfectly reflecting mirror, so in a lossless box total EM energy is conserved (bounded, no drift). A describes an absorbing boundary (ABC), not a PEC. C confuses tracks — that's the heat problem. D would require an energy source, which a passive wall is not.

Q5. Halfway through grading you double the resolution of your heat plate (halve h). To stay stable with the explicit scheme, the time step must shrink by about a factor of…
A. 2
B. sqrt(2)
C. unchanged — dt is independent of h
D. 4
Show answer

D. Since dt <= h2/(4 alpha), dt scales as h2; halving h cuts the allowed dt by 4, so a 2x finer grid costs roughly 4x more steps per unit time (and 8x total work in 2D). A and B mistake h2 for a linear or sqrt dependence; C ignores the stability bound entirely.

  • The capstone has two tracks — a 2D heat plate (parabolic, smoothing) or an FDTD pulse (hyperbolic, propagating) — sharing one skill set: stencil, stability, diagnostic.

  • To verify the stability condition first — dt <= h2/(4 alpha) for 2D diffusion, the Courant limit c·dt·sqrt(n)/h <= 1 for FDTD — and derive dt from the grid.
  • To track a conserved or monotonic diagnostic: total heat Q(t) (flat if insulated, monotone otherwise) or field energy U(t) (bounded in a PEC box, decaying with an ABC).
  • To package convergence, stability, and conservation into a defensible deliverable — a verified solver with a receipt, not a pretty picture.
