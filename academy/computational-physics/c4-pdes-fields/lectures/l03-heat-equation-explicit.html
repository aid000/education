Week 3 · Lecture 3

The Diffusion (Heat) Equation — Explicit Scheme

Heat doesn't decide where to go — every point just keeps nudging toward the average of its neighbors, and that single rule is enough to make a hot spot melt away into a smooth glow.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the 1D heat (diffusion) equation and explain what each term means physically.
  • Derive the explicit forward-time centered-space (FTCS) update from finite differences.
  • See how FTCS makes each grid point relax toward a weighted average of its neighbors, smoothing gradients.
  • Identify the diffusion number r = αΔt/Δx² as the single dial that controls a step.
  • Implement a 1D heat solver in NumPy and watch a hot spot spread and flatten over time.

3.1What the Heat Equation Actually Says

Drop a spoon into hot soup and the handle slowly warms; pour cream into coffee and the swirl blurs out. These are the same process — diffusion — and they obey one of the most important partial differential equations in all of physics, the heat (diffusion) equation. In one spatial dimension, with temperature u(x, t):

∂u/∂t = α · ∂²u/∂x²The rate of change in time equals the thermal diffusivity α times the spatial curvature of the temperature profile.

Read it slowly. The left side, ∂u/∂t, is how fast the temperature at a point is changing. The right side, ∂²u/∂x², is the curvature of the temperature profile — the second spatial derivative. The constant α > 0 (the thermal diffusivity, units m²/s) sets the timescale.

The deep insight is in the sign of the curvature. Where the profile is concave-up (a cold dip, curvature positive), ∂u/∂t is positive and the point warms. Where it is concave-down (a hot bump, curvature negative), the point cools. In both cases the point is being pulled toward the trend of its surroundings. Nothing in the equation points heat in a direction; the flow is entirely a consequence of curvature.

AnalogyPicture a row of people each holding a thermometer, told to constantly adjust their reading toward the average of their two neighbors. Nobody knows the global picture — each only watches the two people beside them. Yet a sharp spike of "hot" people in the middle will inexorably bleed outward and flatten. The heat equation is exactly this local averaging rule, taken to a continuous limit.

3.2Discretizing in Space and Time: the FTCS Update

We can't store a continuous u(x, t) on a computer, so (as in Week 2) we lay down a grid: positions xi = i·Δx and times tn = n·Δt. Write uin for the temperature at node i and time level n. Now replace each derivative with a finite difference.

For the time derivative we use a forward difference (we only know the present, and want the future):

∂u/∂t ≈ (uin+1 − uin) / ΔtForward in time: the unknown future value appears alone on the left — this is what makes the scheme explicit.

For the spatial second derivative we use the centered three-point stencil from Week 2:

∂²u/∂x² ≈ (ui+1n − 2uin + ui-1n) / Δx²Centered in space: it measures how far node i sits below the average of its two neighbors — exactly a discrete curvature.

Substituting both into ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x² and solving for the only future quantity, uin+1, gives the forward-time centered-space (FTCS) update. Defining the dimensionless diffusion number r = αΔt/Δx²:

uin+1 = uin + r · (ui+1n − 2uin + ui-1n)r = α·Δt/Δx². Every new value is computed only from old (time-level n) values — no system to solve.

This is called explicit because each new value uin+1 is written down directly from values you already have. There is no linear system to invert (that comes in a later lecture with implicit methods). You just sweep the grid once per time step.

3.3Why Gradients Smooth Out: Relaxing Toward the Average

The FTCS update hides a beautiful interpretation. Rearrange it by grouping the coefficients of each old value:

uin+1 = (1 − 2r)·uin + r·ui+1n + r·ui-1nA weighted average: keep a fraction of yourself, take a fraction r from each neighbor.

The three weights sum to (1 − 2r) + r + r = 1, so the new value is a genuine weighted average of the point and its two neighbors. Notice the special case r = ½: the center weight vanishes and the point becomes the exact average ½(ui+1 + ui-1) of its neighbors — it forgets its own value entirely.

Averaging is a smoothing operation. A sharp spike, which is far from the average of its neighbors, gets knocked down hard on the next step; a flat region, already equal to its neighborhood average, barely changes. Step after step, the bumps are sanded away and the profile relaxes toward something smooth. In frequency terms (a preview of the stability lecture), high-frequency wiggles are damped fastest — diffusion is a low-pass filter.

Common mistakeThe averaging picture only holds when all three weights are non-negative. If r > ½ the center weight (1 − 2r) goes negative, so a hot point overshoots into being colder than both neighbors — an unphysical oscillation that grows each step until the solution blows up. Choosing Δt too large for your Δx is the single most common way an explicit heat solver explodes. The exact threshold r ≤ ½ is the CFL-type stability condition we prove next week; for now, treat r ≤ ½ as a hard rule.

3.4Boundary & Initial Conditions Make It a Real Problem

The PDE alone is not enough — it describes the rule, not the situation. To pin down a unique solution we need an initial condition u(x, 0) (the starting temperature everywhere) and boundary conditions at the two ends of the rod for all time. Two common types:

  • Dirichlet — the value is fixed: e.g. the ends held at u = 0 (clamped to an ice bath). In code, you simply never update u[0] and u[-1].
  • Neumann — the flux (derivative) is fixed: e.g. ∂u/∂x = 0 means an insulated end, no heat leaving. In code, you mirror the neighbor: u[0] = u[1].

For a classic test, start with all the heat concentrated in the middle of a rod whose ends are held at zero. The interior averaging spreads the spike both ways; the fixed-zero ends drain heat out; and eventually the whole rod cools to zero. The total heat (the integral of u) decays — which is exactly what the physics demands and a good sanity check on your code.

∫ u(x, t) dx  decreases with t under Dirichlet ends; stays constant under insulated (Neumann) endsConservation check: insulated boundaries trap heat (sum of u constant); clamped boundaries let it escape.
✦ Worked Example — one FTCS step by hand

A rod is sampled at 5 nodes (i = 0..4) with Δx = 0.1 m, diffusivity α = 0.01 m²/s. The ends (i = 0 and i = 4) are held at 0. The current interior values are u₁ = 0, u₂ = 100, u₃ = 0 (a hot spike in the middle). Take Δt = 0.25 s and advance one step.

  1. Diffusion number: r = αΔt/Δx² = (0.01 · 0.25) / (0.1)² = 0.0025 / 0.01 = 0.25. Since r = 0.25 ≤ 0.5, the step is stable.
  2. Update node 1 (neighbors u₀ = 0, u₂ = 100): u₁ⁿ⁺¹ = u₁ + r(u₂ − 2u₁ + u₀) = 0 + 0.25(100 − 0 + 0) = 25.
  3. Update node 2 (neighbors u₁ = 0, u₃ = 0): u₂ⁿ⁺¹ = 100 + 0.25(0 − 200 + 0) = 100 − 50 = 50.
  4. Update node 3 (neighbors u₂ = 100, u₄ = 0): by symmetry with node 1, = 0 + 0.25(0 − 0 + 100) = 25.
  5. Read the result: the spike (0, 100, 0) became (25, 50, 25). The peak dropped by half and the heat spilled into the neighbors — the bump is already shorter and wider. The interior sum is unchanged this step (25+50+25 = 100), but the cold ends u₀ = u₄ = 0 now sit beside warm nodes, so on the next step heat leaks out through them and the rod steadily cools. Smoothing is visible after a single step.

🛠️ Hands-On: watch a hot spot spread

Everything runs on the free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack you installed in Week 1 (see the install details in Lecture 1 of C1 if you need it again). No new tools are required for this lecture.

We put a hot spike in the middle of a rod with ice-bath (Dirichlet zero) ends and march FTCS forward. NumPy lets us update the whole interior in one vectorized line — no Python loop over space.

# heat_ftcs.py — Lecture 3: explicit 1D heat equation
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- grid & physics ---
L      = 1.0                 # rod length (m)
nx     = 101                 # number of nodes
dx     = L / (nx - 1)
alpha  = 1.0e-2              # thermal diffusivity (m^2/s)

r      = 0.4                 # diffusion number r = alpha*dt/dx^2  (MUST be <= 0.5)
dt     = r * dx**2 / alpha   # pick dt to hit that r
nsteps = 4000

# --- initial condition: a hot spike in the middle, ends at 0 ---
x = np.linspace(0, L, nx)
u = np.zeros(nx)
u[(x > 0.45) & (x < 0.55)] = 100.0   # a hot block

snapshots = {}
for n in range(nsteps + 1):
    if n in (0, 100, 500, 2000, 4000):
        snapshots[n] = u.copy()
    # vectorized FTCS update of the INTERIOR nodes (1..nx-2)
    u[1:-1] = u[1:-1] + r * (u[2:] - 2*u[1:-1] + u[:-2])
    # Dirichlet ends stay 0 automatically (we never touch u[0], u[-1])

for n, snap in snapshots.items():
    plt.plot(x, snap, label=f"step {n}")
plt.xlabel("x (m)"); plt.ylabel("temperature"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

print("total heat at start:", snapshots[0].sum())
print("total heat at end:  ", u.sum())   # smaller — leaked out the cold ends

Run it: the sharp block sags into a smooth bell that widens and shrinks, and the printed total heat decays. Now set r = 0.6 and rerun — within a few dozen steps you'll see a jagged saw-tooth erupt and the values explode to nan. That instability is exactly what next week's CFL condition explains.

🔗 Where this is heading

FTCS is your first PDE marcher, and its limit — the r ≤ ½ ceiling — is the headline of next week's lecture on the CFL condition, where we prove the threshold with von Neumann stability analysis. To escape the punishing Δt ~ Δx² cost, implicit schemes (Crank–Nicolson) later in this course solve a tridiagonal system each step and stay stable for any Δt. The same averaging-of-neighbors idea generalizes to 2D and 3D fields (heat sinks on a chip, temperature in the ground) and, with a sign change, to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation in the Quantum track and to option-pricing PDEs in the Quant track. Master the explicit heat solver and you have the template for nearly every field simulation that follows.

💬 Discussion

The explicit FTCS scheme is the simplest possible PDE solver and it works beautifully — until it doesn't. Its stability ceiling, r ≤ ½, means r = αΔt/Δx², so if you halve Δx to get a finer, more accurate picture, the largest stable Δt drops by a factor of four, and you need four times as many steps to reach the same final time — eight times the total work. When is explicit FTCS still the right tool despite this cost, and when should you reach for an implicit method instead? Argue using a concrete scenario (think about how fine your grid is, how long you must simulate, and whether you care about the fast transient or only the slow steady state).

Show a model response

FTCS shines when the grid is coarse-to-moderate and you genuinely care about the fast transient — the early spreading of a hot spot. On a coarse grid Δx is not tiny, so the stable Δt is not punishingly small, and resolving the rapid early dynamics already forces small time steps anyway. In that regime FTCS is cheap, trivial to code, and embarrassingly parallel (each node only reads its neighbors), so it maps perfectly onto vectorized NumPy or a GPU.

Reach for an implicit method (Crank–Nicolson / backward Euler) when the grid is fine and you mostly want the long-time or steady-state answer. There the Δt ~ Δx² ceiling crushes you: a 10× finer grid means a 100× smaller stable Δt and 1000× more total work to reach a fixed final time. Implicit schemes are unconditionally stable, so you can take large time steps sized by accuracy, not stability, and solve a cheap tridiagonal system each step — far less total work despite the per-step cost. The trade-off is the local averaging rule: explicit looks only at the past (one matrix-free sweep), implicit couples all unknowns at the new time level (one linear solve).

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the heat equation ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x², the right-hand side ∂²u/∂x² physically represents…
A. the slope (first derivative) of the temperature profile
B. the curvature of the profile — how far a point sits below the average of its neighbors
C. the total heat stored in the rod
D. the speed at which a heat wave travels
Show answer

B. The second spatial derivative is the curvature; discretized, it measures how far a node lies below its neighbors' average, which drives the relaxation. The slope (A) is the first derivative and does not appear. (C) is the integral of u, and (D) misreads diffusion as wave propagation — diffusion has no fixed signal speed.

Q2. The FTCS scheme is called "explicit" because…
A. it uses the exact analytic solution
B. it is second-order accurate in time
C. each new value is written directly from known old (time-level n) values, with no system to solve
D. it can use any time step without going unstable
Show answer

C. "Explicit" means the future value is given by an explicit formula in already-known quantities — no linear solve. (A) is false (it's an approximation). (B) is false — FTCS is only first-order in time. (D) describes implicit schemes; explicit FTCS is conditionally stable (r ≤ ½).

Q3. Writing the FTCS update as a weighted average uin+1 = (1−2r)ui + r·ui+1 + r·ui-1, what is special about r = ½?
A. the point keeps its own value and ignores its neighbors
B. the scheme becomes implicit
C. the new value becomes the exact average of the two neighbors, forgetting its own value
D. diffusivity α drops out of the problem
Show answer

C. At r = ½ the center weight (1−2r) is zero, so uin+1 = ½(ui+1 + ui-1). (A) is the opposite. The scheme is still explicit (B false), and α is baked into r so it hasn't vanished (D false). r = ½ is also the stability ceiling.

Q4. A rod has Δx = 0.1 m and α = 0.01 m²/s. What is the largest stable time step Δt for explicit FTCS?
A. Δt = 0.5 s (using r = ½)
B. Δt = 5 s
C. Δt = 0.05 s
D. any Δt is fine
Show answer

A. Stability needs r = αΔt/Δx² ≤ ½, so Δt ≤ ½·Δx²/α = 0.5·(0.01)/(0.01) = 0.5 s. (B) gives r = 5 (blows up); (C) is stable but not the largest (r = 0.05); (D) is wrong — explicit FTCS is only conditionally stable.

Q5. You run FTCS with r = 0.7 and the solution erupts into a growing saw-tooth of alternating signs. The cause is…
A. a bug — FTCS is always stable, so the code must be wrong
B. round-off error from machine epsilon
C. the boundary conditions were set to Neumann instead of Dirichlet
D. r > ½ makes the self-weight (1−2r) negative, so points overshoot their neighbors and high-frequency modes grow
Show answer

D. For r > ½ the center weight goes negative and the averaging becomes an amplification of the highest-frequency (alternating) mode — the classic FTCS instability. It is not a code bug (A) — the math itself is unstable here. It is far too large to be round-off (B), and the boundary type (C) does not cause this blow-up.

✓ What you learned

  • The heat equation ∂u/∂t = α ∂²u/∂x² says each point changes in proportion to its local curvature — pulled toward its neighbors' trend.
  • The FTCS update uin+1 = ui + r(ui+1 − 2ui + ui-1) comes from a forward time + centered space difference, with r = αΔt/Δx².
  • FTCS is a weighted average of a point and its neighbors, so gradients smooth out and high-frequency wiggles damp fastest.
  • The scheme is explicit (no system to solve) but only conditionally stable: r ≤ ½, or the self-weight goes negative and it explodes.
  • You need an initial condition plus boundary conditions (Dirichlet vs Neumann), and you watched a NumPy hot spot spread, flatten, and leak heat out the cold ends.
