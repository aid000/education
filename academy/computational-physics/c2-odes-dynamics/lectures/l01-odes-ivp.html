ODEs & Initial-Value Problems

Newton's second law, a planet's orbit, a decaying nucleus — they are all the same sentence in one language: "tell me the rate of change, and where you start."

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why an ordinary differential equation (ODE) is the natural language of physical law, with Newton's second law as the prototype.
  • Define an initial-value problem (IVP) and identify the data — the equation plus the initial state — needed to pin down one solution.
  • Rewrite any higher-order ODE as an equivalent first-order system using a state vector.
  • State, intuitively, when an IVP has a unique solution (the existence-and-uniqueness picture).
  • Articulate what "solving an IVP numerically" means — and run your first solver with SciPy's solve_ivp.

1.1The Language of Physics Is a Rate of Change

Physical laws almost never tell you where something is. They tell you how fast it is changing right now, given where it is right now. That is exactly what a differential equation says: an equation linking a quantity to its own derivatives. When the unknown depends on a single variable (usually time), and only ordinary derivatives appear, we call it an ordinary differential equation — an ODE.

The cleanest example is the most famous law in mechanics. Newton's second law, F = m·a, is a statement about the second derivative of position:

m · d²x/dt² = F(x, dx/dt, t)Acceleration (the second time-derivative of position) is set by the force, which may depend on where you are, how fast you move, and the clock.

Radioactive decay says the number of nuclei falls at a rate proportional to how many remain: dN/dt = -k·N. A cooling cup of coffee obeys dT/dt = -k·(T - T_room). Population, circuits, chemical kinetics, orbital mechanics — each is a rule for the instantaneous rate of change. The job of dynamics is to turn that local rule into a global story: the full trajectory over time.

AnalogyAn ODE is a set of turn-by-turn driving directions, not a map. At every instant it tells you "from here, head this way, at this speed." It never hands you the destination directly. To learn where you end up, you must follow the directions step by step from a known starting point — which is precisely what a numerical solver does.

1.2Initial-Value Problems: the Equation Plus a Starting Point

A rate rule alone is not enough. The directions "drive north at 60 km/h" describe infinitely many journeys — one for every place you could have started. Newton's law dx²/dt² = F/m is satisfied by a whole family of trajectories. To single out one, you must also state the initial conditions: where the system is, and how fast it is moving, at the starting time.

An initial-value problem (IVP) bundles both pieces together. In its simplest first-order form:

dy/dt = f(t, y),   y(t0) = y0The differential equation gives the slope at every point; the initial condition y(t0) = y0 fixes which solution curve you ride.

Geometrically, f(t, y) defines a slope field: at every point in the (t, y) plane it plants a little arrow showing which way the solution must go. The initial condition drops you onto one specific point, and the solution is the unique curve that threads through that point while staying tangent to every arrow it passes. Change the starting point and you ride a different curve through the same field.

1.3Any Higher-Order ODE Is a First-Order System

Newton's law is second order — it involves d²x/dt². Numerical solvers, however, are built for first-order problems of the form dy/dt = f(t, y). The bridge between them is one of the most useful tricks in computational physics: introduce a state vector that carries not just position but every derivative up to one below the highest.

Take a 1-D oscillator: m·ẍ = -k·x. Define the state to hold position and velocity:

y = [ x , v ],  where v = dx/dtThe "state" is everything you must know right now to predict the future: where you are and how fast you are going.

Now differentiate each component. The position's rate is the velocity; the velocity's rate is the acceleration from the force law. The single second-order equation becomes a coupled pair of first-order equations:

dy/dt = [ dx/dt , dv/dt ] = [ v , -(k/m)·x ]Component 1 says dx/dt = v; component 2 says dv/dt = -(k/m)x. Same physics, now first-order — exactly the form a solver wants.

This generalizes perfectly. An n-th order ODE becomes a system of n first-order equations by stacking the function and its first n-1 derivatives into one vector. A planet in 3-D gives a 6-component state (three positions, three velocities); a double pendulum gives four. Master this reduction and you can hand essentially any dynamics problem to a single, general-purpose first-order solver.

Common mistakeDo not drop the velocity from the state and try to integrate position alone — the right-hand side for x needs v, and the right-hand side for v needs x, so they must travel together. A related slip is mismatching the order of your state vector and your derivative vector: if you define y = [x, v], your function must return [v, -(k/m)*x] in that same order, or you will silently solve a different system.

1.4Uniqueness, and What "Solving Numerically" Means

Before trusting any solver, ask: does this IVP even have a single, well-defined answer? The reassuring result is the Picard–Lindelöf theorem. Intuitively: if f(t, y) is continuous and does not change too violently as y changes — formally, if it is Lipschitz continuous in y, meaning its slope with respect to y is bounded — then through every initial point there passes exactly one solution curve, at least for some interval of time.

| f(t, y1) - f(t, y2) | <= L · | y1 - y2 |The Lipschitz condition: the right-hand side cannot stretch differences in y by more than a fixed factor L. This bound is what guarantees a single, non-branching trajectory.

When this fails, trajectories can branch or blow up. The classic cautionary tale is dy/dt = sqrt(y) with y(0) = 0: its slope in y is unbounded at the origin, and indeed both y(t) = 0 and y(t) = t²/4 solve it — uniqueness is lost. Most physical force laws are smooth enough that uniqueness holds, which is why the same step-by-step integration works across the field.

So what does it mean to solve an IVP numerically? Because the exact solution is a continuous curve we cannot write down in general, we settle for a discrete approximation: starting from y0, we use the slope f(t, y) to take a small step forward in time, landing at an estimate of y a little later, then repeat. The solver returns a table of (t, y) samples — a polyline that approximates the true curve. The whole rest of this course is about taking those steps accurately and stably, controlling the error that accumulates over thousands of them.

✦ Worked Example — reduce a pendulum to a first-order system

A simple pendulum of length L obeys the second-order ODE d²θ/dt² = -(g/L)·sin θ. Cast it as a first-order IVP ready for a solver, with the bob released from rest at θ = 0.20 rad. Use g = 9.81 m/s², L = 1.0 m.

  1. Choose a state vector. The equation is second order, so the state needs the angle and the angular velocity: y = [θ, ω], where ω = dθ/dt.
  2. Write each component's rate. The angle's rate is the angular velocity: dθ/dt = ω. The angular velocity's rate is the angular acceleration from the equation: dω/dt = -(g/L)·sin θ.
  3. Assemble the first-order system. dy/dt = f(t, y) = [ ω , -(g/L)·sin θ ]. With our numbers, g/L = 9.81 / 1.0 = 9.81 s⁻².
  4. Set the initial condition. "Released from rest" means zero initial velocity, so y(0) = [0.20, 0.0].
  5. Sanity-check the slope at t = 0. f(0, [0.20, 0]) = [0, -9.81·sin(0.20)] = [0, -9.81·0.1987] ≈ [0, -1.949]. The angle is momentarily not changing (it was just released) while gravity pulls the angular velocity negative — exactly the expected first instant of a swing back toward vertical.

🛠️ Hands-On: your first IVP solve with SciPy

The free Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib stack powers this entire specialization. Week 1 setup: install it once below, then reuse it for every lecture and the course project.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed (from python.org or your package manager), create an environment and install the scientific stack:

python3 -m venv compphys-env
source compphys-env/bin/activate     # macOS / Linux
compphys-env\Scripts\activate         # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import scipy; print(scipy.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. (numba is optional and only added later for speed.)

Encode the pendulum as a first-order system and let SciPy's solve_ivp integrate it. Notice that the function returns the derivative of the state — the slope field — in the same order as the state itself.

# pendulum_ivp.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

g, L = 9.81, 1.0

def rhs(t, y):                          # y = [theta, omega]
    theta, omega = y
    return [omega, -(g / L) * np.sin(theta)]   # [dtheta/dt, domega/dt]

y0 = [0.20, 0.0]                        # released from rest at 0.20 rad
t_span = (0.0, 10.0)
t_eval = np.linspace(*t_span, 500)

sol = solve_ivp(rhs, t_span, y0, t_eval=t_eval, rtol=1e-8)

print("solver succeeded:", sol.success)
print("slope at t=0:", rhs(0.0, y0))    # [0.0, -1.949...]

plt.plot(sol.t, sol.y[0])               # theta(t)
plt.xlabel("time (s)"); plt.ylabel("angle (rad)")
plt.title("Pendulum: a solved initial-value problem")
plt.show()

Try it: change y0 to [1.5, 0.0] (a big swing) and watch the period grow — a real pendulum is not perfectly periodic in amplitude, a fact the linear "small-angle" approximation hides. You just used a numerical IVP solver to see physics the textbook formula misses.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why is an initial condition required to "solve" an ODE like dy/dt = f(t, y)?
A. Without it the equation has no solutions at all
B. The equation defines a whole family of curves; the initial condition selects the one curve to follow
C. It makes the equation first order instead of second order
D. It is only needed for nonlinear equations
Show answer

B. The slope field admits infinitely many solution curves; y(t0) = y0 picks the single one passing through the start point. (A) is wrong — solutions exist regardless; (C) confuses order reduction with the role of data; (D) is false, linear ODEs need initial data too.

Q2. To prepare the oscillator m·ẍ = -k·x for a first-order solver with state y = [x, v], the right-hand side dy/dt should be…
A. [x, v]
B. [-(k/m)·x, v]
C. [v, -(k/m)·x]
D. [v, -(k/m)·v]
Show answer

C. The position's rate is the velocity (dx/dt = v) and the velocity's rate is the acceleration (dv/dt = -(k/m)x), in the same order as the state. (B) swaps the two components; (D) wrongly drives v from v; (A) returns the state, not its derivative.

Q3. A particle moves in 3-D under a force depending on its position and velocity. How many components does its first-order state vector have?
A. 3
B. 6
C. 2
D. 9
Show answer

B. Three position components plus three velocity components give a 6-vector. (A) forgets velocity; (C) is the 1-D case; (D) over-counts — acceleration is computed from the state, not stored in it.

Q4. The Picard–Lindelöf theorem guarantees a unique IVP solution when f(t, y) is continuous and, in y, is…
A. Lipschitz continuous (its rate of change in y is bounded)
B. exactly linear
C. equal to zero somewhere
D. periodic in time
Show answer

A. A bounded Lipschitz constant in y prevents trajectories from branching, giving uniqueness. (B) is far too strong — many nonlinear f are fine; (C) and (D) are irrelevant to uniqueness. The failure of (A) is exactly why dy/dt = sqrt(y), y(0)=0 has two solutions.

Q5. "Solving an IVP numerically" produces…
A. an exact closed-form formula for y(t)
B. the symbolic antiderivative of f
C. a proof that the solution exists
D. a discrete table of (t, y) samples approximating the true curve, built by stepping along the slope
Show answer

D. Numerical solvers march forward in small steps using f as the local slope, returning sampled points with controllable accumulated error. (A) and (B) describe analytic/symbolic methods; (C) is the job of the existence theorem, not the solver.

✓ What you learned

  • An ODE encodes a physical law as a rate of change; Newton's second law m·ẍ = F is the prototype.
  • An initial-value problem = the differential equation plus a starting state, which selects one curve from the slope field.
  • Any higher-order ODE becomes a first-order system via a state vector stacking the function and its lower derivatives.
  • The Picard–Lindelöf (Lipschitz) condition is the intuition for when a unique solution exists.
  • Solving an IVP numerically means stepping along the slope to build a discrete approximation — and you ran your first solve_ivp.
