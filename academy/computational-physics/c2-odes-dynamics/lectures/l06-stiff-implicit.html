Week 6 · Lecture 6

Stiff Equations & Implicit Methods

Some systems punish an explicit solver for taking a sensible step — learn to recognize that trap and step around it with a method that bends instead of breaking.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define what makes an ODE system stiff in terms of widely separated timescales (eigenvalues).
  • Show why an explicit method's stability limit forces an absurdly tiny step on a stiff problem.
  • Derive backward (implicit) Euler and analyze its stability on the linear test equation.
  • State the meaning of A-stability and contrast forward vs backward Euler stability regions.
  • Recognize stiffness in practice and pick an implicit solver (solve_ivp with BDF/Radau) instead of fighting it explicitly.

6.1What Makes a System Stiff

A problem is stiff when it contains processes that evolve on wildly different timescales, yet the fast process is not what you care about — it has already died away, but it still dictates how big a step an explicit solver may take. The textbook definition is qualitative on purpose: stiffness shows up as a numerical method being forced to take steps far smaller than the smoothness of the solution would suggest.

The cleanest way to make this precise is to linearize. Near a solution, a system y′ = f(t, y) behaves like y′ = J y, where J is the Jacobian matrix. Its eigenvalues λk set the local timescales: each mode decays (or oscillates) like eλkt. Stiffness is the ratio of the fastest to the slowest of these:

stiffness ratio S = max|Re(lambda_k)| / min|Re(lambda_k)|When S is large (often 10^3 to 10^9), the system is stiff: a fast-decaying mode coexists with a slowly-evolving one you actually want to follow.

Consider two decaying modes with rates 1 and 1000. After a brief transient the fast mode (rate 1000) is gone — the solution is essentially the slow mode (rate 1). You would like to step on the timescale of the slow mode, but as we will see, an explicit method must keep stepping on the timescale of the dead fast mode just to stay numerically alive.

AnalogyThink of driving a car whose suspension has a very stiff spring. The spring oscillates and settles in milliseconds; the car's actual journey takes minutes. To simulate the trip you do not care about the spring at all — but a naive simulator insists on sampling fast enough to resolve every spring twitch, so a five-minute drive needs millions of frames. Stiffness is exactly that mismatch: a fast component you have stopped caring about still governs your step size.

6.2Why Explicit Methods Choke: the Test Equation

To analyze stability we use the scalar linear test equation, the building block for every eigenvalue of a linear system:

y' = lambda * y, with Re(lambda) < 0 so the true solution y(t) = y0 * e^(lambda t) decays to 0lambda stands for one Jacobian eigenvalue; a stable method should keep the numerical solution decaying too.

Apply forward (explicit) Euler, yn+1 = yn + h·f. With f = λy this gives yn+1 = (1 + hλ) yn, so after n steps yn = (1 + hλ)n y0. The numerical solution decays only if the amplification factor stays inside the unit circle:

|1 + h*lambda| < 1For real lambda < 0 this forces h < 2 / |lambda| — the step is capped by the FASTEST mode, not the one you want to resolve.

Here is the trap. If your fast mode has λ = −1000, forward Euler demands h < 2/1000 = 0.002 for the entire integration, even after that mode has vanished and the solution is smooth on a scale of 1. Want to integrate to t = 50? That is 25,000 tiny steps spent enforcing the stability of a mode that contributes nothing. Step any larger and the numerical solution does not just lose accuracy — it explodes, oscillating with growing amplitude.

Common mistakeConcluding "my solver blew up, so I need a smaller step for accuracy." On a stiff problem the blow-up is an instability, not an accuracy failure. Shrinking h fixes the symptom at ruinous cost. The right move is to switch to an implicit method whose stability does not depend on h at all — fighting stiffness with smaller explicit steps can mean billions of evaluations where an implicit solver needs a few hundred.

6.3Backward Euler & A-Stability

The fix is to evaluate the slope at the end of the step instead of the start. Backward (implicit) Euler is:

y_(n+1) = y_n + h * f(t_(n+1), y_(n+1))The unknown y_(n+1) appears on BOTH sides — that's what "implicit" means; you must solve an equation each step.

On the test equation f = λy this reads yn+1 = yn + hλ yn+1, which we solve for the amplification factor:

y_(n+1) = y_n / (1 - h*lambda), so the factor is R(z) = 1 / (1 - z), z = h*lambdaFor any Re(lambda) < 0 and any h > 0, the denominator has magnitude > 1, so |R| < 1: the numerical solution decays no matter how big h is.

This is the headline result. Backward Euler is unconditionally stable on decaying modes: there is no step-size limit from stability. A method whose stability region contains the entire left half-plane (every z with Re(z) < 0) is called A-stable. Backward Euler is A-stable; forward Euler is not — its stable region is just a small disk. The figure makes the contrast vivid.

The cost is real but worth it: each step now requires solving an equation for yn+1. For a linear system that is a linear solve with (I − hJ); for a nonlinear system it is a Newton iteration each step, which is why implicit solvers need the Jacobian. You trade a cheap-but-tiny explicit step for an expensive-but-enormous implicit step — and on stiff problems the trade is overwhelmingly in your favor.

6.4Recognizing Stiffness & Choosing a Solver

Stiffness is common precisely where physics has fast and slow processes coexisting: chemical kinetics (some reactions in microseconds, others in hours), circuit simulation (a fast RC node next to a slow one), combustion and atmospheric chemistry, reaction-diffusion systems, and the method-of-lines discretization of diffusion PDEs (Course 4), where fine grid spacing produces huge negative eigenvalues.

Tell-tale signs at runtime

You likely have a stiff problem when: an explicit solver takes a huge number of tiny steps despite a smooth-looking solution; the solver reports it cannot reach the requested tolerance; or the solution explodes the moment you let the step grow. A quick diagnostic is to estimate the Jacobian's eigenvalues and look at the stiffness ratio.

AnalogyChoosing a solver is like choosing a vehicle for terrain. An explicit method is a sports car: fast and cheap per step on smooth, non-stiff roads. An implicit method is an all-terrain truck: each "step" costs more (you carry the engine of a linear/Newton solve), but it crawls over the stiff cliffs that would launch the sports car off the road. Use the truck only when the terrain demands it.

In scipy.integrate.solve_ivp the explicit defaults (RK45, RK23, DOP853) are right for non-stiff problems. For stiff ones, switch to an implicit method — BDF (backward differentiation formulas, the workhorse) or Radau (a high-order implicit Runge-Kutta, both A-stable). Supplying an analytic jac makes them dramatically faster, since they need the Jacobian for their internal Newton solves.

✦ Worked Example — the step that breaks forward Euler

Integrate y′ = −1000(y − cos t) − sin t, a classic stiff scalar problem. The exact solution decays a fast transient and then tracks cos t. Compare the explicit and implicit step limits.

  1. Find the fast rate. The dominant term is −1000(y − cos t); near the solution the Jacobian is ∂f/∂y = −1000, so λ = −1000. The fast mode decays like e−1000t — essentially gone by t ≈ 0.005.
  2. Forward Euler stability limit. Need |1 + hλ| < 1, i.e. h < 2/|λ| = 2/1000 = 0.002. To reach t = 10 that is at least 10/0.002 = 5000 steps, all to chase a mode that died at t = 0.005.
  3. Try h = 0.01 with forward Euler. Then 1 + hλ = 1 + (0.01)(−1000) = 1 − 10 = −9. Each step multiplies the error by −9: |−9|n → ∞. The solution oscillates and explodes — not slightly inaccurate, catastrophically unstable.
  4. Backward Euler at h = 0.01. Factor R = 1/(1 − hλ) = 1/(1 − (0.01)(−1000)) = 1/(1 + 10) = 1/11 ≈ 0.091. |R| < 1 — stable. You can even take h = 1 (R = 1/1001) and stay stable; accuracy, not stability, now sets the step.
  5. Takeaway. Backward Euler reaches t = 10 in perhaps a few dozen steps governed by how accurately you want to trace cos t — roughly 100× fewer steps than forward Euler, each only modestly more expensive. That is the entire payoff of going implicit.

🛠️ Hands-On: explicit vs implicit on a stiff problem

Uses the free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the install details lives in the early lectures). No new tools needed — solve_ivp's BDF and Radau methods ship with SciPy.

Solve the stiff problem from the worked example two ways and compare the step counts. Watch the explicit solver crawl while the implicit solver strolls.

# stiff_demo.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# y' = -1000 (y - cos t) - sin t ; exact solution tracks cos t after a fast transient
def f(t, y):
    return -1000.0 * (y - np.cos(t)) - np.sin(t)

def jac(t, y):
    return np.array([[-1000.0]])      # Jacobian dy'/dy = -1000 (lambda)

y0 = [0.0]
t_span = (0.0, 10.0)

# Explicit RK45: stability forces tiny steps -> many evaluations
sol_rk = solve_ivp(f, t_span, y0, method="RK45", rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9)
print("RK45  : steps =", sol_rk.t.size, " f-evals =", sol_rk.nfev)

# Implicit BDF (A-stable family): big steps, far fewer evaluations
sol_bdf = solve_ivp(f, t_span, y0, method="BDF", jac=jac, rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9)
print("BDF   : steps =", sol_bdf.t.size, " f-evals =", sol_bdf.nfev)

# Implicit Radau is another A-stable choice
sol_rad = solve_ivp(f, t_span, y0, method="Radau", jac=jac, rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9)
print("Radau : steps =", sol_rad.t.size, " f-evals =", sol_rad.nfev)

# Expect: RK45 uses ~thousands of steps; BDF/Radau use ~tens. Same accuracy.

Now sharpen the contrast: change the stiff coefficient from 1000 to 100000 and rerun. RK45's step count scales with the fast rate (it explodes toward intractable), while BDF barely changes — that flat scaling is A-stability paying off. Then implement backward Euler by hand (solve y_new = y + h*f(t+h, y_new) for this linear f in closed form) and confirm it stays bounded at h = 0.1.

💬 Discussion

Backward Euler is unconditionally stable, so why don't we simply use it (or an A-stable implicit method) for every ODE and forget explicit methods entirely? Think about non-stiff problems, the cost per step, accuracy/order, and what physical quantities a method might silently distort. When would reaching for an implicit solver actually be the wrong choice?

Show a model response

Stability is not the whole story — there are three good reasons not to default to implicit:

1. Cost per step. An implicit step solves an equation for the unknown state, which for nonlinear systems means a Newton iteration with Jacobian assembly and a linear solve every step. On a non-stiff problem an explicit method takes comparably large steps but each is far cheaper, so explicit wins on wall-clock time. Implicit machinery is overhead you only want to pay when stiffness forces it.

2. Accuracy and order. Backward Euler is only first-order accurate; an explicit RK45 is higher order and may hit a given tolerance with bigger steps on a smooth, non-stiff problem. Unconditional stability says the solution won't blow up — it says nothing about it being accurate.

3. Distorting physics. Backward Euler is strongly damping: applied to an undamped oscillator it artificially loses energy, spiraling a true circle inward. For Hamiltonian / conservative systems (orbital mechanics, molecular dynamics) that's a serious flaw — you want a structure-preserving symplectic method (next week), not a stable-but-dissipative one. The "right" method depends on what you must conserve, not just on staying bounded.

✓ Self-check rubric. A strong answer (a) states implicit is more expensive per step and so loses on non-stiff problems; (b) separates stability from accuracy/order; (c) names at least one physical pitfall such as artificial damping of conservative systems; and (d) concludes that solver choice is problem-dependent — match the method to the stiffness and conservation structure of the system.

Q1. A system of ODEs is called stiff when…
A. its solution is discontinuous
B. it contains processes on widely separated timescales, forcing an explicit solver into tiny steps
C. the Jacobian is singular
D. it has no analytic solution
Show answer

B. Stiffness is the coexistence of fast and slow timescales (large stiffness ratio of Jacobian eigenvalues), which caps an explicit step at the fast rate. A singular Jacobian (C) or lack of a closed form (D) are unrelated; stiff solutions are typically smooth, not discontinuous (A).

Q2. For forward Euler on y′ = λy with real λ < 0, the step size must satisfy…
A. h < 2 / |λ| for the numerical solution to stay bounded
B. any h > 0 — it is unconditionally stable
C. h > |λ|
D. h = 1 exactly
Show answer

A. The amplification factor is 1 + hλ; stability needs |1 + hλ| < 1, i.e. h < 2/|λ|. Forward Euler is not unconditionally stable (that's B, which describes backward Euler). C and D are not stability conditions.

Q3. Backward Euler applied to y′ = λy gives the amplification factor…
A. 1 + hλ
B. 1 − hλ
C. 1 / (1 − hλ), whose magnitude is < 1 for every Re(λ) < 0 and any h > 0
D. ehλ
Show answer

C. Solving yn+1 = yn + hλyn+1 gives yn+1 = yn/(1 − hλ). For Re(λ) < 0 the denominator exceeds 1 in magnitude, so it's stable for all h. A is forward Euler; D is the exact factor, which numerical methods only approximate.

Q4. A method is called A-stable when its stability region contains…
A. a disk of radius 1 around the origin
B. the entire left half of the complex z = hλ plane (all Re(z) < 0)
C. only the negative real axis
D. the whole complex plane including Re(z) > 0
Show answer

B. A-stability means every decaying mode (Re(z) < 0) is stable for any step size — the full left half-plane. A describes a bounded explicit region; C is too restrictive; D would absurdly make growing modes "stable" too.

Q5. Your explicit solver is taking thousands of tiny steps on a smooth-looking solution and warns it can't meet tolerance. The best response is to…
A. increase the step size manually and hope it converges
B. add more output points to the time array
C. lower the tolerance until the warning disappears
D. switch to an implicit solver such as BDF or Radau, ideally supplying the Jacobian
Show answer

D. These are textbook symptoms of stiffness; an A-stable implicit method removes the step-size cap. A makes an explicit method blow up; B only changes sampling, not the internal steps; C hides the problem by accepting a worse answer.

✓ What you learned

  • A system is stiff when fast and slow timescales coexist — quantified by the stiffness ratio of Jacobian eigenvalues.
  • Forward Euler's stability needs |1 + hλ| < 1, capping the step at the fastest mode (h < 2/|λ|) and exploding otherwise.
  • Backward (implicit) Euler has factor 1/(1 − hλ), so it is stable for any h on decaying modes — at the cost of solving an equation each step.
  • A-stability means the stability region covers the whole left half-plane; backward Euler has it, forward Euler does not.
  • Recognize stiffness by tiny-step/blow-up symptoms and reach for an implicit solver (BDF, Radau in solve_ivp) with a Jacobian.
