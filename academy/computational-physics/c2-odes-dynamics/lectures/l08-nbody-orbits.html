Week 8 · Lecture 8

N-Body Dynamics & Orbits

Give a handful of masses nothing but gravity and an initial nudge, and a whole universe of orbits — clean ellipses, slingshots, and irreducible chaos — falls out of one tidy system of ODEs.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write the gravitational N-body problem as a first-order system of ODEs in positions and velocities.
  • Assemble the pairwise inverse-square forces and explain why the cost grows as O(N²).
  • Contrast the closed-form Kepler two-body ellipse with the unpredictable three-body problem.
  • Apply force softening to keep the integrator stable when two bodies pass close.
  • Diagnose a simulation by tracking conserved total energy and momentum over time.

8.1Gravity as a System of ODEs

Newton gives us two ingredients. First, every pair of masses attracts along the line joining them with a force that falls off as the inverse square of their separation. Second, force equals mass times acceleration. Put them together for a collection of N bodies and you get the entire dynamics of stars, planets, and dust in one stroke: the acceleration of body i is the sum of the pulls from all the others.

a_i = G · Σj≠i m_j · (r_j − r_i) / |r_j − r_i|³The vector (r_j − r_i)/|r_j − r_i| points from i toward j; dividing by |r_j − r_i|² gives the inverse-square magnitude. The cube in the denominator absorbs the unit vector.

Acceleration is a second derivative of position, but every solver we have built in this course (Euler, RK4 in Week 4, the symplectic Verlet of Week 7) eats first-order systems. The standard trick is to promote velocity to its own variable. Each body then carries a position and a velocity, and the state of the whole system is one long vector:

d r_i/dt = v_i     d v_i/dt = a_i(r_1, ..., r_N)2N vector unknowns (6N scalars in 3D). The right-hand side for position is trivial; all the physics lives in a_i.

That is the whole problem. A simulation of the Solar System, a star cluster of a million suns, or three stars locked in a deadly dance is the same equations with different N and different initial conditions. The art is entirely in evaluating a_i efficiently and integrating it without leaking energy.

AnalogyThink of N children on a frictionless ice rink, each holding the end of an elastic band tied to every other child — except the bands pull harder the closer two children get. At any instant each child feels one net tug, the sum of all the bands. Let go and watch: the equations do not say where anyone ends up, only how each tug bends each path moment to moment. The future is something you have to play forward, step by step.

8.2Pairwise Forces and the O(N²) Wall

To advance one step you must know every body's acceleration, and each acceleration is a sum over the other N−1 bodies. So a single force evaluation touches every pair. The number of distinct pairs is the classic handshake count:

pairs = N(N−1)/2  ~  N²/210 bodies = 45 pairs; 1000 bodies = ~500,000 pairs; a million bodies = ~5×10¹¹ pairs — per timestep.

Because the force on j from i is exactly minus the force on i from j (Newton's third law), you can compute each pair once and add it to both bodies, halving the work — but the scaling is still quadratic. Double N and the cost quadruples. This O(N²) wall is the central performance fact of direct N-body simulation, and it is why galaxy-scale work uses cleverer tree or mesh methods (a forward pointer below).

Common mistakeWriting the inner loop as for j in range(N) and including j == i. A body must not feel its own gravity: the term (r_i − r_i)/|...|³ is 0/0, which NumPy returns as nan and which then poisons every subsequent step. Always skip the self-term (or zero the diagonal of a vectorized distance matrix).

8.3Two Bodies are Tame; Three are Wild

For exactly two bodies, the problem is completely solved on paper. Switching to the relative coordinate, the motion reduces to a single particle in an inverse-square field, and the orbit is a conic section — Kepler's first law. Whether you get an ellipse, parabola, or hyperbola is decided entirely by the total energy:

E = (1/2)·mu·v² − G·M·mu / r     E < 0 -> ellipse (bound)mu is the reduced mass and M the total mass. Negative energy means the bodies can never escape to infinity, so the orbit closes.

A bound orbit is a perfect, eternally repeating ellipse, and its period obeys Kepler's third law, T² ∝ a³, where a is the semi-major axis. Two bodies are integrable: give us the initial state and we hand you the position at any future time without stepping forward at all.

Add just one more body and that gift evaporates. The general three-body problem has no closed-form solution in elementary functions — a fact proven in the late nineteenth century. Generic three-body motion is chaotic: nearby starting states diverge exponentially, so the only honest way to know the future is to integrate numerically and accept that tiny errors grow. A handful of exact special solutions exist (the Lagrange equilateral configuration, the figure-eight choreography), but they are isolated islands in an ocean of unpredictability.

8.4Softening, and Checking Your Conservation Laws

The inverse-cube in the acceleration is a landmine. When two bodies drift very close, |r_j − r_i| → 0 and the force blows up to infinity. A fixed-step integrator cannot resolve that spike: it takes one enormous, wrong kick and the bodies rocket apart with absurd energy. The standard cure in collisionless simulations is force softening — add a small length ε inside the square root so the denominator can never reach zero:

a_i = G · Σj≠i m_j · (r_j − r_i) / (|r_j − r_i|² + ε²)^(3/2)For separations much larger than ε the force is unchanged; within ε it smoothly flattens instead of diverging. ε is the resolution scale below which you stop trusting close encounters.

Softening trades physical fidelity at tiny separations for a stable, finite integration — exactly the right deal when you care about the large-scale flow of a cluster, not individual collisions. (For true few-body work where collisions matter, you instead use adaptive timesteps or regularization, not softening.)

How do you know your simulation is trustworthy? You watch the conserved quantities. An isolated, self-gravitating system has no external forces and no external torques, so its total energy, total linear momentum, and total angular momentum must stay constant for all time. Total energy is kinetic plus the (negative) gravitational potential:

E = Σi (1/2)·m_i·|v_i|² − G · Σi<j m_i·m_j / |r_i − r_j|Sum over distinct pairs i<j for the potential so each pair is counted once. A good integrator holds E flat to a small relative drift; a bad one shows E sliding away.

In practice you log E, total momentum P = Σ m_i v_i, and angular momentum L at every step and plot their relative drift. A symplectic integrator like Verlet (Week 7) keeps energy bounded and oscillating about the true value over millions of steps; plain RK4 conserves momentum exactly (it is linear in the velocities) but slowly bleeds energy. Conservation is your built-in lie detector.

AnalogyConservation checks are the accountant's trial balance. The physics guarantees the books must balance — energy in equals energy out, total momentum unchanged. If your end-of-day totals have drifted, you do not argue with the law; you know your bookkeeping (the integrator or timestep) is at fault and go fix it.
✦ Worked Example — a circular two-body orbit, by the numbers

Use scaled units where G = 1. Put a heavy "sun" of mass M = 1 at the origin and a light planet of mass m = 0.001 at distance r = 1. What speed gives a circular orbit, what is the period, and what total energy should your simulation hold constant?

  1. Circular-orbit speed: set gravity equal to the centripetal requirement, G·M·m/r² = m·v²/r. The planet mass cancels: v² = G·M/r = 1·1/1 = 1, so v = 1.0 (perpendicular to the radius).
  2. Period: a circle of radius r = 1 at speed v = 1 has circumference 2πr = 2π, so T = 2πr/v = 2π ≈ 6.283 time units. (Check against Kepler: T² = 4π²a³/(G·M) = 4π², so T = 2π. ✓)
  3. Kinetic energy: KE = (1/2)·m·v² = (1/2)(0.001)(1) = 5.0×10⁻⁴.
  4. Potential energy: U = −G·M·m/r = −(1)(1)(0.001)/1 = −1.0×10⁻³.
  5. Total energy: E = KE + U = 5.0×10⁻⁴ − 1.0×10⁻³ = −5.0×10⁻⁴. It is negative (bound, as it must be for a closed orbit) and equals −KE — the virial relation for a circular orbit. Your integrator should hold E near −5.0×10⁻⁴; if it creeps toward zero, the orbit is artificially spiraling outward.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate the Sun–Earth orbit and watch energy

The free Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 is all you need — no new tools this week. We build a vectorized acceleration with softening, hand it to scipy.integrate.solve_ivp, and then plot both the orbit and the relative energy drift to grade our own work.

# nbody.py — Lecture 8 (G = 1 scaled units)
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = 1.0
m   = np.array([1.0, 1e-3])             # sun, planet
eps = 1e-3                              # softening length

def accelerations(pos):                 # pos: (N,2)
    d = pos[None, :, :] - pos[:, None, :]   # d[i,j] = r_j - r_i
    r2 = np.sum(d*d, axis=-1) + eps**2
    inv = r2**-1.5
    np.fill_diagonal(inv, 0.0)          # no self-force (avoid nan)
    return G * np.sum(d * inv[:, :, None] * m[None, :, None], axis=1)

def rhs(t, y):
    pos = y[:4].reshape(2, 2)
    vel = y[4:].reshape(2, 2)
    return np.concatenate([vel.ravel(), accelerations(pos).ravel()])

# sun at origin (resting), planet at (1,0) moving up at v=1 -> circular
y0 = np.array([0,0, 1,0,  0,0, 0,1.0])
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, [0, 20], y0, rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12, dense_output=True)

def energy(y):
    pos = y[:4].reshape(2,2); vel = y[4:].reshape(2,2)
    ke = 0.5*np.sum(m*np.sum(vel*vel, axis=1))
    d  = pos[0]-pos[1]; r = np.sqrt(d@d + eps**2)
    return ke - G*m[0]*m[1]/r

E = np.array([energy(sol.y[:, k]) for k in range(sol.y.shape[1])])
print("relative energy drift:", np.ptp(E)/abs(E[0]))   # should be tiny

plt.plot(sol.y[2], sol.y[3]); plt.gca().set_aspect('equal')
plt.title("planet orbit"); plt.show()

Try it: bump the initial speed from 1.0 to 1.3 and the circle stretches into an ellipse; push past √2 ≈ 1.414 (escape speed at r = 1) and the planet flies off on a hyperbola, never to return. Then add a third body and watch the energy-drift number — and your patience — get tested.

🔗 Where this is heading

Direct O(N²) summation is fine for the Solar System but hopeless for a galaxy of 10¹¹ stars. Production codes break the wall with tree methods (Barnes–Hut, O(N log N)) that lump distant clusters into single effective masses, or with particle-mesh solvers that compute gravity on a grid via the FFT-based Poisson techniques you will meet in Course 4 (PDEs). The chaos you glimpsed in three bodies is the whole subject of Week 9, where the double pendulum and Lorenz system make sensitive dependence quantitative. Project checkpoint: by the end of this week your capstone simulator should integrate at least three gravitating bodies with softening and produce a plot of relative energy drift over the run — the deliverable that proves your integrator is honest before you scale it up.

Q1. To hand the N-body problem to an ODE solver like solve_ivp, the second-order equation a = F/m is rewritten as…
A. a first-order system in both position and velocity (dr/dt = v, dv/dt = a)
B. a single algebraic equation with no time derivative
C. a third-order system by adding jerk as a variable
D. a boundary-value problem solved by shooting
Show answer

A. Promoting velocity to its own variable converts one second-order equation into two first-order ones, the form every standard solver expects. B drops the dynamics entirely; C adds an unnecessary variable (jerk is not needed); D is a different problem class — N-body is an initial-value problem.

Q2. A direct N-body force evaluation costs how much as N grows, and why?
A. O(N), one operation per body
B. O(N log N), because of the tree structure
C. O(N²), because every body interacts with every other (all pairs)
D. O(2^N), it grows exponentially
Show answer

C. There are N(N−1)/2 ~ N²/2 distinct pairs, and each must be summed. A only counts bodies, not interactions. B is the cost of a tree method (Barnes–Hut), not direct summation. D is far too pessimistic — pairwise interaction is polynomial, not exponential.

Q3. Why is force softening (adding ε² inside the distance) introduced?
A. to make the simulation run in fewer timesteps
B. to keep the inverse-square force from diverging when two bodies pass very close
C. to make total momentum exactly conserved
D. to convert the problem into a two-body problem
Show answer

B. As separation → 0 the unsoftened force → ∞ and a fixed-step integrator takes a catastrophic kick; ε² floors the denominator. A is incidental at best. C is false — softening does not change the momentum bookkeeping (forces stay equal and opposite either way). D is unrelated; softening does not reduce the body count.

Q4. Which statement about two- versus three-body gravity is correct?
A. Both have simple closed-form solutions for all initial conditions
B. Neither can be solved even numerically
C. The three-body problem is simpler because there are more constraints
D. The two-body orbit is a closed conic, but the general three-body problem has no elementary closed-form solution and is typically chaotic
Show answer

D. Two bodies reduce to an integrable one-body conic (Kepler); the general three-body problem famously has no closed-form solution and shows sensitive dependence on initial conditions. A is wrong for three bodies; B is wrong — numerical integration works fine; C inverts the truth, three bodies are harder, not simpler.

Q5. Your N-body run shows total energy slowly drifting toward zero while total momentum stays constant. The most likely culprit is…
A. a bug that violates Newton's third law in the force loop
B. integrator/timestep error accumulating (energy is not exactly conserved by, e.g., RK4)
C. the gravitational constant being set too large
D. using double precision instead of single precision
Show answer

B. Momentum staying constant rules out a third-law bug (that would break momentum first); the signature of a non-symplectic integrator like RK4 is exactly a slow energy drift while momentum holds. A would show as momentum drift, not just energy. C changes the orbit but not the conservation property. D would make things slightly worse, not produce a systematic one-directional drift.

✓ What you learned

  • The N-body problem is a first-order system: dr/dt = v and dv/dt = a, with all the physics in the inverse-square acceleration.
  • Direct force summation touches every pair, so it scales as O(N²) — the central cost of the method.
  • Two bodies give a closed Kepler conic; three or more are generally chaotic with no closed-form solution.
  • Force softening (a small ε² in the denominator) prevents blow-ups when bodies pass close.
  • Conserved energy and momentum are your built-in correctness check — for the capstone, ship a relative-energy-drift plot to prove the integrator is honest.
