Week 4 · Lecture 4

Adaptive Step-Size Control

Stop wasting steps where nothing happens and stop blowing past the action — let the solver feel the dynamics and choose its own step size.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a fixed step size is wasteful when a system's dynamics speed up and slow down.
  • Estimate the local truncation error of a step for free using an embedded Runge-Kutta pair (RK45 / Dormand-Prince).
  • Apply the accept/reject rule and the step-size update formula that drives the error toward a tolerance.
  • Configure SciPy's solve_ivp with rtol, atol, and dense_output to automate all of this.
  • Reason about how adaptivity concentrates effort exactly where the solution is hardest.

4.1Why One Step Size Can't Fit Every Problem

In Week 3 you built RK4 with a fixed step h. That works, but it forces a single rhythm on a solution that may not have one. Consider a comet on a long elliptical orbit: for years it drifts almost in a straight line far from the Sun, then whips around perihelion in days. A fixed h that resolves the fast swing wastes thousands of tiny steps on the boring straightaway; a fixed h that is efficient on the straightaway misses the swing entirely and the orbit drifts off.

The dynamics tell you how big a step they can tolerate. Where the solution curves sharply — a stiff spring snapping back, a chemical reaction igniting, a near-collision — the local truncation error of a step grows fast, so h must shrink. Where the solution is nearly straight, a large h is perfectly accurate. Adaptive step-size control measures the error of each candidate step and resizes h on the fly to hold that error at a user-set tolerance.

total steps with fixed h ~ T / h_min  vs  adaptive ~ Σ (T_i / h_i)A fixed scheme pays the cost of its smallest needed step everywhere; an adaptive scheme pays it only where needed.
AnalogyDriving a winding mountain road, you do not hold one fixed speed. You accelerate on the long straight, then ease off and take many small careful adjustments through the hairpin. Adaptive stepping is cruise control that watches the road's curvature and sets the pace so you never overshoot a turn — and never crawl down an empty highway.

4.2Measuring a Step's Error for Free: Embedded RK Pairs

To resize h we need to know how wrong a single step is — its local truncation error. The naive way (step-doubling) is to take one step of size h and compare it to two steps of size h/2, but that triples the work. The elegant way is an embedded Runge-Kutta pair.

Recall that a Runge-Kutta method evaluates the slope at several intermediate "stages" and combines them with weights. The trick: from the same set of stage evaluations you can form two different weighted combinations — one of order p (say 5th order) and one of order p−1 (4th order). Their difference is an estimate of the local error, obtained at essentially no extra cost:

y_hi = y_n + h Σ b_i k_i  (order 5)
y_lo = y_n + h Σ b*_i k_i  (order 4)
err = | y_hi − y_lo |Both solutions reuse the same stage slopes k_i; only the weights b_i vs b*_i differ. Their gap estimates the truncation error of the step.

The classic example is the Dormand-Prince RK45 pair (the engine behind SciPy's default RK45). It uses seven stage evaluations per step and reports a 5th-order solution alongside a 4th-order companion to gauge the error. Because the two share all seven slopes, you get an error estimate for the price of essentially one good step.

4.3Accept, Reject, and Resize: the Control Law

With an error estimate err in hand and a target tolerance, the controller follows a simple loop. First it builds a scaled tolerance that blends a relative and an absolute floor (so the rule behaves sensibly even when y passes through zero):

tol = atol + rtol · |y|rtol controls significant digits; atol is a floor that keeps the test meaningful when |y| is tiny.

It then compares err to tol. If err ≤ tol the step is accepted — y advances and the next h may grow. If err > tol the step is rejected — y does not advance and the step is retried with a smaller h. Either way the new step size comes from the same formula, which exploits that the error of a method of order p scales as hp+1:

h_new = h · S · (tol / err)^(1/(p+1))For RK45, p = 4 so the exponent is 1/5. S ~ 0.9 is a safety factor; updates are usually clamped to grow/shrink by at most ~5x per step.

Read the formula physically: if err is far below tol, the ratio is large and h grows to be more efficient. If err overshoots tol, the ratio is below 1 and h shrinks to recover accuracy. The exponent 1/(p+1) is the precise amount needed to land the next step's error right on the tolerance, because halving h cuts the local error by 2p+1.

Common mistakeConfusing local and global error. Adaptive control holds the per-step local error near tol; it does not guarantee the final answer is accurate to tol. Errors accumulate over many steps and can be amplified by the dynamics. Tightening rtol/atol reduces global error too, but always validate convergence by re-running with a tighter tolerance and checking the answer barely moves.

4.4How SciPy's solve_ivp Automates It

You almost never code this loop by hand in practice. SciPy's scipy.integrate.solve_ivp wraps a family of adaptive solvers and runs the entire accept/reject/resize machinery internally. You hand it the right-hand side f(t, y), the time span, the initial state, and a tolerance — it chooses every step size for you.

solve_ivp(f, (t0, tf), y0, method='RK45', rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9)RK45 = Dormand-Prince embedded pair (the default). Returns the accepted t values and states; sol.nfev reports how many slope evaluations it used.

Key knobs worth knowing now: method picks the solver (RK45 for smooth non-stiff problems, DOP853 for very tight tolerances, Radau/BDF for stiff systems you will meet in Week 6); rtol and atol set the per-step error target; dense_output=True builds a cheap interpolant so you can evaluate the solution at any time, not just the adaptively chosen points; and t_eval lets you request output at specific times without forcing the solver to actually step there.

Because the chosen t points are unevenly spaced (dense in the hard regions, sparse in the easy ones), dense_output or t_eval is how you get a clean, evenly-sampled curve for plotting on top of the efficient internal stepping.

✦ Worked Example — resizing a step with RK45

An RK45 step of size h = 0.10 returns a local error estimate err = 8.0×10⁻⁵. The tolerance for this step is tol = 1.0×10⁻⁶. Decide accept or reject and compute the new step size. Use safety factor S = 0.9 and order p = 4.

  1. Compare: err = 8.0×10⁻⁵ is larger than tol = 1.0×10⁻⁶, so the step is rejected — y does not advance.
  2. Error ratio: tol / err = (1.0×10⁻⁶)/(8.0×10⁻⁵) = 0.0125.
  3. Exponent: for RK45, 1/(p+1) = 1/5 = 0.2.
  4. Raise to the power: (0.0125)0.2 = e^(0.2 · ln 0.0125) = e^(0.2 · (−4.382)) = e^(−0.876) ≈ 0.416.
  5. Apply safety factor: h_new = 0.10 · 0.9 · 0.416 ≈ 0.0375. The solver retries the step at h ≈ 0.037.
  6. Sanity check: at the new h the predicted error scales as h⁵, so err shrinks by (0.0375/0.10)⁵ ≈ 0.0075, giving ≈ 8.0×10⁻⁵ · 0.0075 ≈ 6×10⁻⁷ — just under tol, as designed.

🛠️ Hands-On: let the solver feel the dynamics

This uses the same free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (the install details lives in the Week-1 lecture if you need it again). No new tools required — solve_ivp ships inside SciPy.

We integrate the Van der Pol oscillator, whose dynamics alternate between slow drift and fast snaps — a perfect showcase for adaptivity. Watch how the gaps between the solver's chosen time points stretch and shrink.

# adaptive_vdp.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

mu = 8.0   # larger mu -> sharper, faster snaps -> more adaptivity

def vdp(t, y):
    x, v = y
    return [v, mu * (1 - x**2) * v - x]

sol = solve_ivp(vdp, (0, 40), [2.0, 0.0],
                method='RK45', rtol=1e-6, atol=1e-9,
                dense_output=True)

print("accepted steps:", sol.t.size)
print("slope evals (nfev):", sol.nfev)

# step sizes the solver actually chose -- highly uneven
dt = np.diff(sol.t)
print(f"h ranges from {dt.min():.2e} to {dt.max():.2e}")

# smooth curve via the dense interpolant, then mark the real step points
tt = np.linspace(0, 40, 2000)
xx = sol.sol(tt)[0]
plt.plot(tt, xx, lw=1)
plt.plot(sol.t, sol.y[0], '.', ms=4)   # dots bunch at the snaps
plt.xlabel('t'); plt.ylabel('x'); plt.title('Van der Pol — adaptive steps')
plt.show()

Now bump mu to 100 and rerun: the snaps get steeper, the solver takes far more steps near them, and you will see RK45 struggle (many rejected steps) — a preview of stiffness. Switch to method='Radau' and compare nfev; the implicit stiff solver wins dramatically. Also try tightening rtol to 1e-9 and confirm the curve barely changes (convergence check).

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. An embedded Runge-Kutta pair (such as Dormand-Prince RK45) estimates the local error of a step by…
A. running the whole integration twice with different seeds
B. combining the same shared stage slopes two ways and taking the difference of the two solutions
C. measuring how long the step takes to compute
D. comparing against the exact analytic solution
Show answer

B. The pair reuses one set of stage evaluations to form an order-5 and an order-4 estimate; their difference is the error, computed at almost no extra cost. A is nonsense (ODE solving is deterministic, not seeded), C confuses runtime with accuracy, and D is impossible — you generally have no exact solution.

Q2. In the controller, a step is rejected when…
A. the estimated error exceeds the scaled tolerance, so y is not advanced and h is reduced
B. the estimated error is far below tolerance, so h grows
C. the step size reaches exactly h = 0.1
D. the right-hand side returns a negative value
Show answer

A. Rejection means err > tol: discard the step and retry with a smaller h. B describes acceptance with growth, C is an arbitrary number, and D is irrelevant — the sign of f says nothing about step error.

Q3. For RK45 (method order p = 4), the step-update formula h_new = h · S · (tol/err)^(1/(p+1)) uses which exponent?
A. 1/2
B. 1/4
C. 1/5
D. 1/8
Show answer

C. The exponent is 1/(p+1) = 1/(4+1) = 1/5, because the local error of an order-p method scales as h^(p+1). A and D do not correspond to any p+1 here; B = 1/4 wrongly uses 1/p instead of 1/(p+1).

Q4. The role of atol (absolute tolerance) in tol = atol + rtol·|y| is to…
A. set the maximum number of steps the solver may take
B. provide a meaningful error floor so the test still works when |y| is near zero
C. select the Runge-Kutta method
D. control how many CPU cores SciPy uses
Show answer

B. If a component of y passes through zero, the relative term rtol·|y| vanishes and a pure relative test becomes meaningless; atol keeps a sensible floor. A, C, and D are unrelated knobs.

Q5. Holding the per-step local error near tol guarantees that the final solution at t_f is accurate to tol.
A. True — local and global error are the same thing
B. True — solve_ivp internally enforces the global bound
C. True — only if you set dense_output=True
D. False — local errors accumulate (and can be amplified) into a larger global error; validate by re-running with a tighter tolerance
Show answer

D. Adaptive control bounds the local per-step error only; global error is the accumulation of many such errors and depends on the dynamics. A and B assert a guarantee that does not exist, and C confuses dense_output (an interpolant for output) with error control.
