Week 2 · Lecture 2

Euler's Method & Local/Global Error

The simplest way to march a differential equation forward in time — and a vivid lesson in how small per-step lies pile up into a large final one.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the explicit Euler method directly from a Taylor expansion of the solution.
  • Distinguish local truncation error O(h²) per step from global error O(h) at a fixed final time.
  • Explain why a first-order method loses one order of accuracy when errors accumulate over 1/h steps.
  • Recognize numerical instability when the step h is too large for the problem's time scale.
  • Observe energy drift in an Euler-integrated oscillator and explain why first-order Euler is rarely accurate enough.

2.1From a Taylor Expansion to Euler's Method

Last week we wrote every dynamics problem as a first-order initial-value problem: given y′ = f(t, y) and a starting value y(t₀) = y₀, find y(t) for later times. The catch is that for almost any realistic f there is no closed-form solution — so we march the solution forward in small steps of size h instead.

Where does the simplest marching rule come from? Pretend for a moment we already know the true solution y(t). Taylor-expand it one step ahead:

y(t + h) = y(t) + h·y′(t) + (h²/2)·y″(t) + O(h³)An exact statement about the true solution — no approximation yet.

Now substitute the ODE itself, y′(t) = f(t, y(t)), and simply throw away every term past the linear one. What remains is the explicit (forward) Euler method:

yn+1 = yn + h · f(tn, yn)Step forward along the tangent line. Repeat: tn+1 = tn + h.

Geometrically this could not be more intuitive: f(t, y) is the slope of the solution at the current point, so we follow that straight tangent line for a time h, land somewhere new, read off the slope there, and turn. The solution we trace is a polyline — a chain of short straight segments approximating a smooth curve.

AnalogyEuler's method is steering a car by glancing at the road only once per second and holding the wheel fixed in between. On a gentle curve you stay roughly on track; take your eyes off long enough (large h) on a sharp bend and you drift wide — exactly as the polyline cuts the inside of a curving true solution.

2.2Local Truncation Error: O(h²) per Step

How wrong is a single Euler step? Compare the exact Taylor expansion with the Euler update, assuming we start the step from the exact value y(tn):

y(tn+h) − [yn + h·f] = (h²/2)·y″(ξ)Everything Euler discarded — the leading dropped term is the local truncation error (LTE).

So one step introduces an error proportional to h². We call this the local truncation error — the error committed in a single step, assuming no error was present beforehand. Halve the step and a single step becomes four times more accurate. That sounds great. The trouble is that we never take just one step.

Why the exponent is one higher than you might want

The constant in front is ½·y″ — the solution's curvature. Where the true solution bends sharply (large second derivative), each Euler step strays more. Where it is nearly straight, Euler is nearly exact. This is the precise meaning of the car analogy: the danger is in the curves.

2.3Global Error: O(h) — One Order Is Lost

To reach a fixed final time T starting from t₀, you take N = (T − t₀)/h steps. Each step adds about (h²/2)|y″| of fresh error, and — this is the subtle part — earlier errors are carried forward and amplified by the dynamics. Adding up the per-step errors over N ∝ 1/h steps gives the global error:

global error ≈ N · (local error) ≈ (T/h) · O(h²) = O(h)One power of h is eaten by the growing number of steps. Local O(h²) becomes global O(h).

This is the defining fact about Euler's method: it is a first-order method. The local error is second order, but at a fixed final time the global error is only first order. Halving h only halves the final error — a painfully slow trade. To gain one more decimal digit you must do ten times the work.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the two errors. Students often quote "Euler is O(h²)" — that is the local truncation error for a single step. The accuracy you actually experience after integrating to a fixed time is the global error, O(h). Mixing them up makes you expect ten-fold accuracy gains where you only get two-fold.

2.4Instability and Energy Drift: Why First-Order Euler Falls Short

Accuracy is not the only problem. Apply Euler to the simple decay y′ = −λy (a stiff cooling or RC-discharge problem) and the update becomes yn+1 = (1 − λh)·yn. The true solution decays smoothly to zero, but the numerical one is multiplied by (1 − λh) each step:

|1 − λh| < 1  ⟺   h < 2/λThe stability condition for explicit Euler on y' = -λy. Cross it and the numbers oscillate and blow up.

If h exceeds 2/λ the factor's magnitude exceeds 1 and the solution explodes with growing oscillations — even though the true answer is a gentle decay. This is numerical instability: a failure caused by the method, not the problem. The fastest time scale in your system (here 1/λ) sets a hard ceiling on h.

For oscillatory systems the failure is subtler and more revealing. Integrate a frictionless harmonic oscillator (a pendulum, an LC circuit, a planetary orbit) with explicit Euler and the total energy does not stay constant — it grows every period. The orbit spirals outward; the pendulum swings ever higher from nothing. Energy that should be conserved drifts upward without bound.

These three flaws — slow O(h) accuracy, a hard stability ceiling on h, and systematic energy drift — are why explicit Euler is almost never the production tool. It is the right place to start, because every better method (Runge-Kutta next week, symplectic integrators later) is best understood as a cure for one of Euler's diseases.

✦ Worked Example — Euler on y′ = y, and the global O(h) penalty

Solve y′ = y with y(0) = 1 up to t = 1. The exact answer is y(1) = e ≈ 2.71828. Take h = 0.5 (two steps), then predict what happens when you refine.

  1. Step 1: y₁ = y₀ + h·f = 1 + 0.5·(1) = 1.5 (true y(0.5) = e0.5 ≈ 1.6487).
  2. Step 2: y₂ = y₁ + h·f = 1.5 + 0.5·(1.5) = 2.25. So Euler gives y(1) ≈ 2.25 vs the true 2.71828.
  3. Error at h = 0.5: |2.71828 − 2.25| ≈ 0.468. Notice the estimate is too low — the curve is concave up (y″ = y > 0), so the tangent steps fall below, exactly as the figure shows.
  4. Refine to h = 0.25 (4 steps): each step multiplies by (1 + h) = 1.25, so y(1) = 1.25⁴ ≈ 2.4414, error ≈ 0.277.
  5. Confirm the order: halving h took the error from 0.468 to 0.277 — a factor of about 1.7, i.e. roughly ½ once h is small enough. That is the signature of a first-order O(h) method: halving h only halves the error, no more.

First confirm the O(h) global error on y′ = y, then integrate an undamped oscillator and watch its energy creep upward — the energy-drift fingerprint of explicit Euler.

# euler_error.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

# --- 1. Global error order on y' = y, exact y(1) = e ---
def euler(f, y0, t0, t1, h):
    n = int(round((t1 - t0) / h))
    y, t = y0, t0
    for _ in range(n):
        y = y + h * f(t, y)             # the one Euler step
        t = t + h
    return y

for h in [0.1, 0.05, 0.025, 0.0125]:
    y_end = euler(lambda t, y: y, 1.0, 0.0, 1.0, h)
    err = abs(y_end - np.e)
    print(f"h={h:.4f}  y(1)={y_end:.5f}  error={err:.2e}")
# error roughly HALVES each time h halves  ->  first-order, O(h)

# --- 2. Energy drift: undamped oscillator x'' = -x  ->  (x'=v, v'=-x) ---
h, steps = 0.05, 2000
x, v = 1.0, 0.0
E0 = 0.5 * (x*x + v*v)
for _ in range(steps):
    x, v = x + h*v, v - h*x            # explicit Euler (uses OLD x for v)
E = 0.5 * (x*x + v*v)
print(f"energy: start={E0:.3f}  end={E:.3f}  ->  grew by {E/E0:.2f}x")
# energy GROWS — explicit Euler injects energy into a conservative system

Try the tiny tweak v_new = v - h*x; x = x + h*v_new (use the updated v). This is symplectic / semi-implicit Euler — rerun and watch the energy stay bounded. We will explain why in the symplectic-integrators lecture; for now compare it against scipy.integrate.solve_ivp as your trusted reference.

🔗 Where this is heading

Euler is the seed every later integrator grows from. Next week (RK4) samples the slope four times per step to reach fourth-order global accuracy — error ∝ h⁴ instead of h — for a tiny extra cost. Adaptive solvers then estimate the local error on the fly and resize h automatically, the engine behind solve_ivp. For conservative dynamics — orbits, molecular dynamics, plasma particle pushers — symplectic integrators cure the energy drift you saw here, keeping the phase-space orbit closed for billions of steps. And stiff problems (fast and slow time scales together, ubiquitous in chemical kinetics and circuit simulation) force us toward implicit methods that escape Euler's h < 2/λ ceiling entirely. Every one of these is a targeted fix for a flaw you can now name.

❓ Lecture 2 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Explicit Euler, yn+1 = yn + h·f, is obtained from the Taylor expansion of the true solution by…
A. keeping the y(t) and h·y′ terms and discarding the (h²/2)y″ and higher terms
B. keeping all terms up to and including (h²/2)y″
C. integrating f exactly over the step
D. averaging the slope at the start and end of the step
Show answer

A. Euler truncates the Taylor series after the linear h·y′ term. (B) would be a higher-order Taylor method; (C) is impossible in general (that's the whole problem); (D) describes the implicit trapezoidal / Heun idea, not Euler.

Q2. The local truncation error of a single explicit-Euler step scales as…
A. O(h)
B. O(h²)
C. O(h³)
D. O(1), independent of h
Show answer

B. The leading dropped Taylor term is (h²/2)y″, so one step errs by O(h²). O(h) (A) is the global error after accumulation; (C) would be a second-order method's local error; (D) is wrong since the step shrinks the error.

Q3. Integrating to a fixed final time T, the global error of Euler is O(h) rather than O(h²) because…
A. round-off error dominates the truncation error
B. the number of steps N = T/h grows as 1/h, so N·O(h²) = O(h)
C. the local error is actually O(h) per step
D. the solution becomes unstable
Show answer

B. Summing ~1/h local errors of size O(h²) loses one power of h. (A) is unrelated to the order; (C) is false — local error is O(h²); (D) is a separate failure mode, not the reason for the order.

Q4. For y′ = −λy with λ > 0, explicit Euler stays bounded (stable) only when…
A. h > 2/λ
B. h = 1/λ exactly
C. h < 2/λ
D. any h, since the true solution decays
Show answer

C. The update multiplies by (1 − λh); stability needs |1 − λh| < 1, i.e. h < 2/λ. (A) is exactly the unstable regime; (B) is just one allowed value, not the condition; (D) is the classic trap — the method can blow up even when the true solution decays.

Q5. When explicit Euler integrates an undamped harmonic oscillator over many periods, the total energy…
A. stays exactly constant, as it should
B. decays steadily toward zero
C. oscillates but averages to the true value
D. drifts upward, so the orbit spirals outward
Show answer

D. Explicit Euler injects energy into a conservative system, so energy grows and the phase-space orbit spirals out. (A) holds only for symplectic methods; (B) describes the opposite drift (semi-implicit choices can lose energy, but explicit Euler gains it); (C) is wrong because the error is systematic, not zero-mean.

✓ What you learned

  • Explicit Euler's method yn+1 = yn + h·f comes from truncating the solution's Taylor series after the linear term — stepping along the tangent.
  • The local truncation error is O(h²) per step, governed by the solution's curvature y″.
  • Over N ∝ 1/h steps the errors accumulate into a global error of O(h) — Euler is a first-order method, so halving h only halves the error.
  • Numerical instability: for y′ = −λy, explicit Euler needs h < 2/λ or the solution blows up despite a decaying true answer.
  • Explicit Euler suffers energy drift on oscillators — orbits spiral outward — which is why first-order Euler is rarely accurate enough for real dynamics.
