Week 9 · Lecture 9

Chaos: The Double Pendulum & Lorenz System

Two systems with the same rules and the same equations, started a hair apart, drift into utterly different futures — that is chaos, and it is everywhere.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define deterministic chaos and explain sensitive dependence on initial conditions.
  • Describe the double pendulum as a simple mechanical system that is nonetheless chaotic.
  • Write down the Lorenz system and explain what its strange attractor is and is not.
  • Define the Lyapunov exponent and use it to quantify how fast nearby trajectories separate.
  • Integrate a chaotic ODE in SciPy and measure its largest Lyapunov exponent numerically.

9.1Deterministic Chaos & Sensitive Dependence

Through eight weeks of this course we trusted a comforting idea: give a differential equation an initial condition, and its future is fixed. The flow is deterministic — no randomness, no dice. Chaos does not break this. A chaotic system is fully deterministic. What it adds is a vicious sensitivity: two starts that differ by one part in a billion produce trajectories that, after a while, look completely unrelated.

The signature is exponential separation. Let two nearby trajectories start a tiny distance δ0 apart. In a chaotic regime their separation grows like:

δ(t) ≈ δ0 · eλtλ > 0 is the largest Lyapunov exponent. Positive λ is the mathematical fingerprint of chaos.

Contrast this with a stable, non-chaotic system, where nearby starts stay nearby (separation grows at most linearly, δ ∝ t) or even shrink toward a common path (λ < 0). The exponential blow-up is what makes long-term prediction impossible in practice: every measurement has finite precision, and that initial uncertainty is amplified by eλt until it fills the whole accessible region.

AnalogyDrop two identical leaves into a smooth stream side by side and they float downstream together — that is a stable, predictable flow. Drop them just above a rocky rapid and watch: a sliver of difference in where they land sends one into an eddy and the other over the falls. The water obeys the same fluid equations for both leaves; nothing is random. The rapid just amplifies microscopic differences explosively. Chaos is a rocky rapid in the equations themselves.
Common mistakeChaotic does not mean random, and it does not mean noisy. The same initial condition always yields the exact same trajectory. The catch is that you can never specify the initial condition exactly, and the tiny unavoidable error grows exponentially. "Deterministic" and "unpredictable" are not contradictions here — they coexist.

9.2The Double Pendulum: Chaos from Two Rods

You do not need a complicated system to find chaos. Hang one pendulum from the end of another — two rods, two masses, gravity — and you have a system whose equations a first-year student can write down, yet whose motion is chaotic for all but the smallest swings.

The state needs four numbers: the two angles θ1, θ2 and their angular velocities. The equations of motion (from the Lagrangian we built in earlier weeks) are coupled and strongly nonlinear because of the sin and cos of the angle difference:

state = (θ1, ω1, θ2, ω2),   dθi/dt = ωiThe angular accelerations dωi/dt depend on sin(θ1 − θ2), the coupling that breeds chaos.

For tiny swings the double pendulum is well-behaved: it reduces to two coupled harmonic oscillators (normal modes, from Week 6). But push the amplitude up and the nonlinearity dominates. The energy is conserved (no friction), so the motion is not random — yet it is unpredictable in detail. This is the cleanest mechanical demonstration of chaos you can build on a tabletop.

9.3The Lorenz System & Its Strange Attractor

In 1963 a meteorologist studying a drastically simplified model of atmospheric convection wrote down three coupled ODEs and stumbled into the modern theory of chaos. The Lorenz system is the most famous chaotic system in science:

dx/dt = σ(y − x)
dy/dt = x(ρ − z) − y
dz/dt = xy − βzClassic chaotic parameters: σ = 10, ρ = 28, β = 8/3. Here x, y, z are convection-roll variables, not spatial coordinates.

The remarkable thing is what the trajectory does. It never settles to a fixed point, never repeats a periodic loop, yet it never flies off to infinity either. Instead it wanders forever on a bounded, intricately folded surface — the famous two-lobed "butterfly." This object is a strange attractor: an attractor (nearby trajectories are drawn onto it) that is strange because it has a fractal, non-integer dimension and any two points on it separate exponentially.

Common mistakeThe x, y, z of the Lorenz system are not physical positions in space, and the butterfly is not a picture of fluid. They are abstract amplitudes of convection modes plotted in a state space (phase space). Likewise, the "butterfly effect" name is a happy coincidence with the attractor's shape — it actually refers to sensitive dependence (a butterfly's wingbeat altering a distant storm), not to the drawing.

9.4The Lyapunov Exponent: Measuring Chaos

"Sensitive dependence" is a feeling; the Lyapunov exponent λ turns it into a number. Take the separation law from 9.1, δ(t) ≈ δ0 eλt, take logs, and solve for the rate:

λ = limt→∞ (1/t) · ln( δ(t) / δ0 )The largest Lyapunov exponent: the long-time average exponential rate at which infinitesimally close trajectories separate.

The sign of the largest λ classifies the dynamics. λ < 0: trajectories converge to a stable fixed point or limit cycle (predictable). λ = 0: marginal, as on a conservative periodic orbit. λ > 0: chaos. For the Lorenz system with the classic parameters, λ ≈ 0.906 (per unit time). Its reciprocal is the Lyapunov time τ ≈ 1/0.906 ≈ 1.1 — the timescale on which prediction degrades.

This is brutally practical. To keep prediction error below 1, an initial error δ0 survives only until δ0eλt ≈ 1, i.e. for a time t ≈ (1/λ)·ln(1/δ0). Notice the logarithm: improving your measurements by a factor of 1000 (three decimal digits) buys you only ln(1000) ≈ 6.9 extra Lyapunov times. This is exactly why weather forecasts are useless beyond a couple of weeks no matter how good the sensors get.

AnalogyThink of λ as the "interest rate on ignorance." Your initial uncertainty is the principal δ0; chaos compounds it continuously at rate λ. A bigger λ means your tiny debt of ignorance balloons faster. And because growth is exponential (compound, not simple interest), pouring in more precision only delays the inevitable by a logarithmic amount.
✦ Worked Example — how long can you predict the Lorenz system?

You know the Lorenz state to a relative precision of δ0 = 10−6 (six good digits). The largest Lyapunov exponent is λ ≈ 0.906 per time unit. About how long until your prediction error grows to order 1 (totally wrong)? What if you improve precision to δ0 = 10−9?

  1. Set up the threshold: prediction fails when δ(t) = δ0eλt ≈ 1.
  2. Solve for t: eλt = 1/δ0, so t = (1/λ)·ln(1/δ0).
  3. Plug in δ0 = 10−6: ln(106) = 6·ln 10 ≈ 6·2.303 = 13.8. Then t ≈ 13.8 / 0.906 ≈ 15.2 time units (about 14 Lyapunov times).
  4. Now δ0 = 10−9: ln(109) = 9·2.303 = 20.7, so t ≈ 20.7 / 0.906 ≈ 22.9 time units.
  5. Takeaway: a thousand-fold improvement in precision (6 → 9 digits) extended the useful horizon from ~15 to ~23 units — a gain of only ln(1000)/λ ≈ 7.6 units. Exponential error growth makes the prediction horizon crawl logarithmically with effort. That is the hard wall chaos puts on forecasting.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate Lorenz and measure λ

We use the same free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack from Week 1 (the one-time install details lives in Lecture 1). No new tools are needed — solve_ivp from Week 3 does all the integrating.

First integrate two Lorenz trajectories whose starts differ by 10−9, plot the attractor, and watch the separation grow exponentially. Then fit a line to ln(separation) versus time — its slope is the largest Lyapunov exponent.

# lorenz_chaos.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

sigma, rho, beta = 10.0, 28.0, 8.0/3.0

def lorenz(t, s):
    x, y, z = s
    return [sigma*(y - x), x*(rho - z) - y, x*y - beta*z]

t_span = (0.0, 40.0)
t_eval = np.linspace(*t_span, 8000)
s0 = [1.0, 1.0, 1.0]
s1 = [1.0 + 1e-9, 1.0, 1.0]              # start 1e-9 apart

# tight tolerances so the chaos is real, not solver noise
a = solve_ivp(lorenz, t_span, s0, t_eval=t_eval, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
b = solve_ivp(lorenz, t_span, s1, t_eval=t_eval, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)

sep = np.linalg.norm(a.y - b.y, axis=0)  # distance vs time

# fit ln(sep) ~ lambda*t over the exponential-growth window
mask = (a.t > 2) & (sep < 1.0)            # before saturation
lam = np.polyfit(a.t[mask], np.log(sep[mask]), 1)[0]
print(f"largest Lyapunov exponent ~ {lam:.3f}")   # ~0.9

# optional: plot the attractor (x-z projection) and sep(t) on a log axis
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
fig, (ax1, ax2) = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(10, 4))
ax1.plot(a.y[0], a.y[2], lw=0.4); ax1.set_xlabel("x"); ax1.set_ylabel("z")
ax2.semilogy(a.t, sep); ax2.set_xlabel("t"); ax2.set_ylabel("separation")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Try it: change s1's offset to 10−3 and watch the separation saturate sooner (it starts closer to 1). The fitted slope λ should stay the same — it is a property of the attractor, not of your starting gap. Then loosen rtol to 1e-3 and see the two trajectories diverge from solver error alone: a cautionary tale about chaos and tolerances.

💬 Discussion

If the double pendulum and the Lorenz system are perfectly deterministic — same equations, same start, same future every time — then in what concrete sense are they "unpredictable"? Is the unpredictability a fact about the system, or a fact about us (our instruments, our computers, our knowledge)? Use the Lyapunov exponent in your answer, and say what would have to be true for chaos to be "predictable in principle."

Show a model response

Model response. The unpredictability is not in the equations — the flow is a deterministic map from state to future, so in a mathematical idealization with an exactly specified real-valued initial condition the trajectory is unique and "predictable in principle." The unpredictability is a fact about the interface between the system and us. Two things conspire. First, we can only ever know the initial state to finite precision δ0 (no instrument gives infinitely many digits). Second, the system has a positive Lyapunov exponent λ, so that uncertainty grows as δ0eλt. Prediction stays useful only for t ≈ (1/λ)ln(1/δ0), which grows merely logarithmically with our precision — so no realistic instrument upgrade buys meaningful forecast horizon. For chaos to be "predictable in principle" you would need either λ ≤ 0 (then it is not chaotic) or an exactly specified initial condition with infinite precision and exact arithmetic (no round-off, per Lecture 1) — both physically impossible. So chaos sits in a strange place: ontologically deterministic, epistemically unpredictable. The system is honest; our knowledge is finite.

Q1. The defining mathematical signature of deterministic chaos is…
A. a positive largest Lyapunov exponent (nearby trajectories separate exponentially)
B. random forcing terms added to the equations
C. trajectories that always diverge to infinity
D. energy that is not conserved
Show answer

A. Chaos is λ > 0 with bounded, aperiodic motion. (B) is wrong — chaos has no randomness; the equations are deterministic. (C) is wrong — chaotic trajectories stay bounded on an attractor. (D) is wrong — the double pendulum is chaotic yet conserves energy.

Q2. Why is the long-term behavior of a double pendulum impossible to predict in practice?
A. friction makes the motion irreversible
B. any tiny error in the initial angles grows exponentially in time
C. the equations of motion are unknown
D. the motion is genuinely random at the microscopic level
Show answer

B. Sensitive dependence: δ(t) ≈ δ0eλt. (A) is wrong — chaos appears even with no friction. (C) is wrong — the equations are known exactly (Lagrangian). (D) is wrong — it is deterministic, not random.

Q3. The Lorenz "butterfly" is best described as…
A. a periodic orbit the trajectory repeats every cycle
B. a snapshot of the fluid velocity field in real space
C. a strange attractor: a bounded, aperiodic, fractal set in state space
D. a single stable fixed point the system settles to
Show answer

C. It is an attractor (nearby paths are drawn onto it) that is fractal and aperiodic. (A) is wrong — it never exactly repeats. (B) is wrong — x,y,z are convection-mode amplitudes, not spatial positions. (D) is wrong — the foci are unstable; the system never settles.

Q4. You know a chaotic system's state to precision δ0 and its largest Lyapunov exponent is λ. The useful prediction horizon scales roughly as…
A. proportional to 1/δ0 (linear in precision)
B. proportional to λ (bigger λ means longer horizon)
C. independent of both δ0 and λ
D. (1/λ)·ln(1/δ0) — logarithmic in precision
Show answer

D. Setting δ0eλt ≈ 1 gives t ≈ (1/λ)ln(1/δ0). (A) is far too optimistic — the dependence is logarithmic, not linear. (B) has it backwards — larger λ shortens the horizon. (C) is wrong — both clearly matter.

Q5. When numerically estimating λ from two trajectories that start very close, why do we fit ln(separation) vs. time only over an intermediate window?
A. because separation grows exponentially forever and never saturates
B. early on the transient hasn't settled, and late on the separation saturates at the attractor's size
C. because solver round-off makes the early data the only reliable part
D. because the Lyapunov exponent is only defined for periodic orbits
Show answer

B. The clean exponential eλt regime is in the middle: after transients die out and before the separation reaches the attractor's diameter and flattens. (A) is wrong — it does saturate (bounded attractor). (C) is wrong — round-off is a danger throughout, not a reason to trust early data. (D) is wrong — λ is defined precisely for aperiodic chaotic motion.

✓ What you learned

  • Deterministic chaos is fully deterministic yet unpredictable: nearby trajectories separate as δ0eλt.
  • The double pendulum — two rods, four state variables, conserved energy — is chaotic for all but tiny swings.
  • The Lorenz system traces a bounded, aperiodic strange attractor (the butterfly) in state space, not real space.
  • The Lyapunov exponent λ quantifies chaos: λ > 0 means chaos, and 1/λ is the Lyapunov time.
  • Prediction horizon grows only as (1/λ)ln(1/δ0) — logarithmically — so more precision buys little time.
