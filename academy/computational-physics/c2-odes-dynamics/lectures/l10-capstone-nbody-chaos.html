Capstone: N-Body Simulator / Chaos & Lyapunov

Everything you've built — solvers, adaptive stepping, phase space, chaos — converges into one project that either keeps the planets in orbit or measures how fast a pendulum forgets where it started.

10.1The Capstone: Two Faces of the Same ODE

For nine weeks you assembled the toolkit: Euler and its error (Wk 2), Runge–Kutta (Wk 3), adaptive step control (Wk 4), systems and phase space (Wk 5), stiffness and implicit methods (Wk 6), and chaos in the double pendulum and Lorenz system (Wk 9). The capstone fuses all of it into a single program with a single demand: be right for a long time. You will pick one of two tracks, both of which are just initial-value problems q̈ = F(q)/m integrated forward in time.

Track A — N-body gravitational simulator. Integrate a small number of point masses (Sun + planets, or a three-body system) under mutual Newtonian gravity. The defining physics is conservation: total energy and angular momentum must not drift. The right tool is a symplectic integrator, and your headline diagnostic is the energy-drift curve over thousands of orbits.

Track B — Double-pendulum Lyapunov study. Integrate one chaotic double pendulum, then a second one launched a hair's-breadth away, and measure how fast they diverge. The defining physics is sensitive dependence: the separation grows exponentially. The right tool is a high-accuracy adaptive solver (RK4(5)), and your headline number is the estimated largest Lyapunov exponent.

q̈ = a(q),   a = F/m   (the whole capstone)Both tracks are second-order ODEs for positions q. Track A wants the geometry of the flow preserved; Track B wants the divergence of nearby flows measured.
AnalogyThe two tracks are the two questions you can ask of any long simulation. Track A is a marathon runner's heartbeat: does the system stay healthy mile after mile (does a conserved quantity hold)? Track B is a weather forecast: how many days until two almost-identical forecasts disagree completely (how fast do errors blow up)? One prizes stability; the other measures instability — and you need different instruments for each.

10.2Track A: Symplectic Integration & Energy Diagnostics

Gravity is conservative, so the true orbit conserves total energy E = T + U forever. A generic solver like RK4 does not respect this: its tiny per-step truncation errors are biased, so over thousands of orbits the energy drifts secularly (steadily up or down) and a bound planet slowly spirals in or flies off — even though each individual step looked accurate. The fix is a symplectic integrator, which preserves the phase-space geometry of Hamiltonian flow. The simplest is velocity Verlet (algebraically the leapfrog scheme):

v_half = v + (h/2) a(x)
x_new = x + h v_half
v_new = v_half + (h/2) a(x_new)One force evaluation per step (reuse a(x_new) next step), second-order accurate, and time-reversible — the property that bounds energy error.

The magic is not that energy is conserved exactly — it isn't. Instead a symplectic method conserves a nearby shadow Hamiltonian, so the energy error oscillates within a fixed band rather than drifting. Over a million steps RK4's energy walks away linearly while Verlet's stays in its lane. That bounded oscillation is exactly what you plot to prove your simulator is trustworthy.

Common mistakeJudging your N-body code by how "smooth" or "pretty" the orbit looks. A decaying orbit can look perfectly elliptical for hundreds of revolutions before it visibly spirals in. Always plot the conserved quantities (energy and angular momentum) versus time — a downward-sloping energy line is a failing grade no matter how nice the trajectory looks. Equally: do not soften gravity to mask a blow-up unless you state the softening length; an unphysical close encounter at h too large is a bug, not a feature.

10.3Track B: Chaos and the Lyapunov Exponent

A chaotic system shows sensitive dependence on initial conditions: two states that start separated by a tiny distance d0 drift apart exponentially fast. Quantify it with the largest Lyapunov exponent lambda. If d(t) is the separation between two trajectories,

d(t) ~ d0 e^(lambda t)  =>   lambda = lim (1/t) ln( d(t)/d0 )lambda > 0 is the signature of chaos. Its reciprocal 1/lambda is the "predictability horizon" — the time for errors to grow by a factor e.

You cannot just let d(t) grow forever: once the trajectories are far apart, the separation saturates at the size of the attractor and the exponential law breaks. The standard fix is the Benettin renormalization method: integrate both trajectories for a short interval, measure the separation, accumulate ln(d/d0), then pull the second trajectory back along the same direction to distance d0 and repeat. Averaging the per-step logarithmic stretch gives lambda:

lambda ~ (1/(N tau)) * sum_k ln( d_k / d0 )tau = renormalization interval, N steps, d_k = separation at end of step k before rescaling. Keeps the perturbation infinitesimal so you measure the local stretch rate, not the attractor's diameter.
AnalogyThink of two dye droplets dripped a micron apart into a stirred coffee. For a moment they move together; then the swirling pulls them apart, doubling their gap again and again until they're smeared across the whole cup and can't separate further. The doubling rate while they're still close is lambda; the cup's diameter is the saturation ceiling. Benettin's trick is to keep re-injecting the droplets a micron apart so you always measure the local stretch, never the cup.

10.4Framing the Deliverable

A capstone is graded on whether the science is defensible, not on lines of code. Whatever track you pick, the deliverable is three things: working code, two or three diagnostic figures, and a one-page report. The report must answer four questions a reviewer always asks.

The four questions every report must answer

1. What method, and why? Name the integrator (velocity Verlet for Track A; RK4(5) adaptive for Track B) and state its order and the property you rely on (symplecticity / high local accuracy). 2. What is the error budget? For Track A, the energy-drift band; for Track B, the fit window and uncertainty on lambda. 3. What is the result? A bounded-energy stable orbit, or a positive lambda with a number and units. 4. How did you validate it? A convergence check: halve h (Track A) and confirm the energy band shrinks by ~4x (second order); or vary d0 and tau (Track B) and confirm lambda is stable.

deliverable = code + diagnostics(conservation OR divergence) + report(method, error, result, validation)The diagnostic plot is the centerpiece — it is the evidence that converts a running program into a scientific result.

Scope it realistically: Track A with the inner Solar System or a figure-eight three-body orbit is plenty; Track B needs only a single double pendulum. Resist adding bodies or features until the diagnostics are clean. A small, correct, well-validated simulation beats a sprawling one whose energy quietly drifts.

✦ Worked Example — estimate a Lyapunov exponent and its horizon

A double-pendulum run starts two trajectories at separation d0 = 1×10⁻⁹ (radians, in state-space norm). After renormalizing every tau = 0.5 s for N = 4 intervals, the measured end-of-interval separations (before each rescale) are d = [3.0×10⁻⁹, 2.7×10⁻⁹, 3.3×10⁻⁹, 2.5×10⁻⁹]. Estimate lambda and the predictability horizon.

  1. Per-interval log stretch: ln(d_k/d0) for each: ln(3.0)=1.099, ln(2.7)=0.993, ln(3.3)=1.194, ln(2.5)=0.916.
  2. Sum: 1.099 + 0.993 + 1.194 + 0.916 = 4.202.
  3. Average rate: lambda = (1/(N·tau))·sum = 4.202 / (4 × 0.5 s) = 4.202 / 2.0 s = 2.1 s⁻¹.
  4. Sanity check: lambda > 0, so the system is chaotic — consistent with the divergence we saw in Week 9.
  5. Predictability horizon: 1/lambda ≈ 0.48 s — the time for an uncertainty to grow by a factor e. To predict 10× longer you'd need ln(10)/lambda ≈ 1.1 s more of perfect knowledge; errors of 10⁻⁹ swell to order-1 in only ~10/lambda ≈ 10 s. That is the quantitative meaning of "the double pendulum is unpredictable."

🛠️ Hands-On: a symplectic N-body core with an energy diagnostic

This uses the same free Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (the install details lives in Lecture 1) — no new tools needed. For the heavy inner loops, Numba (optional, introduced earlier for speed) can JIT the acceleration function, but it is not required for a small system.

Below is a complete velocity-Verlet integrator for N bodies under gravity, with the all-important energy diagnostic. Run it, then plot energy vs time and confirm the relative drift stays in a bounded band.

# nbody_verlet.py — Capstone Track A core
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

G = 1.0                                  # work in scaled units
m = np.array([1.0, 3e-6, 9e-7])          # Sun, Earth-like, Venus-like
x = np.array([[0,0],[1.0,0],[0.72,0]])   # initial positions
v = np.array([[0,0],[0,1.0],[0,1.18]])   # initial velocities

def accel(x):
    a = np.zeros_like(x)
    for i in range(len(x)):
        for j in range(len(x)):
            if i == j: continue
            d = x[j] - x[i]
            r = np.hypot(*d) + 1e-9       # softening avoids /0
            a[i] += G * m[j] * d / r**3
    return a

def energy(x, v):
    T = 0.5 * np.sum(m * np.sum(v**2, axis=1))
    U = 0.0
    for i in range(len(x)):
        for j in range(i+1, len(x)):
            U -= G*m[i]*m[j] / (np.hypot(*(x[j]-x[i])) + 1e-9)
    return T + U

h, steps = 1e-3, 200_000
E = np.empty(steps); xs = np.empty((steps, len(x), 2))
a = accel(x)
for k in range(steps):                   # velocity Verlet
    v += 0.5*h*a
    x += h*v
    a = accel(x)
    v += 0.5*h*a
    E[k] = energy(x, v); xs[k] = x

E0 = E[0]
print("max relative energy drift:", np.max(np.abs((E-E0)/E0)))
plt.plot((E-E0)/abs(E0)); plt.xlabel("step"); plt.ylabel("dE/E")
plt.title("Energy drift (should be bounded, not secular)"); plt.show()

Now the experiment: replace the Verlet loop with scipy.integrate.solve_ivp(..., method='RK45') over the same span and overlay its dE/E. You should see RK45 drift while Verlet stays bounded. For Track B, reuse your Week-9 double-pendulum RHS with solve_ivp, launch a twin at y0 + 1e-9, and accumulate ln(sep/d0) with renormalization every tau to estimate lambda.

Q1. Why is a symplectic integrator (velocity Verlet) preferred over RK4 for a long N-body gravitational run?
A. Its energy error stays in a bounded band instead of drifting secularly
B. It is higher order, so each step is more accurate
C. It conserves total energy exactly to machine precision
D. It never requires evaluating the gravitational force
Show answer

A. Symplectic methods preserve phase-space geometry, conserving a shadow Hamiltonian, so energy error oscillates within a bound rather than walking away. B is false (Verlet is only 2nd order, lower than RK4); C is false (energy is not conserved exactly, only bounded); D is false (it still needs the force a(x) each step).

Q2. A positive largest Lyapunov exponent lambda for a system means…
A. the system is at a stable fixed point
B. energy is being added to the system over time
C. nearby trajectories separate exponentially — the system is chaotic
D. the integrator is unstable and the step size is too large
Show answer

C. lambda > 0 is the defining signature of sensitive dependence on initial conditions. A would give lambda < 0 (contraction); B confuses a dynamical-systems quantity with thermodynamics; D is a numerical artifact, not the physical exponent (and a real chaotic lambda persists as you refine h).

Q3. In the Benettin renormalization method, why do you pull the second trajectory back to separation d0 after each interval?
A. To keep the perturbation infinitesimal so you measure the local stretch rate, not the attractor's size
B. To save memory by storing fewer points
C. To force the two trajectories to converge
D. Because solve_ivp cannot integrate two trajectories at once
Show answer

A. Without rescaling, the separation saturates at the attractor diameter and the exponential law breaks; renormalizing keeps the gap small so each interval measures the true local divergence rate. B and D are irrelevant/false; C is backward — chaotic trajectories diverge, they are not forced to converge.

Q4. Your N-body orbit looks like a clean ellipse for 300 revolutions. What is the correct way to judge whether the simulation is trustworthy?
A. Trust it — a smooth-looking orbit is sufficient evidence
B. Plot total energy and angular momentum vs. time and check they don't drift
C. Increase the number of bodies until it looks more realistic
D. Use a larger step size so the run finishes faster
Show answer

B. A decaying orbit can look elliptical for hundreds of revolutions before visibly spiraling in; the conserved quantities are the real test. A is the exact mistake the lecture warns against; C adds complexity without validating anything; D makes accuracy and stability worse.

Q5. A double pendulum has lambda ≈ 2.0 s⁻¹. Roughly how long until an initial uncertainty grows by a factor of e?
A. about 2.0 s
B. about 20 s
C. it never grows; lambda > 0 means errors shrink
D. about 0.5 s
Show answer

D. The predictability horizon is 1/lambda = 1/2.0 ≈ 0.5 s — the e-folding time. A inverts the relationship wrongly (that would need lambda = 0.5); B is off by an order of magnitude; C is false — positive lambda means errors grow, not shrink.
