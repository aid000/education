Symplectic Integrators: Verlet & Leapfrog

RK4 is more accurate per step — yet a planet integrated with it slowly spirals into the sun, while a humble two-line update keeps the orbit closed for a billion years. This is the secret of symplectic integration.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why even RK4 — accurate per step — secularly drifts in energy for conservative (Hamiltonian) systems.
  • Derive the velocity-Verlet and leapfrog updates from a Taylor expansion and a symmetric kick–drift structure.
  • State what "symplectic" means: exact preservation of phase-space volume, and conservation of a nearby shadow Hamiltonian.
  • Explain why symplectic methods keep energy bounded and oscillating over arbitrarily long runs rather than drifting.
  • Implement velocity-Verlet in NumPy and compare its long-term energy behavior against RK4 on a Kepler orbit.

7.1Why Accurate-Per-Step Is Not Enough

A conservative mechanical system — a planet, a pendulum, a molecule with no friction — is governed by a Hamiltonian H(q, p), the total energy as a function of position q and momentum p. Hamilton's equations move a point through phase space:

dq/dt = ∂H/∂p,   dp/dt = −∂H/∂qThe flow exactly conserves H: energy is constant, and orbits are closed curves of constant H.

Now hand this to RK4. Lecture-by-lecture you learned RK4 has a tiny local error of order h5, so its solution tracks the true trajectory beautifully over a few periods. But RK4 was never designed to respect the geometry of Hamiltonian flow. Its energy error does not stay bounded — it accumulates a small systematic bias each orbit, so the total energy drifts monotonically. Over millions of periods the modeled planet either spirals inward or flings outward, even though every single step looked superbly accurate.

The lesson of this week: for long-time integration of conservative systems, what matters is not per-step accuracy but whether the integrator preserves the underlying structure. A method that conserves that structure exactly — to machine precision, forever — is called symplectic.

AnalogyRK4 is a sprinter: blazing speed over a short distance, but it slowly veers off the track. A symplectic integrator is a marathon runner who stays a fixed distance from the centerline lap after lap. Per lap the sprinter is closer to perfect; over ten thousand laps the marathoner is the only one still on the track.

7.2The Verlet and Leapfrog Updates

Consider a particle with H = p²/(2m) + V(q), so the force is a(q) = −V′(q)/m. The velocity-Verlet scheme advances position and velocity together using the acceleration at the start and end of the step:

xn+1 = xn + vn h + ½ an h²
vn+1 = vn + ½ (an + an+1) hOne force evaluation per step (reuse a_{n+1} as next step's a_n). Second-order accurate, error per step ~ h³.

The mathematically identical leapfrog form stores the velocity at the half-steps, so positions and velocities "leap" over one another:

vn+1/2 = vn + ½ an h
xn+1 = xn + vn+1/2 h
vn+1 = vn+1/2 + ½ an+1 h"Kick–drift–kick." Algebraically equal to velocity-Verlet; the half-step velocities reveal the symmetry.

The crucial structural property is that each line is a shear map in phase space: a "drift" updates x using fixed v, a "kick" updates v using fixed x. Each shear is area-preserving, and a composition of area-preserving maps is area-preserving — that is exactly the symplectic condition. RK4, by contrast, mixes the stages in a way that does not preserve area.

7.3Phase-Space Volume and the Shadow Hamiltonian

Liouville's theorem says the true Hamiltonian flow preserves phase-space volume: a blob of initial conditions can stretch and fold but never changes its area. A symplectic integrator reproduces this exactly, step by step, because each kick and drift is a shear with unit Jacobian determinant.

det(∂(xn+1, vn+1) / ∂(xn, vn)) = 1Symplectic map: the phase-space area element is preserved to machine precision, for every step.

The deep payoff comes from backward error analysis. A symplectic method does not solve the original Hamiltonian H exactly — but it does solve a nearby shadow (modified) Hamiltonian H̃ = H + h² H₂ + h⁴ H₄ + … almost exactly, for exponentially long times. Because the method conserves H̃, and H̃ differs from H by only O(h²), the true energy H cannot wander: it merely oscillates within a band of width O(h²) set by the shadow.

|H(t) − H(0)| ≤ C·h²  for all t (no secular drift)Energy is bounded forever. Compare RK4: error grows roughly linearly with t (and the orbit slowly decays or expands).

7.4The Trade-off: Fixed Step, Superb Long-Term Stability

Symplectic integrators are not free lunch — they come with constraints you must respect:

  • Fixed step size. The bounded-energy guarantee relies on the map being the same symplectic map every step. Naive adaptive step control breaks the symplectic structure and reintroduces drift — so most symplectic codes run at a constant h (or use special time-symmetric / regularized step schemes).
  • Only second-order accurate (Verlet/leapfrog). Per step it is less accurate than RK4. For a few periods RK4 wins on raw accuracy; the symplectic method wins decisively only over many periods, where bounded energy beats shrinking-but-drifting error.
  • Separable / conservative form. The clean kick–drift split needs H = T(p) + V(q). Velocity-dependent forces (magnetic, drag) require modified schemes.

This is why molecular dynamics, planetary ephemerides, particle accelerators, and galaxy simulations almost universally use Verlet/leapfrog: they integrate the same system for billions of steps, and only bounded energy makes the long-time statistics (temperature, orbital resonances, phase-space distributions) physically trustworthy.

Common mistakeBolting standard adaptive step-size control onto Verlet to "get more accuracy." Changing h every step destroys the constant-map property that guarantees bounded energy — you lose the very thing that made the method worth using, and the secular drift comes right back. If you need adaptivity, use a purpose-built symmetric/reversible step controller, not a generic error-tolerance one.
✦ Worked Example — one velocity-Verlet step on a harmonic oscillator

Take a unit spring: m = 1, k = 1, so a(x) = −x and H = ½(v² + x²). Start at x₀ = 1, v₀ = 0 (energy H₀ = 0.5). Take one Verlet step with h = 0.1 and check the energy.

  1. Initial acceleration: a₀ = −x₀ = −1.
  2. Position update: x₁ = x₀ + v₀ h + ½ a₀ h² = 1 + 0 + ½(−1)(0.01) = 0.995.
  3. New acceleration: a₁ = −x₁ = −0.995.
  4. Velocity update: v₁ = v₀ + ½(a₀ + a₁)h = 0 + ½(−1 − 0.995)(0.1) = −0.09975.
  5. Energy check: H₁ = ½(v₁² + x₁²) = ½(0.009950 + 0.990025) = 0.4999875. The error is about −1.25×10⁻⁵ — tiny, and crucially it will oscillate around 0.5 forever rather than drift. (One RK4 step would be closer this instant, but its error grows secularly over many periods.)

🛠️ Hands-On: Verlet vs RK4 on a Kepler orbit

Uses the free Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib stack from the Week 1–2 setup (the one-time install details lives in the early lectures — reuse that environment here). No new tools needed: we hand-code Verlet and compare it to SciPy's RK4 via solve_ivp.

Integrate a 2-D Kepler orbit (inverse-square gravity, GM = 1) for many periods and plot the energy over time. Watch RK4 drift while Verlet stays flat.

# verlet_vs_rk4.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

GM = 1.0
def accel(r):                       # inverse-square gravity
    d = np.linalg.norm(r)
    return -GM * r / d**3

def energy(r, v):
    return 0.5*np.dot(v, v) - GM/np.linalg.norm(r)

# mildly eccentric orbit: r=1, v slightly below circular
r0 = np.array([1.0, 0.0]); v0 = np.array([0.0, 0.9])
h, n_steps = 0.01, 200_000          # ~ many orbital periods

# --- velocity-Verlet (symplectic) ---
r, v, a = r0.copy(), v0.copy(), accel(r0)
E_verlet = []
for _ in range(n_steps):
    r = r + v*h + 0.5*a*h**2        # drift
    a_new = accel(r)
    v = v + 0.5*(a + a_new)*h        # kick
    a = a_new
    E_verlet.append(energy(r, v))

# --- RK4 via solve_ivp for the same span ---
def rhs(t, y):
    r_, v_ = y[:2], y[2:]
    return np.concatenate([v_, accel(r_)])
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, [0, h*n_steps], np.concatenate([r0, v0]),
                method="RK45", rtol=1e-9, atol=1e-12, dense_output=True)
ts = np.linspace(0, h*n_steps, n_steps)
ys = sol.sol(ts)
E_rk = [energy(ys[:2, i], ys[2:, i]) for i in range(n_steps)]

plt.plot(ts, np.array(E_rk)  - E_rk[0],     label="RK4 (drifts)")
plt.plot(ts, np.array(E_verlet) - E_verlet[0], label="Verlet (bounded)")
plt.xlabel("time"); plt.ylabel("energy error"); plt.legend(); plt.show()
# Verlet: a flat oscillating band ~h^2.  RK4: a slow systematic drift.

Now halve h to 0.005 and rerun. The Verlet energy band should shrink by about 4× (it is O(h²)), while its qualitative shape — bounded oscillation, no drift — is unchanged. Then plot the (x, y) trajectory: Verlet's ellipse stays closed; RK4's slowly spirals.

Q1. Why does RK4 perform poorly for very long integrations of a conservative orbit, despite its small per-step error?
A. Its local error is actually larger than Verlet's
B. It is not symplectic, so energy accumulates a systematic drift over many periods
C. It cannot handle inverse-square forces
D. It requires complex arithmetic
Show answer

B. RK4 has excellent local accuracy but does not preserve phase-space structure, so energy drifts secularly. (A) is false — RK4's per-step error is much smaller (~h⁵). (C) and (D) are irrelevant; RK4 handles such forces fine and uses real arithmetic.

Q2. The velocity-Verlet / leapfrog scheme is built from "kick" and "drift" sub-steps. Geometrically, each sub-step is…
A. a rotation that changes phase-space area
B. a random perturbation
C. a shear map with unit Jacobian, so it preserves phase-space area
D. a projection that discards a coordinate
Show answer

C. A drift updates x at fixed v and a kick updates v at fixed x; each is a shear with determinant 1, so the composition preserves area (symplectic). (A) is wrong because shears, not rotations, are used and area is preserved. (B) and (D) are not what the deterministic update does.

Q3. Backward error analysis shows a symplectic integrator exactly (for very long times) conserves…
A. a nearby "shadow" Hamiltonian H̃ = H + O(h²), which keeps the true energy bounded
B. the exact original Hamiltonian H with zero error
C. the total angular momentum but never the energy
D. nothing — it just happens to drift slowly
Show answer

A. It nearly conserves a modified Hamiltonian H̃ differing from H by O(h²); since H̃ is conserved, the true H stays within an O(h²) band. (B) is false — there is a small O(h²) offset. (C) misstates it (energy is what's bounded). (D) contradicts the bounded-energy result.

Q4. A student adds standard adaptive step-size control to a leapfrog integrator to boost accuracy. The most likely consequence is…
A. energy is conserved even better
B. the integrator becomes fourth-order automatically
C. the symplectic structure is broken and secular energy drift returns
D. the orbit reverses direction
Show answer

C. The bounded-energy guarantee relies on applying the same symplectic map each step; changing h every step destroys that and the drift returns. (A) is the opposite of what happens. (B) order is unrelated to step control. (D) is nonsense.

Q5. Velocity-Verlet is a second-order method. If you halve the step size h, the width of its long-term energy-error band changes by roughly a factor of…
A. 2 (it halves)
B. unchanged
C. 16
D. 4 (it shrinks by ~4×)
Show answer

D. The energy band is O(h²), so halving h shrinks it by 2² = 4. (A) would be a first-order method; (C) would be fourth-order; (B) is wrong because the band does depend on h.

✓ What you learned

  • Even RK4 drifts in energy for conservative systems because it ignores the symplectic geometry of Hamiltonian flow.
  • The velocity-Verlet and leapfrog updates — algebraically identical — advance state via symmetric kick–drift shears.
  • Each shear has unit Jacobian, so the method preserves phase-space volume exactly (Liouville's theorem), step after step.
  • It conserves a nearby shadow Hamiltonian, keeping the true energy bounded in an O(h²) band with no secular drift.
  • The trade-off: fixed step and only second-order per step, bought for superb long-term orbit stability — verified in NumPy vs RK4.
