Week 3 · Lecture 3

Runge-Kutta Methods (RK4)

Euler trusts a single slope at the start of a step; RK4 scouts ahead, samples four slopes, and blends them — buying four orders of accuracy for the price of four function calls.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why one slope estimate per step (Euler) is crude, and how sampling several slopes within a step does better.
  • Write down the classic fourth-order Runge-Kutta (RK4) formulas and interpret each stage k₁…k₄ geometrically.
  • Understand why RK4's weighted average of slopes lifts the global error to O(h⁴) — gaining a factor of 16 each time h is halved.
  • Read a Butcher tableau and recognize that Euler, midpoint, and RK4 are all members of one Runge-Kutta family.
  • Weigh accuracy against cost, and implement RK4 by hand and in NumPy / SciPy for a real dynamical system.

3.1One Slope Is Not Enough

We want to advance the solution of y′ = f(t, y) from a known point (tₙ, yₙ) across a step of width h. The exact change in y over that step is the integral of the slope f along the way — but we don't yet know the path, so we don't know the slope everywhere. Euler's method (Week 2) makes the boldest possible guess: it assumes the slope at the start of the step is good enough for the whole step.

yn+1 = yn + h · f(tn, yn)Euler: one slope sample, taken at the left edge of the step. Global error O(h) — first order.

If the true solution curves at all, that one slope is wrong almost immediately, and the error per step is O(h²) locally, piling up to O(h) globally. The Runge-Kutta family attacks this by sampling the slope at several points inside the step, then combining those samples into one effective slope that matches the true average far better. No new derivatives of f are needed — only extra evaluations of f itself, which is what makes the idea so practical.

AnalogyImagine steering a boat across a river with a shifting current. Euler reads the current only at the near bank and holds that heading the whole way — and lands downstream of the target. A Runge-Kutta navigator instead takes a tentative half-crossing, re-reads the current at midstream (twice), then again near the far bank, and steers by a weighted average of all those readings. Same number of glances, dramatically straighter line.

3.2The Classic RK4 Method

The most famous member of the family is the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method, RK4. In each step it computes four slope estimates and combines them. Reading them in order tells a story:

k1 = f(tn, yn)
k2 = f(tn + h/2, yn + (h/2)·k1)
k3 = f(tn + h/2, yn + (h/2)·k2)
k4 = f(tn + h, yn + h·k3) k₁ = slope at the start · k₂, k₃ = two midpoint slopes · k₄ = slope at the end (using k₃ to get there).

The update is the weighted average of these four, with the two midpoint slopes counting double:

yn+1 = yn + (h/6)·(k1 + 2k2 + 2k3 + k4)Weights 1, 2, 2, 1 sum to 6. This is exactly Simpson's rule applied to the slope across the step.

Notice the weights 1 : 2 : 2 : 1. They are not arbitrary — they are precisely the weights of Simpson's rule for integrating a function using its value at the two endpoints and (twice) the midpoint. RK4 is, in spirit, "integrate the slope across the step with Simpson's rule, but discover the midpoint and endpoint slopes by bootstrapping from the slopes you already computed."

3.3Why Averaging Slopes Buys O(h⁴)

The deep reason RK4 works is a Taylor-series match. Expand the true solution y(tₙ + h) in powers of h. The exact answer contains terms in h, h², h³, h⁴, and beyond. Each new slope sample inside the step gives the method another tunable coefficient, and the stage points (½, ½, 1) and weights (1, 2, 2, 1) are chosen so that the method's Taylor expansion agrees with the true one through the h⁴ term. The first mismatch is at h⁵.

local error per step = O(h5)  ⟹   global error = O(h4)The per-step (local truncation) error is one power higher than the accumulated global error, because you take ~1/h steps to cross a fixed interval.

That "global is one order below local" rule is general for one-step methods: a local error of O(hp+1) accumulates over N = (b−a)/h steps into a global error of O(hp). For RK4, p = 4. The payoff in the order of convergence from Lecture 1 is dramatic:

error ≈ C · h4  ⟹   halving h cuts the error by 24 = 16Compare Euler (p = 1): halving h only halves the error. RK4 reaches a target accuracy with vastly fewer, larger steps.
Common mistakeRK4 being "fourth order" does not mean it is exact, nor that it stays fourth order forever. (1) The order describes truncation error only; push h tiny enough and round-off (Lecture 1) dominates, so error stops falling and eventually rises — there is still a best h. (2) For stiff problems (Week 6) RK4 can blow up unless h is absurdly small; high order does not imply stability. Order and stability are separate properties.

3.4The Butcher Tableau & the Accuracy–Cost Trade-off

Every explicit Runge-Kutta method is fully specified by its stage offsets, its inner coefficients, and its final weights. These are organized in a compact grid called a Butcher tableau: the column on the left holds the time offsets ci, the lower-left block holds the coefficients aij that say how much of each earlier slope feeds the next stage, and the bottom row holds the final averaging weights bi.

0  |
1/2 |  1/2
1/2 |  0   1/2
1   |  0   0   1
-----+----------------
    |  1/6 2/6 2/6 1/6 The RK4 tableau. Read the bottom row as the 1:2:2:1 weights; the c-column 0, 1/2, 1/2, 1 as where each slope is sampled in time.

This little grid is a unifying lens. Euler is the one-stage tableau (c = 0, b = 1). The midpoint method is a two-stage, second-order tableau. RK4 is the four-stage classic above. Higher-order schemes (RK5, RK8) and the embedded adaptive pairs of next week are just larger tableaus. Once you can read a tableau, you can read any explicit Runge-Kutta solver in a library.

What does the extra accuracy cost?

Each RK4 step needs four evaluations of f, versus one for Euler. So at the same step size, RK4 costs 4× more per step. The point is that you almost never run at the same step size: because RK4 is O(h⁴), it reaches a given accuracy with enormously larger h, so it wins overwhelmingly in total work for any non-trivial accuracy target.

cost ≈ (stages per step) × (number of steps)RK4: 4 evals/step but very few steps. Euler: 1 eval/step but a huge number of steps. RK4 wins for any serious accuracy.

There is a known wall, though: for explicit methods, you cannot get order p with only p stages once p > 4. Reaching order 5 needs at least 6 evaluations — the famous "order barrier." That diminishing return is exactly why RK4 is the sweet spot and the default first choice across physics, engineering, and graphics.

✦ Worked Example — one RK4 step by hand

Solve y′ = f(t, y) = y (so the exact answer is y = et) starting from y(0) = 1. Take a single RK4 step of size h = 0.5 and compare to the exact value e0.5 ≈ 1.648721.

  1. k₁ = f(0, 1) = 1.
  2. k₂ = f(0 + 0.25, 1 + 0.25·1) = f(0.25, 1.25) = 1.25.
  3. k₃ = f(0.25, 1 + 0.25·1.25) = f(0.25, 1.3125) = 1.3125.
  4. k₄ = f(0.5, 1 + 0.5·1.3125) = f(0.5, 1.65625) = 1.65625.
  5. Combine: y₁ = 1 + (0.5/6)·(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄) = 1 + (0.0833…)·(1 + 2.5 + 2.625 + 1.65625) = 1 + 0.0833…·7.78125 = 1.648438.
  6. Error: |1.648438 − 1.648721| ≈ 2.8×10⁻⁴. A single Euler step here gives 1 + 0.5·1 = 1.5, an error of 0.149 — RK4 is ~500× more accurate from the same starting data and the same step.

🛠️ Hands-On: RK4 from scratch, then check the order

Use the free Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib stack from the Week 1 setup (the one-time install details lives in Lecture 1 of C1 — no new tools are needed here). We'll code RK4 ourselves, integrate the harmonic oscillator x″ = −x written as a first-order system, and confirm the O(h⁴) slope numerically against SciPy's solve_ivp.

# rk4.py — Lecture 3: classic RK4 and an order check
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

def rk4_step(f, t, y, h):
    k1 = f(t, y)
    k2 = f(t + h/2, y + h/2 * k1)
    k3 = f(t + h/2, y + h/2 * k2)
    k4 = f(t + h,   y + h   * k3)
    return y + (h/6) * (k1 + 2*k2 + 2*k3 + k4)

def integrate(f, y0, t0, t1, h):
    n = int(round((t1 - t0) / h))
    t, y = t0, np.array(y0, float)
    for _ in range(n):
        y = rk4_step(f, t, y, h); t += h
    return y

# harmonic oscillator: x'' = -x  ->  state u = [x, v], u' = [v, -x]
def f(t, u):
    return np.array([u[1], -u[0]])

u0 = [1.0, 0.0]                       # x=1, v=0  ->  exact x(t)=cos(t)
T  = 2*np.pi                          # integrate one full period
exact = np.array([np.cos(T), -np.sin(T)])

print("h         error        ratio")
prev = None
for h in [0.4, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05]:
    err = np.linalg.norm(integrate(f, u0, 0.0, T, h) - exact)
    ratio = (prev/err) if prev else float('nan')
    print(f"{h:<8} {err:.3e}   {ratio:.1f}")   # ratio -> ~16 confirms O(h^4)
    prev = err

# SciPy's adaptive RK45 (next week's idea) for comparison:
sol = solve_ivp(f, [0, T], u0, method='RK45', rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)
print("solve_ivp final:", sol.y[:, -1], " exact:", exact)

Watch the ratio column converge to ~16 as h halves — that is O(h⁴) in the wild. Then shrink h toward 10⁻⁴ and beyond: the error stops improving and starts rising as round-off (Lecture 1) takes over. Try swapping in a single Euler step instead and watch the ratio collapse to ~2.

💬 Discussion

RK4 evaluates the right-hand side f four times per step, while Euler evaluates it once. Yet practitioners overwhelmingly reach for RK4 (or its adaptive cousins) as the default. Suppose a colleague argues: "For my simulation, function evaluations are the expensive part, so I should use Euler with a tiny step instead of RK4 — fewer evaluations per step means cheaper." Where does this reasoning go wrong, and under what (rare) circumstances might Euler-like, low-order methods actually be the right call? Frame your answer in terms of order of convergence, total cost, and the kind of problem being solved.

Show a model response

The colleague is confusing cost per step with total cost to reach an accuracy. Total cost is (evaluations per step) × (number of steps), and the number of steps is set by the accuracy you need, not by taste. To hit error ε, Euler (O(h)) needs h ∝ ε, i.e. roughly 1/ε steps; RK4 (O(h⁴)) needs only h ∝ ε^(1/4), i.e. ~1/ε^(1/4) steps. Multiply by evaluations per step (1 vs 4): for any demanding ε the RK4 product is vastly smaller. Concretely, going from ε = 10⁻² to 10⁻⁶ multiplies Euler's step count by ~10⁴ but RK4's by only ~10. So "fewer evaluations per step" is a false economy — Euler pays it back many times over in step count, and also accumulates more round-off across those millions of tiny steps.

When might low order genuinely win? (1) When you only need crude accuracy (a quick sketch, a game physics tick where "looks plausible" suffices). (2) When f is extremely cheap but you need symplectic/structure-preserving behavior over long times — there a simple symplectic Euler or leapfrog (Week 5) can beat plain RK4 on energy drift, despite lower order. (3) For stiff problems, where the limiting factor is stability, not truncation order, and an implicit low-order method outperforms any explicit high-order one. The lesson: order, cost, and stability are three separate axes — choose the method for the problem, not by reflex.

Q1. In RK4, what are the weights applied to the four slope estimates k₁, k₂, k₃, k₄ in the final update?
A. 1 : 1 : 1 : 1 (a plain average)
B. 1 : 2 : 2 : 1, divided by 6
C. 1 : 3 : 3 : 1, divided by 8
D. 1 : 4 : 1 : 0, divided by 6
Show answer

B. The update is (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄) — the two midpoint slopes are weighted double, and the weights sum to 6 (Simpson's rule). A plain average (A) would not match the Taylor series to fourth order; 1:3:3:1 (C) are binomial, not RK4; (D) discards k₄.

Q2. RK4 has global error O(h⁴). If you halve the step size h, the global error is reduced by a factor of about…
A. 2
B. 4
C. 8
D. 16
Show answer

D. A method of order p improves by 2ᵖ when h is halved; for p = 4 that is 2⁴ = 16. Factor 2 (A) is first-order Euler; 4 (B) is second order; 8 (C) is third order.

Q3. The local (per-step) truncation error of RK4 is O(h⁵), yet its global error is O(h⁴). Why is the global order one lower?
A. Round-off error adds exactly one power of h
B. Crossing a fixed interval takes ~1/h steps, so per-step errors of O(h⁵) accumulate to O(h⁴)
C. The midpoint stages are only second-order accurate
D. Global error is always defined as local error squared
Show answer

B. You take N = (b−a)/h ∝ 1/h steps, so a per-step error O(h⁵) accumulates to roughly (1/h)·O(h⁵) = O(h⁴). This local→global drop of one order is general for one-step methods. (A) misstates round-off; (C)/(D) are not the mechanism.

Q4. A Butcher tableau for an explicit Runge-Kutta method encodes which of the following?
A. The stage time-offsets, the inner stage coefficients, and the final averaging weights
B. Only the number of function evaluations per step
C. The round-off error and machine epsilon of the hardware
D. The exact analytic solution of the ODE
Show answer

A. The tableau lists the c-offsets (when each slope is sampled), the a-coefficients (how earlier slopes feed later stages), and the b-weights (the final blend). It is a complete recipe for the method — Euler, midpoint, and RK4 are all just different tableaus. (B) is only the stage count; (C)/(D) are unrelated.

Q5. Why is RK4 usually preferred over Euler even though it costs four f-evaluations per step instead of one?
A. Because RK4 is exact for any ODE
B. Because RK4 is unconditionally stable for stiff problems
C. Because its fourth-order accuracy lets it reach a target error with far larger steps, so the total work is much lower
D. Because RK4 never accumulates round-off error
Show answer

C. Total cost = evals/step × number of steps; RK4's O(h⁴) accuracy lets h be much larger, so far fewer steps are needed and it wins overall. (A) no method is exact in general; (B) RK4 is explicit and can blow up on stiff problems; (D) round-off still accumulates and eventually limits accuracy.

✓ What you learned

  • Euler trusts one slope per step; the Runge-Kutta family samples several slopes within a step and blends them — no extra derivatives needed.
  • RK4 computes k₁…k₄ (start, two midpoints, end) and updates with the Simpson weights (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄).
  • Matching the Taylor series through h⁴ gives local error O(h⁵), global error O(h⁴) — halving h cuts error by 16.
  • A Butcher tableau (c-offsets, a-coefficients, b-weights) is the universal recipe; Euler, midpoint, and RK4 are all members.
  • The accuracy–cost trade-off: 4 evals/step but far fewer steps make RK4 the default workhorse — while order, stability, and round-off remain separate concerns.
