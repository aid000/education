Week 5 · Lecture 5

Systems of ODEs & Phase Space

Stop watching a single number wiggle in time — watch the whole state of a system trace a living curve through the space of all its possibilities.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Rewrite any high-order ODE as a first-order system and integrate it as a single vector equation.
  • Define phase space and read a phase portrait as the family of all trajectories of a system.
  • Locate fixed points and classify their stability from the eigenvalues of the Jacobian.
  • Recognize the closed elliptical orbits of the harmonic oscillator and the energy-decay spiral of a damped one.
  • Integrate a coupled system in Python with solve_ivp and plot its trajectory in state space.

5.1From One Equation to a Vector: Coupled Systems

Real dynamics rarely involve a single quantity evolving alone. A planet has a position and a velocity; a chemical reactor has several concentrations; an ecosystem has predators and prey. Each variable's rate of change depends on the others — they are coupled. The unifying trick of this whole course is that every such problem, no matter how many variables or how high the order of the derivatives, can be written as one first-order system of the form:

dy/dt = f(t, y),   y = (y1, y2, ..., yn)One vector unknown y(t), one vector-valued right-hand side f. The state lives in n dimensions.

The key move is order reduction: a second-order equation becomes two first-order equations by naming the velocity as a new variable. Newton's law for a mass on a spring, m·x″ = −k·x, has a second derivative. Introduce v = x′ and the single second-order equation splits into a coupled pair:

x' = v    v' = -(k/m) xState y = (x, v). Now everything is first-order, exactly the form a solver wants.

This is not a cosmetic rewrite. A solver like Runge–Kutta from Week 3 was built for dy/dt = f(t, y); feeding it a vector y and a vector f makes it integrate the whole coupled system in lockstep, advancing every component with the same step. The same nine lines that integrated one scalar equation now integrate a six-dimensional planetary orbit.

AnalogyThink of the state vector y as the full readout of a cockpit dashboard at one instant — altitude, heading, fuel, airspeed, all at once. A scalar ODE tracks one dial; a system tracks the entire panel. The function f is the flight physics that says how every dial is turning right now, given where all the dials currently sit.

5.2Phase Space and the Phase Portrait

Here is the conceptual leap that organizes all of classical dynamics. Instead of plotting each variable against time, plot the state vector itself as a point in an n-dimensional space whose axes are the variables. This is phase space. For the spring, phase space is the (x, v) plane: position on one axis, velocity on the other.

At any instant the system is a single point in this space. As time runs, that point moves, sweeping out a curve — the trajectory. Crucially, the right-hand side f(t, y) assigns to every point a velocity arrow: the direction the state will head next. The whole field of arrows is the vector field, and the family of curves that follow it is the phase portrait.

The payoff is geometric insight without ever solving the equations. The shape of the orbits tells you the dynamics at a glance: closed loops mean periodic motion, spirals inward mean decay, and curves that run off to infinity mean instability. And because f assigns exactly one arrow per point, trajectories never cross — if they did, the system would have two futures from one state, which is impossible for a deterministic ODE.

5.3Fixed Points and Their Stability

The skeleton of any phase portrait is its fixed points (equilibria): states where nothing moves, f(y*) = 0. The pendulum hanging straight down and balanced straight up are both fixed points — but one is stable and one is not. To classify them, we ask what happens to a tiny nudge. Linearize the dynamics about y* using the Jacobian matrix J of partial derivatives, then a small displacement δ obeys δ' = J·δ. The eigenvalues of J decide everything:

eigenvalues lambda = a +/- i*b   of J = [ df_i / dy_j ]Re(lambda) sets growth or decay; Im(lambda) sets rotation. The sign of the real part is the whole story for stability.
  • Both real parts negative -> nudges shrink: a stable node or stable spiral (sink). Damped systems settle here.
  • Any real part positive -> nudges grow: an unstable point (source or saddle). The inverted pendulum is a saddle.
  • Purely imaginary (real part zero) -> nudges neither grow nor decay: a center, ringed by closed orbits, as in the undamped oscillator above.
Common mistakeStability is decided by the real parts of the eigenvalues, not their magnitude. A pair like lambda = -0.01 +/- 5i is stable (it spirals slowly inward) even though the imaginary part is large; a pair like lambda = +0.01 +/- 5i is unstable despite looking almost identical. Never read the imaginary part as "how stable" — it only sets the rotation/oscillation frequency.

5.4Oscillators in Phase Space: Closed Loops, Spirals, Coupled Modes

The harmonic oscillator is the Rosetta Stone of phase space. Undamped, its energy E = ½m·v² + ½k·x² is conserved — a constant. Curves of constant E in the (x, v) plane are ellipses, which is exactly why the phase portrait in §5.2 is a nest of closed loops around a center. Conservation of energy is visible as a geometric fact: the orbit closes.

Add damping (a force -c·v) and energy steadily leaks away. The trajectory can no longer stay on a fixed ellipse; it spirals inward toward the origin. The center becomes a stable spiral, and the eigenvalues pick up a negative real part:

x' = v    v' = -(k/m) x - (c/m) v  ->  lambda = -(c/2m) +/- i*omegaThe real part -(c/2m) is the decay rate; the imaginary part is the ringing frequency. More damping = tighter inward spiral.

Coupled oscillators — two masses joined by springs — live in a four-dimensional phase space (x1, v1, x2, v2). That is hard to draw, but a change of variables to normal modes (in-phase and out-of-phase combinations) decouples them into two independent oscillators, each tracing its own ellipse in its own plane. This is the computational backbone of how vibrating molecules, suspension bridges, and crystal lattices are analyzed: find the modes, and a tangled high-dimensional motion becomes a sum of simple loops.

AnalogyA phase portrait is a topographic map of the dynamics. Fixed points are the summits and basins; closed orbits are like contour lines you can walk around forever; a stable spiral is water swirling down a drain. Once you can read the map, you predict where any starting point ends up without taking a single step in time.
✦ Worked Example — classify the damped oscillator's fixed point

A damped spring has m = 1, k = 4, c = 1, giving x″ = −4x − v. Write the system, find the fixed point, build the Jacobian, and classify it.

  1. First-order system: with y = (x, v), we get x' = v and v' = -4x - v.
  2. Fixed point: set both rates to zero. v = 0 and -4x - v = 0 give x = 0, v = 0 — the origin is the only equilibrium.
  3. Jacobian: J = [[ d(x')/dx, d(x')/dv ], [ d(v')/dx, d(v')/dv ]] = [[0, 1], [-4, -1]].
  4. Eigenvalues: solve det(J - lambda I) = lambda² + lambda + 4 = 0, so lambda = (-1 +/- sqrt(1 - 16))/2 = -0.5 +/- 1.936 i.
  5. Classify: real part -0.5 < 0 and nonzero imaginary part -> a stable spiral. The mass oscillates at omega ≈ 1.94 rad/s while its amplitude decays like e^(-0.5 t). In phase space the trajectory spirals into the origin.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate a system and draw its phase portrait

Use the same free Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (the one-time install details lives in the early lectures — reuse that environment here). We pass a vector right-hand side to solve_ivp and let it integrate the coupled system, then plot the trajectory in the (x, v) plane.

# phase_space.py — Lecture 5: damped oscillator m=1, k=4, c=1
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def rhs(t, y):                       # y = [x, v]; return [x', v']
    x, v = y
    return [v, -4.0*x - 1.0*v]       # the coupled first-order system

t_span = (0.0, 20.0)
t_eval = np.linspace(*t_span, 2000)
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, t_span, [1.0, 0.0], t_eval=t_eval, rtol=1e-9)

x, v = sol.y                          # each row is one state component vs time

# --- phase portrait: trajectory in state space ---
plt.plot(x, v)                        # spirals inward toward (0, 0)
plt.plot(0, 0, 'o')                   # the stable-spiral fixed point
plt.xlabel("x  (position)"); plt.ylabel("v  (velocity)")
plt.title("Damped oscillator: trajectory spirals into the origin")
plt.axhline(0, lw=.5); plt.axvline(0, lw=.5)
plt.show()
# Try c = 0  -> closed ellipse (center). Try c = 5 -> overdamped, no loop.

Confirm the theory numerically: print np.linalg.eigvals([[0,1],[-4,-1]]) and check you get −0.5 ± 1.94i, matching the worked example. Then set c = 0 and watch the spiral close into the constant-energy ellipse predicted in §5.4.

🔗 Where this is heading

Phase space is the stage for everything that follows. Next week's stiff equations are systems whose Jacobian eigenvalues differ by orders of magnitude — fast modes decaying alongside slow ones — which is precisely why explicit solvers choke on them. The same eigenvalue thinking returns in Course 3, where a matrix's spectrum governs linear-solver accuracy, and again when nonlinear systems develop chaos and strange attractors in higher-dimensional phase spaces. In the Quantum track the state vector becomes a wavefunction and "phase space" becomes Hilbert space; in the Plasma and Quant tracks, ensembles of trajectories fill phase space and we track their distribution. Master the geometry of trajectories now and these later abstractions feel like home.

Q1. To integrate the second-order equation x″ = −(k/m)x with a standard ODE solver, you first…
A. rewrite it as the first-order system x′ = v, v′ = −(k/m)x with state (x, v)
B. differentiate it once more to get a third-order equation
C. drop the second derivative since solvers can't handle it
D. solve for x algebraically first
Show answer

A. Order reduction: name v = x′ to split one 2nd-order equation into two coupled 1st-order ones — the form solvers require. B raises the order (wrong direction); C discards the physics; D is impossible for a differential equation.

Q2. In a phase portrait, two distinct trajectories of an autonomous ODE can never cross because…
A. the plot would look too cluttered
B. each point has exactly one velocity arrow, so a single state has one unique future
C. energy is always conserved
D. trajectories are always straight lines
Show answer

B. The right-hand side f(y) assigns one direction per point, so determinism forbids two futures from one state. C is false (damped systems lose energy yet still don't cross); A is aesthetic, not a law; D is false in general.

Q3. The Jacobian of a 2D system at a fixed point has eigenvalues λ = −0.3 ± 2i. The fixed point is a…
A. saddle
B. source (unstable node)
C. stable spiral
D. center with closed orbits
Show answer

C. Negative real part (−0.3) means nudges decay; nonzero imaginary part means they rotate — an inward spiral. A saddle needs mixed-sign real parts; a source needs positive real parts; a center needs real part exactly zero.

Q4. An undamped harmonic oscillator traces which shape in the (x, v) plane, and why?
A. a spiral inward, because energy is lost
B. a straight line through the origin
C. a curve running off to infinity
D. a closed ellipse, because energy is conserved (a constant-E contour)
Show answer

D. Constant energy ½mv² + ½kx² traces an ellipse, so the orbit closes — periodic motion. A would be the damped case; B and C describe non-oscillatory or unstable dynamics, not a conservative oscillator.

Q5. Two masses joined by springs form a phase space of how many dimensions, and what simplifies the analysis?
A. 2 dimensions; nothing simplifies it
B. 4 dimensions; transforming to normal modes decouples it into independent oscillators
C. 1 dimension; the masses move identically
D. infinite dimensions; it cannot be analyzed
Show answer

B. Two positions plus two velocities = 4D state space; normal-mode coordinates decouple it into two independent 1D oscillators, each a simple ellipse. A and C undercount the state (each mass needs position AND velocity); D is false — finite systems are finite-dimensional.

✓ What you learned

  • Any high-order ODE becomes a first-order system dy/dt = f(t, y) by order reduction — name the velocity as a new variable.
  • Phase space plots the state vector itself; the field of velocity arrows is the vector field, and its curves form the phase portrait.
  • Fixed points are equilibria; the Jacobian's eigenvalues classify them — the sign of the real part decides stability.
  • Undamped oscillators trace closed ellipses (centers, conserved energy); damping turns them into inward spirals (sinks).
  • Integrate any system with solve_ivp by passing a vector right-hand side, then plot the trajectory as a curve in state space.
