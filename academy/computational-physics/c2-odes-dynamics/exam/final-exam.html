ODEs, IVPs & Euler Error · Weeks 1–2
1. A physics student has the second-order equation for a pendulum's swing, θ″ = −(g/L)·sin θ. To hand this to a general-purpose numerical ODE solver such as RK4, the first step is to…
Answer: A. Naming the angular velocity as a new state variable turns one second-order equation into two coupled first-order ones — the form every standard solver expects. No closed-form solution exists in general, and dropping sin θ or ω would discard real physics.
2. Why does the IVP dy/dt = f(t, y) require an initial condition y(t₀) = y₀ before it can be considered "solved"?
Answer: B. f(t, y) plants a slope arrow at every point in the (t, y) plane; infinitely many curves stay tangent to that field. y(t₀) = y₀ drops you onto one specific point and picks out the single curve through it. Linear ODEs need initial data too, and order is unaffected.
3. The local truncation error committed by a single explicit-Euler step, assuming the starting value is exact, scales as…
Answer: C. Euler keeps the Taylor series through the linear term and drops (h²/2)y″ — that dropped term is the local truncation error, O(h²). O(h) (B) is the global error after ~1/h steps accumulate it; the error clearly shrinks with h, ruling out (A).
4. Applying explicit Euler to y′ = −λy with λ > 0 real, the numerical solution stays bounded only when the step size satisfies…
Answer: D. The update multiplies by (1 − λh); stability needs |1 − λh| < 1, i.e. h < 2/λ. (A) is exactly the unstable regime, and (C) is the classic trap — the method can blow up with growing oscillations even though the true answer decays smoothly.
Runge-Kutta & Adaptive Stepping · Weeks 3–4
5. In the classical fourth-order Runge-Kutta method (RK4), the four slope estimates k₁, k₂, k₃, k₄ are combined in the final update with weights…
Answer: B. y_(n+1) = y_n + (h/6)(k₁ + 2k₂ + 2k₃ + k₄) — the two midpoint slopes count double, matching Simpson's rule. A plain average would not match the Taylor series through h⁴, and 1:3:3:1 are binomial coefficients, not RK4's.
6. RK4's local (per-step) truncation error is O(h⁵). Crossing a fixed time interval requires about 1/h steps, so the resulting global error is…
Answer: C. Summing ~1/h per-step errors of size O(h⁵) accumulates to roughly (1/h)·O(h⁵) = O(h⁴) — one order is always lost going from local to global for a one-step method. (D) ignores that accumulation entirely.
7. A Butcher tableau for an explicit Runge-Kutta method fully specifies…
Answer: D. The tableau is a complete recipe: when each slope is sampled (c), how earlier slopes feed later stages (a), and how they're blended (b). Euler, midpoint, and RK4 are all just different tableaus; (A) only counts stages, and (B)/(C) are unrelated.
8. An embedded Runge-Kutta pair such as Dormand-Prince RK45 estimates the local error of a step essentially for free by…
Answer: A. Both solutions reuse the same stage evaluations; only the weights differ, so the error estimate costs almost nothing extra. ODE solving is deterministic (no seeds, ruling out B), and an exact solution (C) is generally unavailable — that's the whole reason to estimate.
9. In the adaptive step-size controller, a trial step is rejected and retried with a smaller h whenever…
Answer: C. err > tol means the step overshot the accuracy target, so it's discarded and retried smaller. (A) describes acceptance with room to grow h; (B) is an arbitrary number; (D) is irrelevant — the sign of f says nothing about step error.
10. For an embedded pair of order p = 4 (such as RK45), the step-size update formula h_new = h·S·(tol/err)^(1/(p+1)) uses the exponent…
Answer: D. The exponent is 1/(p+1) = 1/(4+1) = 1/5, because the local error of an order-p method scales as h^(p+1). (B) wrongly uses 1/p instead of 1/(p+1).
Phase Space & Stiffness · Weeks 5–6
11. In a phase portrait of an autonomous system dy/dt = f(y), two distinct trajectories can never cross one another because…
Answer: A. Determinism forbids two futures from one state. (B) is false — damped systems lose energy yet still don't cross; (C) is aesthetic, not a law; (D) is false in general.
12. A fixed point's Jacobian has eigenvalues λ = −0.4 ± 3i. This fixed point is classified as a…
Answer: B. Negative real part (−0.4) means nudges decay; the nonzero imaginary part means they also rotate — an inward spiral. A saddle needs mixed-sign real parts, a source needs positive real parts, and a center needs real part exactly zero.
13. A system of ODEs is called stiff when…
Answer: D. Stiffness is the coexistence of fast and slow timescales (a large stiffness ratio of Jacobian eigenvalues), which caps an explicit step at the fast rate. A singular Jacobian (A) or lack of a closed form (B) are unrelated, and stiff solutions are typically smooth, not discontinuous (C).
14. For the linear test equation y′ = λy with Re(λ) < 0, forward (explicit) Euler stays numerically stable only when the step size h satisfies…
Answer: A. The amplification factor is 1 + hλ; stability needs |1 + hλ| < 1, i.e. h < 2/|λ|. Forward Euler is not unconditionally stable (that describes backward Euler, B), and C/D are not real stability conditions.
15. Backward (implicit) Euler applied to y′ = λy gives the amplification factor R(z) = 1/(1 − hλ). This method is called A-stable because…
Answer: B. A-stability means every decaying mode is stable for any step size — the full left half-plane of z = hλ. (A) describes forward Euler's small bounded disk; (C) is false for nonlinear problems (implicit methods need the Jacobian for Newton iteration); (D) confuses stability with accuracy — backward Euler is only first order.
16. Your explicit RK45 solver is taking thousands of tiny steps on a problem whose solution looks smooth, and it warns that it cannot meet the requested tolerance. The appropriate fix is to…
Answer: C. These are textbook symptoms of stiffness; an implicit solver with a large enough stability region removes the step-size cap entirely (Radau is fully A-stable; SciPy's BDF is A-stable at the low orders it mostly uses and the weaker A(α)-stable at higher orders). (A) hides the problem by accepting a worse answer, (B) would make an explicit method blow up, and (D) only changes output sampling, not the internal steps.
Symplectic Integration & N-Body · Weeks 7–8
17. Integrating a planet's orbit with RK4 over millions of periods, the total energy does not stay constant — it drifts steadily in one direction. This happens because…
Answer: A. RK4 has excellent local accuracy (O(h⁵) local error) but does not respect the geometric structure of Hamiltonian flow, so energy accumulates a systematic drift over many periods. (B) is false, and (C)/(D) are irrelevant — RK4 handles such forces fine with ordinary real arithmetic.
18. The velocity-Verlet / leapfrog update is built from alternating "kick" (update velocity at fixed position) and "drift" (update position at fixed velocity) sub-steps. Geometrically, each of these sub-steps is…
Answer: B. A drift updates x at fixed v and a kick updates v at fixed x; each is a shear with determinant 1, so their composition preserves area — the symplectic condition. (A) wrongly calls it a rotation, and (C)/(D) describe nothing the deterministic update actually does.
19. Backward error analysis shows that a symplectic integrator like velocity-Verlet nearly exactly conserves a nearby "shadow" Hamiltonian H̃ = H + O(h²). The practical consequence is that…
Answer: C. Because H̃ is conserved and differs from the true H by only O(h²), the true energy cannot wander — it merely oscillates within a band of that width, for arbitrarily long runs. (A) overstates it (there is a small O(h²) offset), and (B)/(D) contradict the bounded-oscillation result.
20. Direct summation of the gravitational force in an N-body simulation costs how much computational work per step, and why?
Answer: D. There are N(N−1)/2 ~ N²/2 distinct pairs and every one must be summed. (A) ignores gravity's long range, (B) is the cost of a tree method like Barnes-Hut, not direct summation, and (C) is not how the scaling works — it has nothing to do with spatial dimensionality.
21. In an N-body simulation, adding a small softening length ε inside the force law, a_i ∝ (r_j − r_i)/(|r_j − r_i|² + ε²)^(3/2), is done chiefly to…
Answer: A. As separation → 0 the unsoftened force → ∞ and a fixed-step integrator takes a catastrophic kick; ε² floors the denominator so it stays finite. (B) is false — softening doesn't change the momentum bookkeeping — and (C)/(D) are unrelated to what softening actually does.
Chaos & Capstone · Weeks 9–10
22. The largest Lyapunov exponent λ of a dynamical system measures…
Answer: B. λ quantifies sensitive dependence directly: a positive λ means infinitesimally close starts diverge exponentially, the mathematical fingerprint of chaos. (A), (C), and (D) describe unrelated quantities.
23. The Lorenz attractor's x, y, z variables and its famous two-lobed "butterfly" shape should be understood as…
Answer: C. x, y, z are convection-mode amplitudes, not positions in real space; the trajectory wanders forever on a bounded, fractal, aperiodic surface. (A) misreads the axes, (B) is wrong because it never exactly repeats, and (D) is wrong because the foci are unstable — the system never settles.
24. You know a chaotic system's initial state to relative precision δ₀, and its largest Lyapunov exponent is λ. The time until your prediction becomes useless (error reaches order 1) scales as…
Answer: D. Setting δ₀e^(λt) ≈ 1 and solving gives t ≈ (1/λ)·ln(1/δ₀). (A) is far too optimistic — the dependence is logarithmic, not linear; (B) has the relationship backwards (a larger λ shortens the horizon); (C) is wrong since both clearly matter.
25. In the course capstone, Track A (N-body) and Track B (double-pendulum chaos) each demand a specific diagnostic to prove the simulation is scientifically trustworthy. For Track A that diagnostic is energy and angular momentum plotted versus time, showing a bounded, non-drifting band; for Track B it is…
Answer: A. Track B's defining physics is sensitive dependence, so the headline diagnostic is the growth rate of ln(separation) between two nearby runs — its slope is the Lyapunov exponent, and Benettin renormalization keeps the measurement in the clean exponential regime instead of letting it saturate at the attractor's size. (B)–(D) are not scientific validation.
