Floating-Point Arithmetic & Numerical Error · Week 1
1. What does machine epsilon (ε ≈ 2.22×10⁻¹⁶ for IEEE-754 double precision) represent?
Answer: B. ε = 2⁻⁵² is the gap between 1.0 and the next double — the relative precision of the format. The smallest positive double (A) is a far tinier denormalized number.
2. A forward-difference derivative estimate f′(x) ≈ [f(x+h) − f(x)]/h is computed for smaller and smaller h. What happens to the total error?
Answer: D. Truncation error falls with h but round-off from cancellation grows as ε/h; the total bottoms out near h ≈ √ε ≈ 10⁻⁸ and rises again — smaller h is not always better.
Root Finding — Bisection, Newton & Secant · Weeks 2–3
3. For a continuous function f, which condition on [a, b] guarantees (via the Intermediate Value Theorem) that a root is trapped inside the interval?
Answer: A. Opposite signs at the endpoints plus continuity invoke the IVT, trapping a root in (a, b). Equal endpoint values (B) or a stationary point (C) give no such guarantee.
4. A root is bracketed in [0, 32]. About how many bisection steps are needed to reach a tolerance of τ = 10⁻⁴ on the interval width?
Answer: C. n ≥ log₂((32)/10⁻⁴) = log₂(320000) ≈ 18.29, round up to 19. Bisection predicts its iteration count exactly before running.
5. Newton's update xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f′(xn) is derived by…
Answer: B. Keep the linear Taylor term, set it to zero, and solve — that is exactly the tangent line's x-intercept. (A) is bisection; (C) is unrelated; (D) describes a different interpolation-based scheme.
6. Near a simple root, Newton's method satisfies en+1 ≈ C·en². This means…
Answer: A. Squaring a small error is dramatic: 10⁻² → 10⁻⁴ → 10⁻⁸ → …, so the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. This is quadratic convergence, order p = 2.
7. A bare Newton iteration is most likely to overshoot or diverge when…
Answer: D. The Newton step is −f/f′, so a tiny derivative makes the step enormous and flings the guess far away — the classic overshoot/divergence failure mode, which motivates a bracketing fallback like Brent's method.
Interpolation & Numerical Differentiation · Weeks 4–5
8. Through n+1 points with distinct x-values, how many polynomials of degree ≤ n pass exactly through all of them?
Answer: C. The Vandermonde matrix is invertible when the x-values are distinct, so the interpolating polynomial exists and is unique. Lagrange and Newton forms both compute this same unique polynomial.
9. The Runge phenomenon describes…
Answer: B. On equispaced nodes, raising the interpolant's degree makes the node-product error term blow up near the endpoints — a defect of node placement, cured by Chebyshev nodes or piecewise splines.
10. The central difference [f(x+h) − f(x−h)] / (2h) has truncation error of order…
Answer: A. Subtracting the forward and backward Taylor expansions cancels the f″ term, leaving a leading error ∝ h² — second order, one better than the O(h) forward/backward difference.
11. For the central-difference derivative, truncation error ~h² and round-off error ~ε/h compete. The step size that minimizes the total error scales as…
Answer: D. Minimizing E(h) ≈ (h²/6)|f‴| + ε|f|/h gives h* ~ ε1/3 ≈ 6×10⁻⁶, with best error ~ε2/3 — better than the forward difference's h* ~ √ε.
Numerical Integration — Trapezoid, Simpson & Gauss · Weeks 6–7
12. The composite trapezoidal rule has truncation error proportional to…
Answer: C. Etrap = −(b−a)/12 · h²·f″(ξ) — second order, so halving h cuts the error by about 4. (D) is Simpson's rate, not the trapezoid's.
13. Simpson's rule fits which curve through three consecutive points, then integrates it exactly?
Answer: A. Simpson fits a quadratic through three points and integrates it exactly, giving the 1–4–1 stencil and (by a happy symmetry) exactness for cubics too — hence its fourth-order composite error.
14. Simpson's rule's h⁴ accuracy advantage over the trapezoid is least reliable when the integrand is…
Answer: D. Simpson's h⁴ bound assumes a bounded fourth derivative. Noise or a kink makes f⁽⁴⁾ blow up, so the supposed order advantage vanishes — the trapezoid (or smoothing first) is then the safer choice.
15. An n-point Gaussian quadrature rule is exact for all polynomials up to degree…
Answer: B. Freeing both the n nodes and n weights gives 2n degrees of freedom, enough to match polynomials through degree 2n−1 — double the exactness of Newton–Cotes (degree n−1) for the same cost.
16. The nodes of an n-point Gauss–Legendre rule are located at…
Answer: C. Orthogonality of the Legendre polynomials makes the rule exact when the nodes sit at the roots of Pn — never at the endpoints, and never equally spaced.
Convergence, Order & Richardson Extrapolation · Week 8
17. You plot log(error) vs log(h) for a numerical method and the points fall on a straight line. The slope of that line equals…
Answer: A. Since log(error) ≈ log(C) + p·log(h), the slope of the line is exactly p — the single most useful diagnostic for confirming a method behaves as the theory predicts.
18. For a method of order p, Richardson extrapolation combines A(h) and A(h/2) as Abetter = …
Answer: D. This weighted combination exactly cancels the leading C·hp error term, raising the order to q > p — a plain average (A) or raw difference (B) does not, and (C) has the sign reversed.
19. Your input data is known to only 1% accuracy, but you refine your numerical method until its truncation error is 10⁻¹². The sound conclusion is…
Answer: B. The total error is bounded by the sum of truncation, round-off, and modelling error. A 1% input uncertainty swamps a 10⁻¹² truncation term, so further refinement buys nothing — spend effort on the dominant term instead.
Linear Systems & Conditioning · Week 9
20. Why factor A = LU once instead of re-running Gaussian elimination for every new right-hand side b?
Answer: C. The expensive elimination depends only on A, not b. Once L and U are stored, every new right-hand side is solved cheaply by forward and backward substitution.
21. In the 2-norm, the condition number κ(A) equals…
Answer: A. κ(A) = ‖A‖·‖A⁻¹‖, which in the 2-norm equals σmax/σmin. The determinant, trace, and matrix size say nothing about error amplification.
22. You solve Ax = b and find a tiny residual r = b − Ax̂ ≈ 10⁻¹⁵, but κ(A) ≈ 10¹². What can you conclude about the relative error in x̂?
Answer: D. The bound ‖e‖/‖x‖ ≤ κ(A)·‖r‖/‖b‖ gives 10¹²·10⁻¹⁵ = 10⁻³ — only about 3 trustworthy digits. A tiny residual does not by itself guarantee a tiny error when κ is large.
Capstone: High-Precision Physics Integral · Week 10
23. The exact pendulum period T = 4√(L/g)·K(k) with k = sin(θ₀/2) reduces to the familiar T₀ = 2π√(L/g) as θ₀ → 0 because…
Answer: B. As θ₀ → 0, k = sin(θ₀/2) → 0 and K(0) = ∫₀^(π/2) dφ = π/2, giving T = 4√(L/g)·(π/2) = 2π√(L/g) = T₀ exactly.
24. As θ₀ → 180° (k → 1), the K(k) integrand 1/√(1 − k²sin²φ) develops a sharp spike at φ = π/2. Why does this demand an adaptive or endpoint-aware quadrature rule?
Answer: C. At φ = π/2 the denominator is √(1 − k²) = cos(θ₀/2), which heads to zero as θ₀ → 180°, so the integrand spikes there. A uniform rule wastes points elsewhere; an adaptive or endpoint-aware rule concentrates samples where they are needed.
25. Using the leading correction T/T₀ − 1 ≈ θ₀²/16, the crossover amplitude where the small-angle pendulum model first exceeds a 1% tolerance is approximately…
Answer: A. Set θ₀²/16 = 0.01 → θ₀ = √0.16 = 0.40 rad ≈ 23° (a root-finding problem wrapped around quadrature; the exact value is ≈ 22.9°). At 90° the model is already off by ~18%, so (B) is far past the crossover.
