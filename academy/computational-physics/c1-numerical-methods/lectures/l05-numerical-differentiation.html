Numerical Differentiation & Finite Differences

A derivative is a limit no computer can take — so we sneak up on it with finite steps, and the art is choosing a step neither too big nor too small.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the forward, backward, and central finite-difference formulas from a Taylor expansion.
  • State each formula's truncation order and explain why the central difference is one order more accurate.
  • Build a second-derivative stencil and use it on real applications.
  • Explain the truncation-vs-round-off trade-off and estimate the optimal step size for each scheme.
  • Use Richardson extrapolation to boost accuracy, and compute velocity and acceleration from sampled position data with NumPy.

5.1The Derivative as a Limit We Can't Take

The calculus definition of a derivative is a limit: f′(x) = limh→0 [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h. A computer cannot take a limit — it can only plug in a small but finite step h. The simplest choice just drops the limit symbol:

f′(x) ≈ [f(x+h) − f(x)] / h  (forward difference)Slope of the secant line through (x, f(x)) and (x+h, f(x+h)).

How wrong is this? Expand f(x+h) in a Taylor series about x:

f(x+h) = f(x) + h·f′(x) + (h²/2)·f″(x) + (h³/6)·f‴(x) + …

Solve for f′(x) and the leading leftover term is (h/2)·f″(x). So the forward difference carries an error proportional to h — it is first order, written O(h). A backward difference [f(x) − f(x−h)] / h is also first order. Both lean to one side of x, and that lopsidedness is exactly what costs them accuracy.

AnalogyEstimating a hiker's speed from a GPS track: the forward difference asks "where will they be one second from now?" and the backward difference asks "where were they one second ago." Either is biased if the hiker is speeding up. Averaging the two — "where were they a second ago and a second from now?" — cancels that bias. That average is the central difference, and it is the hero of this lecture.

5.2The Central Difference and Higher Derivatives

Write the Taylor expansion both forward and backward and subtract them. The even-power terms (the f″ term) cancel, leaving only odd powers:

f(x+h) − f(x−h) = 2h·f′(x) + (h³/3)·f‴(x) + …

Divide by 2h and the leading error term is now (h²/6)·f‴(x). The central difference is therefore second order, O(h²):

f′(x) ≈ [f(x+h) − f(x−h)] / (2h)  error ~ h²One extra function value buys a whole extra order of accuracy — for the same number of evaluations as a forward+backward pair.

If instead we add the forward and backward expansions, the f′ terms cancel and the f″ term survives. Solving gives the workhorse second-derivative stencil:

f″(x) ≈ [f(x+h) − 2·f(x) + f(x−h)] / h²  error ~ h²The three weights (1, −2, 1) are the discrete Laplacian — the engine of every diffusion, wave, and Schrodinger PDE solver.
Common mistakeForgetting the factor of 2 in the central-difference denominator. The points are x+h and x-h, a distance 2h apart, so you must divide by 2*h, not h. Dividing by h gives an answer that is exactly twice too large and no warning is raised.

5.3The Optimal Step Size — Truncation Meets Round-off

It is tempting to make h microscopically small. But (as in Lecture 1) the numerator subtracts two nearly-equal numbers, and that catastrophic cancellation injects round-off noise of size ~ε·|f| / h. Two errors fight for the central difference:

E(h) ≈ (h²/6)·|f‴|  +  ε·|f| / hFirst term = truncation (falls like h²). Second term = round-off from cancellation (grows like 1/h).

Minimize the sum by setting dE/dh = 0. The truncation term falls but the round-off term rises, so the total bottoms out at a finite step:

h* ~ ε1/3 ≈ 6 × 10−6   with min error ~ ε2/3 ≈ 4 × 10−11The central difference keeps ~11 digits at its best; the forward difference (Lecture 1) bottoms out near h ~ sqrt(eps) keeping only ~8.

Richardson extrapolation — a free order boost

If the central difference has error E(h) = a·h² + b·h⁴ + …, we can compute it at h and at h/2 and combine them to cancel the leading h² term. Let D(h) be the central-difference estimate:

Dbetter = [4·D(h/2) − D(h)] / 3  error ~ h⁴This is Richardson extrapolation: two cheap O(h²) estimates combine into one O(h⁴) estimate. Same idea upgrades the trapezoidal rule into Romberg integration next week.

5.4Velocity and Acceleration from Position Data

Experiments and simulations rarely hand you a formula — they hand you a table of positions sampled at times t0, t1, …, spaced by Δt. Finite differences turn that table into velocity and acceleration, the bread and butter of mechanics, tracking, and motion analysis:

vi ≈ (xi+1 − xi−1) / (2Δt)    ai ≈ (xi+1 − 2xi + xi−1) / Δt²Central velocity and the second-derivative stencil for acceleration, both O(Δt²) at interior points.

At the endpoints you have no neighbor on one side, so a central formula is impossible — you fall back to a one-sided (forward or backward) difference there. This is exactly what NumPy's gradient does automatically: central in the interior, one-sided at the edges.

Common mistakeDifferentiating noisy measured data directly. Differentiation amplifies high-frequency noise: a tiny wiggle of amplitude δ at spacing Δt becomes a velocity error ~ δ/Δt and an acceleration error ~ δ/Δt², which explodes as Δt shrinks. With real sensor data, smooth or fit first (e.g. a Savitzky-Golay filter or a spline), then differentiate.
✦ Worked Example — forward vs central on a known function

Estimate f′(1) for f(x) = ex using h = 0.1. The exact value is e1 = 2.71828. Compare forward and central differences, then check the error orders.

  1. Function values: f(0.9) = e0.9 = 2.45960, f(1.0) = 2.71828, f(1.1) = e1.1 = 3.00417.
  2. Forward difference: [f(1.1) − f(1.0)] / 0.1 = (3.00417 − 2.71828)/0.1 = 0.28589/0.1 = 2.8589. Error = 2.8589 − 2.71828 = +0.1406.
  3. Central difference: [f(1.1) − f(0.9)] / (2·0.1) = (3.00417 − 2.45960)/0.2 = 0.54457/0.2 = 2.72285. Error = 2.72285 − 2.71828 = +0.00457.
  4. Order check: the central error (0.0046) is about 30× smaller than the forward error (0.14). Predicted: forward ≈ (h/2)·f″ = 0.05·2.718 = 0.136 ✓; central ≈ (h²/6)·f‴ = (0.01/6)·2.718 = 0.0045 ✓ — both match.
  5. Takeaway: for the same step, the central difference is dramatically more accurate. Halving h to 0.05 would cut the forward error in half but the central error by a factor of four.

🛠️ Hands-On: differentiate a noisy trajectory

The free Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib stack handles everything here. If you have not set it up yet, the one-time install lives in the Week 1 lecture. We will use np.gradient (central in the interior, one-sided at the edges) and scipy.signal.savgol_filter to tame measurement noise before differentiating.

# numerical_diff.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
from scipy.signal import savgol_filter

# Synthetic "measured" vertical motion: x(t) = 5t - 4.9 t^2, plus noise
t  = np.linspace(0.0, 1.0, 101)
dt = t[1] - t[0]
x_true = 5.0*t - 4.9*t**2
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x_meas = x_true + rng.normal(0, 0.01, t.size)   # 1 cm sensor noise

# 1) Naive central difference on raw data -> velocity, then acceleration
v_raw = np.gradient(x_meas, dt)                  # O(dt^2) interior, one-sided edges
a_raw = np.gradient(v_raw, dt)                   # noise BLOWS UP here

# 2) Smooth first, then differentiate -> clean acceleration ~ -9.8
x_smooth = savgol_filter(x_meas, window_length=21, polyorder=3)
v = np.gradient(x_smooth, dt)
a = np.gradient(v, dt)

print(f"raw    accel (mean of interior): {a_raw[10:-10].mean():.2f} m/s^2")
print(f"smooth accel (mean of interior): {a[10:-10].mean():.2f} m/s^2")
# raw ~ wildly noisy; smoothed ~ -9.8  (the true 2 * -4.9)

Then add a Richardson step on a clean function: compute the central difference of np.sin at x=1 with h and h/2, combine as (4*D_half - D)/3, and watch the error drop from ~10⁻³ (h²) to ~10⁻⁶ (h⁴). Predict the new error before you print it.

❓ Lecture 5 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The central difference [f(x+h) − f(x−h)] / (2h) has a truncation error of order…
A. O(h²)
B. O(h)
C. O(h³)
D. O(1), independent of h
Show answer

A. Subtracting the forward and backward Taylor expansions cancels the f″ term, leaving a leading error ∝ h², so it is second order. O(h) (B) is the forward/backward difference; O(h³) (C) and O(1) (D) are simply wrong for this stencil.

Q2. Why is the central difference generally more accurate than the forward difference for the same step h?
A. It uses fewer function evaluations
B. Its symmetry cancels the leading (even-order) error term, raising the order from h to h²
C. It is immune to round-off error
D. It does not require the function to be differentiable
Show answer

B. The symmetric stencil cancels the f″ term, so the error drops from O(h) to O(h²). It actually uses the same number of evaluations as a forward+backward pair (A is false); it still suffers round-off (C is false); and it still needs a smooth function (D is false).

Q3. As you make h smaller and smaller for a central-difference derivative, the total error will…
A. decrease toward zero without limit
B. stay constant
C. fall until round-off (cancellation) dominates near h ~ ε^(1/3), then rise
D. increase monotonically from the start
Show answer

C. Truncation falls like h² but round-off from cancellation grows like ε/h, so the total bottoms out near h ~ ε1/3 ≈ 10⁻⁶ and then rises. It never reaches zero (A), is not constant (B), and improves before it worsens, so it is not monotonic (D).

Q4. The stencil [f(x+h) − 2f(x) + f(x−h)] / h² approximates which quantity?
A. the first derivative f′(x)
B. the integral of f over [x−h, x+h]
C. the average value of f
D. the second derivative f″(x), to O(h²)
Show answer

D. Adding the forward and backward Taylor expansions cancels f′ and isolates f″; dividing by h² gives the second derivative with O(h²) error. The first derivative (A) uses a different stencil; (B) and (C) are integration/averaging, not differentiation.

Q5. You compute a central difference at step h and at step h/2, giving D(h) and D(h/2). Richardson extrapolation combines them as…
A. [D(h) + D(h/2)] / 2, giving the simple average
B. [4·D(h/2) − D(h)] / 3, cancelling the h² term to reach O(h⁴)
C. D(h/2) − D(h), the raw difference
D. [D(h) − 4·D(h/2)] / 3, which lowers the order
Show answer

B. Since error ∝ h², halving h cuts it by 4; the weights 4 and −1 (over 3) cancel that leading term, yielding O(h⁴). A plain average (A) keeps the h² error; the raw difference (C) is just an error estimate, not an improved value; and (D) has the sign flipped and would not cancel the term.

✓ What you learned

  • Derivatives become finite differences: forward and backward are O(h); the symmetric central difference is O(h²).
  • The Taylor series both derives each formula and predicts its truncation order and constant.
  • The second-derivative stencil (1, −2, 1)/h² — the discrete Laplacian behind every diffusion and wave solver.
  • The truncation–round-off trade-off gives an optimal step h ~ ε1/3, and Richardson extrapolation lifts O(h²) to O(h⁴) for free.
  • How to get velocity and acceleration from sampled data with np.gradient — and why you must smooth noisy data first.
