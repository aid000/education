Numerical Integration II — Gaussian Quadrature

Stop forcing your sample points onto a uniform grid: if you let the algorithm choose where to sample as well as how much to weight each sample, a handful of points can integrate high-degree polynomials exactly.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why choosing both nodes and weights doubles the degrees of freedom of a quadrature rule.
  • State the Gaussian quadrature guarantee: n points integrate polynomials up to degree 2n−1 exactly.
  • Describe the role of orthogonal (Legendre) polynomials in placing the optimal nodes.
  • Compare Gauss–Legendre with the equispaced Newton–Cotes rules (trapezoidal, Simpson) on accuracy per evaluation.
  • Use SciPy to evaluate a tricky physics integral with far fewer function calls than a uniform-grid rule.

7.1Two Knobs Instead of One

Last week's rules — trapezoidal and Simpson — placed sample points on an equally spaced grid and then chose clever weights. The node positions were fixed in advance; only the weights were free. Gaussian quadrature asks a deeper question: what if we are also allowed to choose where the points go?

Any quadrature rule approximates an integral by a weighted sum of function values:

∫−11 f(x) dx ≈ Σi=1..n wi · f(xi)The xi are the nodes (where we sample); the wi are the weights (how much each sample counts).

With n points we have n weights — that is n free numbers, enough to make the rule exact for polynomials up to degree n−1 (Newton–Cotes does exactly this). But if the n node positions are also free, we suddenly have 2n unknowns. Matching 2n moments of the integrand pins down a rule that is exact for polynomials up to degree 2n−1 — twice the degree for the same number of expensive function evaluations.

We work on the canonical interval [−1, 1]. Any real interval [a, b] is mapped onto it by the linear change of variable x = ((b−a)t + (a+b))/2, which contributes a constant Jacobian factor (b−a)/2. So solving the problem once on [−1, 1] solves it everywhere.

AnalogyNewton–Cotes is like a photographer forced to stand their tripod legs on pre-painted floor marks — they can only adjust the lens. Gaussian quadrature hands the photographer a free-roaming tripod: by also choosing where to stand, they capture the same scene sharply with far fewer shots. The "best places to stand" turn out not to be evenly spaced — they cluster toward the ends of the interval.

7.2Why the Nodes Are Roots of Legendre Polynomials

Where should those 2n degrees of freedom point the nodes? The magic is that the optimal nodes are the roots of the Legendre polynomial Pn(x), the n-th member of the family of polynomials that are mutually orthogonal on [−1, 1]:

∫−11 Pm(x) Pn(x) dx = 0 for m ≠ nOrthogonality: P0=1, P1=x, P2=(3x²−1)/2, P3=(5x³−3x)/2, … each Pn has n real roots inside (−1, 1).

Here is the one-line reason it works. Take any polynomial f of degree ≤ 2n−1. Divide it by Pn: f = q·Pn + r, where the quotient q and remainder r both have degree ≤ n−1. Integrating,

∫ f = ∫ q·Pn + ∫ r = 0 + ∫ rThe first integral vanishes because q (degree ≤ n−1) is a combination of P0…Pn−1, all orthogonal to Pn.

Now place the nodes at the roots of Pn. At each node Pn(xi) = 0, so f(xi) = r(xi): the rule "sees" only the remainder r. Because r has degree ≤ n−1, an n-point rule with the right weights integrates r exactly — and that equals ∫f. So the rule is exact for every polynomial up to degree 2n−1. The weights themselves are then chosen to integrate the n basis polynomials exactly; a clean closed form is wi = 2 / [(1−xi²)·(P′n(xi))²].

Common mistakeGaussian nodes are not evenly spaced, and they never include the endpoints ±1 (the roots of Pn lie strictly inside the interval). A frequent bug is to evaluate the integrand at the interval endpoints anyway — fatal if f has an integrable singularity at an endpoint, where Newton–Cotes would blow up but plain Gauss–Legendre quietly succeeds because it never samples there.

7.3Gauss–Legendre vs Newton–Cotes

The payoff is dramatic. Simpson's rule (3 equally spaced points) is exact only through degree 3. A 3-point Gauss–Legendre rule is exact through degree 2·3−1 = 5 — for the same three function evaluations. Doubling the exactness for free is why almost every production integrator (including SciPy's adaptive routine) is built on Gaussian rules.

For a smooth integrand, increasing the order of a single Gaussian rule gives exponential (spectral) convergence — the error drops faster than any fixed power of n. Newton–Cotes cannot match this: high-order equispaced rules suffer Runge's phenomenon (oscillating, growing weights) and can even diverge. That is the second reason Gaussian quadrature dominates: it is both more accurate and more stable as you push the order up.

7.4Tailoring the Weight: Gauss–Hermite and Friends

Many physics integrals carry a built-in weight function — a factor that is awkward for a polynomial rule but natural for an orthogonal family. The general form is

∫ab W(x) f(x) dx ≈ Σi wi f(xi)The weight W(x) is folded into the wi; nodes are roots of the polynomials orthogonal with respect to W on [a, b].

Each choice of W and interval names a classical Gauss rule. For W = 1 on [−1, 1] the orthogonal family is Legendre. For W = e−x² on (−∞, ∞) it is the Hermite polynomials — exactly the weight that appears in the quantum harmonic oscillator and in Gaussian (normal) expectation values. For W = e−x on [0, ∞) it is the Laguerre family, ideal for radial atomic integrals. Choosing the rule whose weight matches your integrand absorbs the hard part of the integrand into the nodes and weights, so only the smooth factor f is sampled.

AnalogyThink of the weight function as the "lighting" of a scene. A general-purpose camera (Gauss–Legendre) works, but if you know the scene is lit by a Gaussian glow that fades at the edges (W = e−x²), you bring the camera built for that light (Gauss–Hermite) and capture it perfectly with a couple of shots. Matching the rule to the weight is choosing the right gear for the light you already have.
✦ Worked Example — two-point Gauss–Legendre by hand

Evaluate ∫−11 (x⁴ + 1) dx with a 2-point Gauss–Legendre rule, and check it against the exact value. A 2-point rule should be exact through degree 2·2−1 = 3 — does it still do well on a degree-4 integrand?

  1. Exact value: ∫−11(x⁴+1)dx = [x⁵/5 + x]−11 = (1/5 + 1) − (−1/5 − 1) = 2/5 + 2 = 2.4.
  2. Nodes: the roots of P₂(x) = (3x²−1)/2 are x = ±1/√3 ≈ ±0.57735.
  3. Weights: for the 2-point rule both weights are w₁ = w₂ = 1 (they must sum to the interval length 2 by symmetry).
  4. Apply the rule: Σ wᵢ f(xᵢ) = 1·f(−0.57735) + 1·f(+0.57735). With f(x)=x⁴+1 and x⁴ = (1/3)² = 1/9 ≈ 0.1111 at each node, each term is 0.1111 + 1 = 1.1111. Sum = 2.2222.
  5. Compare: the estimate 2.2222 misses the true 2.4 by 0.178 — because degree 4 is just past the 2-point rule's exact range (≤ 3). For contrast, the trapezoidal rule on the same interval gives f(−1)+f(1) = 2+2 = 4, an error of 1.6 — eight times worse with the same two evaluations. Add a third Gauss point and the degree-4 result becomes exact.

🛠️ Hands-On: a tricky physics integral, two ways

The free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack runs everything here — reuse the Week 1 environment (the one-time install lives in Lecture 1). No new tools are needed: numpy.polynomial.legendre.leggauss hands you the nodes and weights directly, and scipy.integrate.quad wraps an adaptive Gauss rule for production use.

We integrate the blackbody-style integrand ∫₀10 x³/(eˣ−1) dx, which appears in the Stefan–Boltzmann law. First we roll our own Gauss–Legendre on a mapped interval, then compare against the trusted adaptive routine and a coarse Simpson rule.

# gauss_quadrature.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad, simpson

# integrand from the blackbody / Stefan-Boltzmann integral
def f(x):
    return x**3 / (np.expm1(x))          # expm1(x) = e^x - 1, stable near x=0

a, b = 1e-6, 10.0                        # avoid the removable point at x=0

# --- our own n-point Gauss-Legendre, mapped from [-1,1] to [a,b] ---
def gauss_legendre(func, a, b, n):
    x, w = np.polynomial.legendre.leggauss(n)   # nodes & weights on [-1,1]
    t = 0.5*(b - a)*x + 0.5*(b + a)             # map nodes into [a,b]
    return 0.5*(b - a) * np.sum(w * func(t))    # Jacobian (b-a)/2

for n in [2, 4, 6, 8]:
    print(f"Gauss n={n}: {gauss_legendre(f, a, b, n):.10f}")

# --- trusted reference (adaptive Gauss-Kronrod under the hood) ---
ref, err = quad(f, a, b)
print("scipy.quad     :", ref)

# --- equispaced Simpson with the SAME 9 points as Gauss n=8+1 ---
xs = np.linspace(a, b, 9)
print("Simpson (9 pts):", simpson(f(xs), x=xs))
# Gauss n=8 matches quad to ~8 digits; Simpson with 9 points is far coarser

Try swapping the finite [0,10] for the full [0,∞) integral using numpy.polynomial.laguerre.laggauss (weight e⁻ˣ) — match the rule to the integrand's natural decay and watch the node count you need drop. Plot Gauss vs Simpson error against n on a log axis to see the spectral slope from §7.3.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. An n-point Gaussian quadrature rule integrates exactly all polynomials up to degree…
A. n − 1
B. n
C. 2n − 1
D. 2n + 1
Show answer

C. Choosing both the n nodes and n weights gives 2n free parameters, enough to match polynomials through degree 2n−1. Option A (n−1) is the Newton–Cotes degree when only the weights are free; B and D are not achievable with n points.

Q2. Where are the optimal nodes of an n-point Gauss–Legendre rule located?
A. equally spaced across [−1, 1]
B. at the roots of the Legendre polynomial Pn, strictly inside (−1, 1)
C. always including both endpoints ±1
D. at the extrema of f(x)
Show answer

B. The nodes are the n roots of Pn, which all lie strictly inside the interval. A describes Newton–Cotes; C is false (plain Gauss never uses the endpoints — that is Gauss–Lobatto); D confuses nodes with features of the integrand, which the rule does not even know.

Q3. Compared with Simpson's rule using the same 3 function evaluations, 3-point Gauss–Legendre is exact through degree…
A. 5, versus Simpson's 3
B. 3, the same as Simpson
C. 2, worse than Simpson
D. 9, versus Simpson's 7
Show answer

A. 3-point Gauss is exact through 2·3−1 = 5, while Simpson (3 equispaced points) reaches only degree 3. B understates Gauss; C is backwards; D's numbers are simply wrong for n = 3.

Q4. An integral has the form ∫₀∞ e⁻ˣ f(x) dx with f smooth. Which rule is the natural fit?
A. Gauss–Hermite (weight e⁻ˣ²)
B. the equispaced trapezoidal rule
C. Simpson's rule on a truncated interval
D. Gauss–Laguerre, whose weight is exactly e⁻ˣ on [0, ∞)
Show answer

D. Gauss–Laguerre is built for weight e⁻ˣ on [0, ∞), so it absorbs the decay and samples only the smooth f. A uses the wrong weight (e⁻ˣ², the Hermite case); B and C must truncate the infinite tail and converge only as power laws.

Q5. Why does pushing a single Gauss–Legendre rule to high order behave better than a high-order equispaced Newton–Cotes rule?
A. Gauss rules use fewer bits of precision
B. high-order equispaced rules develop large oscillating weights (Runge's phenomenon) and can diverge, while Gauss rules stay stable and converge spectrally for smooth f
C. Newton–Cotes cannot be applied beyond degree 3
D. Gauss rules require the integrand to be a polynomial
Show answer

B. Equispaced high-order rules get unstable, sign-changing weights and may diverge; Gauss rules keep positive weights and converge geometrically for smooth integrands. A is irrelevant; C is false (Newton–Cotes exists at any order, just badly); D is false — Gauss works on any function, it is only exact for polynomials.

✓ What you learned

  • Freeing the node positions as well as the weights gives 2n degrees of freedom, so n points are exact through degree 2n−1.
  • The optimal nodes are the roots of the Legendre polynomial Pn, thanks to orthogonality killing the high-degree part of the integrand.
  • Gauss–Legendre doubles the exactness of Newton–Cotes for the same cost and converges spectrally for smooth integrands, avoiding Runge's phenomenon.
  • Matching the rule's weight function (Hermite for e⁻ˣ², Laguerre for e⁻ˣ) absorbs hard factors and slashes the node count for physics integrals.
  • In code, leggauss supplies nodes/weights and scipy.integrate.quad is the adaptive Gauss workhorse — far fewer evaluations than a uniform grid.
