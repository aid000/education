Week 9 · Lecture 9

Linear Systems & Conditioning

Solving Ax = b looks like the easiest thing a computer does — until the matrix quietly turns a microscopic input wobble into a catastrophic answer.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Solve Ax = b by Gaussian elimination and recognize it as an LU factorization with pivoting.
  • Explain why LU factorization is the smart way to reuse work across many right-hand sides.
  • Define the condition number κ(A) and state how it bounds error amplification.
  • Explain why a small residual does not guarantee a small error for an ill-conditioned matrix.
  • Diagnose conditioning in a physics problem (a resistor network) using NumPy and SciPy.

9.1Gaussian Elimination and LU Factorization

A linear system Ax = b is the workhorse of computational physics: it appears every time you discretize a static field, balance currents in a circuit, find forces in a truss, or solve the implicit step of a stiff ODE. The algorithm you learned by hand — Gaussian elimination — is exactly what the computer does. You add multiples of one row to another to zero out the entries below each pivot, turning A into an upper-triangular matrix U, then solve by back-substitution from the bottom row up.

The bookkeeping of "what multiple of row j did I subtract from row i" is itself a matrix: a unit lower-triangular L holding those multipliers. The result is the LU factorization:

A = L U   (with partial pivoting: P A = L U)L is unit lower-triangular (1s on the diagonal, multipliers below); U is upper-triangular; P is a row-swap permutation that puts the largest available pivot on the diagonal.

Why factor instead of just eliminating once? Because the expensive part — the O(n³) elimination — depends only on A, not on b. Once you have L and U, every new right-hand side is solved by two cheap O(n²) triangular sweeps:

solve L y = P b  (forward),  then  U x = y  (backward)Factor once, reuse forever: ideal when the same physical system is driven by many different loads or sources.

Pivoting is not optional. If a pivot is zero (or tiny) you must swap in a row with a larger entry; otherwise you divide by something near zero and the multipliers explode, manufacturing round-off catastrophes out of a perfectly solvable system. Partial pivoting — always choosing the largest-magnitude candidate pivot in the column — keeps the multipliers bounded by 1 and is what every production solver does.

AnalogyLU factorization is like learning a building's wiring diagram once. Elimination is the slow survey that maps every junction (O(n³)). After that, asking "if I flip this breaker, what turns off?" — a new right-hand side — is a quick trace through the diagram you already drew (O(n²)). You would never re-survey the building for every question.

9.2The Condition Number: How Much Error Gets Amplified

Now the subtle part. Suppose your right-hand side b carries a small uncertainty — measurement noise, or just the round-off of storing it. How much does the solution x change? The answer is governed by a single number attached to the matrix: the condition number.

Using a vector norm and its induced matrix norm, the condition number is defined as the product of the norm of A and the norm of its inverse:

κ(A) = ||A|| · ||A-1||   (κ ≥ 1 always)In the 2-norm, κ equals the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value: κ = σ_max / σ_min.

Its meaning is a worst-case amplification factor. A relative perturbation of size δ in the data can grow into a relative error of up to κ·δ in the answer:

||Δx|| / ||x||  ≤  κ(A) · ||Δb|| / ||b||Multiply your input's relative error by κ to bound the output's relative error.

The rule of thumb writes itself in decimal digits: if κ(A) ≈ 10k, you can lose up to k significant digits going from data to solution. Since a double carries ~16 digits, a matrix with κ ≈ 1010 leaves you only ~6 trustworthy digits — and κ ≈ 1016 means the answer may be pure noise. A matrix with small κ (near 1) is well-conditioned; one with huge κ is ill-conditioned.

9.3The Residual Trap: Small Residual ≠ Small Error

Here is the mistake that bites every newcomer. You solve Ax = b and get a computed answer x̂. To check it, you compute the residual r = b − Ax̂ and find it is tiny — machine-precision tiny. You declare victory. You may be completely wrong.

The residual measures how well x̂ satisfies the equation; the error e = x̂ − x measures how far x̂ is from the true solution. They are linked, but the link is κ:

||e|| / ||x||  ≤  κ(A) · ||r|| / ||b||A tiny residual only guarantees a tiny error when κ is small. For a large κ the error can be κ times bigger than the residual suggests.

For an ill-conditioned matrix, a near-parallel pair of equations can be almost satisfied by a wide range of x values — so a solution far from the truth can still leave a microscopic residual. The residual is necessary but not sufficient; to trust an answer you must also know κ(A).

Common mistakeReporting "my residual is 10-15, so my solution is correct to 15 digits." False. If κ(A) ≈ 1012, that same residual is consistent with an error of about 10-15 · 1012 = 10-3 — you may have only 3 correct digits. Always pair a residual check with a condition-number check (numpy.linalg.cond).

What can you do about ill-conditioning? Sometimes it is intrinsic to the physics (the problem really is sensitive). Often it signals a poor formulation: bad units that span huge magnitude ranges (fixable by scaling rows/columns), redundant equations, or a discretization that is too fine. When κ is genuinely large, switch to a more stable factorization (QR, or the SVD) or add regularization to trade a little bias for a lot of stability.

9.4A Physics Example: a Resistor Network

Conditioning is not an abstraction — it falls out of real hardware. Take a resistor network and apply Kirchhoff's laws. At each node, currents in equal currents out (current conservation); around each loop, voltage drops sum to zero. Writing nodal voltages as unknowns gives a linear system whose matrix is the conductance matrix G, with G x = i_source, where x are node voltages and i_source the injected currents.

G v = i   G_jj = sum of conductances at node j,  G_jk = -(conductance between j and k)The same symmetric, structured matrix appears for a static truss (stiffness matrix K u = f) and for a discretized Poisson equation — all are "conservation on a network."

When all resistors are comparable, G is well-conditioned and the node voltages are pinned down precisely. But put a huge spread of resistor values in the network — say a 1 ohm resistor next to a 109 ohm resistor — and G inherits that spread in its singular values. κ(G) balloons, and the computed voltages on the high-resistance branch become untrustworthy. The identical phenomenon shows up in a truss with one extremely stiff member among flexible ones, or in a Poisson grid with wildly varying material coefficients.

AnalogyThink of solving the network like weighing a feather and an anvil on the same scale at once. If the scale must resolve both, tiny errors in reading the anvil swamp the feather entirely. A huge resistor (or a super-stiff truss member) is the anvil: it forces the matrix to span many orders of magnitude, and the delicate quantities lose their digits. Rescaling units is like weighing each object on a scale matched to its size.
✦ Worked Example — a nearly-singular 2x2 system

Solve Ax = b with A = [[1, 1], [1, 1.0001]] and b = [2, 2.0001]. The exact solution is x = [1, 1]. Now perturb b to b' = [2, 2.0002] and watch what happens.

  1. Exact solve. Subtracting row 1 from row 2: 0.0001·x₂ = 0.0001, so x₂ = 1, then x₁ = 2 − 1 = 1. Solution x = [1, 1]. Good.
  2. Perturb b by one part in 10⁴. b' = [2, 2.0002] (we nudged the second entry up by 0.0001). Re-solve: 0.0001·x₂ = 0.0002, so x₂ = 2, then x₁ = 2 − 2 = 0. New solution x' = [0, 2].
  3. Compare. The data changed by a relative ~5×10⁻⁵, but the solution jumped from [1,1] to [0,2] — a relative change of order 1. The amplification is enormous.
  4. Condition number. The singular values of A are σ_max ≈ 2.0000 and σ_min ≈ 0.00005, so κ(A) = σ_max/σ_min ≈ 4×10⁴. That predicts up to ~4 lost digits — exactly the order-of-magnitude blow-up we saw.
  5. The residual lies. The wrong-looking x' = [0, 2] gives residual r = b − A·x' that is essentially zero in floating point, yet x' is nowhere near [1,1]. Small residual, large error — the signature of large κ.
  6. Takeaway. Always compute κ(A). Here κ ≈ 4×10⁴ warns you that the 4th–5th digits of any answer are meaningless, no matter how clean the residual looks.

🛠️ Hands-On: factor, solve, and probe the condition number

The free Python + NumPy/SciPy stack from Week 1 has everything you need (the one-time install details lives in Lecture 1). We will LU-factor a small resistor-network matrix, solve it for several source currents reusing the factorization, then deliberately push the network ill-conditioned and watch κ explode while the residual stays tiny.

# linear_systems.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
from scipy.linalg import lu_factor, lu_solve

# Conductance matrix G for a small resistor network (G v = i).
# Diagonal = sum of conductances at a node; off-diagonal = -(shared conductance).
G = np.array([[ 3.0, -1.0, -1.0],
              [-1.0,  4.0, -1.0],
              [-1.0, -1.0,  3.0]])

# Factor ONCE (O(n^3)); reuse for many source vectors (each O(n^2)).
lu, piv = lu_factor(G)
for i in [np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0]),
          np.array([0.0, 1.0, -1.0])]:
    v = lu_solve((lu, piv), i)
    r = i - G @ v                          # residual
    print("v =", np.round(v, 4), " ||r|| =", f"{np.linalg.norm(r):.1e}")

print("cond(G) =", f"{np.linalg.cond(G):.1f}")   # well-conditioned, ~ a few

# Now make it ILL-conditioned: a 1e9-ohm branch -> conductance 1e-9.
Gbad = np.array([[1e-9 + 1.0, -1.0,       0.0],
                 [-1.0,        1.0 + 1e9, -1e9],
                 [0.0,        -1e9,        1e9]])
b = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])
v = np.linalg.solve(Gbad, b)
r = b - Gbad @ v
print("cond(Gbad) =", f"{np.linalg.cond(Gbad):.1e}")  # huge!
print("||residual|| =", f"{np.linalg.norm(r):.1e}")   # still tiny — DON'T be fooled
# Lost digits ~ log10(cond): the small residual hides a large possible error.

Predict cond(Gbad) before running (hint: it scales with the spread of conductances, ~10⁹). Then try scaling each row of Gbad by its diagonal and re-checking cond — symmetric scaling often shrinks κ dramatically, recovering lost digits for free.

You hand a colleague a circuit simulation. They report: "I solved G v = i and my residual is 2×10⁻¹⁶ — basically machine zero — so the node voltages are correct." Is their reasoning sound? Under what circumstances would you trust their voltages, and what single additional number would you ask them to compute before you sign off on the result? Show a model response

The reasoning is not sound on its own. A tiny residual r = i − G·v only proves that v nearly satisfies the equation; it does not prove that v is close to the true voltages. The connection runs through the condition number: the relative error in v can be as large as κ(G) times the relative residual. So a residual of 10⁻¹⁶ is fully consistent with a wrong answer if κ(G) is, say, 10¹².

I would ask my colleague for one more number: κ(G) = numpy.linalg.cond(G). If κ is small (a handful, up to maybe 10³–10⁴), the small residual genuinely implies an accurate solution and I would sign off. If κ is large (10⁸ or more), the residual is meaningless as an accuracy certificate and we need to re-examine the formulation.

Physically, the network that worries me most is one with a huge spread of resistor magnitudes — e.g. a near-open branch (gigaohm) sitting beside ordinary 1-ohm paths. That spread forces G's singular values to span many orders of magnitude, so σ_max/σ_min = κ blows up and the delicate voltages lose their significant digits. The cure is usually to rescale (nondimensionalize) so all quantities are comparable in magnitude, or to switch to a more stable factorization.

❓ Lecture 9 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why factor A = LU instead of just running Gaussian elimination each time you get a new right-hand side b?
A. LU is the only way to detect a singular matrix
B. The O(n³) factorization is done once, so each new b costs only two O(n²) triangular solves
C. LU avoids all round-off error
D. LU works without any pivoting
Show answer

B. The costly elimination depends only on A; once L and U are stored, every new b is solved cheaply. A is false (a zero pivot, not LU specifically, flags singularity); C is false (LU still rounds); D is false — stable LU needs partial pivoting.

Q2. The condition number κ(A) in the 2-norm equals…
A. the determinant of A
B. the sum of the diagonal entries (the trace)
C. the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value, σ_max / σ_min
D. the number of rows in A
Show answer

C. κ = ||A||·||A⁻¹||, which in the 2-norm is σ_max/σ_min. The determinant (A) can be tiny or huge independent of conditioning; trace (B) and size (D) say nothing about error amplification.

Q3. You solve Ax = b and find a residual r = b − Ax̂ of about 10⁻¹⁵. The matrix has κ(A) ≈ 10¹². The relative error in your solution could be as large as roughly…
A. 10⁻¹⁵ (the residual guarantees the error)
B. 10⁻³
C. exactly zero
D. 10⁻²⁷
Show answer

B. The bound is relative error ≤ κ · relative residual ≈ 10¹² · 10⁻¹⁵ = 10⁻³ — about 3 trustworthy digits. A wrongly assumes residual = error; C and D ignore the amplification by κ entirely.

Q4. A resistor network has one 10⁹-ohm branch alongside several 1-ohm branches. What do you expect of the conductance matrix G?
A. κ(G) becomes large because the conductances span ~9 orders of magnitude, so node voltages lose accuracy
B. κ(G) stays near 1 regardless of resistor spread
C. G becomes non-square
D. the residual is guaranteed to be large
Show answer

A. A wide spread of conductances spreads G's singular values, inflating κ and eroding the delicate voltages. B is backwards; C is false (G stays n×n by node count); D is false — the residual often stays small even as the error grows.

Q5. Partial pivoting in Gaussian elimination is used primarily to…
A. make the algorithm faster than O(n³)
B. reduce the matrix's condition number permanently
C. guarantee the answer is exact
D. avoid dividing by a tiny or zero pivot, keeping the multipliers bounded and the process stable
Show answer

D. Swapping in the largest-magnitude pivot keeps multipliers ≤ 1 and prevents round-off blow-ups. It does not change asymptotic cost (A), does not alter κ(A) itself (B — conditioning is a property of A), and cannot make finite-precision arithmetic exact (C).

✓ What you learned

  • Gaussian elimination is an LU factorization (P A = L U with partial pivoting); factor once in O(n³), reuse for each b in O(n²).
  • The condition number κ(A) = ||A||·||A⁻¹|| = σ_max/σ_min bounds error amplification: relative error ≤ κ · relative data error.
  • κ ≈ 10ᵏ means you may lose k significant digits; well-conditioned κ is near 1, ill-conditioned κ is huge.
  • A small residual does not guarantee a small error — for large κ a far-wrong solution can still nearly satisfy the equation.
  • Physics systems (resistor networks, trusses, Poisson grids) become ill-conditioned when their quantities span many orders of magnitude; rescaling or QR/SVD restores stability.
