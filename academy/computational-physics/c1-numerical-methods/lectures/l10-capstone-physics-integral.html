Week 10 · Lecture 10

Capstone: A High-Precision Physics Integral

Ten weeks of error analysis, root finding, and quadrature converge on one deliverable — computing the true period of a pendulum to twelve digits and pinning down exactly where the textbook formula lies to you.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the exact pendulum period as a complete elliptic integral and recover the small-angle limit from it.
  • Choose a quadrature rule fit for an integrand with a near-singular endpoint and justify the choice.
  • Carry out a full error and convergence analysis: estimated error, observed order, and Richardson confirmation.
  • Build an error budget that separates truncation, round-off, and model (small-angle) error.
  • Find the crossover angle where the small-angle model fails a stated tolerance, and frame the capstone deliverable.

10.1The Exact Period Is an Integral the Textbook Hides

Every introductory course tells you a pendulum of length L swings with period T₀ = 2π√(L/g), independent of amplitude. That is a lie of convenience — true only in the limit of vanishing angle. The honest equation of motion is θ″ = −(g/L) sin θ, and because sin θ is not θ, the period actually grows with amplitude. Energy conservation turns the swing into a single, exact integral.

Releasing the bob from rest at amplitude θ₀, energy conservation gives the angular speed at angle θ, and integrating dt = dθ / θ′ over one quarter swing (from 0 to θ₀) yields the full period:

T(θ₀) = 4 √(L/g) · ∫₀^(θ₀) dθ / √( 2( cos θ − cos θ₀ ) )Exact for any amplitude θ₀. The factor 4 collects the four symmetric quarter-swings of one full oscillation.

A clean substitution removes the awkward cos θ − cos θ₀ form. Let k = sin(θ₀/2) and sin φ = sin(θ/2)/k. The integral collapses into the complete elliptic integral of the first kind K(k) — a named function that appears all over physics:

T(θ₀) = 4 √(L/g) · K(k),  k = sin(θ₀/2),  K(k) = ∫₀^(pi/2) dφ / √( 1 − k² sin²φ )The whole amplitude dependence lives in the dimensionless ratio T/T₀ = (2/pi)·K(k).

This is the capstone's target: compute K(k) to high precision by numerical quadrature, turn it into a period, and compare against the small-angle value T₀. Setting k → 0 (small θ₀) gives K(0) = π/2, which recovers T = 2π√(L/g) exactly — so the famous formula is just the first term of a series we will now measure the error of.

AnalogyThe small-angle formula is like reading a map projection near the equator: locally flat and faithful. Push toward the poles — toward large swing angles — and the projection stretches without bound. K(k) is the exact curved-earth distance; T₀ is the flat-map estimate that quietly accumulates error the farther you travel from θ₀ = 0.

10.2Choosing a Quadrature Rule for a Near-Singular Endpoint

K(k) looks tame, but watch the endpoint. As φ → π/2 and k → 1 (amplitude approaching 180°), the denominator √(1 − k² sin²φ) → √(1 − k²) = cos(θ₀/2), which heads to zero. The integrand develops a tall, sharp spike at the right edge. A naive uniform rule wastes points where the function is flat and undersamples where it explodes.

We have three honest options from this course, each with a known truncation order on a smooth integrand:

  • Composite Simpson (Week 7): error ∝ h⁴, order p = 4. Simple, predictable, but it needs many points near a sharp endpoint.
  • Gauss–Legendre (Week 8): n nodes integrate degree-(2n−1) polynomials exactly. For an analytic integrand it converges spectrally (error falls faster than any power of 1/n).
  • Adaptive Gauss–Kronrod (the engine inside scipy.integrate.quad): refines only where the integrand is hard, so it crushes the endpoint spike automatically.

For a smooth method on a smooth integrand the convergence model from Week 1 still rules everything:

error(n) ≈ C · h^p,  h ∝ 1/n  =>   doubling n cuts error by 2^pEstimate the observed order from two grids: p ≈ log2( error(n) / error(2n) ). For Simpson on a smooth K we expect p ≈ 4.
Common mistakeReporting a single number with no uncertainty. "I computed K = 1.6858" is worthless without an error bar. The capstone requires an estimated error (from the rule itself or from Richardson extrapolation) and an observed convergence order — a result you cannot defend is not a result.

10.3The Error Budget: Three Errors, Not One

The deliverable lives or dies on separating three completely different errors. Treating them as one number is the classic beginner failure; a professional report lists each line.

  • Truncation (method) error — the quadrature rule's own ∝ h^p error in computing K(k). You control it by refining n; you estimate it with Richardson or the rule's built-in estimate.
  • Round-off error — the floor at ~ε·|result| ≈ 10⁻¹⁶ from finite precision (Week 1). Refining n past the point where truncation drops below this floor buys nothing.
  • Model error — the gap between the small-angle T₀ and the true T(θ₀). This is physics error, not numerical error, and it is the whole point of the comparison.

The model error has a clean closed form from expanding K(k) in powers of k = sin(θ₀/2):

T(θ₀) / T₀ = 1 + (1/16)θ₀² + (11/3072)θ₀⁴ + ...Leading correction grows as θ₀². At θ₀ = 0.4 rad (≈ 23°) the small-angle model is already ~1% low.

The strategy of the report writes itself: drive the numerical error (truncation + round-off) far below the model error you are trying to measure. If your quadrature is good to 10⁻¹² but T₀ is wrong by 1%, the comparison is trustworthy — the discrepancy you see is real physics, not a numerical artifact. This separation, drilled since Week 1, is the single most important habit the capstone certifies.

10.4The Crossover Angle: Where the Textbook Formula Dies

"The small-angle approximation is fine for small angles" is not an engineering statement until you attach a tolerance. The crossover angle θ* is the amplitude at which the model error first exceeds a stated relative tolerance τ:

find θ* such that  T(θ*)/T₀ − 1 = τ  (e.g. τ = 0.01 for 1%)This is a root-finding problem (Weeks 2–4) wrapped around a quadrature (Weeks 7–8): define g(θ₀) = T/T₀ − 1 − τ and bisect for its root.

Using the leading term T/T₀ − 1 ≈ θ₀²/16, a 1% tolerance gives θ* ≈ √(0.16) ≈ 0.40 rad ≈ 23°; a 0.1% tolerance tightens it to θ* ≈ 7.2°. The exact root from quadrature plus bisection lands at θ* ≈ 22.9° for 1% — the series estimate is excellent here, which itself validates the chain.

AnalogyThe crossover angle is a speed limit sign for a model. Below θ* the small-angle formula is "in spec" and you may use it; above θ* you must pay for the full elliptic integral. Engineering is precisely the discipline of knowing where your cheap approximation stops being legal.
✦ Worked Example — period of a 1 m pendulum at θ₀ = 90°

A pendulum of length L = 1.000 m on Earth (g = 9.81 m/s²) is released from horizontal, θ₀ = 90° = π/2 rad. Find the small-angle period, the true period, and the model error.

  1. Small-angle period: T₀ = 2π√(L/g) = 2π√(1/9.81) = 2π(0.31944) = 2.0071 s.
  2. Modulus: k = sin(θ₀/2) = sin(45°) = √2/2 = 0.70711, so k² = 0.5.
  3. Compute K(k) by quadrature: K(0.70711) = ∫₀^(π/2) dφ/√(1 − 0.5 sin²φ) = 1.85407 (Gauss–Legendre, 16 nodes; matches the known closed form K(1/√2) = 1.854074677… to all shown digits).
  4. True period: T = 4√(L/g)·K(k) = 4(0.31944)(1.85407) = 2.3690 s.
  5. Model error: T/T₀ − 1 = 2.3690/2.0071 − 1 = 0.1803, so the small-angle formula is ~18% low at 90°. The leading series term θ₀²/16 = (π/2)²/16 = 0.1542 already flags a large miss; higher terms supply the rest.
  6. Numerical error check: doubling the node count changes K in the 12th digit only — truncation error ≈ 10⁻¹², far below the 18% physics gap, so the comparison is rock-solid.

🛠️ Hands-On: the full capstone computation

Use the same free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (the one-time install details lives in Lecture 1). Nothing new to install — this script is the capstone in miniature: compute K(k), build the convergence table, the error budget, and the crossover scan.

# capstone_pendulum.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np
from numpy.polynomial.legendre import leggauss
from scipy.integrate import quad
from scipy.special import ellipk          # reference: complete elliptic K(m), m = k**2

def K_gauss(k, n):
    # Gauss-Legendre quadrature of K(k) on [0, pi/2]
    x, w = leggauss(n)                     # nodes/weights on [-1, 1]
    a, b = 0.0, np.pi/2
    phi = 0.5*(b-a)*x + 0.5*(b+a)          # map to [0, pi/2]
    f = 1.0/np.sqrt(1.0 - k**2 * np.sin(phi)**2)
    return 0.5*(b-a) * np.dot(w, f)

theta0 = np.pi/2                           # 90 degrees
k = np.sin(theta0/2)
ref = ellipk(k**2)                         # SciPy uses parameter m = k**2
print("reference K =", ref)                # 1.8540746773...

# --- convergence table: observed order p = log2(e_n / e_2n) ---
prev = None
for n in [4, 8, 16, 32]:
    err = abs(K_gauss(k, n) - ref)
    p = np.log2(prev/err) if prev else float('nan')
    print(f"n={n:3d}  K={K_gauss(k,n):.12f}  err={err:.2e}  p~{p:.2f}")
    prev = err

# --- error budget ---
L, g = 1.0, 9.81
T0 = 2*np.pi*np.sqrt(L/g)
T  = 4*np.sqrt(L/g)*K_gauss(k, 32)
print(f"T0(small-angle)={T0:.6f}s  T(exact)={T:.6f}s  model err={T/T0-1:.4%}")

# --- crossover angle: bisect g(th)=T/T0-1 - tol ---
def model_err(th):
    kk = np.sin(th/2)
    return (2/np.pi)*ellipk(kk**2) - 1.0
tol = 0.01
lo, hi = 1e-3, np.pi-1e-3
for _ in range(60):                        # bisection (Week 2)
    mid = 0.5*(lo+hi)
    if model_err(mid) - tol > 0: hi = mid
    else: lo = mid
print(f"crossover theta* = {np.degrees(0.5*(lo+hi)):.2f} deg for 1% tol")

Expected output: the Gauss table shows the error plunging by far more than 2⁴ per doubling (spectral, not order-4 — the integrand is analytic at k = 1/√2), bottoming near 10⁻¹⁵; model error prints ≈ 18.03%; and the crossover lands at ≈ 22.9°. Then push θ₀ to 179° and watch quad earn its keep against the endpoint spike where fixed Gauss starts to struggle.

Q1. The exact pendulum period is T = 4√(L/g)·K(k) with k = sin(θ₀/2). In the small-angle limit θ₀ → 0, this reduces to T₀ = 2π√(L/g) because…
A. K(k) diverges, cancelling the prefactor
B. K(0) = π/2, so 4·(π/2) = 2π
C. k → 1 as θ₀ → 0
D. the substitution removes the factor of 4
Show answer

B. As θ₀ → 0, k = sin(θ₀/2) → 0 and K(0) = ∫₀^(π/2)dφ = π/2, giving T = 4√(L/g)(π/2) = 2π√(L/g) = T₀. (A) is wrong — K(0) is finite. (C) is backwards: k → 0, not 1. (D) misreads the algebra; the 4 survives and combines with π/2.

Q2. Why does the K(k) integrand demand an adaptive or endpoint-aware quadrature rule as θ₀ → 180°?
A. the integrand oscillates infinitely fast
B. the integration interval becomes infinite
C. as k → 1 the denominator → cos(θ₀/2) → 0 at φ = π/2, creating a sharp endpoint spike
D. the integrand becomes negative
Show answer

C. At φ = π/2 the denominator is √(1 − k²) = cos(θ₀/2), which → 0 as θ₀ → 180°, so the integrand spikes at the right endpoint. The interval stays [0, π/2] finite (B wrong), it does not oscillate (A) and stays positive (D).

Q3. A capstone "error budget" for this problem must separate which three errors?
A. human, compiler, and rounding error
B. truncation (method), round-off (precision), and model (small-angle) error
C. absolute, relative, and percentage error
D. systematic, random, and gross error
Show answer

B. The defensible report separates the quadrature's truncation error (∝ h^p), the ~10⁻¹⁶ round-off floor, and the physics model error (T₀ vs true T). (C) lists ways to measure one error, not three distinct sources; (A) and (D) are generic metrology categories, not what this analysis tracks.

Q4. You estimate K(k) at n and 2n Gauss nodes and want the observed convergence order. The right formula is…
A. p ≈ log₂( error(n) / error(2n) )
B. p ≈ error(n) − error(2n)
C. p ≈ error(2n) / error(n)
D. p ≈ n · error(n)
Show answer

A. With error ≈ C·h^p and h ∝ 1/n, halving h scales error by 2^(−p); taking log₂ of the ratio of the larger to the smaller error recovers p. (C) is the inverted ratio (gives 2^(−p) < 1), (B) and (D) have wrong units/scaling.

Q5. Using the leading correction T/T₀ − 1 ≈ θ₀²/16, the crossover amplitude where the small-angle model first exceeds a 1% tolerance is about…
A. 90°
B. 45°
C. 5°
D. 23°
Show answer

D. Set θ₀²/16 = 0.01 → θ₀ = √0.16 = 0.40 rad ≈ 23° (the exact quadrature+bisection value is ≈ 22.9°). At 90° (A) the error is already ~18%; 45° (B) gives ~4%; 5° (C) is far inside tolerance at ~0.05%.

✓ What you learned

  • The exact pendulum period is T = 4√(L/g)·K(k) with k = sin(θ₀/2); the famous T₀ = 2π√(L/g) is just the K(0) = π/2 limit.
  • K(k)'s integrand spikes at the endpoint as θ₀ → 180°, so the rule must be adaptive or endpoint-aware; on the smooth interior, Gauss converges spectrally.
  • A defensible result carries a convergence analysis — estimated error and observed order p ≈ log₂(eₙ/e₂ₙ).
  • The error budget separates truncation, round-off, and model error; you drive the numerical error far below the physics gap you are measuring.
  • The crossover angle (θ* ≈ 23° for 1%) is a root-finding problem wrapped around quadrature — the precise statement of where the textbook formula fails.
