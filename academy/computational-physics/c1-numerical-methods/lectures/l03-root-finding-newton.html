Week 3 · Lecture 3

Root Finding II — Newton & Secant

Bisection always wins the race but plods; Newton's method sprints by riding the tangent line — and learning when it stumbles is what separates a working solver from a hanging one.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive Newton's method from the tangent line of a Taylor expansion and explain its update rule.
  • State and interpret quadratic convergence — why the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step.
  • Derive the secant method as a derivative-free Newton, and know its golden-ratio convergence order.
  • Recognize the failure modes — overshoot, cycling, and divergence — and why a bracketing fallback (like Brent's method) is wise.
  • Compare bisection, secant, and Newton on cost-per-iteration versus rate, and implement a safeguarded solver in SciPy.

3.1Newton's Method: Ride the Tangent Line

Last week, bisection found a root by relentlessly halving a bracket. It is safe but slow — it ignores everything about the shape of f except the sign at the endpoints. Newton's method asks a sharper question: if I am standing at a guess xn, which way is the root, and how far? The answer is hidden in the slope.

Expand f in a Taylor series about the current guess xn and keep only the linear term:

f(x) ≈ f(x_n) + f'(x_n)·(x - x_n)Replace the true curve with its tangent line at the current point — a "local linear model" of f.

The tangent line is an honest stand-in for the curve nearby. Where does that line cross zero? Set the right side to 0 and solve for x; call the answer the next guess xn+1:

x_(n+1) = x_n - f(x_n) / f'(x_n)The Newton iteration: slide down the tangent line to the x-axis, then re-linearize there and repeat.

Geometrically: draw the tangent at your guess, follow it to the x-axis, and that landing point is your new guess. Each step the curve looks more like its tangent (you are closer to the root), so the corrections shrink fast.

AnalogyYou are descending a hill in thick fog toward sea level (the root). You can't see the shoreline, but you can feel the slope under your feet. Newton's rule is: read the slope, extrapolate in a straight line to where that slope would hit sea level, and walk there. If the hill is smooth and you started reasonably close, each guess overshoots far less than the last — you home in astonishingly fast.

3.2Quadratic Convergence — Why Digits Double

The reason Newton's method feels magical is its convergence order. Let en = xn − r be the error from the true root r. A second-order Taylor expansion of the iteration shows that, near a simple root,

e_(n+1) ≈ [ f''(r) / (2 f'(r)) ] · e_n^2The new error is proportional to the SQUARE of the old error: convergence of order p = 2.

Squaring a small error is dramatic. If your error is 10−2, the next is about 10−4, then 10−8, then 10−16 — machine precision in four steps. Informally: the number of correct decimal digits roughly doubles every iteration. Contrast bisection, which gains a fixed one bit (about 0.3 digit) per step — linear convergence.

Newton: order 2 (digits double)  |  Secant: order ≈ 1.618  |  Bisection: order 1 (linear)Higher order means fewer iterations — but each Newton step costs an extra derivative evaluation.

Two important caveats hide in that error formula. First, it assumes a simple root, where f'(r) ≠ 0. At a double root (f' = 0 too), the 1/f' factor misbehaves and Newton degrades to merely linear convergence. Second, the quadratic rate is local: it only kicks in once you are close enough. Far away, anything can happen — which is the subject of the next section.

Common mistakeReporting "Newton converges quadratically" as if it were unconditional. The order-2 result is local and assumes a simple root with a continuous second derivative. At a multiple root, or from a bad starting point, Newton can crawl linearly, oscillate, or fly off entirely. Never quote the rate without the hypotheses.

3.3The Secant Method: Newton Without a Derivative

Newton's update needs f'(xn) at every step. But in real physics code the function is often a black box — the output of a simulation, an experimental fit, or a tangled expression whose analytic derivative is painful or impossible. The secant method sidesteps this by approximating the slope from the two most recent points:

f'(x_n) ≈ [ f(x_n) - f(x_(n-1)) ] / ( x_n - x_(n-1) )A finite-difference slope using points you already evaluated — no extra cost, no derivative needed.

Substitute that into Newton's rule and you get the secant iteration. Geometrically, instead of the tangent line you draw the secant line through your two latest points and follow it to the x-axis:

x_(n+1) = x_n - f(x_n) · ( x_n - x_(n-1) ) / ( f(x_n) - f(x_(n-1)) )Requires two starting guesses; each new step reuses the previous f value, so only ONE new evaluation per step.

The secant method converges with order φ ≈ 1.618, the golden ratio — slower than Newton's 2, but it needs no derivative and only one function evaluation per step (Newton needs two: f and f'). When a function call is expensive — as it is when each evaluation runs a whole simulation — secant often wins the wall-clock race even though it takes more iterations.

3.4Failure Modes & the Bracketing Fallback

Speed has a price: Newton and secant carry no safety net. Bisection can never leave its bracket, so it cannot diverge. The tangent-line gamble, by contrast, can throw you anywhere. Three classic failure modes recur:

  • Overshoot / divergence. Near a point where f' is small (a near-flat spot), the tangent is nearly horizontal, so the step −f/f' is huge and flings the guess far away — possibly diverging to infinity.
  • Cycling. Some functions trap Newton in a periodic orbit, e.g. the iteration bounces x → −x → x forever. The canonical example is f(x) = x1/3, where Newton's step is exactly −2xn, so |x| grows each step.
  • Wrong basin. With multiple roots, a poor start lands you at a different root than intended — or at one far from your region of interest.
x_(n+1) = x_n - f(x_n)/f'(x_n)  ->   blows up when f'(x_n) ≈ 0A near-zero derivative is the danger sign: the step length is f/f', which explodes as f' -> 0.

The professional answer is a hybrid solver: take a fast Newton or secant step when it lands inside the current bracket, but if the step would jump outside, fall back to a guaranteed bisection step instead. You keep the bracket's safety and Newton's speed. This is exactly the idea behind Brent's method (combining bisection, secant, and inverse-quadratic interpolation), the default in SciPy's brentq and the workhorse for most production root-finding.

Common mistakeShipping a bare Newton loop with while abs(f(x)) > tol and no iteration cap or bracket. On a hard function it can loop forever or return a number from a divergent trajectory. Always add a maximum-iteration guard and, when you have a sign-change bracket, prefer a safeguarded method like brentq.
✦ Worked Example — Newton for the square root of 2

Solve f(x) = x² − 2 = 0 (so the root is √2 ≈ 1.41421356). With f'(x) = 2x, Newton's rule simplifies to the famous "Babylonian" iteration x ← (x + 2/x)/2. Start at x₀ = 1 and watch the digits double.

  1. Iteration 1: x₁ = (1 + 2/1)/2 = 1.5. Error vs √2: e₁ ≈ 0.0858.
  2. Iteration 2: x₂ = (1.5 + 2/1.5)/2 = 1.41666667. Error e₂ ≈ 2.45×10⁻³ — roughly e₁² as predicted.
  3. Iteration 3: x₃ = (1.41666667 + 2/1.41666667)/2 = 1.41421569. Error e₃ ≈ 2.12×10⁻⁶ — again about e₂².
  4. Iteration 4: x₄ = 1.41421356237469. Error e₄ ≈ 1.6×10⁻¹² — essentially e₃². Correct digits: 1 → 3 → 6 → 12, doubling each step.
  5. Iteration 5: x₅ = 1.4142135623730951 — full double precision (e₅ below 10⁻¹⁶). Newton hit machine epsilon in five steps; bisection on [1, 2] would need about 52 steps to match.

🛠️ Hands-On: race Newton, secant, and bisection

Use the same free Python + NumPy/SciPy stack from Week 1 (the one-time install lives in Lecture 1). No new tools needed here — SciPy already ships newton (which also does secant) and brentq.

Below, find a root of f(x) = x³ − x − 2 (real root ≈ 1.5214). We count iterations for hand-rolled Newton and secant, then call SciPy's safeguarded solvers and the bisection from last week.

# root_finding_newton.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import newton, brentq, bisect

f  = lambda x: x**3 - x - 2
fp = lambda x: 3*x**2 - 1          # analytic derivative

# --- hand-rolled Newton: watch quadratic convergence ---
x, root = 1.5, 1.5213797068045676
for k in range(1, 7):
    x = x - f(x)/fp(x)
    print(f"Newton step {k}: x={x:.15f}  err={abs(x-root):.2e}")

# --- hand-rolled secant: no derivative, two seeds ---
a, b = 1.0, 2.0
for k in range(1, 9):
    a, b = b, b - f(b)*(b-a)/(f(b)-f(a))
    print(f"Secant step {k}: x={b:.15f}  err={abs(b-root):.2e}")

# --- SciPy: derivative-based Newton, derivative-free secant, safeguarded Brent ---
print("scipy newton (with fprime):", newton(f, x0=1.5, fprime=fp))
print("scipy secant (no fprime)  :", newton(f, x0=1.5))      # falls back to secant
print("scipy brentq on [1,2]     :", brentq(f, 1.0, 2.0))    # safeguarded, always converges
print("plain bisection on [1,2]  :", bisect(f, 1.0, 2.0))

Try a pathological start: call newton(lambda x: x**3 - 5*x, x0=1.0). With f'(1) small relative to the curvature, the bare Newton step overshoots and may converge to the wrong root or warn. Then solve the same with brentq(..., -1, 0.5) and confirm the safeguarded method lands cleanly.

🔗 Where this is heading

Newton's method is the seed of a vast family. In Week 4 we interpolate functions with polynomials — the very tool inside Brent's "inverse-quadratic" step. The multivariable generalization, where 1/f' becomes the inverse of a Jacobian matrix, drives nonlinear solvers everywhere: finding equilibrium in circuit and power-grid simulation, implicit time-stepping for stiff ODEs (Course 2), self-consistent field iterations in quantum chemistry (the Quantum track), and the Newton step at the heart of optimization and maximum-likelihood fitting (the Quant track). Master the tangent-line idea here and you have met the engine that powers most of computational science.

💬 Discussion

You are writing a root-finder that will be called millions of times inside a physics pipeline, on a function that is moderately expensive (each evaluation runs a small simulation) and whose analytic derivative is unavailable. You can bracket the root cheaply. Which method would you reach for — bisection, secant, or a safeguarded hybrid like Brent's — and why? Argue in terms of convergence order, cost per iteration, and robustness. Where does "fastest convergence order" stop being the right thing to optimize?

Show a model response

I'd choose a safeguarded hybrid (Brent's method / brentq). Here is the reasoning, weighed on three axes:

Convergence order. No derivative is available, so pure Newton (order 2) is off the table unless I pay for a finite-difference derivative — which costs an extra expensive evaluation per step and reintroduces the round-off/step-size trade-off from Lecture 1. Secant gives order ≈ 1.618 with just one new evaluation per step, so per-evaluation it is actually competitive with Newton. Bisection is only linear.

Cost per iteration. Because each call runs a simulation, the metric that matters is total function evaluations, not iteration count. Secant minimizes evaluations-per-step; bisection wastes them by ignoring function values.

Robustness. Called millions of times on varied inputs, a bare secant will eventually hit a near-flat region and overshoot or diverge — and a hung or wrong solve in an automated pipeline is far costlier than a few extra evaluations. Since I can bracket cheaply, Brent's method gives me secant/inverse-quadratic speed in the easy case while guaranteeing the bracket can never be lost.

Where order stops mattering: once a method is "fast enough" to reach tolerance in a handful of steps, additional convergence order buys almost nothing, while a single divergence costs the whole run. Past that point I optimize for guaranteed termination and bounded evaluations, not raw rate.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer: (1) names the no-derivative constraint and rules out plain Newton or prices its finite-difference cost; (2) distinguishes iteration count from total function evaluations given the expensive call; (3) invokes the safety of bracketing against the real failure modes (overshoot/divergence/cycling); (4) explicitly states that beyond "fast enough," robustness and guaranteed termination outrank convergence order.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Newton's update xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f'(xn) comes from…
A. halving the interval where f changes sign
B. finding where the tangent line at xn crosses the x-axis
C. averaging the two previous guesses
D. fitting a parabola through three points
Show answer

B. Keep the linear Taylor term f(x) ≈ f(xn) + f'(xn)(x − xn), set it to zero, and solve — that is the tangent's x-intercept. (A) is bisection; (C) is unrelated; (D) describes inverse-quadratic/Muller-style steps, not Newton.

Q2. "Quadratic convergence" of Newton's method near a simple root means…
A. the error halves each iteration
B. the method needs a quadratic function to work
C. the new error is proportional to the square of the old error, so correct digits roughly double each step
D. it takes two iterations per root
Show answer

C. en+1 ≈ C·en², so 10⁻² → 10⁻⁴ → 10⁻⁸ → … and the number of correct digits doubles. (A) describes linear convergence (bisection). (B) and (D) are misreadings of the word "quadratic."

Q3. The secant method differs from Newton chiefly in that it…
A. requires the exact analytic derivative at every step
B. approximates the slope from the two latest points, needing no derivative
C. is guaranteed never to leave its bracket
D. converges faster than Newton
Show answer

B. Secant replaces f'(xn) with the finite-difference slope [f(xn) − f(xn−1)]/(xn − xn−1). (A) is Newton; (C) is bisection (secant has no bracket guarantee); (D) is false — secant's order ≈ 1.618 is below Newton's 2.

Q4. A bare Newton iteration is most likely to diverge or overshoot when…
A. the starting guess is exactly the root
B. f is a straight line
C. the function is bracketed with a sign change
D. the derivative f'(xn) is near zero (a near-flat spot)
Show answer

D. The step is −f/f', so a tiny f' makes the step enormous and flings the guess far away. (A) converges instantly; (B) makes Newton land exactly on the root in one step; (C) is the condition that helps a safeguarded fallback.

Q5. Why combine Newton/secant with bisection in a hybrid like Brent's method?
A. to get bisection's guaranteed convergence (the bracket can't be lost) plus Newton/secant's speed
B. because bisection alone converges quadratically
C. to avoid ever evaluating the function
D. because Newton's method cannot find any root without it
Show answer

A. Take a fast open step when it stays inside the bracket; otherwise fall back to a safe bisection step — safety plus speed. (B) is false (bisection is linear). (C) is impossible. (D) is false — Newton works fine from a good start.

✓ What you learned

  • Newton's method follows the tangent line to the axis: xn+1 = xn − f(xn)/f'(xn).
  • Quadratic convergence near a simple root — en+1 ∝ en² — so correct digits roughly double each step.
  • The secant method replaces the derivative with a chord slope (order ≈ 1.618), ideal when no derivative is available.
  • The failure modes — overshoot, cycling, divergence near f' ≈ 0 — and why open methods need a bracketing fallback.
  • Cost vs rate: bisection is safe but linear; Newton/secant are fast but unsafe; Brent's method (brentq) gives both.
