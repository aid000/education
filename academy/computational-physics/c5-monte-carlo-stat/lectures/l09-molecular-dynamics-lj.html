Week 9 · Lecture 9

Molecular Dynamics & Lennard-Jones

Give a few hundred atoms a position, a velocity, and Newton's second law — and watch matter melt, freeze, and flow without ever telling it the rules of a phase.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how molecular dynamics (MD) is the deterministic, time-resolved complement to Monte Carlo sampling.
  • Write the Lennard-Jones pair potential and derive the interatomic force as its negative gradient.
  • Integrate the equations of motion with velocity-Verlet and state why it conserves energy so well.
  • Set and control temperature through the equipartition theorem and velocity rescaling.
  • Compute and read the radial distribution function g(r) as a structural fingerprint of solid, liquid, and gas.

9.1Two Roads to a Thermal Average

For eight weeks we sampled configurations with Monte Carlo: propose a move, accept it with the Metropolis probability, and let a random walk explore the Boltzmann distribution. We never asked how the system got from one state to the next — only that, in the long run, states appear with weight e−E/kBT. Monte Carlo is a machine for static averages.

Molecular dynamics takes the opposite road. It is fully deterministic: every atom obeys Newton's second law, and we integrate those equations forward in time. The system explores phase space not by random hops but by genuinely moving — atoms accelerate, collide, and rearrange exactly as classical mechanics dictates.

mi  d²ri/dt² = Fi = −∇i U(r1, …, rN)One coupled second-order ODE per atom; the force on atom i is the negative gradient of the total potential energy U.

Both methods sample the same equilibrium ensemble — that is the ergodic hypothesis: a long time average equals an ensemble average. But MD gives something Monte Carlo cannot: real dynamics. Diffusion coefficients, viscosity, vibrational spectra, the rate a protein folds — anything that depends on time, not just on the distribution of states, must come from MD.

AnalogyMonte Carlo is like surveying a city by teleporting to random addresses weighted by population — you learn where people live but never how they commute. Molecular dynamics is like riding along in every car at once: slower to cover the map, but you also measure the traffic, the routes, and the rush-hour timing. The static census agrees; only one of them gives you travel times.

9.2The Lennard-Jones Potential and Its Force

To run MD we need U. For neutral atoms (think argon) the textbook choice is the Lennard-Jones (LJ) pair potential. Each pair of atoms a distance r apart contributes:

U(r) = 4ε [ (sigma/r)12 − (sigma/r)6 ]ε sets the depth of the attractive well; sigma sets the distance where U crosses zero. (In SVG text we write the Greek letter sigma in ASCII.)

The physics lives in two competing terms. The r−12 term is a steep, short-range repulsion — a stand-in for the Pauli exclusion of overlapping electron clouds; atoms simply cannot interpenetrate. The −r−6 term is a gentler, longer-range attraction from induced-dipole (van der Waals) forces. Their balance carves a potential well whose minimum sits at:

rmin = 21/6 sigma ≈ 1.122 sigma,   U(rmin) = −εThe equilibrium separation: deep enough to bind a solid, shallow enough that thermal energy can melt it.

Force is the negative derivative of U with respect to r, directed along the line joining the two atoms. Differentiating gives a tidy closed form:

F(r) = −dU/dr = (24ε/r) [ 2(sigma/r)12 − (sigma/r)6 ]Positive (repulsive, pushing apart) for r < rmin; negative (attractive, pulling together) for r > rmin; exactly zero at rmin.

In practice we truncate the potential at a cutoff (commonly rc = 2.5 sigma, where U has fallen to about −1.6% of ε) so the force on each atom is a finite sum over near neighbors. We also work in reduced units: lengths in sigma, energies in ε, masses in m. Then ε = sigma = m = 1 and every result is dimensionless and transferable.

9.3Velocity-Verlet: Marching Newton Forward

We now have forces; we need a time integrator. The naive choice — Euler's method from Course 2 — drifts in energy and blows up over the millions of steps a real MD run needs. The standard MD workhorse is velocity-Verlet, which updates positions and velocities in a symmetric leap:

r(t+Δt) = r(t) + v(t)Δt + ½ a(t) Δt²
v(t+Δt) = v(t) + ½ [ a(t) + a(t+Δt) ] ΔtCompute new positions, evaluate the force there to get a(t+Δt), then finish the velocity update using the average of old and new accelerations.

Why this scheme and not a fancier high-order one? Velocity-Verlet is symplectic and time-reversible: it respects the geometric structure of Hamiltonian mechanics. The consequence is remarkable — although the energy of any single step has an O(Δt²) error, that error does not accumulate. Instead the total energy oscillates within a tight bound essentially forever. A Runge-Kutta solver, by contrast, is more accurate per step but slowly bleeds energy, ruining a long thermodynamic run.

Common mistakeChoosing the timestep Δt too large to "save time." The fastest motion an atom can make — rattling against the steep r−12 wall — must be resolved with roughly 20–50 steps per oscillation. In reduced LJ units that means Δt ≈ 0.005. Push to Δt = 0.05 and the integrator overshoots the repulsive wall, energy explodes, and atoms fly off to infinity ("the simulation blew up"). When energy drifts, shrink Δt before you suspect anything else.

One more essential ingredient: periodic boundary conditions. A few hundred atoms in a box is mostly surface, which is unphysical for studying bulk matter. We wrap the box so an atom leaving the right face re-enters on the left, and each atom interacts with the nearest image of every other atom. The small box then mimics an infinite crystal or fluid.

9.4Temperature and the Structural Fingerprint g(r)

Newton's equations conserve energy, not temperature — so where does T come from? From the equipartition theorem: each translational degree of freedom carries ½kBT of kinetic energy on average. Summing over N atoms in three dimensions gives an instantaneous "kinetic temperature":

(3/2) N kB T = ⟨ ½ Σi mi vi² ⟩  ⟹   T = (1 / 3N kB) Σi mi vi²Temperature is simply a measure of the mean kinetic energy of the atoms.

To set a target temperature during equilibration, the simplest thermostat is velocity rescaling: multiply every velocity by the factor λ = √(Ttarget / Tcurrent) so the measured T jumps to the value you want. (Gentler thermostats — Berendsen, Nosé-Hoover — refine this without disturbing the dynamics as crudely; rescaling is enough to build intuition.)

Once equilibrated, how do we tell whether our box is a solid, a liquid, or a gas? The single most informative diagnostic is the radial distribution function g(r): the probability of finding another atom a distance r away, normalized so that an ideal gas gives g(r) = 1 everywhere.

g(r) = (V / N²) ⟨ Σi Σj≠i δ(r − rij) ⟩ / (4π r²)Count neighbor pairs in a shell of radius r and thickness dr, divide by the count expected for a uniform gas of the same density.

The shape of g(r) is a fingerprint. A gas shows a single broad bump then flatlines at 1 — almost no structure. A liquid has a tall first peak (the nearest-neighbor shell), a weaker second peak, and then decays to 1: short-range order, no long-range order. A solid shows a tall, sharp series of peaks at the crystal-lattice spacings that persist out to large r — long-range order. You can see the phase without ever labeling it.

✦ Worked Example — one velocity-Verlet step for two LJ atoms

Two argon atoms in 1D reduced units (ε = sigma = m = 1) sit at x1 = 0 and x2 = 1.5, both at rest. Take one velocity-Verlet step for atom 2 with Δt = 0.01.

  1. Separation: r = 1.5. Since r > rmin = 1.122, expect a (small) attractive force pulling the atoms together.
  2. Force on atom 2: F(r) = (24/r)[2(1/r)12 − (1/r)6]. With 1/r = 0.6667: (1/r)6 = 0.0878, (1/r)12 = 0.00771. So F = (24/1.5)[2(0.00771) − 0.0878] = 16 × (−0.0724) = −1.158 (toward atom 1). Acceleration a = F/m = −1.158.
  3. Position update: x₂(t+Δt) = 1.5 + 0·Δt + ½(−1.158)(0.01)² = 1.5 − 5.79×10⁻⁵ = 1.499942.
  4. New force: at r = 1.499942 the force is essentially unchanged, a(t+Δt) ≈ −1.158.
  5. Velocity update: v₂ = 0 + ½[(−1.158) + (−1.158)](0.01) = −1.158×10⁻². Atom 2 has begun to drift toward atom 1 — exactly the van der Waals attraction, recovered from Newton's law alone.

🛠️ Hands-On: a tiny 2D Lennard-Jones simulator

This uses the same free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the one-time install details lives in the early lectures) — no new tools needed. Numba is optional if you want to push to thousands of atoms. Below: a self-contained N-atom LJ engine with velocity-Verlet, a velocity-rescaling thermostat, and a g(r) histogram.

# lj_md.py — Lecture 9: velocity-Verlet MD of a 2D Lennard-Jones fluid
import numpy as np

N, L = 64, 9.0          # atoms, square box side (reduced units, eps=sig=m=1)
dt, rc = 0.005, 2.5     # timestep and force cutoff
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

pos = rng.uniform(0, L, (N, 2))         # random start (let it relax)
vel = rng.normal(0, 1, (N, 2))
vel -= vel.mean(axis=0)                  # zero total momentum

def forces(pos):
    f = np.zeros_like(pos); pe = 0.0
    for i in range(N):
        d = pos[i] - pos                 # vectors to all atoms
        d -= L * np.round(d / L)         # minimum-image convention (PBC)
        r2 = (d**2).sum(1)
        mask = (r2 < rc*rc) & (r2 > 1e-12)
        inv6 = (1.0 / r2[mask])**3
        fmag = (24 * (2*inv6**2 - inv6) / r2[mask])[:, None]
        f[i] += (fmag * d[mask]).sum(0)
        pe   += (4 * (inv6**2 - inv6)).sum()
    return f, 0.5 * pe                   # halve PE (each pair counted twice)

def temperature(vel):
    return (vel**2).sum() / (2 * N)      # 2D: 2 dof per atom, kB=1

f, _ = forces(pos)
for step in range(4000):
    vel += 0.5 * dt * f                  # half-kick
    pos = (pos + dt * vel) % L           # drift + wrap into box
    f, pe = forces(pos)
    vel += 0.5 * dt * f                  # second half-kick
    if step < 1000:                      # thermostat during equilibration
        vel *= np.sqrt(0.6 / temperature(vel))   # target T* = 0.6

print("final T*:", round(temperature(vel), 3))

Then build g(r): histogram all pair distances under the minimum-image rule and divide each bin by the ideal-gas shell count rho * 2*pi*r*dr. Run at density ρ ≈ 0.3 and T* ≈ 1.0 (gas-like), then at ρ ≈ 0.8 and T* ≈ 0.5 (liquid/solid) and watch the first peak of g(r) sharpen. Track the total energy 0.5*(vel**2).sum() + pe with the thermostat off — it should stay flat to a fraction of a percent, the signature of a working symplectic integrator.

💬 Discussion

Monte Carlo and molecular dynamics both claim to sample the same equilibrium ensemble, yet they reach it by completely different routes — random hops versus deterministic trajectories. Suppose you must measure the self-diffusion coefficient of liquid argon and, separately, the average potential energy per atom. Which method would you choose for each, and why? Where might one method secretly fail to be ergodic, and how would g(r) or an energy-drift plot warn you? Argue from first principles.

Show a model response

Average potential energy per atom is a pure static average — it depends only on how often each configuration appears, not on time-ordering. Either method works, but Monte Carlo is often more efficient: large or cleverly designed moves can decorrelate configurations faster than MD's small physical timesteps, and MC need not resolve fast vibrations. The self-diffusion coefficient is intrinsically dynamical — it is the long-time slope of the mean-squared displacement (or the integral of the velocity autocorrelation). Standard Metropolis MC has no physical time axis, so it simply cannot deliver D; molecular dynamics is the right tool, because its trajectories are the real motion.

Ergodicity can quietly break in both. In MD at low temperature the system can get trapped in one crystalline basin and never explore competing structures within the run — a tell is that g(r) keeps a frozen, glassy peak pattern and the energy plateaus too soon. In MC, poorly chosen moves give vanishing acceptance and the walk stalls in one region. The cheap diagnostics are exactly what we built: a flat-with-bounded-oscillation energy-drift plot confirms the MD integrator is sound (drift means Δt is too large), and comparing g(r) from independent runs started differently reveals whether the system has truly explored its accessible states.

Q1. What is the single most important capability that molecular dynamics has and standard Metropolis Monte Carlo lacks?
A. It can compute the average potential energy
B. It gives genuine time-dependent quantities such as diffusion coefficients
C. It samples the Boltzmann distribution
D. It works only for charged particles
Show answer

B. MD produces real trajectories, so dynamical quantities (D, viscosity, spectra) follow. (A) and (C) are static averages both methods give; (D) is false — LJ models neutral atoms.

Q2. In the Lennard-Jones potential U(r) = 4ε[(σ/r)12 − (σ/r)6], the r−12 term physically represents…
A. gravitational attraction between atoms
B. long-range van der Waals attraction
C. short-range Pauli repulsion of overlapping electron clouds
D. the kinetic energy of the atoms
Show answer

C. The steep r−12 wall models the repulsion that stops electron clouds from overlapping. The −r−6 term (B) is the van der Waals attraction; gravity (A) is utterly negligible at atomic scales; kinetic energy (D) is not part of U.

Q3. Why is velocity-Verlet preferred over a higher-order Runge-Kutta method for long MD runs?
A. It is symplectic and time-reversible, so energy stays bounded instead of drifting
B. It has zero error at every step
C. It does not require evaluating forces
D. It allows an arbitrarily large timestep
Show answer

A. Symplectic, reversible integrators keep total energy oscillating within a tight bound over millions of steps; RK is more accurate per step but slowly bleeds energy. (B) is false (it is O(Δt²) per step), (C) is false (it needs a(t+Δt)), (D) is false — Δt must resolve the fastest vibration.

Q4. A simulation's total energy starts drifting steadily upward and atoms eventually fly apart. The most likely cause is…
A. the temperature was set too low
B. the radial distribution function was computed incorrectly
C. too few atoms in the box
D. the timestep Δt is too large to resolve the fast repulsive-wall motion
Show answer

D. An oversized Δt makes the integrator overshoot the steep r−12 wall, injecting energy until the run "blows up"; shrink Δt first. Temperature setting (A) and g(r) (B) do not cause energy injection, and atom count (C) affects statistics, not stability.

Q5. A radial distribution function shows a tall sharp first peak, a weaker second peak, and then settles to g(r) = 1 with no further structure. This is the fingerprint of a…
A. liquid (short-range order, no long-range order)
B. perfect crystal with long-range order
C. dilute ideal gas
D. vacuum
Show answer

A. One or two near peaks decaying to 1 is the classic liquid signature: neighbors are organized locally but order is lost at distance. A solid (B) keeps sharp peaks out to large r; a gas (C) is nearly featureless at g ≈ 1; a vacuum (D) has no atoms to correlate.

✓ What you learned

  • Molecular dynamics integrates Newton's law deterministically — the time-resolved complement to Monte Carlo's static sampling.
  • The Lennard-Jones potential balances r−12 repulsion against −r−6 attraction; the force is its negative gradient, F = (24ε/r)[2(σ/r)12 − (σ/r)6].
  • Velocity-Verlet is symplectic and time-reversible, so total energy stays bounded over long runs — unlike Euler or RK.
  • Temperature comes from equipartition (T ∝ mean kinetic energy) and can be imposed with velocity rescaling; periodic boundaries mimic bulk matter.
  • The radial distribution function g(r) fingerprints the phase: featureless gas, peaked-then-flat liquid, sharp long-range-ordered solid.
