Week 6 · Lecture 6

The 2D Ising Model

A grid of arrows that each only "talk" to their neighbors — yet together they spontaneously decide to point the same way, and a computer can watch them do it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the 2D Ising model: spins on a lattice, nearest-neighbor coupling, and the Hamiltonian.
  • Compute the local energy change ΔE for flipping a single spin and explain why only its four neighbors matter.
  • State the single-spin Metropolis acceptance rule and connect it to the Boltzmann weight from Week 5.
  • Define the magnetization as the order parameter and interpret what it tells you about the phase.
  • Implement an Ising Monte Carlo sweep in Python with periodic boundaries and measure <|M|>.

6.1Spins on a Lattice: the Model of a Magnet

Why is a bar magnet magnetic, and why does it abruptly lose its magnetism when you heat it past a certain temperature? The simplest model that captures this is the Ising model: a stripped-down cartoon of a magnetic material. Place a tiny magnetic moment — a spin — on every site of a square grid. Each spin is allowed only two states, "up" or "down," written as a number:

si = +1 (up)  or   si = -1 (down)The i-th spin. On an L × L lattice there are N = L2 spins, so 2N possible configurations.

This is a brutal simplification of real quantum spins, but it is exactly enough. Real materials have spins that can point in any direction; Ising keeps only the two states that matter most and throws away everything else. The payoff is that the model is simple enough to simulate yet rich enough to show a genuine phase transition — the magnet-to-non-magnet change — which is the headline result we build toward next week.

Crucially, a spin does not interact with the whole lattice. It only feels its nearest neighbors: on a square lattice that is the four sites directly up, down, left, and right. Magnetism, like a rumor, spreads locally — each spin pressures only its immediate neighbors, and order has to ripple outward from there.

6.2The Hamiltonian: Energy of a Configuration

Physics decides what happens by counting energy. Every full configuration of all N spins has a total energy given by the Hamiltonian. For the Ising model with no external field it is a sum over all nearest-neighbor pairs:

E = -J · Σ<i,j> si sjThe sum runs over each neighboring pair once; J > 0 is the coupling strength. We set J = 1 and k_B = 1 as our energy and temperature units.

Read the minus sign carefully. When two neighbors agree (both +1 or both -1), the product sisj = +1, and the term contributes -J — low energy. When they disagree, the product is -1 and the term contributes +J — high energy. Because nature seeks low energy, the model has a built-in preference: neighbors want to align. That alignment tendency, fighting against the randomizing kick of temperature, is the entire drama.

If we add an external magnetic field h that pulls spins toward up, the Hamiltonian gains a second term, -hΣisi. This lecture sticks with h = 0, where the up and down states are perfectly symmetric — which is what makes spontaneous symmetry breaking so striking.

AnalogyThink of a packed stadium where everyone can either stand or sit, and each person only cares about matching the four people immediately around them. There is social "energy" in disagreeing with a neighbor. At low excitement (cold) the whole crowd settles into one state — a giant wave of all-standing or all-sitting. Crank up the excitement (heat) and people flip at random, with no agreement across the stadium. The Ising lattice is exactly this crowd, and J is how strongly each person feels the pressure to conform.
Common mistakeDo not double-count the bonds. The sum Σ<i,j> is over distinct pairs, so each bond appears once. If you instead loop over every site and add up all four of its neighbor products, you count every bond twice and must divide the total by 2. Mixing these conventions silently halves or doubles your energy.

6.3Single-Spin Metropolis Updates & the Local Energy Change

We cannot enumerate all 2N configurations — for a modest 32 × 32 lattice that is 21024 states, more than the atoms in the universe. Instead we use the Metropolis algorithm from Week 5 to sample configurations in proportion to their Boltzmann weight e-E/T. The genius move for Ising is that we propose the tiniest possible change: flip one spin.

Here is why one spin is so cheap. Flipping spin i changes the energy only through the bonds touching i — its four neighbors. Every other bond is untouched. So the energy change is local. Let si be the current value and let the four neighbor spins sum to S = sup + sdown + sleft + sright. Flipping si to -si gives:

ΔE = 2 J si SS is the sum of the four neighbor spins (an even integer from -4 to +4). No global recomputation needed.

To see this, the four bonds touching site i contribute -J si S before the flip and -J(-si)S = +J si S after. The difference is ΔE = (+J siS) - (-J siS) = 2 J si S. With J = 1 the possible values of ΔE are just {-8, -4, 0, +4, +8}. We then apply the Metropolis rule:

accept the flip with probability P = min(1, e-ΔE / T)If ΔE ≤ 0 (lowers or keeps energy) always accept. If ΔE > 0, accept only if a uniform random number r < e^(-ΔE/T).

Sweeping once through all N spins (in order or at random) is called one Monte Carlo sweep, the natural unit of simulation time. Because ΔE takes only a handful of values, we can precompute the few possible Boltzmann factors e-ΔE/T once and just look them up — a classic speed trick.

6.4Magnetization: the Order Parameter

How do we tell, from the configuration, whether the lattice is a magnet or not? We measure the magnetization: the net alignment of all spins, usually reported per spin so it lives between -1 and +1.

M = (1/N) Σi siM = +1: all spins up. M = -1: all spins down. M = 0: equal numbers of up and down (no net order).

M is the model's order parameter — a single number that flags which phase the system is in. At low temperature the alignment tendency wins: almost all spins agree, so |M| is close to 1 and the lattice is a magnet. At high temperature thermal kicks scramble the spins, up and down balance out, and M hovers near 0 — no magnetism. Between them lies the critical temperature Tc, where the order parameter collapses; for the 2D square lattice the exact value (Onsager, 1944) is:

Tc = 2J / ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269 JOne of the few exactly solved interacting models — a gold-standard benchmark for any Monte Carlo code.

One subtlety at h = 0: because up and down are perfectly symmetric, a long run below Tc can tunnel between the all-up and all-down states, so the signed average <M> drifts toward 0 even in the ordered phase. The fix is to track the absolute magnetization <|M|>, which stays near 1 below Tc and falls toward 0 above it. That curve, plotted next week, is the fingerprint of the phase transition.

Common mistakeMeasuring M before the system has equilibrated. A simulation started from a random (or all-up) configuration needs a burn-in of many sweeps to reach typical configurations at temperature T. Averaging in measurements from those early, atypical sweeps biases your estimate. Always discard an equilibration window first, then average.
✦ Worked Example — one Metropolis update by hand

A spin si = +1 sits among neighbors {up=+1, down=-1, left=+1, right=+1}. We run the simulation at temperature T = 2.0 (with J = 1). Decide whether the proposed flip is accepted, given that the random number generator returns r = 0.37.

  1. Neighbor sum: S = (+1) + (-1) + (+1) + (+1) = +2.
  2. Energy change: ΔE = 2 J si S = 2(1)(+1)(+2) = +4. The flip raises energy (it would break a majority-aligned site), so it is not automatic.
  3. Acceptance probability: since ΔE > 0, P = e-ΔE/T = e-4/2.0 = e-2 ≈ 0.135.
  4. Compare to the random draw: we accept iff r < P. Here r = 0.37 and P = 0.135, so r > P → the flip is rejected. The spin stays +1.
  5. Sanity check: at this temperature an energy-raising flip of +4 is accepted only ~13.5% of the time, so most such proposals are refused — exactly the behavior that keeps the lattice ordered when T is low. Had T been very large, e-4/T → 1 and the flip would almost always pass, scrambling order.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate a 2D Ising magnet

This uses the same free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack you installed in Week 1 (see the install panel in Lecture 1 if you need it). Numba is optional — it just speeds up the loop with a @njit decorator if you have it. The code below runs fine on plain NumPy.

We build an L × L lattice with periodic boundaries (the modulo % wraps edges so every spin has four neighbors), run Metropolis sweeps, and measure the average absolute magnetization at one temperature.

# ising2d.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np

def delta_E(s, i, j, L, J=1.0):
    # neighbor sum with periodic boundaries (wrap with %)
    nb = s[(i+1)%L, j] + s[(i-1)%L, j] + s[i, (j+1)%L] + s[i, (j-1)%L]
    return 2.0 * J * s[i, j] * nb

def sweep(s, T, L):
    # one Monte Carlo sweep: attempt N=L*L single-spin flips
    for _ in range(L * L):
        i, j = np.random.randint(L), np.random.randint(L)
        dE = delta_E(s, i, j, L)
        if dE <= 0 or np.random.random() < np.exp(-dE / T):
            s[i, j] *= -1          # accept the flip
    return s

L, T = 32, 2.0
s = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(L, L))   # random start

for _ in range(1000):        # equilibrate: discard these sweeps
    sweep(s, T, L)

mags = []
for _ in range(2000):        # measure
    sweep(s, T, L)
    mags.append(abs(s.sum()) / (L * L))      # |M| per spin

print(f"T={T}  <|M|> = {np.mean(mags):.3f}")
# T=2.0 is below Tc=2.269, so expect <|M|> well above 0 (ordered)

Try it: loop T from 1.0 to 3.5 in steps of 0.1, record <|M|> at each, and plot with Matplotlib. You will see the magnetization plunge from near 1 toward 0 around T ≈ 2.27 — your first measured phase transition. (Next week we make that curve sharp.)

💬 Discussion

At h = 0 the Ising Hamiltonian treats "all up" and "all down" identically — the energy is unchanged if you flip every spin at once. Yet below Tc a real magnet (and a long-enough simulation) settles into one of these two states, not a 50/50 blur. How can a perfectly symmetric model produce an asymmetric outcome? And why do we measure <|M|> rather than <M> to detect the ordered phase? Argue from energy, probability, and the practical behavior of a finite-time simulation.

Show a model response

This is spontaneous symmetry breaking. The Hamiltonian is symmetric under flipping all spins, so the all-up and all-down states have identical energy and are equally probable in principle. But to get from one to the other, the system must pass through configurations with a domain wall slicing across the whole lattice — a high-energy barrier that grows with system size. Below Tc, thermal kicks almost never supply enough energy to cross it, so the system is effectively trapped in whichever well it fell into first. The symmetry is unbroken for the model as a whole but broken for any single realization — like a pencil balanced on its tip that must fall some direction.

This directly explains the <|M|> choice. In an infinite system the two states never mix, so <M> would be +M₀ or -M₀. But in a finite simulation run long enough, the system occasionally does tunnel between all-up and all-down, and those rare crossings drag the signed average <M> toward 0 even deep in the ordered phase — falsely suggesting no magnetism. Taking the absolute value per measurement removes the sign ambiguity: <|M|> stays near 1 below Tc and falls toward 0 above it, faithfully tracking the order whether or not the run tunneled.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) names spontaneous symmetry breaking and ties it to the global spin-flip symmetry of the h = 0 Hamiltonian; (2) explains the trapping via a domain-wall energy barrier that grows with size; (3) connects <|M|> vs <M> to finite-time tunneling between the two ordered states; (4) notes that in the true thermodynamic (infinite) limit the symmetry breaking becomes exact.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the Ising Hamiltonian E = -J Σ sisj with J > 0, two neighboring spins that agree contribute…
A. +J (high energy), because agreement is penalized
B. -J (low energy), because the model favors alignment
C. exactly 0, since equal spins cancel
D. a value that depends on temperature T
Show answer

B. Agreeing spins give sisj = +1, so the term is -J(+1) = -J, the low-energy state. A is the disagreeing case; C is wrong because the product is +1 not 0; D is wrong because the Hamiltonian itself has no T (temperature enters only through the Boltzmann acceptance).

Q2. A spin si = -1 has neighbor spins summing to S = -4 (all four neighbors are down). The energy change to flip it is ΔE = 2J si S =…
A. +8
B. 0
C. -4
D. +8J with the wrong sign convention
Show answer

A. ΔE = 2(1)(-1)(-4) = +8. Flipping a spin that already agrees with all four neighbors breaks four bonds, raising energy by 8J — the most expensive possible flip. B and C use the wrong arithmetic; D misstates the convention (with J = 1 the answer is simply +8).

Q3. Why does flipping a single spin let us compute ΔE from only four neighbors instead of recomputing the whole lattice energy?
A. Because spins far away are always parallel
B. Because the magnetization is conserved by a flip
C. Because periodic boundaries remove all distant bonds
D. Because the interaction is nearest-neighbor, so only bonds touching that spin change
Show answer

D. The Hamiltonian couples only nearest neighbors, so a single flip alters just the four bonds at that site; every other bond is unchanged. A is false (distant spins are not fixed); B is false (a flip changes M by 2/N); C is false (periodic boundaries add bonds, they do not remove distant interactions).

Q4. Under the Metropolis rule at temperature T, a proposed flip with ΔE = +4 is accepted with probability…
A. 1, always
B. 0, never
C. e-4/T
D. 4/T
Show answer

C. For ΔE > 0 the acceptance is min(1, e-ΔE/T) = e-4/T. A applies only when ΔE ≤ 0; B is wrong because energy-raising moves are still sometimes accepted (that is what lets the system explore); D is not a probability and can exceed 1.

Q5. Why do we usually report <|M|> (the average absolute magnetization) rather than <M> when detecting the ordered phase?
A. |M| is faster to compute than M
B. M can exceed 1, but |M| cannot
C. Magnetization is only defined as a positive quantity
D. At h = 0 a finite run can tunnel between all-up and all-down, dragging <M> toward 0 even when ordered
Show answer

D. The h = 0 model is symmetric, so a long finite-time run occasionally flips between the two ordered states; the signed average <M> then washes out to 0 and hides the order, while <|M|> stays near 1. A is irrelevant; B is false (both lie in [-1, 1]); C is false (M can be negative, e.g. all spins down gives M = -1).
