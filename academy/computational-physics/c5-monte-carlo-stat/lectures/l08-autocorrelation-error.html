Week 8 · Lecture 8

Autocorrelation & Error Estimation

A million Markov-chain samples can be worth only a few hundred independent ones — here's how to find out, and how to put an honest error bar on a Monte-Carlo result.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why successive Markov-chain Monte-Carlo samples are correlated, not independent.
  • Define the autocorrelation function and the integrated autocorrelation time τ.
  • Convert a chain of N samples into an effective number of independent samples N/(2τ) and correct the error bar accordingly.
  • Use blocking (binning) analysis to estimate the true statistical error without ever computing τ explicitly.
  • Report a Monte-Carlo result as value ± uncertainty with a defensible number of significant digits.

8.1Why Markov-Chain Samples Remember Their Past

By Week 8 you have built a Metropolis sampler: to draw the next configuration it perturbs the current one and accepts or rejects. That is exactly what makes a Markov chain efficient — and exactly what makes consecutive samples correlated. If step t sits in a high-magnetization region of an Ising lattice, step t+1 differs by at most one flipped spin, so it almost certainly has high magnetization too. The chain remembers where it was.

Independent sampling (like rolling fresh dice) is the assumption behind the textbook error formula. The standard error of the mean of N independent samples with variance σ² is:

SE = σ / √NThe naive error bar — valid ONLY if the N samples are statistically independent.

If you blindly apply this to a correlated chain, you are pretending you have far more information than you really do. The error bar comes out too small, sometimes by an order of magnitude, and you report a result that looks far more precise than it is. Quantifying that overlap is the whole job of this lecture.

AnalogyPolling a crowd to estimate average height: if you survey 1000 different people you learn a lot. But a Markov chain is more like following one person around all day and re-measuring — the readings barely change, so 1000 measurements carry the information of maybe 50 truly distinct people. Autocorrelation time tells you how long to wait before the next measurement counts as "a new person."

8.2The Autocorrelation Function and Time

Let xt be the observable (energy, magnetization, …) at chain step t, with mean <x>. The autocorrelation function at lag k measures how much a sample resembles one k steps later:

C(k) = <(xt - <x>)(xt+k - <x>)>Normalized form: rho(k) = C(k)/C(0), which starts at rho(0) = 1 and decays toward 0.

For a well-behaved chain rho(k) decays roughly exponentially, rho(k) ≈ e−k/τexp. The single number that controls your error bar is the integrated autocorrelation time:

τ = 1/2 + Σk=1∞ rho(k)Some authors define τ_int without the 1/2 and the leading factor; here 2τ is the spacing (in steps) between effectively independent samples.

The key result, derived from the variance of a sum of correlated terms, is that the variance of the sample mean is inflated by a factor of 2τ relative to the independent case:

Var(mean) = (σ² / N) · 2τ  ⇒   SE = σ √(2τ / N)When τ = 1/2 (no correlation) this collapses to the textbook σ/√N. Larger τ means a bigger honest error bar.

8.3Effective Sample Size

The cleanest way to think about all of this is to ask: how many independent samples is my chain actually worth? Comparing the inflated variance to the independent formula, σ²(2τ)/N = σ²/Neff, gives the effective sample size:

Neff = N / (2τ)A chain of N steps carries as much statistical information as N/(2tau) independent draws.

This number is brutally honest. Run 1,000,000 Metropolis sweeps near a critical point where τ = 500, and you have Neff = 1,000,000 / 1000 = 1000 effective samples — three orders of magnitude fewer than the raw count suggests. This is why critical slowing down (Week 7) is so painful: near a phase transition τ diverges, so each new sweep buys you almost no fresh information, and cluster algorithms exist precisely to crush τ back down.

Common mistakeReporting σ/√N straight from np.std(data)/np.sqrt(len(data)) on a Markov chain. That treats correlated samples as independent and understates the error by √(2τ) — a factor of ~30 when τ = 500. Reviewers spot this instantly: an error bar that shrinks suspiciously fast with chain length, or two "incompatible" runs that actually overlap once corrected. Always estimate τ or block the data first.

8.4Blocking: Honest Error Bars Without Computing τ

Estimating τ directly is fiddly — the tail of rho(k) is noisy and the sum has to be truncated carefully. Blocking (also called binning or the Flyvbjerg–Petersen method) sidesteps it. The idea: chop the chain into consecutive blocks, average within each block, and treat the block means as your new data points.

If a block is much longer than the correlation length, neighboring block means are nearly independent. So you repeatedly double the block size and recompute the naive standard error of the block means:

block size b -> 2b -> 4b -> ...   recompute SE each timeAs b grows past the correlation length, the estimated SE rises and then flattens onto a plateau.

The error estimate grows with block size at first (correlation is being removed) and then reaches a plateau. The plateau value is the true, correlation-corrected standard error. No τ required — though you can recover it afterward as τ = 1/2 · (SEplateau / SEnaive)².

✦ Worked Example — correcting an Ising energy error bar

A 2D Ising run gives N = 100,000 energy samples with sample standard deviation σ = 0.80 (per spin, in units of J). The integrated autocorrelation time is measured to be τ = 25. Report the mean energy <E> = −1.420 with an honest error.

  1. Naive (wrong) error: SEnaive = σ/√N = 0.80 / √100000 = 0.80 / 316 ≈ 0.0025.
  2. Effective sample size: Neff = N/(2τ) = 100000 / 50 = 2000 independent samples — not 100,000.
  3. Corrected error: SE = σ / √Neff = 0.80 / √2000 = 0.80 / 44.7 ≈ 0.0179. Equivalently SE = SEnaive · √(2τ) = 0.0025 · √50 ≈ 0.0179.
  4. The correction factor is √(2τ) = √50 ≈ 7.1. The honest error bar is about 7× larger than the naive one.
  5. Report it: round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures and match the value's last digit: <E> = −1.420 ± 0.018. Quoting "−1.4200 ± 0.0025" would be a false claim of precision.

🛠️ Hands-On: measure τ and block your data

Uses the same free Python + NumPy / Matplotlib stack you installed in Week 1 (see the install <details> in Lecture 1) — nothing new to install here. We synthesize a correlated chain with a known τ so you can check the methods against the truth, then apply both the direct estimator and blocking.

# autocorr_error.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# Build a correlated chain: AR(1) process x_t = a*x_{t-1} + noise.
# For AR(1), tau_int = 1/2 + a/(1-a).  Choose a so the true tau is known.
a = 0.92
N = 200_000
x = np.zeros(N)
for t in range(1, N):
    x[t] = a * x[t-1] + rng.normal(0, np.sqrt(1 - a*a))
tau_true = 0.5 + a / (1 - a)
print(f"true tau = {tau_true:.1f}")        # ~12.0

# --- Direct integrated autocorrelation time ---
xc = x - x.mean()
n = len(xc)
acf = np.correlate(xc, xc, mode="full")[n-1:] / (np.arange(n, 0, -1) * xc.var())
# sum rho(k) up to a self-consistent window (rho noisy in the tail)
tau = 0.5
for k in range(1, n):
    if k > 6 * tau:                         # automatic windowing
        break
    tau += acf[k]
print(f"measured tau = {tau:.1f}")

# --- Blocking (binning) analysis ---
def blocked_se(data):
    d = data.copy()
    while len(d) >= 4:
        se = d.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(len(d))
        print(f"  blocks={len(d):>7d}  SE={se:.5f}")
        d = (d[0:len(d)//2*2:2] + d[1:len(d)//2*2:2]) / 2   # pair-average
    return se

print("blocking (SE rises then plateaus):")
blocked_se(x)

naive = x.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(N)
corrected = naive * np.sqrt(2 * tau)
print(f"naive SE     = {naive:.5f}")
print(f"corrected SE = {corrected:.5f}  (factor {np.sqrt(2*tau):.1f})")

Watch the blocking SE climb from the naive value and flatten — the plateau should agree with corrected SE. Then raise a to 0.98 (τ ≈ 50) and confirm both the measured τ and the plateau grow, while the mean of x stays near 0. This is the diagnostic you run on every production chain.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why are consecutive samples from a Metropolis Markov chain correlated?
A. Each new sample is generated by perturbing the current configuration, so neighbors are similar
B. Floating-point round-off links the samples
C. The random number generator has too short a period
D. They are not correlated — MCMC samples are always independent
Show answer

A. A Markov step proposes a small change to the current state, so xt+1 resembles xt. B and C are unrelated artifacts (a decent RNG has an astronomically long period); D is exactly the false assumption this lecture warns against.

Q2. A chain has N = 1,000,000 samples and integrated autocorrelation time τ = 250. The effective number of independent samples is about…
A. 1,000,000
B. 2,000
C. 250
D. 500,000
Show answer

B. Neff = N/(2τ) = 1,000,000 / 500 = 2,000. The divisor is 2τ = 500, never τ alone (which would give 4,000, and C's 250 is just τ), never the raw count N (A), and not N/2 (D).

Q3. In a blocking (binning) analysis, the estimated standard error as you increase the block size typically…
A. decreases steadily toward zero
B. stays exactly constant
C. rises and then flattens onto a plateau that is the true error
D. oscillates with no discernible trend
Show answer

C. Small blocks are still correlated and underestimate the error; as blocks exceed the correlation length the SE estimate rises and plateaus at the honest value. A describes the naive trend you would wrongly get if more data always shrank the bar; B/D ignore the correlation structure.

Q4. If you ignore autocorrelation (τ = 30) and report σ/√N, your error bar is wrong by roughly what factor?
A. a factor of 2τ = 60 too large
B. a factor of √(2τ) ≈ 7.7 too small
C. exactly correct
D. a factor of τ = 30 too large
Show answer

B. The true SE = σ√(2τ/N) = (σ/√N)·√(2τ), so the naive bar is too small by √(2τ) = √60 ≈ 7.7. The variance is inflated by 2τ (A confuses variance with SE and the wrong direction); C is the mistake itself; D uses τ instead of 2τ and the wrong direction.

Q5. A run gives mean = −1.42 with corrected SE = 0.018. The best way to report it is…
A. −1.4200000 ± 0.0180000
B. −1.42 (no uncertainty needed)
C. −1.4 ± 0.5
D. −1.420 ± 0.018
Show answer

D. Round the uncertainty to one or two significant figures and match the value's last digit to it: −1.420 ± 0.018. A fakes precision the data cannot support; B omits the uncertainty entirely; C invents an error far larger than the computed 0.018.

✓ What you learned

  • Markov-chain samples are correlated because each is a small perturbation of the last, so σ/√N understates the error.
  • The autocorrelation function rho(k) and the integrated autocorrelation time τ = 1/2 + Σ rho(k) quantify that memory.
  • The true variance is inflated by 2τ, so SE = σ√(2τ/N) and the effective sample size is N/(2τ).
  • Blocking gives the corrected error as a plateau — no explicit τ needed — and recovers τ afterward.
  • A credible result is reported as value ± uncertainty with matched significant figures; your capstone observable needs blocking-corrected bars.
