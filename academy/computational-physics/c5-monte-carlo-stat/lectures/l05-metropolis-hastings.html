The Metropolis-Hastings Algorithm

A single accept-or-reject coin flip turns an aimless random walk into a machine that draws samples from any distribution you can write down — even one you cannot normalize.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the Metropolis-Hastings accept/reject rule and apply it to the Boltzmann distribution.
  • Choose a proposal distribution and explain how its width controls the acceptance rate.
  • Show that the accept/reject rule satisfies detailed balance, so the chain's stationary distribution is the target.
  • Understand why ratios of probabilities let us sample without ever knowing the partition function Z.
  • Diagnose burn-in / equilibration and decide when a chain has thermalized.

5.1The Problem: Sampling the Boltzmann Distribution

In statistical physics a system in contact with a heat bath at temperature T visits each microstate with energy E with the Boltzmann probability

P(state) = e−E/kT / Z,   Z = Σstates e−E/kTk is Boltzmann's constant; Z, the partition function, is the sum over every microstate.

We want averages — energy, magnetization, heat capacity — which are sums over states weighted by P. The trouble is Z. For an Ising spin lattice of N = 100 spins there are 2100 ≈ 1030 states; we can no more enumerate them than count atoms in a planet. Worse, the vast majority of states have negligible weight, so even random sampling wastes almost all its effort on configurations that never matter.

The escape is importance sampling by a Markov chain: instead of drawing independent states, walk from one state to a neighbor, accepting or rejecting each step so that — in the long run — we visit states exactly in proportion to their Boltzmann weight. Then a simple average over the states the chain visits estimates the thermal average. The genius of the Metropolis rule is that it needs only the ratio of two probabilities, and in that ratio the unknown Z cancels.

AnalogyImagine a hiker wandering a foggy mountain range at night, where altitude = probability. She can't see the whole range (can't compute Z), but at each step she can compare her current height to a nearby spot. If she follows a rule of "always step up, and step down only sometimes," she ends up spending most of her time on the peaks and proportionally little in the valleys — a histogram of where she stood traces the landscape itself, without her ever having surveyed the whole mountain.

5.2The Accept/Reject Rule and Proposals

Metropolis-Hastings builds the chain in two moves. First a proposal: from the current state x, draw a candidate x' from a proposal distribution g(x' | x) — for a spin system, "flip a random spin"; for a continuous variable, "add a small Gaussian nudge." Second an accept/reject step that decides whether to move to x' or stay at x.

For a symmetric proposal (g(x'|x) = g(x|x'), true of a spin flip or a Gaussian nudge) the acceptance probability is the original 1953 Metropolis rule:

A(x -> x') = min(1, P(x')/P(x)) = min(1, e−ΔE/kT),   ΔE = E(x') − E(x)If the move lowers the energy (ΔE < 0) accept it always; if it raises the energy, accept with probability e^(−ΔE/kT).

Notice the ratio P(x')/P(x) = e−E(x')/kT/e−E(x)/kT = e−ΔE/kT: Z cancels exactly. We never need the normalization. When the proposal is asymmetric, Hastings' generalization multiplies by the ratio of proposal densities to keep things balanced:

A(x -> x') = min(1, [P(x') g(x | x')] / [P(x) g(x' | x)])The Hastings factor g(x|x')/g(x'|x) corrects for a proposal that prefers one direction.

The proposal width is the key tuning knob. Tiny steps are almost always accepted but crawl through state space; huge steps land in low-probability regions and are almost always rejected. Either extreme explores slowly. A common target acceptance rate is roughly 20–50% for continuous problems — large enough to move, small enough to be accepted often.

Common mistakeRejecting a move does not mean "skip this iteration." When x' is rejected, the chain stays at x and records x again as the next sample. Forgetting to re-count the current state systematically over-weights accepted moves and biases every average. The number of samples equals the number of steps, not the number of accepted steps.

5.3Why It Works: Detailed Balance

Recall from Week 4 that a Markov chain converges to a unique stationary distribution π if π satisfies detailed balance: for every pair of states the probability flux from x to x' equals the flux back.

P(x) · T(x -> x') = P(x') · T(x' -> x)T is the full transition probability = propose times accept: T(x -> x') = g(x'|x) A(x -> x').

Let us verify the Metropolis rule produces detailed balance with π = the Boltzmann distribution P. Take a symmetric proposal, g(x'|x) = g(x|x'), and suppose E(x') > E(x) so ΔE > 0. Then A(x -> x') = e−ΔE/kT while the reverse move is downhill, so A(x' -> x) = 1. Substitute:

P(x) · g · e−ΔE/kT  =?=  P(x') · g · 1Left side: P(x)·e^(−(E(x')−E(x))/kT) = e^(−E(x)/kT)·e^(−(E(x')−E(x))/kT)/Z = e^(−E(x')/kT)/Z = P(x'). Equality holds.

The two sides are identical, so detailed balance holds for every pair (the symmetric case where E(x') < E(x) follows by swapping labels). Because the chain is also ergodic — any state reachable from any other in finite steps — the stationary distribution is unique, and it is exactly the Boltzmann distribution we wanted. That is the whole proof of correctness, and it never mentioned Z.

AnalogyDetailed balance is like foot traffic between two cafes that has reached equilibrium: even though individuals keep moving back and forth, the number walking A→B each minute exactly equals the number walking B→A, so each cafe's headcount stays put. The accept/reject rule is engineered to make that flux balance hold with headcounts proportional to the Boltzmann weight.

5.4Burn-In and Knowing You've Thermalized

Detailed balance guarantees the chain converges to Boltzmann, but only in the limit. You always start from some arbitrary initial state — all spins up, a random configuration — that is generally not typical of the target distribution. The early samples are contaminated by this starting bias. The fix is burn-in (also called equilibration): run the chain for some steps and discard them before you begin collecting data.

How do you know thermalization is done? The cleanest diagnostic is to plot a slow observable — energy or magnetization — versus step number (the trace). At first it drifts systematically from its starting value toward an equilibrium band; once it merely fluctuates around a stable mean with no trend, the chain has equilibrated.

<O> ≈ (1 / (M − Mburn)) Σt=Mburn+1M O(xt)Average only over steps after burn-in M_burn; the discarded steps would bias the estimate toward the start state.

Two practical cautions. First, samples from a Markov chain are correlated — consecutive states differ by one move — so the effective number of independent samples is far smaller than M. The autocorrelation time τ sets how many steps between roughly independent samples; near a phase transition τ can explode (critical slowing down, Week 6). Second, a flat trace is necessary but not sufficient: running multiple chains from different starts and checking they overlap is a stronger test.

✦ Worked Example — one uphill move on a spin

A single spin sits in a field; flipping it would raise the energy by ΔE = 2.0 (in units where the coupling is 1). The temperature is kT = 1.5. The proposal "flip this spin" is symmetric. Should we accept, and with what probability?

  1. Compute the energy change: ΔE = E(flipped) − E(current) = +2.0. Because ΔE > 0 the move is uphill, so it is not automatically accepted.
  2. Acceptance probability: A = min(1, e−ΔE/kT) = min(1, e−2.0/1.5) = min(1, e−1.333) = min(1, 0.2636) = 0.264.
  3. Draw a uniform random number r in [0, 1). Suppose r = 0.18. Since 0.18 < 0.264, accept the flip; the new state becomes the current state.
  4. Counter-case: had r = 0.55, then 0.55 > 0.264, so we reject — the spin stays unflipped, and we record the unflipped state again as the next sample.
  5. Temperature check: raise kT to 6.0 and A = e−2/6 = e−0.333 = 0.717 — uphill moves get accepted far more often, so the chain explores wildly (high T = disorder). Lower kT to 0.3 and A = e−6.67 = 0.00127 — almost no uphill moves, so the system freezes into low-energy states (low T = order). The single rule reproduces the entire physics of thermal fluctuations.

🛠️ Hands-On: sample a 1-D Boltzmann well

The free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack (from the Week 1/2 setup — the install details block in the early lectures) is all you need; no new tools here. We'll sample a continuous "energy" E(x) = ½x² (a harmonic well) at kT = 1, which should give a Gaussian, and watch burn-in disappear.

# metropolis_well.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
kT = 1.0
def E(x): return 0.5 * x**2            # harmonic energy; Boltzmann -> Gaussian

def metropolis(n_steps, step=2.0, x0=8.0):
    x = x0                              # start FAR from the typical region
    chain = np.empty(n_steps)
    accepts = 0
    for t in range(n_steps):
        xp = x + rng.normal(0, step)    # symmetric Gaussian proposal
        dE = E(xp) - E(x)
        if dE < 0 or rng.random() < np.exp(-dE / kT):
            x = xp                      # accept
            accepts += 1
        chain[t] = x                    # record EVERY step (accept or not)
    return chain, accepts / n_steps

chain, acc = metropolis(20000)
print(f"acceptance rate: {acc:.2f}")    # ~0.5 with step=2.0

burn = 2000
samples = chain[burn:]                   # discard burn-in
print(f"mean={samples.mean():.3f}  var={samples.var():.3f}")  # ~0, ~1 (Gaussian)

plt.hist(samples, bins=60, density=True)
xs = np.linspace(-4, 4, 200)
plt.plot(xs, np.exp(-0.5*xs**2)/np.sqrt(2*np.pi))  # exact Boltzmann
plt.show()

Experiment: plot chain[:500] to see the burn-in drop from x=8 toward 0. Then sweep step over 0.1, 2, 20 and print the acceptance rate — you'll find tiny steps give ~99% acceptance but barely move, huge steps give <5% and waste effort, and step≈2 lands in the healthy 20–50% band that mixes fastest.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A proposed move would lower the energy: ΔE = −0.8 at kT = 1. The Metropolis acceptance probability is…
A. 1 — downhill moves are always accepted
B. e−0.8 ≈ 0.45
C. 0.5
D. 0 — the move is rejected
Show answer

A. A = min(1, e−ΔE/kT) = min(1, e+0.8) = min(1, 2.23) = 1. Any move that lowers energy is accepted with certainty. B uses the wrong sign; C and D ignore the min(1, ·) form entirely.

Q2. Why can Metropolis-Hastings sample the Boltzmann distribution without computing the partition function Z?
A. Z is approximated by Monte Carlo first
B. The rule uses only the ratio P(x')/P(x), in which Z cancels
C. Z equals 1 for spin systems
D. Z is negligible at high temperature
Show answer

B. P(x')/P(x) = e−E(x')/kT/e−E(x)/kT = e−ΔE/kT; the shared 1/Z cancels. A is false (we never compute Z at all); C and D are simply wrong — Z is a huge nontrivial sum.

Q3. When a proposed move is rejected, the correct bookkeeping is to…
A. skip the iteration and propose again without recording anything
B. record the proposed state anyway
C. record the current (unchanged) state again as the next sample
D. restart the chain from a new initial state
Show answer

C. A rejected step still produces a sample — the chain stays put and the current state is counted again. A would over-weight accepted moves and bias averages; B records a state that was never visited; D throws away equilibration.

Q4. The property that guarantees the Metropolis chain's stationary distribution is the Boltzmann distribution is…
A. the central limit theorem
B. the law of large numbers
C. energy conservation
D. detailed balance: P(x)T(x→x') = P(x')T(x'→x)
Show answer

D. Detailed balance (plus ergodicity) forces the unique stationary distribution to equal the target P. The CLT (A) and LLN (B) govern the error of the resulting averages, not which distribution is sampled; C is irrelevant — energy is not conserved in this stochastic dynamics.

Q5. You start a simulation with all spins aligned and plot energy versus step: it falls steeply for a while, then fluctuates around a stable mean. The correct conclusion is…
A. the simulation is broken — energy should be constant
B. the proposal width is far too large
C. the chain has reached equilibrium; discard the initial drop as burn-in
D. you must lower the temperature to continue
Show answer

C. The steep drop is the chain leaving its atypical start (burn-in); the stable fluctuating band is the equilibrium distribution. Discard the burn-in and average over the rest. A misreads a healthy trace; B is unsupported (acceptance rate would tell you that); D changes the physics you're trying to measure.

✓ What you learned

  • The Boltzmann distribution P ∝ e−E/kT is what we want to sample, but the partition function Z is uncomputable for large systems.
  • The Metropolis-Hastings rule accepts a move with probability min(1, e−ΔE/kT) — using only ratios, so Z cancels.
  • The proposal width tunes the acceptance rate; aim for the ~20–50% band, and always re-count the current state on rejection.
  • Detailed balance plus ergodicity proves the stationary distribution is exactly Boltzmann.
  • Burn-in removes the start-state bias; a flat trace (best confirmed across multiple chains) signals thermalization.
