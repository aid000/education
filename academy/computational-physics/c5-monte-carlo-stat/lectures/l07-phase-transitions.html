Week 7 · Lecture 7

Phase Transitions & Critical Phenomena

Cool a magnet through one special temperature and a billion spins suddenly agree — out of pure noise, order is born, and our simulation feels every fluctuation along the way.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the ferromagnetic phase transition and define the critical temperature Tc.
  • Use the magnetization per spin as an order parameter that distinguishes the ordered and disordered phases.
  • Explain why the magnetic susceptibility and specific heat peak at Tc and connect those peaks to fluctuations.
  • Recognize critical slowing down and finite-size effects as the two great obstacles to simulating a transition.
  • Run an Ising Monte-Carlo temperature sweep in Python and locate Tc from the susceptibility peak.

7.1The Ferromagnetic Transition: Order from Noise

Take a slab of iron and heat it red-hot: it is not magnetic. Let it cool below about 1043 K (its Curie temperature) and it spontaneously becomes a magnet, with a north and south pole, even though nothing told it which way to point. That abrupt, qualitative change in the bulk behavior of matter as you tune a single knob — temperature — is a phase transition. The ferromagnet is the cleanest example we can simulate, and last week's 2D Ising model is exactly the toy that captures it.

Recall the model: a lattice of spins si = ±1, with neighbors that prefer to align (energy −J for each aligned pair). Two opposing tendencies fight at every temperature:

  • Energy wants order — all spins aligned costs the least energy.
  • Entropy wants disorder — there are vastly more random configurations than aligned ones.

The equilibrium state minimizes the free energy F = E − TS, so which tendency wins depends on temperature. The Boltzmann weight is what our Metropolis sampler already encodes:

P(config) ∝ e−E/(kBT)    F = E − T·SAt low T the energy term dominates (order wins); at high T entropy dominates (disorder wins). They trade places at Tc.

At high temperature, thermal kicks scramble the spins faster than they can align — the lattice is a fizzing mess of up and down, with no net magnetization. At low temperature, the alignment energy wins and a macroscopic fraction of spins lock together. Somewhere in between sits a single sharp boundary: the critical temperature Tc.

AnalogyThink of a crowded stadium. When it is loud and chaotic (high T), people clap at random — zero rhythm. As the noise dies down (lower T), a faint beat catches, then suddenly the whole crowd is clapping in sync. No one organized it; below a tipping point, local imitation between neighbors snowballs into stadium-wide order. Tc is that tipping point, and the synchronized clapping is the spontaneous magnetization.

7.2The Order Parameter and Spontaneous Magnetization

How do we measure "order" with one number? We use the order parameter — for a magnet, the magnetization per spin:

m = (1/N) Σi si    m ∈ [−1, +1]N = total number of spins. m = ±1 means perfect alignment; m = 0 means equal up and down (disorder).

An order parameter is, by definition, zero in the disordered phase and nonzero in the ordered phase. Above Tc, the time-averaged |m| is essentially zero. Below Tc, |m| grows toward 1 as T falls — the spins develop a permanent net direction even with no external field applied. That is spontaneous magnetization: order appears with nothing to break the tie except a chance fluctuation.

For the 2D Ising model on a square lattice the transition is continuous (second-order): m rises smoothly from zero rather than jumping. Lars Onsager solved this model exactly in 1944, and near Tc the magnetization follows a power law:

m(T) ∝ (Tc − T)β  for T < Tc,   with β = 1/8The critical exponent β = 0.125 governs how steeply order emerges below Tc. Above Tc, m = 0.

7.3Why Susceptibility and Specific Heat Peak at Tc

The order parameter tells you which phase you are in. To find Tc precisely, the sharper signals come from fluctuations. Near a continuous transition the system can't decide between order and disorder, so it wavers wildly — and those wild swings make two response functions blow up.

Statistical mechanics gives a beautiful result: a response function equals the variance of the quantity it responds to (a fluctuation–dissipation relation). The magnetic susceptibility measures how strongly m responds to a tiny field, and it equals the variance of the magnetization:

χ = (N / kBT) ( ⟨m2⟩ − ⟨m⟩2 )Susceptibility = scaled variance of m. Big swings in magnetization -> big χ.

Likewise the specific heat equals the variance of the energy:

C = (1 / kBT2) ( ⟨E2⟩ − ⟨E⟩2 )Specific heat = scaled variance of E. Big energy swings -> big C.

Away from Tc the system is comfortably in one phase, fluctuations are small, and both χ and C are modest. At Tc the system teeters on the edge between phases, fluctuations of all sizes appear at once, and both quantities spike to a sharp peak (diverging in the infinite system). This gives us our most practical recipe: scan temperature, watch for the peak in χ or C, and read off Tc at the peak. The exact 2D answer is famous:

kBTc / J = 2 / ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269Onsager's exact critical temperature for the 2D square-lattice Ising model (J = coupling, kB = Boltzmann constant).
Common mistakeDo not estimate Tc from where the magnetization "looks like it hits zero." On a finite lattice |m| never truly reaches zero (random fluctuations keep it positive), so eyeballing the m(T) curve gives a biased, lattice-size-dependent answer. The peak of χ or C is the sharp, well-defined locator — use it.

7.4Critical Slowing Down and Finite-Size Effects

The same physics that makes Tc interesting makes it brutally hard to simulate. Two problems dominate.

Critical slowing down

Near Tc, correlated patches of aligned spins grow to a size called the correlation length ξ, which diverges at the transition. A single-spin-flip Metropolis update only nudges one spin at a time, so it takes enormous numbers of sweeps to rearrange a patch of size ξ. The autocorrelation time τ (how many sweeps until samples become independent — next week's topic) explodes:

τ ∝ ξz,   with z ≈ 2.17 for 2D Ising + MetropolisThe dynamic exponent z. As ξ grows near Tc, consecutive samples stay correlated for longer and longer -> your error bars stop shrinking.

This is critical slowing down: exactly where you most want good statistics, your Monte-Carlo samples become most correlated and least informative. (Cluster algorithms like Wolff and Swendsen–Wang, which flip whole correlated patches at once, were invented precisely to beat this — a topic for a later week.)

Finite-size effects

A real transition is sharp only in an infinite system. On an L×L lattice the correlation length cannot exceed L, so the susceptibility peak is rounded and shifted, not a true divergence. The apparent critical temperature drifts with system size as:

Tc(L) − Tc(∞) ∝ L−1/ν,   ν = 1 (2D Ising)Finite-size scaling: measure Tc(L) for several L and extrapolate L -> ∞ to recover the true critical temperature.
AnalogyCritical slowing down is like stirring honey that thickens as you approach a target temperature — each stir moves less, so you must stir far longer to mix it. Finite-size rounding is like measuring a knife's edge with a ruler whose smallest mark is the box width L: the true edge is infinitely sharp, but your coarse ruler always reports it as a smooth bump. Use several rulers (several L) and extrapolate.
✦ Worked Example — locating Tc from a susceptibility sweep

You simulate a 16×16 Ising lattice (N = 256 spins) at three temperatures (in units where kB = J = 1) and record the magnetization per spin across many equilibrium samples. Find the susceptibility at each T and estimate Tc.

  1. The data. At T = 2.0: ⟨m⟩ = 0.78, ⟨m2⟩ = 0.62. At T = 2.27: ⟨m⟩ = 0.45, ⟨m2⟩ = 0.34. At T = 2.6: ⟨m⟩ = 0.10, ⟨m2⟩ = 0.022.
  2. Variance of m at each T. T=2.0: 0.62 − 0.78² = 0.62 − 0.6084 = 0.0116. T=2.27: 0.34 − 0.45² = 0.34 − 0.2025 = 0.1375. T=2.6: 0.022 − 0.10² = 0.022 − 0.010 = 0.012.
  3. Susceptibility χ = (N/T)·Var(m). T=2.0: (256/2.0)·0.0116 = 128·0.0116 ≈ 1.48. T=2.27: (256/2.27)·0.1375 = 112.8·0.1375 ≈ 15.5. T=2.6: (256/2.6)·0.012 = 98.5·0.012 ≈ 1.18.
  4. Find the peak. χ is small (≈1.5) in the ordered phase, small (≈1.2) in the disordered phase, and roughly ten times larger at T = 2.27. The susceptibility peaks at T ≈ 2.27.
  5. Estimate Tc. The peak sits at Tc(L=16) ≈ 2.27, gratifyingly close to Onsager's exact 2.269 — though a true measurement would run more temperatures, fit the peak, and extrapolate over several L to remove the finite-size shift.

🛠️ Hands-On: sweep the temperature and find the peak

This uses the same free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (install details in Lecture 1) and the Metropolis Ising sweep from Week 6. No new tools needed — Numba stays optional for speed on big lattices.

We run the Ising lattice at a ladder of temperatures, discard a warm-up, then accumulate ⟨|m|⟩ and the susceptibility. The χ curve should spike near T ≈ 2.27.

# phase_transition.py — Lecture 7 (depends on Week 6 ising_step)
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

def metropolis_sweep(s, beta):
    L = s.shape[0]
    for _ in range(L * L):                       # one sweep = N attempts
        i, j = np.random.randint(0, L, size=2)
        nb = (s[(i+1)%L, j] + s[(i-1)%L, j] +
              s[i, (j+1)%L] + s[i, (j-1)%L])
        dE = 2 * s[i, j] * nb                     # J = 1
        if dE <= 0 or np.random.rand() < np.exp(-beta * dE):
            s[i, j] = -s[i, j]
    return s

L, N = 16, 16*16
temps = np.linspace(1.6, 3.2, 17)
chi, mag = [], []
for T in temps:
    beta = 1.0 / T
    s = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(L, L))
    for _ in range(1000):                         # equilibrate (warm-up)
        metropolis_sweep(s, beta)
    ms = []
    for _ in range(2000):                         # measurement sweeps
        metropolis_sweep(s, beta)
        ms.append(abs(s.sum()) / N)               # |m| per spin
    ms = np.array(ms)
    mag.append(ms.mean())
    chi.append(N / T * ms.var())                  # chi = (N/T) Var(m)

Tc_est = temps[int(np.argmax(chi))]
print(f"estimated Tc ~ {Tc_est:.2f}  (Onsager exact: 2.269)")
plt.plot(temps, chi, 'o-'); plt.axvline(2.269, ls='--')
plt.xlabel('T'); plt.ylabel('susceptibility'); plt.show()

Now rerun with L = 8, 16, 32. Watch the peak grow taller and drift toward 2.269 as L increases — you are seeing finite-size scaling and critical slowing down with your own eyes (the L = 32 run takes noticeably longer near Tc).

🔗 Where this is heading

Critical slowing down sets up Week 8, where we make the autocorrelation time τ quantitative and learn to attach honest error bars to correlated Monte-Carlo data (binning and the integrated autocorrelation time). The diverging fluctuations here are also the gateway to the renormalization group and universality — the deep reason a magnet, a boiling liquid, and a polymer can share the same critical exponents. Beyond this course, the order-parameter and fluctuation language reappears across the Quantum track (quantum phase transitions at T = 0), condensed-matter simulation, and even machine learning, where the loss landscape near a transition shows the same critical slowing down you just measured.

Q1. For the ferromagnetic Ising model, the magnetization per spin m serves as the order parameter because it is…
A. always equal to 1 regardless of temperature
B. zero in the disordered (high-T) phase and nonzero in the ordered (low-T) phase
C. the total energy of the lattice
D. independent of the spin configuration
Show answer

B. An order parameter vanishes in the disordered phase and turns on in the ordered phase, exactly what |m| does. A is wrong (|m| drops to 0 above Tc); C confuses it with energy; D is false since m is literally the average of the spins.

Q2. Why do the magnetic susceptibility χ and specific heat C peak sharply at Tc?
A. because the simulation runs out of memory there
B. because the spins stop interacting at Tc
C. because fluctuations (the variances of m and E) become huge as the system teeters between phases
D. because the temperature is highest there
Show answer

C. χ and C equal the scaled variances of m and E; near Tc the system can't choose a phase, so those fluctuations explode. A is unrelated to physics; B is false (the coupling J is fixed); D is wrong — Tc is a middling temperature, not the maximum.

Q3. "Critical slowing down" near Tc means that…
A. consecutive Monte-Carlo samples become highly correlated, so independent data takes far more sweeps
B. the computer's clock speed drops at high temperature
C. the magnetization goes to zero
D. the Metropolis acceptance rate falls to exactly zero
Show answer

A. As ξ (and thus the autocorrelation time τ ∝ ξz) grows near Tc, single-spin updates need many more sweeps to decorrelate. B is nonsense (hardware-unrelated); C describes the high-T phase, not slowing down; D is false — acceptance stays finite.

Q4. On a finite L×L lattice, the best way to locate Tc is to…
A. read off where |m| first appears to hit exactly zero
B. use the temperature of the highest single energy ever sampled
C. find the peak of χ (or C), and extrapolate over several L
D. pick the lowest temperature simulated
Show answer

C. The susceptibility/specific-heat peak is the sharp, well-defined locator, and finite-size scaling over several L removes the size-dependent shift. A is biased because |m| never truly reaches zero on a finite lattice; B and D are arbitrary and unphysical.

Q5. For the 2D square-lattice Ising model (kB = J = 1), the exact critical temperature is approximately…
A. 0.5
B. 1.0
C. 5.4
D. 2.269
Show answer

D. Onsager's exact result is kBTc/J = 2/ln(1+√2) ≈ 2.269. A and B are far too low (still deep in the ordered phase); C is far too high (deep in the disordered phase).

✓ What you learned

  • A ferromagnetic phase transition is a battle between energy (order) and entropy (disorder) decided at a single critical temperature Tc.
  • The order parameter m = (1/N)Σsi is zero above Tc and grows as (Tc − T)1/8 below it — spontaneous magnetization.
  • Susceptibility χ and specific heat C equal scaled variances of m and E, so they peak at Tc — your sharpest locator.
  • Critical slowing down (τ ∝ ξz) and finite-size effects are the two great obstacles, fixed by cluster algorithms and finite-size scaling.
  • You located Tc ≈ 2.269 from an Ising susceptibility sweep in NumPy, matching Onsager's exact answer.
