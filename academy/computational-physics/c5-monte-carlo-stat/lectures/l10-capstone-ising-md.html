10.1The Capstone: From Algorithm to a Physical Number

For nine weeks you built tools one at a time: pseudo-random numbers, Monte-Carlo integration with importance sampling, random walks and diffusion, Markov chains and detailed balance, the Metropolis-Hastings rule, the Ising model, autocorrelation and error estimation, and finally molecular dynamics with the Lennard-Jones potential. The capstone — due this week — is where those pieces stop being exercises and become a small piece of science. You pick one of two tracks and carry it all the way from code to a reported, error-barred result.

Track A — 2D Ising. Sweep the temperature across the phase transition and measure how the magnetization, susceptibility, and specific heat behave. The deliverable is a located critical temperature Tc with an uncertainty, compared to the exact Onsager value.

Track B — Lennard-Jones MD. Integrate Newton's equations for a box of atoms interacting through the Lennard-Jones potential, equilibrate to a target temperature, and measure the radial distribution function g(r) — the fingerprint of solid, liquid, or gas structure.

Both tracks share the same intellectual spine, which is the real lesson of the specialization: a raw simulation produces a stream of correlated samples; turning that stream into a number with an honest ± is the work. A result without an error bar is not a measurement — it is a rumor.

AnalogyThink of the capstone like an experimental lab report. The simulation is your apparatus; equilibration is letting the apparatus warm up and settle; the production run is data-taking; and blocking analysis is the error analysis you would do on any real measurement. Nobody trusts a lab value quoted to six digits with no uncertainty — and nobody should trust a simulation number quoted that way either.

10.2Track A: Locating Tc in the 2D Ising Model

Recall the 2D Ising model on an L x L lattice of spins si = ±1 with nearest-neighbor coupling. In units where the coupling J = 1 and Boltzmann's constant kB = 1, the energy and magnetization per configuration are:

E = -Σ<ij> si sj    M = Σi siThe sum <ij> runs over nearest-neighbor bonds (each counted once) on a periodic lattice.

You already wrote a Metropolis updater in Week 6. The capstone wraps it in a temperature sweep: for each T, equilibrate, then average. The order parameter is the mean absolute magnetization per spin, and the two response functions come straight from fluctuations of M and E — the fluctuation-dissipation relations:

χ = (1/N) (<M2> - <|M|>2) / T    C = (1/N) (<E2> - <E>2) / T2Susceptibility chi and specific heat C, with N = L*L spins. Both are computed from variances, not derivatives.

As T rises through the critical point, the magnetization collapses from near 1 to near 0, while χ and C develop sharp peaks. On a finite lattice those peaks are rounded and shifted, but their location estimates Tc. The exact answer, found by Onsager, is the benchmark you check against:

Tc = 2 / ln(1 + √2) ≈ 2.269The exact critical temperature of the 2D Ising model (J = 1, k_B = 1). Your peak should land near here and approach it as L grows.
Common mistakeReporting the bare peak temperature on one small lattice as "Tc." On a finite L x L lattice the peak is shifted by a finite-size correction that scales like 1/L. Run a couple of sizes (e.g. L = 16, 24, 32), note that the peak drifts toward 2.27 as L grows, and say so. Quoting 2.31 from L = 16 as "the" critical temperature without that caveat is the single most common capstone error.

10.3Track B: Structure from Lennard-Jones MD

The Lennard-Jones potential from Week 9 models a simple atomic fluid. In reduced units (σ = ε = 1) the pair energy and the force you integrate are:

U(r) = 4[(1/r)12 - (1/r)6]    F(r) = -dU/drSteep r^-12 repulsion (overlap), gentle r^-6 attraction (dispersion). Energy minimum at r ~ 1.122.

You integrate Newton's equations with the velocity-Verlet scheme, rescale velocities to hit a target temperature during equilibration, then take production data. The headline observable is the radial distribution function g(r): the probability of finding another atom a distance r away, relative to an ideal gas of the same density.

g(r) = (V / N2) < Σi Σj≠i δ(r - rij) > / (4π r2)In practice: histogram all pair distances, then normalize by the ideal-gas shell count 4*pi*r^2*dr*(N/V).

The shape of g(r) is the structural fingerprint of the phase. A dilute gas gives g(r) ≈ 1 with a single soft bump. A liquid gives a tall first peak near r ≈ 1.1 (the contact shell), a second weaker peak, and decay to 1 — short-range order, no long-range order. A solid shows many sharp split peaks at the crystal-lattice distances.

10.4The Shared Spine: Error Bars from Blocking

Whichever track you pick, the samples your simulation produces are correlated in time: successive Metropolis sweeps or MD steps are not independent draws. The naive standard error of the mean, σ/√N, badly underestimates the true uncertainty because the effective number of independent samples is N/(2τint), where τint is the integrated autocorrelation time from Week 7.

The blocking method (Flyvbjerg-Petersen) fixes this without ever computing τ directly. Group the time series into non-overlapping blocks of size b, average within each block, then estimate the error from the block averages — which become independent once b exceeds the correlation time:

SE(b) = √( Var(block means) / (nb - 1) )    nb = N / bRepeatedly double b. The estimate SE(b) RISES then reaches a plateau; the plateau value is the true standard error.

The signature you look for is a plateau: as you double the block size, SE(b) climbs while blocks are still correlated, then flattens once blocks are effectively independent. The plateau height is your honest error bar. If SE(b) never flattens, your run is too short — collect more data.

AnalogyBlocking is like polling. Asking the same person ten times in a row gives ten correlated answers that look like a tight, confident result — but it is really one opinion. Grouping (blocking) until each group is an independent "voter" reveals the true spread. The plateau is the point where each block has become a genuine independent voter.
Common mistakeQuoting σ/√N on the raw correlated samples. Near a phase transition autocorrelation time explodes (critical slowing down), so the raw formula can underestimate the true error by an order of magnitude — exactly where you most need a trustworthy bar. Always block, and always check that the plateau actually exists before trusting it.
✦ Worked Example — an honest error bar by blocking

An Ising run at T near Tc gives N = 8000 measurements of |M|/N with sample mean 0.452 and sample standard deviation s = 0.180. The integrated autocorrelation time is τint = 25 sweeps. What error bar should you report?

  1. Naive standard error (wrong): s/√N = 0.180/√8000 = 0.180/89.4 ≈ 0.0020. This pretends all 8000 samples are independent.
  2. Effective sample count: Neff = N / (2τint) = 8000 / (2·25) = 8000/50 = 160 independent samples — far fewer than 8000.
  3. Corrected standard error: s/√Neff = 0.180/√160 = 0.180/12.65 ≈ 0.0142 — about 7x larger than the naive value (factor √(2τ) = √50 ≈ 7.07).
  4. Blocking check: a blocking analysis on the same series should plateau near 0.014, confirming the correction. If you had reported 0.452 ± 0.002, your stated precision would be 7x too optimistic.
  5. Report: <|M|/N> = 0.452 ± 0.014 at this temperature. That bar is what makes the susceptibility peak — and hence Tc — defensible.

🛠️ Hands-On: the capstone driver and blocking

Everything runs on the free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack you set up in Week 1 (with optional Numba for speed, introduced in Week 6 to accelerate the Ising sweeps). No new installs are needed for the capstone — reuse your compphys-env environment.

Below is the spine common to both tracks: a temperature (or production) loop that collects a time series, plus the blocking routine that turns it into an error bar. Drop in your Week-6 Ising sweep or Week-9 Verlet/g(r) code where marked.

# capstone_blocking.py — Lecture 10 (works for both tracks)
import numpy as np

def block_error(x):
    # Flyvbjerg-Petersen blocking: returns SE estimates vs block size
    x = np.asarray(x, dtype=float)
    ses = []
    while len(x) >= 4:
        n = len(x)
        se = np.std(x, ddof=1) / np.sqrt(n)   # SE of current series
        ses.append(se)
        x = (x[0:n-n%2:2] + x[1:n-n%2:2]) / 2 # pair-average -> halve length
    return np.array(ses)                       # look for the PLATEAU (the max-ish tail)

# --- TRACK A: Ising temperature sweep (uses your Week-6 metropolis_sweep) ---
def run_temperature(T, L=24, n_equil=2000, n_prod=8000):
    spins = np.random.choice([-1, 1], size=(L, L))
    for _ in range(n_equil):
        metropolis_sweep(spins, T)             # discard: equilibration
    mags = np.empty(n_prod)
    for k in range(n_prod):
        metropolis_sweep(spins, T)
        mags[k] = abs(spins.sum()) / spins.size
    ses = block_error(mags)
    return mags.mean(), ses.max()              # plateau ~ tail/max of SE curve

temps = np.linspace(1.6, 3.2, 17)
for T in temps:
    m, err = run_temperature(T)
    print(f"T={T:.2f}  <|m|>={m:.3f} +/- {err:.3f}")
# susceptibility/specific heat: collect M, M**2, E, E**2 the same way,
# then chi = (<M^2> - <|M|>^2)/(N*T),  C = (<E^2> - <E>^2)/(N*T**2)
# Tc ~ temperature of the susceptibility peak; repeat for L=16,24,32 and extrapolate.

For Track B, replace run_temperature with your velocity-Verlet integrator: equilibrate with velocity rescaling, then histogram all pair distances each production step into g(r), normalizing by the ideal-gas shell volume 4*pi*r**2*dr*(N/V). Feed the per-step potential energy through block_error for its error bar. Either way, the deliverable is a labeled plot with error bars and one reported physical number (Tc or the first-peak position) compared to the known value.

Q1. In the 2D Ising capstone, how is the magnetic susceptibility χ computed at each temperature?
A. From the fluctuations (variance) of the magnetization, χ = (<M²> − <|M|>²)/(N·T)
B. By numerically differentiating <|M|> with respect to an applied field
C. As the mean energy divided by temperature
D. From the slope of g(r) at its first peak
Show answer

A. The fluctuation-dissipation relation gives χ from the variance of M — no applied field or differentiation needed (B is the harder, noisier route). C confuses it with energy; D is a Lennard-Jones quantity, not Ising.

Q2. Why does the naive standard error σ/√N underestimate the true uncertainty of a Monte-Carlo average?
A. Because NumPy uses single precision by default
B. Because successive samples are correlated, so the effective sample count is N/(2τint) < N
C. Because the mean itself is biased
D. Because the random number generator repeats too soon
Show answer

B. Autocorrelation means there are fewer independent samples than N, so the true error is larger by √(2τint). NumPy uses double precision (A false); the sample mean is unbiased (C false); modern PRNG periods are astronomically long (D false).

Q3. In a blocking analysis, what feature of the SE-vs-block-size curve gives the correct error bar?
A. The value at block size 1
B. The minimum of the curve
C. The plateau the curve reaches once blocks are effectively independent
D. The point where the curve crosses zero
Show answer

C. SE(b) rises as blocks grow and then plateaus once block size exceeds the correlation time; that plateau is the true error. Block size 1 (A) is the underestimate; the curve increases rather than dipping to a minimum (B); SE is positive and never crosses zero (D).

Q4. A Lennard-Jones MD run gives a g(r) with a tall first peak near r ≈ 1.1σ, a smaller second peak, and oscillations decaying to 1. This indicates which phase?
A. An ideal gas with no structure
B. A liquid: short-range order, no long-range order
C. A perfect crystal with long-range order
D. A vacuum with no atoms
Show answer

B. A few decaying peaks settling to 1 is the signature of a liquid. An ideal gas gives g(r) ≈ 1 everywhere (A); a crystal shows many sharp split peaks that do not decay (C); D is nonsensical for a populated box.

Q5. You measure the Ising susceptibility peak at T ≈ 2.31 on an L = 16 lattice. Before quoting this as Tc, what should you do?
A. Nothing — 2.31 is the final answer
B. Switch to single precision to sharpen the peak
C. Reduce the number of equilibration sweeps to zero
D. Repeat at larger L (e.g. 24, 32), note the peak drifts toward ≈ 2.27, and report the finite-size trend
Show answer

D. Finite-size effects shift the peak by ~1/L; extrapolating in L recovers the Onsager value 2.269. Precision (B) does not affect the physics; dropping equilibration (C) corrupts the averages with transient data; (A) ignores the finite-size systematic.
