Monte Carlo Integration & Importance Sampling

When you can't do the integral by hand and a grid would take a billion years, you throw random darts — and a little cleverness about where to throw them buys you orders of magnitude.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Estimate an integral with both the hit-or-miss and the sample-mean Monte Carlo estimators.
  • Use a Monte Carlo estimate of π as a warm-up and sanity check.
  • Compute the variance and standard error of an MC estimate and know it falls as 1/√N.
  • Apply importance sampling to slash variance by drawing samples where the integrand is large.
  • Explain why Monte Carlo beats grid quadrature once the dimension is high.

2.1Integrals as Averages: the Sample-Mean Estimator

Last week we learned to generate uniform random numbers. Today we put them to work computing integrals — the single most common job Monte Carlo does in physics, from cross-sections to partition functions to financial expectations.

The key idea is that an integral is an average in disguise. Suppose you want the integral of f over an interval [a, b]. The mean value of f on that interval is the integral divided by the width, so the integral equals the mean times the width:

I = ∫ab f(x) dx = (b − a) · ⟨f⟩The integral is the width of the interval times the average height of f over it.

We can't compute the true average ⟨f⟩, but we can estimate it: draw N points xi uniformly at random in [a, b], evaluate f at each, and average. This is the sample-mean estimator:

I ≈ ÎN = (b − a) · (1/N) ∑i=1N f(xi)xi drawn uniformly on [a, b]. As N grows, the sample average converges to the true average (Law of Large Numbers).

This generalizes immediately to a volume V in any number of dimensions: I ≈ V · (average of f over N uniform points in V). Nothing about the formula cares whether x is a single number or a 1000-component vector — that is the secret to its power in high dimensions, which we return to in §2.4.

AnalogyTo find the average depth of a lake you don't need to map the whole bottom. Drop a weighted line at a few hundred random spots, average the depths, and multiply by the surface area — you get the lake's volume. More drops sharpen the estimate; the lake's shape never has to be written down as a formula.

2.2Hit-or-Miss and Estimating π

There is an even more visual estimator. To find the area under a curve, enclose it in a box of known area, scatter random points uniformly in the box, and count what fraction land under the curve. That fraction times the box area is your estimate. This is the hit-or-miss (or "rejection") estimator.

The classic warm-up is estimating π. Throw points uniformly into the unit square [0,1]×[0,1] and count how many fall inside the quarter circle x² + y² ≤ 1. The quarter circle has area π/4, the square has area 1, so:

π ≈ 4 · (points inside circle) / (total points)The fraction landing inside the quarter circle estimates π/4; multiply by 4.
Common mistakeHit-or-miss is the least efficient estimator and beginners over-rely on it because it is intuitive. It only ever uses 0 or 1 (in/out) per point and throws away the actual value of f. The sample-mean estimator of §2.1 uses the full height f(xi) and almost always has lower variance for the same N. Reach for hit-or-miss to build intuition, not for production work.

2.3How Wrong Is It? Variance and the 1/√N Law

A Monte Carlo answer is a random variable: run it twice with different seeds and you get two different numbers. So we must quote an error bar. The estimator ÎN is unbiased — its expected value is the true I — and its spread is governed by the variance of f.

Let σ² be the variance of the values f(x) when x is drawn from the sampling distribution. The variance of the average of N independent samples is σ²/N, so the standard error of the integral estimate is:

SE = (b − a) · σ / √Nσ is the standard deviation of f over the domain; the error of the mean shrinks like 1/√N.

This is the famous, and famously slow, 1/√N convergence. To cut the error in half you need four times as many samples; for one more decimal digit you need 100× the work. In practice you estimate σ from the same samples (the sample standard deviation), which is free.

to halve the error -> quadruple N   (error ∝ N−1/2)Independent of dimension: this rate is the same in 1-D or 1000-D. That is the headline of §2.4.

Crucially the 1/√N rate does not depend on the dimension. A grid method's accuracy collapses as dimension grows; Monte Carlo's does not. Two ways to beat the constant in front (never the rate) are to reduce σ (importance sampling, next) or to use lower-discrepancy points (quasi-Monte Carlo, a later week).

2.4Importance Sampling & Why MC Wins in High Dimensions

Importance sampling attacks the σ in the error bar. Uniform sampling wastes most of its darts where the integrand is tiny. Instead, draw samples from a chosen probability density p(x) that is large where f is large, and correct for the bias by weighting each sample by 1/p:

I = ∫ f(x) dx = ∫ [f(x)/p(x)] p(x) dx ≈ (1/N) ∑i f(xi)/p(xi)xi drawn from density p. The ratio f/p is the weighted integrand; uniform sampling is the special case p = constant.

The variance now depends on the spread of the ratio f/p, not of f. The ideal p ∝ |f| would make f/p constant and drive the variance to zero. We can't reach that ideal (it requires knowing the answer), but any p shaped like f cuts the variance dramatically. This is how detector simulations sample rare high-energy events and how rare-event estimators work across physics and finance.

Why grids fail in high dimensions. Put just 10 grid points per axis. In 1-D that is 10 evaluations; in d dimensions it is 10d — for d = 20 that is 1020, hopeless. This is the curse of dimensionality. A grid (or Simpson) method has error scaling like N−k/d, which decays painfully slowly as d grows. Monte Carlo's error stays N−1/2 regardless of d. There is a crossover dimension (roughly d ≈ 4-8 depending on the method) beyond which Monte Carlo simply wins — which is why every realistic many-body, lattice, or path-integral calculation is done with Monte Carlo.

grid error ∝ N−k/d  vs.  MC error ∝ N−1/2For fixed total work N, the grid rate collapses with dimension d; the MC rate is flat. High d -> use Monte Carlo.
✦ Worked Example — integrate a peaked function two ways

Estimate I = ∫01 e−10x dx. The exact value is (1 − e−10)/10 ≈ 0.0999955. Compare uniform sampling with importance sampling using p(x) ∝ e−10x.

  1. Uniform estimator: draw xi uniform on [0,1], average f(xi) = e−10xi, multiply by width 1. Works, but most points land where f ≈ 0 (since f drops to e−10 ≈ 4.5×10−5 by x = 1), so f varies wildly across samples.
  2. Estimate the spread: ⟨f⟩ ≈ 0.10 but ⟨f²⟩ = ∫01 e−20x dx ≈ 0.05, so σ² = 0.05 − 0.10² = 0.04, σ ≈ 0.20 — twice the size of the answer itself. With N = 104, SE ≈ 0.20/100 = 2×10−3, only ~2 good digits.
  3. Importance sampling: choose p(x) = 10 e−10x/(1 − e−10), the normalized exponential on [0,1] — sharply peaked exactly like f.
  4. The weighted integrand collapses: f(x)/p(x) = e−10x / [10 e−10x/(1 − e−10)] = (1 − e−10)/10 ≈ 0.0999955 — a constant, independent of x.
  5. Result: every sample of f/p returns the same value, so the variance is essentially zero and even a handful of samples nail the answer. We chose the perfect p here for illustration; in practice you get a p only roughly shaped like f, but the variance still drops by large factors.

🛠️ Hands-On: estimate π and watch the error fall as 1/√N

The free Python + NumPy / Matplotlib stack runs everything in this specialization. Course setup: install it once (below), then reuse it every lecture and for the project.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy / Matplotlib (one-time setup)

With Python 3 installed (from python.org or your package manager), create an environment and install the scientific stack:

python3 -m venv mc-env
source mc-env/bin/activate            # macOS / Linux
mc-env\Scripts\activate                # Windows (PowerShell)

Verify: run python -c "import numpy; print(numpy.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. (Numba is optional and only added later to speed up large MC loops.)

First estimate π by hit-or-miss, then check that the error really shrinks like 1/√N, and finally compare uniform vs importance sampling on the peaked integral from the worked example.

# mc_integration.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(42)        # reproducible stream

# --- estimate pi by hit-or-miss ---
N = 1_000_000
x, y = rng.random(N), rng.random(N)
inside = (x**2 + y**2) <= 1.0
pi_est = 4 * inside.mean()
print("pi estimate:", pi_est)          # ~3.1416, error ~ 1/sqrt(N)

# --- sample-mean integral of f = exp(-10x) on [0,1] ---
f = lambda x: np.exp(-10 * x)
xs = rng.random(N)
I_uniform = f(xs).mean()               # width = 1
se_uniform = f(xs).std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(N)
print(f"uniform:   I={I_uniform:.6f}  SE={se_uniform:.2e}")

# --- importance sampling: draw from p(x) ~ exp(-10x) ---
Z = (1 - np.exp(-10)) / 10             # normalization of f on [0,1]
u = rng.random(N)
xs_imp = -np.log(1 - u * (1 - np.exp(-10))) / 10   # inverse-CDF sampling
p = 10 * np.exp(-10 * xs_imp) / (1 - np.exp(-10))
w = f(xs_imp) / p                      # weighted integrand (here ~ constant)
I_imp, se_imp = w.mean(), w.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(N)
print(f"importance: I={I_imp:.6f}  SE={se_imp:.2e}")   # SE ~ 0, huge win
# exact = (1 - exp(-10))/10 = 0.0999955

Sweep N over [10**k for k in range(1,7)], plot the absolute error against N on log-log axes with Matplotlib, and confirm the slope is about −1/2. Then watch se_imp sit near machine zero while se_uniform stays around 2×10⁻³.

🔗 Where this is heading

Plain Monte Carlo only samples distributions you can draw from directly. Next week's random walks & diffusion show how a sequence of random steps builds a sampling process, which sets up Markov-chain Monte Carlo (Metropolis, Week 4) — the engine that samples the Boltzmann distribution for statistical-physics systems like the Ising model. Importance sampling reappears everywhere: as the reweighting in free-energy methods, as the variance-reduction backbone of detector and radiation-transport simulations, and as the change-of-measure trick behind risk pricing in the Quant track. The 1/√N law you met today is the cost model that justifies every variance-reduction technique in the rest of the course.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The standard error of a Monte Carlo integral estimate scales with the number of samples N as…
A. 1/N
B. 1/N²
C. constant (independent of N)
D. 1/√N
Show answer

D. The variance of an average of N samples is σ²/N, so the standard error is σ/√N. (A) and (B) are faster than MC actually achieves; (C) is wrong because more samples always help, just slowly.

Q2. To estimate π by hit-or-miss, you scatter points uniformly in the unit square and multiply the fraction inside the quarter circle by…
A. 2
B. π
C. 4
D. 1
Show answer

C. The quarter circle has area π/4 and the square has area 1, so the hit fraction estimates π/4; multiply by 4. (B) would require already knowing π; (A) and (D) give the wrong geometry.

Q3. Importance sampling reduces variance by…
A. using more random number generators at once
B. drawing samples from a density p(x) shaped like the integrand and weighting by f/p
C. rounding the integrand to fewer digits
D. always switching to hit-or-miss
Show answer

B. Sampling where f is large and weighting by f/p keeps the estimator unbiased while shrinking the variance of the weighted integrand. (A) does not change variance; (C) introduces error; (D) is the higher-variance estimator we want to avoid.

Q4. Compared to a fixed grid (e.g. Simpson) quadrature, Monte Carlo integration becomes the method of choice when…
A. the integrand is a smooth polynomial in 1-D
B. you need 15 digits of accuracy in 1-D
C. the number of dimensions is large
D. the integration limits are exactly 0 and 1
Show answer

C. Grid error scales like N^(−k/d), collapsing as dimension d grows, while MC stays N^(−1/2) regardless of d. In low-D smooth problems (A, B) grid/Simpson methods are far more accurate; (D) is irrelevant to the choice.

Q5. You run a Monte Carlo estimate with N samples and want to cut the statistical error in half. You should increase N by a factor of about…
A. 4
B. 2
C. 8
D. 16
Show answer

A. Since error ∝ 1/√N, halving the error needs √N to double, i.e. N × 4. (B) would only reduce error by √2; (C) and (D) overshoot — they cut error by √8 and 4 respectively.

✓ What you learned

  • An integral is an average: the sample-mean estimator Î = (b−a)·mean of f at uniform random points.
  • The hit-or-miss estimator and how to estimate π by counting darts inside a quarter circle — intuitive but higher variance.
  • MC answers need an error bar: the standard error is σ/√N, so error falls only as 1/√N (4× the samples to halve it).
  • Importance sampling: draw from p(x) shaped like f and weight by f/p to slash variance, ideally to near zero when p ∝ |f|.
  • Why MC beats grids in high dimensions: grid error N^(−k/d) collapses with d, but MC's N^(−1/2) is dimension-independent.
