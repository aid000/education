Week 3 · Lecture 3

Random Walks & Diffusion

A drunkard stumbling step by step and a drop of ink spreading through water obey the very same law — and that law falls out of pure randomness.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a random walk on a 1D and 2D lattice and simulate one from independent random steps.
  • Show that the mean displacement is zero while the mean-square displacement grows linearly with the number of steps.
  • State the diffusive scaling law and identify the diffusion constant D from it.
  • Connect the microscopic random walk to the macroscopic diffusion equation and its spreading Gaussian solution.
  • Use NumPy to run many walkers, measure <x²> vs N, and extract D from a straight-line fit.

3.1The Random Walk on a Lattice

Picture a walker standing at the origin of a one-dimensional line of integer sites. At each tick of the clock it flips a fair coin: heads it steps one unit right (+1), tails one unit left (−1). After N steps its position is just the running sum of those coin flips. This is the simple symmetric random walk, the cleanest model of any process driven by repeated independent random kicks — a pollen grain jostled by water molecules, a stock price nudged by trades, a photon scattering through the Sun.

Formally, let each step sᵢ be a random variable equal to +1 or −1 with probability ½ each, and let the position after N steps be:

xN = s₁ + s₂ + … + sN ,   with   sᵢ = ±1 (prob ½ each)Each step is independent of all the others — the walker has no memory of where it has been.

In two dimensions the recipe is identical: at each tick choose one of the four neighbours (up, down, left, right) at random and move there. The trajectory is a jagged, self-crossing tangle. Crucially, because every step is unbiased, the walk has no preferred direction — so what, on average, does it accomplish?

3.2Mean Zero, but Mean-Square Grows

Take the average over many independent walkers. Because each step is equally likely to go left or right, its expectation is zero: <sᵢ> = ½(+1) + ½(−1) = 0. Summing, the mean displacement is exactly zero:

<xN> = <s₁> + … + <sN> = 0On average the walker goes nowhere — leftward trips cancel rightward ones.

That is misleading, though: an individual walker does drift away from the origin, it just does so in an unpredictable direction. The honest measure of how far it has wandered is the mean-square displacement (MSD), which cannot cancel because it squares away the sign. Expand the square:

<xN²> = <(Σ sᵢ)²> = Σi <sᵢ²> + Σi≠j <sᵢ sj>The first sum is the diagonal terms; the second is the cross terms.

Each step has sᵢ² = 1, so <sᵢ²> = 1 and the diagonal sum contributes exactly N. The cross terms vanish: because steps are independent, <sᵢ sⱼ> = <sᵢ><sⱼ> = 0 for i ≠ j. What survives is the central result of the whole lecture:

<xN²> = N  ⟹   xrms = √<x²> = √NThe typical distance grows like the square root of the number of steps — diffusive scaling.

This is the signature of diffusion: distance grows as the square root of time, not linearly. To travel twice as far, a diffusing walker needs four times as many steps. Restoring physical units, if each step has length ℓ and takes time τ, then with N = t/τ the MSD in time t is <x²> = ℓ²t/τ ∝ t.

AnalogyImagine a crowd of children released at a doorway, each told to flip a coin every second and step left or right. After a minute the average position of the crowd is still right at the door (mean zero) — but the crowd has visibly spread out into a fuzzy cloud. The width of that cloud is what grows, like √(time), and that width is exactly xrms.

3.3The Diffusion Constant

The proportionality between mean-square displacement and time is so universal that we give its coefficient a name. In d spatial dimensions the standard definition is:

<r²(t)> = 2 d D tr is the distance from the start, d the dimension, D the diffusion constant (units length²/time).

In one dimension this reads <x²> = 2Dt. Matching it to the lattice result <x²> = ℓ²t/τ gives the bridge from microscopic hops to the macroscopic constant:

D = ℓ² / (2τ)The diffusion constant is set by the step length ℓ and the time τ between steps.

So D is not a fudge factor — it is fixed by the microscopic jiggling. Larger or faster hops mean a larger D and faster spreading. This is the heart of Einstein's 1905 analysis of Brownian motion: by relating D to the molecular collisions, he turned a microscope observation of pollen grains into a measurement of atomic sizes, decisive early evidence that atoms are real.

Measuring D in practice is delightfully simple. Plot <r²> against t; the data fall on a straight line through the origin, and the slope is 2dD. Divide the slope by 2d and you have the diffusion constant — no curve fitting beyond a line.

Common mistakeDo not fit a line to x(t) for one walker and call its slope a "speed." A single trajectory is pure noise: its mean displacement is zero and its slope is meaningless. Diffusion lives in the average of x² over many walkers (or many time-origins of one long walk), and that average is linear in t — the position itself is not. Likewise, confusing xrms = √N with a linear "distance = speed × time" law is the classic error; diffusive motion is fundamentally slower than ballistic motion.

3.4From Random Walk to the Diffusion Equation

Now zoom out. Instead of tracking one walker, track the probability P(x, t) of finding the walker at site x at time t. In one lattice step, a walker at x came from its left or right neighbour with probability ½ each:

P(x, t+τ) = ½ P(x−ℓ, t) + ½ P(x+ℓ, t)The master equation: probability flows in from the two neighbours.

Subtract P(x, t) from both sides and Taylor-expand the right side to second order in the small quantities ℓ and τ. The first-order spatial terms cancel by symmetry, leaving the time change proportional to the curvature of P:

∂P/∂t = D ∂²P/∂x² ,   D = ℓ²/(2τ)The diffusion equation (Fick's second law / the heat equation) emerges in the continuum limit.

This is a profound payoff: a smooth, deterministic partial differential equation — the same heat equation that governs how temperature spreads through a metal bar — arises from nothing but unbiased coin flips. The continuum solution starting from a point source is a spreading Gaussian:

P(x, t) = (1 / √(4 pi D t)) · e^(−x² / (4 D t))A bell curve whose width sigma = √(2Dt) grows as √t — the same √t we found from <x²> = 2Dt.

So the two pictures agree perfectly: the microscopic MSD growing like t and the macroscopic Gaussian widening like √t are the same fact seen from two sides. This duality — a stochastic particle simulation on one hand, a deterministic PDE on the other — is one of the most useful ideas in computational physics, because whichever is easier to compute gives you the other for free.

✦ Worked Example — how far, and how fast does it spread?

A molecule does a 1D random walk with step length ℓ = 2 nm taken every τ = 1 ns. Find its diffusion constant, its typical distance after 1 ms, and how long to wander 1 µm on average.

  1. Diffusion constant: D = ℓ²/(2τ) = (2×10⁻⁹ m)² / (2 · 10⁻⁹ s) = (4×10⁻¹⁸) / (2×10⁻⁹) = 2×10⁻⁹ m²/s.
  2. Number of steps in 1 ms: N = t/τ = (10⁻³ s) / (10⁻⁹ s) = 10⁶ steps.
  3. Typical distance after 1 ms: xrms = ℓ√N = (2 nm)·√(10⁶) = (2 nm)·(10³) = 2000 nm = 2 µm. (Check via <x²> = 2Dt = 2·2×10⁻⁹·10⁻³ = 4×10⁻¹² m², so xrms = 2×10⁻⁶ m. ✓)
  4. Time to wander 1 µm: set 2Dt = (10⁻⁶)², so t = (10⁻¹²) / (2·2×10⁻⁹) = (10⁻¹²)/(4×10⁻⁹) = 2.5×10⁻⁴ s ≈ 0.25 ms.
  5. Takeaway — the tyranny of √t: reaching 1 µm took 0.25 ms; reaching 10 µm (ten times farther) needs t = (10⁻⁵)²/(4×10⁻⁹) = 25 ms — one hundred times longer. Diffusion is brutally slow over long distances, which is why cells stay small and why stirring beats waiting.

🛠️ Hands-On: measure D from many walkers

We use the same free Python + NumPy + Matplotlib stack from Weeks 1–2 (the one-time install lives in the early lectures — reuse that environment here; no new tools needed). We will launch ten thousand 1D walkers at once with vectorized NumPy, average x² across them at every step, and confirm it rises linearly — then read off D from the slope.

# random_walk.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n_walkers, n_steps = 10_000, 1_000
ell, tau = 1.0, 1.0                      # step length and time (lattice units)

# each entry is +1 or -1; shape (walkers, steps)
steps = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(n_walkers, n_steps))
x = np.cumsum(steps, axis=1) * ell       # positions after each step

mean_disp = x.mean(axis=0)               # <x> over walkers -> stays near 0
msd = (x**2).mean(axis=0)                # <x^2> over walkers -> grows linearly

N = np.arange(1, n_steps + 1)
slope = np.polyfit(N, msd, 1)[0]         # fit <x^2> = slope * N
D = slope * ell**2 / (2 * tau)           # 1D: <x^2> = 2 D t, t = N*tau
print(f"mean displacement (final): {mean_disp[-1]:.3f}")   # ~0
print(f"slope of MSD: {slope:.3f}  ->  D = {D:.3f}")        # slope~1, D~0.5

plt.plot(N, msd, label="measured <x^2>")
plt.plot(N, N, "--", label="theory: <x^2> = N")
plt.xlabel("steps N"); plt.ylabel("MSD"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

You should see mean displacement hovering near zero, the MSD tracking the dashed line <x²> = N almost perfectly, and D ≈ 0.5 (since D = ℓ²/2τ = 0.5). Try it in 2D by stacking x- and y-walks and plotting <r²> = <x²> + <y²>; predict the slope (now 2dD = 4D·… careful) before you run it.

🔗 Where this is heading

The random-walk–diffusion duality is a launch pad. Next week's Markov chains generalize the unbiased walk into biased walks with arbitrary transition rules, which is exactly the engine behind Metropolis Monte Carlo sampling. The continuum limit you just derived is the bridge into Course 4's PDE solvers, where the heat/diffusion equation is the first equation everyone discretizes. Add a drift term and a random kick together and you get the Langevin equation and stochastic differential equations — the workhorse of soft-matter and biophysics simulation, and (with a sign change) the model behind option pricing in the Quant track. Whenever you see √t spreading anywhere in physics, a random walk is hiding underneath.

💬 Discussion

A bacterium about 1 µm long lives in water where small molecules diffuse with D ≈ 10⁻⁹ m²/s. Using diffusive scaling, estimate the time for a nutrient molecule to diffuse across the cell (~1 µm) versus across a 1 mm droplet of the surrounding fluid. What do your two numbers suggest about why single cells are tiny, and why larger organisms cannot rely on diffusion alone to move materials around? Argue from the math, not just intuition.

Show a model response

Model response. Diffusion time scales as t ≈ L²/(2D). Across the cell (L = 1 µm = 10⁻⁶ m): t ≈ (10⁻⁶)²/(2·10⁻⁹) = 10⁻¹²/2×10⁻⁹ ≈ 5×10⁻⁴ s, about half a millisecond — effectively instantaneous for the cell. Across the 1 mm droplet (L = 10⁻³ m), L is 1000× larger so t grows by 1000² = 10⁶: t ≈ 5×10⁻⁴ × 10⁶ = 500 s, roughly 8 minutes.

The quadratic L² dependence is the whole story. Because distance grows only as √t, doubling the size quadruples the wait, and going from microns to millimeters makes diffusion a million times slower. So diffusion is a superb delivery system at cellular scale (sub-millisecond) but hopelessly slow at organism scale (minutes to hours). That is precisely why large organisms evolved active transport — circulatory systems, stirring, molecular motors — to beat diffusion, and why a single cell that grows too large can no longer feed its interior by diffusion alone and must divide.

✓ Self-check rubric. A strong answer: (1) uses t ≈ L²/D rather than a linear distance law; (2) computes both times to an order of magnitude (~10⁻⁴ s and ~10² s); (3) explicitly attributes the gap to the L² / √t scaling; (4) draws the biological conclusion that diffusion fails at large scale, motivating active transport. Missing the L² scaling (e.g. assuming time scales linearly with size) is the key error to catch.

❓ Lecture 3 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. For a simple symmetric 1D random walk of N unbiased ±1 steps, the mean displacement <xN> is…
A. equal to N
B. equal to √N
C. exactly zero
D. equal to N/2
Show answer

C. Each step averages to zero, so the sum does too: <x> = 0. N (A) and √N (B) describe the mean-square displacement and the rms distance respectively, not the mean; N/2 (D) is not a quantity that arises here.

Q2. Why does the mean-square displacement <xN²> equal N rather than something larger?
A. Because the cross terms <sᵢsⱼ> vanish for independent steps, leaving only the N diagonal terms
B. Because squaring removes the randomness entirely
C. Because each step has length √N
D. Because the steps are correlated and reinforce each other
Show answer

A. Expanding (Σsᵢ)² gives N diagonal terms (each sᵢ²=1) plus cross terms; independence makes <sᵢsⱼ>=0 for i≠j, so only N survives. Squaring does not remove randomness (B); each step has length 1, not √N (C); and the steps are independent, not correlated — correlation (D) would change the scaling.

Q3. The relation <x²> = 2Dt tells us the typical distance from the origin grows like…
A. t (linearly in time)
B. √t
C. t²
D. log t
Show answer

B. The rms distance is √<x²> = √(2Dt) ∝ √t. Linear growth in t (A) is ballistic motion, not diffusion; t² (C) and log t (D) do not match the MSD law.

Q4. On a lattice with step length ℓ and time τ per step, the 1D diffusion constant is…
A. D = ℓτ
B. D = ℓ/τ
C. D = ℓ²/(2τ)
D. D = 2ℓ²τ
Show answer

C. Matching <x²> = ℓ²t/τ to <x²> = 2Dt gives D = ℓ²/(2τ), with units length²/time. Option B (ℓ/τ) has units of a velocity; A and D have the wrong dimensions for a diffusion constant.

Q5. Taking the continuum limit of the lattice master equation P(x,t+τ)=½P(x−ℓ,t)+½P(x+ℓ,t) yields…
A. the wave equation ∂²P/∂t² = c²∂²P/∂x²
B. a linear growth law x = vt
C. Newton's second law
D. the diffusion equation ∂P/∂t = D ∂²P/∂x², with a spreading Gaussian solution
Show answer

D. Taylor-expanding to second order gives ∂P/∂t = D∂²P/∂x² (the heat equation), whose point-source solution is a Gaussian widening as √t. The wave equation (A) is second order in time and gives propagating waves, not spreading; B and C are not PDEs for P at all.

✓ What you learned

  • A random walk is a sum of independent ±1 (or 4-neighbour) steps with no memory — the model for any noise-driven motion.
  • The mean displacement is zero while the mean-square displacement grows linearly: <x²> = N, so xrms = √N.
  • Diffusive scaling means distance grows like √t, not t — the hallmark that separates diffusion from ballistic motion.
  • The diffusion constant D = ℓ²/(2τ) connects microscopic hops to the macroscopic law <r²> = 2dDt; its slope is read straight off an MSD plot.
  • The continuum limit gives the diffusion equation ∂P/∂t = D∂²P/∂x² with a spreading Gaussian — random particles and a deterministic PDE describing the same physics.
