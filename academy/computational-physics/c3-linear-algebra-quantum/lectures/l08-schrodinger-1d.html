Week 8 · Lecture 8

The Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation (1D)

Turn a differential equation about a quantum particle into a matrix — and quantum mechanics becomes an eigenvalue problem you can solve in ten lines of NumPy.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation and identify it as an eigenvalue problem H ψ = E ψ.
  • Discretize a wavefunction on a uniform grid and replace the second derivative with a finite-difference stencil.
  • Assemble the finite-difference Hamiltonian matrix as a kinetic tridiagonal term plus a diagonal potential.
  • Interpret eigenvalues as quantized energy levels and eigenvectors as discretized wavefunctions.
  • Validate the method against the analytic particle-in-a-box spectrum using NumPy and SciPy.

8.1From a Differential Equation to an Eigenvalue Problem

A single quantum particle of mass m moving in one dimension under a potential V(x) is described by a wavefunction ψ(x). Its allowed stationary states — the standing-wave patterns that do not change shape over time — satisfy the time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE):

−(ℏ²/2m) · d²ψ/dx² + V(x) ψ(x) = E ψ(x)Kinetic term (curvature of ψ) plus potential term equals energy E times ψ. ℏ is the reduced Planck constant.

The left side is the Hamiltonian operator H acting on ψ: H = −(ℏ²/2m) d²/dx² + V(x). So the whole physics collapses into one statement — H ψ = E ψ — a continuous eigenvalue problem. The unknowns are pairs: each allowed energy E (an eigenvalue) comes with a wavefunction ψ (an eigenfunction). This is exactly the structure we spent the last seven weeks learning to solve, except H is an operator on an infinite-dimensional function space, not a finite matrix.

The plan for this lecture is the central trick of computational quantum mechanics: replace the operator with a matrix. Sample ψ at a finite set of grid points, approximate the derivative with finite differences, and the operator H becomes a finite matrix whose eigenvalues are the energy levels and whose eigenvectors are the sampled wavefunctions.

AnalogyThink of the wavefunction as the shape of a guitar string clamped at both ends. Only certain wavelengths "fit" between the clamps — those are the allowed harmonics. The Schrödinger equation plays the same game: the boundary conditions (ψ must vanish where the walls are infinitely high) allow only a discrete set of standing waves, each with its own energy. The potential V(x) just makes the "string tension" vary along its length.

8.2Discretizing on a Grid

Lay down N equally spaced interior points across the region of interest [a, b]: x_j = a + j·Δx for j = 1…N, with spacing Δx = (b − a)/(N+1). The wavefunction becomes a vector of samples ψ = (ψ_1, ψ_2, …, ψ_N), where ψ_j ≈ ψ(x_j). Outside the box we impose ψ = 0 at the walls (the endpoints x_0 and x_{N+1}), so they never appear as unknowns.

The only operator we must discretize is the second derivative. Using the central second-difference stencil (the same one you met for derivatives in Course 1, now applied to space):

d²ψ/dx² |_(x_j) ≈ (ψ_(j-1) − 2 ψ_j + ψ_(j+1)) / Δx²Second-order accurate: the error shrinks like Δx². Each grid point couples only to its two nearest neighbours.

Because each row of the stencil touches only the point and its two neighbours, the kinetic operator becomes a tridiagonal matrix — sparse, banded, and exactly the structure Lanczos and ARPACK (Week 7) devour efficiently. The diagonal carries the −2 term; the two off-diagonals carry the +1 neighbour couplings.

8.3Building the Hamiltonian Matrix

Now assemble H = T + V as an N×N matrix. The kinetic part T comes from the second-difference stencil times the prefactor −ℏ²/2m. Plugging the stencil into the kinetic operator gives a tridiagonal matrix with a constant on the diagonal and its neighbours:

T_(jj) = +ℏ²/(m Δx²),   T_(j,j±1) = −ℏ²/(2 m Δx²)The minus signs from −ℏ²/2m flip the stencil: the +2/Δx² becomes a positive diagonal, the −1 neighbours become negative off-diagonals.

The potential part V is even simpler: V(x) is a multiplication operator, so in the grid basis it is just a diagonal matrix holding the potential evaluated at each grid point, V_jj = V(x_j). Adding the two:

H = T + diag(V(x_1), …, V(x_N))A symmetric, real tridiagonal matrix: kinetic energy on the three bands, potential energy added to the diagonal.

Diagonalizing H gives N eigenpairs. The k-th smallest eigenvalue E_k approximates the k-th energy level; the corresponding eigenvector, plotted against x_j, is the discretized wavefunction ψ_k(x). Because H is real symmetric, all eigenvalues are real (as physical energies must be) and the eigenvectors are orthogonal — exactly the spectral theorem from Week 5 doing the heavy lifting.

Common mistakeForgetting the Δx² in the kinetic prefactor. The stencil approximates d²/dx² only after dividing by Δx²; if you build the matrix with bare integers (2 on the diagonal, −1 off) you get eigenvalues that are off by a factor of Δx² and that change every time you refine the grid. Always include the 1/Δx² and the physical constants, or work in a clearly stated unit system where they are set to 1.

8.4The Particle in a Box — The Check Case

Every numerical method needs a problem with a known exact answer to validate against. For the 1D TISE that benchmark is the infinite square well ("particle in a box"): set V(x) = 0 inside a box of width L and infinite outside (which is exactly our wall boundary condition ψ = 0 at the edges). The analytic eigenstates are pure sine standing waves:

ψ_n(x) = sqrt(2/L) · sin(n pi x / L),   E_n = n² pi² ℏ² / (2 m L²),  n = 1, 2, 3, …Energies grow as n²: the levels are 1, 4, 9, 16, … in units of the ground-state energy E_1.

When V = 0 the Hamiltonian is purely the kinetic tridiagonal matrix, and its eigenvectors come out as sampled sine waves with n−1 interior nodes — precisely the harmonics of the clamped string from our analogy. Comparing the computed E_k to the n² pattern (and the eigenvectors to the sines) tells you immediately whether the grid is fine enough.

✦ Worked Example — a 4-point box by hand

Take a box of width L = 1 with N = 4 interior points, and work in units where ℏ = m = 1. So Δx = L/(N+1) = 1/5 = 0.2 and the kinetic prefactor 1/Δx² = 25. With V = 0, build H and estimate the ground-state energy. Exact value: E_1 = π²/(2·1·1²) ≈ 4.9348.

  1. Diagonal: T_jj = ℏ²/(m Δx²) = 1·25 = 25. So every diagonal entry of H is 25.
  2. Off-diagonals: T_(j,j±1) = −ℏ²/(2 m Δx²) = −0.5·25 = −12.5 on the two bands.
  3. The matrix: H is the 4×4 symmetric tridiagonal [25, −12.5; −12.5, 25, −12.5; …] — diagonal 25, neighbours −12.5.
  4. Eigenvalues of this tridiagonal: E_k = 25 − 25·cos(kπ/5) for k = 1…4. For k = 1: 25 − 25·cos(36°) = 25 − 25·0.8090 = 4.775.
  5. Compare: 4.775 vs exact 4.9348 — about 3% low, with only 4 points. The error is the truncation error of the second-difference stencil and shrinks as Δx² when you add grid points; with N = 200 you reach better than 0.01%.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the box and check against n²

The free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup is all you need — no new tools this lecture. We build the tridiagonal Hamiltonian, get its lowest eigenpairs, and compare to the analytic spectrum.

Build H as a dense symmetric tridiagonal and use numpy.linalg.eigh (symmetric eigensolver, real eigenvalues guaranteed). For large N, switch to a sparse matrix and scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh (the ARPACK route from Week 7) to get only the lowest few levels cheaply.

# schrodinger_box.py — Lecture 8 (units: hbar = m = 1)
import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import diags
from scipy.sparse.linalg import eigsh

L  = 1.0                 # box width
N  = 500                 # interior grid points
dx = L / (N + 1)
x  = np.linspace(dx, L - dx, N)        # interior points only

V  = np.zeros(N)         # particle in a box: V = 0 inside

# kinetic prefactor and the three bands
t        = 1.0 / (2 * dx**2)           # hbar^2/(2 m dx^2) = 1/(2 dx^2)
main     = 2 * t + V                   # diagonal: kinetic + potential
off      = -t * np.ones(N - 1)         # neighbour couplings

H = diags([off, main, off], offsets=[-1, 0, 1])    # sparse tridiagonal

# lowest 4 eigenpairs (smallest algebraic = lowest energies)
E, psi = eigsh(H, k=4, which='SA')
order  = np.argsort(E)
E, psi = E[order], psi[:, order]

exact = np.array([(n**2) * np.pi**2 / (2 * L**2) for n in (1, 2, 3, 4)])
for n in range(4):
    print(f"n={n+1}  numeric={E[n]:.4f}  exact={exact[n]:.4f}")
# n=1  numeric=4.9347  exact=4.9348   (matches to 4 digits at N=500)

Now make it a real physics problem: set V = 0.5*k*(x - L/2)**2 for a harmonic oscillator and rerun. Predict the spectrum before you look — the levels should come out evenly spaced (E_n ∝ n + ½), not as n². Then plot psi[:, 0] against x and confirm the ground state is a single nodeless hump.

🔗 Where this is heading

You now have the engine of computational quantum mechanics: any 1D potential — harmonic trap, double well, tunnelling barrier, the Coulomb-like wells of a quantum dot — becomes a diagonal you drop onto the same tridiagonal kinetic matrix. Next week (Week 9) we promote this to crystals: a periodic V(x) plus Bloch's theorem turns the Hamiltonian into the tight-binding model, and its eigenvalues sweep out electronic band structure. Later courses reuse the identical pattern — discretize an operator, build a sparse matrix, extract its spectrum — for the diffusion and wave PDEs of Course 4. Capstone checkpoint: by the end of this project week your solver should reproduce the particle-in-a-box spectrum to under 0.1% and correctly recover the evenly spaced harmonic-oscillator levels — the validated 1D eigensolver you will extend into a 2D quantum-well solver for the capstone.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. After discretizing the 1D TISE on a grid, what do the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the Hamiltonian matrix represent?
A. eigenvalues are energy levels; eigenvectors are the sampled wavefunctions
B. eigenvalues are positions; eigenvectors are momenta
C. eigenvalues are probabilities; eigenvectors are potentials
D. eigenvalues are time steps; eigenvectors are forces
Show answer

A. H ψ = E ψ means each eigenvalue E is an allowed energy and the matching eigenvector is ψ sampled on the grid. B, C, and D mix up unrelated quantities — position, probability, and time are not what diagonalizing H yields.

Q2. Why is the finite-difference kinetic-energy matrix tridiagonal?
A. because the potential is diagonal
B. because the central second-difference stencil couples each grid point only to its two nearest neighbours
C. because the matrix must be symmetric
D. because energies are quantized
Show answer

B. The three-point stencil (ψ_(j-1) − 2ψ_j + ψ_(j+1)) touches only j and j±1, filling exactly three bands. A is about V (which is diagonal, not the reason T is tridiagonal); symmetry (C) and quantization (D) are consequences, not the cause of the band structure.

Q3. How does the potential V(x) enter the finite-difference Hamiltonian matrix?
A. as the off-diagonal bands
B. as a full dense block
C. as the diagonal, with entries V(x_j)
D. it does not appear in the matrix
Show answer

C. V is a multiplication operator, so in the grid basis it is purely diagonal: V_jj = V(x_j). The off-diagonals (A) belong to the kinetic stencil; V is never dense (B); and it certainly appears — it is what distinguishes one physical system from another (D).

Q4. For the particle in a box of width L, how do the exact energy levels scale with the quantum number n?
A. E_n ∝ n
B. E_n ∝ 1/n
C. E_n ∝ √n
D. E_n ∝ n²
Show answer

D. E_n = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²), so the levels go 1, 4, 9, 16, … Linear (A) describes the harmonic oscillator, not the box; 1/n (B) resembles hydrogen's −1/n² only loosely and is still wrong here; √n (C) appears nowhere.

Q5. You build the box Hamiltonian using the bare integer stencil (2 on the diagonal, −1 off-diagonal) and forget the 1/Δx² factor. What happens as you refine the grid?
A. nothing — the integer stencil is exact
B. the computed energies are wrong by a factor of Δx² and drift as N changes, never converging to the analytic values
C. the eigenvectors become complex
D. the matrix stops being symmetric
Show answer

B. The stencil only approximates d²/dx² after dividing by Δx²; omitting it scales every kinetic entry wrong by Δx², so the spectrum shifts and changes whenever you alter N. The matrix stays real symmetric (so C and D are false), and an integer stencil is not exact (A).

✓ What you learned

  • The 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation is an eigenvalue problem H ψ = E ψ for the Hamiltonian operator.
  • Sampling ψ on a uniform grid and using the central second-difference stencil turns the second derivative into a tridiagonal matrix.
  • The Hamiltonian is kinetic (tridiagonal) plus potential (diagonal) — a real symmetric matrix with real energies.
  • Eigenvalues are energy levels, eigenvectors are wavefunctions; the spectral theorem guarantees orthogonal real states.
  • The particle in a box (E_n ∝ n², sine eigenfunctions) is the check case — and your capstone solver should match it to under 0.1%.
