Singular Value Decomposition (SVD)

Every matrix, no matter how lopsided, is secretly just a rotation, a stretch, and another rotation — and the SVD hands you all three.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the singular value decomposition A = U Σ VT and identify what U, Σ, and V hold.
  • Read the SVD geometrically as the composition rotate → stretch → rotate acting on a sphere.
  • Relate singular values to the rank, range, and norm of a matrix.
  • Build the best rank-k approximation of a matrix and quantify the error it leaves behind.
  • Connect the SVD to principal component analysis and use NumPy to compress an image.

5.1Every Matrix Is Three Simple Steps

An eigendecomposition only exists for special (square, diagonalizable) matrices, and even then its eigenvectors need not be orthogonal. The singular value decomposition fixes both problems: every real matrix A, of any shape m×n, can be written as a product of two orthogonal matrices and one diagonal matrix.

A = U Σ VTU is m×m orthogonal, V is n×n orthogonal, Σ is m×n diagonal with entries σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ … ≥ 0.

The columns of V are the right singular vectors (an orthonormal basis of the input space), the columns of U are the left singular vectors (an orthonormal basis of the output space), and the diagonal of Σ holds the singular values σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ … ≥ 0, sorted largest first. Because U and V are orthogonal, UTU = I and VTV = I — they rotate and reflect but never stretch.

The fundamental relation linking the three pieces is wonderfully clean: A maps the i-th right singular vector onto the i-th left singular vector, scaled by σi.

A vi = σi uiThe input direction vi comes out pointing along ui, stretched by the factor σi. Compare with eigenvectors A x = λ x, where input and output share a direction.
AnalogyThink of a matrix as a machine that reshapes clay. The SVD says any such reshaping is really three plain moves done in order: spin the clay (VT), squash or stretch it along fixed axes (Σ), then spin it again (U). No matter how tangled the matrix looks, it never does anything more exotic than turn, scale, turn.

5.2The Geometry: Rotate, Stretch, Rotate

The cleanest way to see the SVD is to watch what A does to the unit sphere (in 2-D, the unit circle). A linear map always sends the unit sphere to an ellipsoid. The SVD names every part of that picture:

  • VT rotates first. It aligns the special input directions v1, v2, … with the coordinate axes.
  • Σ stretches. Each axis is scaled by its singular value σi, turning the circle into an axis-aligned ellipse whose semi-axis lengths are exactly the σi.
  • U rotates last. It spins the ellipse into its final orientation; its columns ui are the ellipse's axis directions.

So the singular values are nothing mysterious: they are the lengths of the ellipse's semi-axes — how far A can stretch a unit vector (σ1, the largest) and how little (σn, the smallest). This is why σ1 equals the matrix's 2-norm, the maximum gain ||Ax|| / ||x||.

Common mistakeSingular values are not eigenvalues. They are always real and non-negative (they are lengths), even when A is non-square or has complex eigenvalues. They are the square roots of the eigenvalues of ATA, not of A itself: σi = √λi(ATA). Confusing the two gives wrong signs and wrong magnitudes.

5.3Singular Values, Rank, and the Sum of Pieces

Count the singular values that are nonzero and you have counted the rank of A — the number of independent directions it can actually reach. A singular value of exactly zero means A collapses one input direction to nothing; a singular value that is tiny but nonzero means A nearly does so, which is the numerical fingerprint of an ill-conditioned matrix. In fact the ratio of largest to smallest nonzero singular value is the condition number κ2(A) = σ1 / σr that governs how errors amplify when you solve Ax = b.

The single most useful way to rewrite the SVD is as a sum of rank-one pieces, one per singular value:

A = Σi=1..r σi ui viTEach term σi ui viT is a rank-one matrix (an outer product). The full matrix is a weighted stack of them, ordered by importance σ1 ≥ σ2 ≥ …

Because the σi are sorted, the early terms carry the most "energy" of the matrix and the late terms carry almost none. That ordering is the seed of everything in the next section. The total energy itself has a clean name — the squared Frobenius norm is the sum of squared singular values:

||A||F2 = Σi σi2So keeping the largest singular values keeps the largest possible share of the matrix's total energy.

5.4Low-Rank Approximation, Compression, and PCA

If the full matrix is a stack of rank-one pieces ordered by importance, then the obvious idea is to throw away the small ones. Keep only the first k terms and you get Ak, the best possible rank-k approximation of A:

Ak = Σi=1..k σi ui viTThe Eckart-Young theorem: no rank-k matrix is closer to A than Ak, in either the 2-norm or the Frobenius norm.

The error you accept by truncating is set exactly by the singular values you discarded — the first one you drop is the spectral-norm error, and the rest determine the Frobenius error:

||A − Ak||2 = σk+1    ||A − Ak||F2 = Σi>k σi2If the singular values decay fast, a small k already captures almost all of A — that is compressibility.

This is the engine behind image compression: a grayscale photo is just an m×n matrix of pixel intensities. Real images have rapidly decaying singular values, so storing only the first k triples (ui, σi, vi) — that is k(m + n + 1) numbers instead of mn — reproduces the image almost perfectly while shrinking the storage dramatically.

The same mathematics is principal component analysis (PCA). Center your data matrix (subtract the mean of each column), take its SVD, and the right singular vectors vi are exactly the principal component directions, while σi2 / (N−1) is the variance captured along each. Keeping the top k components is the same Eckart-Young truncation — you are compressing the data onto the directions of greatest variance.

✦ Worked Example — SVD of a 2×2 matrix by hand

Find the singular values and the rank-1 approximation of A = [[3, 0], [4, 5]] (rows: [3, 0] and [4, 5]). Verify the error formula.

  1. Form ATA: ATA = [[25, 20], [20, 25]] (since the columns of A are (3,4) and (0,5): dot products 25, 20, 20, 25).
  2. Eigenvalues of ATA: for [[25,20],[20,25]] they are 25±20, i.e. λ1 = 45 and λ2 = 5.
  3. Singular values: σi = √λi, so σ1 = √45 ≈ 6.708 and σ2 = √5 ≈ 2.236. Both nonzero, so rank(A) = 2.
  4. Total energy check: σ12 + σ22 = 45 + 5 = 50, which equals ||A||F2 = 32+0+42+52 = 9+16+25 = 50. ✓
  5. Rank-1 error: drop the smallest term, so ||A − A1||2 = σ2 = √5 ≈ 2.236, and ||A − A1||F2 = σ22 = 5. The best rank-1 approximation captures σ12/50 = 45/50 = 90% of the energy with a single rank-one piece.

🛠️ Hands-On: compress an image with the SVD

The free Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (see the install panel in the early lectures) has everything we need — numpy.linalg.svd does the heavy lifting. Below we compress a grayscale image to a handful of singular values and watch the error track the discarded σ's exactly.

# svd_compress.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# A stand-in "image": any m x n grayscale array works the same way.
img = plt.imread("photo.png")
if img.ndim == 3:
    img = img[..., :3].mean(axis=2)        # to grayscale (m x n)

U, s, Vt = np.linalg.svd(img, full_matrices=False)
print("shape:", img.shape, " num singular values:", s.size)

# Energy captured by the first k singular values
energy = np.cumsum(s**2) / np.sum(s**2)

for k in [5, 20, 50]:
    Ak = (U[:, :k] * s[:k]) @ Vt[:k, :]    # rank-k reconstruction
    err = np.linalg.norm(img - Ak, 2)       # spectral-norm error
    print(f"k={k:3d}  energy={energy[k-1]:.4f}  ||A-Ak||2={err:.4f}  (sigma_k+1={s[k]:.4f})")
    # err should equal sigma_{k+1} exactly (Eckart-Young)

# Storage: k*(m+n+1) numbers vs m*n for the full image
m, n = img.shape
for k in [5, 20, 50]:
    print(f"k={k}: {k*(m+n+1)} numbers  vs  {m*n} full  "
          f"({100*k*(m+n+1)/(m*n):.1f}% of original)")

Confirm the printed ||A-Ak||2 matches s[k] to machine precision — that is Eckart-Young in action. Then plot plt.imshow(Ak, cmap="gray") for k = 5, 20, 50 and see how few singular values your eye actually needs.

🔗 Where this is heading

The SVD is the quiet workhorse behind the rest of this specialization. Next week's sparse matrices and large systems rely on truncated and randomized SVDs to compress operators that never fit in memory, and the pseudoinverse A+ = VΣ+UT built from the SVD is the backbone of least-squares fitting. In the quantum half of this course, the same decomposition becomes the Schmidt decomposition that measures entanglement between two subsystems, and its low-rank truncation is precisely what makes tensor-network and matrix-product-state simulations of many-body systems tractable. In later courses the singular-value spectrum reappears as the diagnostic for model reduction in fluid and plasma simulations and for noise filtering in data-driven physics. Master the SVD once and you have one lens for compression, conditioning, fitting, and entanglement all at the same time.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the SVD A = U Σ VT, the diagonal entries of Σ (the singular values) are…
A. always real and non-negative, sorted largest first
B. the eigenvalues of A, which may be negative or complex
C. always equal to 1 because U and V are orthogonal
D. the diagonal entries of A itself
Show answer

A. Singular values are lengths (semi-axes of the image ellipsoid): real, ≥ 0, and conventionally sorted descending. B confuses them with eigenvalues — they are √ of the eigenvalues of ATA, never negative or complex. C describes U and V's columns being unit length, not Σ. D is false in general.

Q2. Geometrically, applying A to the unit sphere produces an ellipsoid. The singular values are…
A. the angles between the axes of the ellipsoid
B. the lengths of the ellipsoid's semi-axes
C. the volume of the ellipsoid
D. always zero unless A is symmetric
Show answer

B. Σ stretches each axis by its singular value, so the semi-axis lengths ARE the σi. A is wrong — the axes are orthogonal (set by U), not described by angles. C: the volume is the product of the σi (|det A| up to a constant), not a single σ. D is false; nonzero singular values exist for any nonzero matrix.

Q3. A 100×100 matrix has exactly 7 nonzero singular values. Its rank is…
A. 100
B. 93
C. 7
D. impossible to tell from the SVD
Show answer

C. The rank equals the number of nonzero singular values — here 7. A would require all 100 to be nonzero. B (100−7) confuses rank with the nullity / number of zero singular values. D is wrong: the SVD reveals rank exactly, which is precisely why it is the numerically reliable way to compute rank.

Q4. By the Eckart-Young theorem, the best rank-k approximation Ak leaves a spectral-norm error ||A − Ak||2 equal to…
A. σ1, the largest singular value
B. the sum of all singular values
C. zero, always
D. σk+1, the first discarded singular value
Show answer

D. Truncating after k terms leaves the dropped pieces; the largest of those is σk+1uk+1vk+1T, so the 2-norm error is exactly σk+1. A is the error of keeping nothing (k=0). B describes neither the 2-norm nor the (squared-summed) Frobenius error. C only holds if k ≥ rank(A).

Q5. How is the SVD related to principal component analysis (PCA)?
A. They are unrelated; PCA needs eigenvectors, SVD does not use them
B. The right singular vectors of the mean-centered data are the principal components, with variance ∝ σi2
C. PCA keeps the smallest singular values to remove signal
D. PCA only works on square matrices, unlike the SVD
Show answer

B. Center the data, take its SVD: the vi are the principal directions and σi2/(N−1) is the variance captured along each. A is wrong — the SVD of centered data is exactly an eigendecomposition of the covariance matrix. C is backwards: PCA keeps the largest σ (most variance). D is false; both handle any m×n data.

✓ What you learned

  • Every matrix factors as A = U Σ VT — two orthogonal rotations around one diagonal stretch.
  • The SVD is geometry: rotate (VT), stretch by σi (Σ), rotate (U), turning the unit sphere into an ellipsoid whose semi-axes are the singular values.
  • The number of nonzero singular values is the rank, σ1 is the 2-norm, and σ1/σr is the condition number.
  • The Eckart-Young rank-k truncation Ak is the best low-rank fit, with error set exactly by the discarded σ's.
  • Low-rank truncation powers image compression and PCA, both implemented with one call to np.linalg.svd.
