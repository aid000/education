Vectors, Matrices & Linear Transformations

A matrix is not a box of numbers — it is a verb: it grabs space and rotates, stretches, and shears it, and that single idea unlocks all of quantum mechanics.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Read a vector as a physical arrow (or a list of measured components) and a matrix as a linear transformation of space.
  • Compute a matrix-vector product and identify it as rotation, scaling, or shear by watching where the basis vectors land.
  • Define a basis, perform a change of basis, and explain why the same physical state has different component lists in different bases.
  • Use the inner product and norm to measure lengths, angles, and orthogonality — the geometry behind quantum probabilities.
  • Build and manipulate vectors and matrices in NumPy, and call numpy.linalg for products, norms, and solving a linear system.

1.1Vectors and Matrices as Physical Objects

A vector is the most honest object in physics: it is an arrow with a length and a direction that exists before you pick any coordinate system. A velocity, a force, an electric field, the state of a spin — all are vectors. Once you choose axes, you can write the same arrow as a list of components, the numbers you would measure along each axis:

v = (3, 2)  means  v = 3·e_x + 2·e_yThe components 3 and 2 are not the vector — they are its shadow on a particular pair of axes e_x, e_y.

A matrix is a rectangular array of numbers, but that description hides its real nature. The deep idea of this course is that a matrix is a machine that eats a vector and spits out another vector, and it does so linearly: it respects addition and scaling. In physics, matrices represent measurements (observables), rotations of apparatus, time-evolution of a quantum state, and the coupling between coordinates in a vibrating molecule. Throughout, we write a vector as a column and stack components vertically:

v = [ v1 ; v2 ; v3 ]  (a 3x1 column)   A = [ a b ; c d ]  (a 2x2 matrix)Semicolons separate rows. A matrix with m rows and n columns maps an n-vector to an m-vector.
AnalogyThink of a vector as an address written in a particular language: "3 blocks east, 2 blocks north." The destination (the physical arrow) is fixed, but the words depend on which compass you hand someone. A matrix is then a renovation crew: feed it the whole city grid and it rotates, stretches, or skews every address at once, consistently — that consistency is linearity.

1.2The Matrix-Vector Product Is a Linear Transformation

Here is the single most important picture in linear algebra. A linear map is completely determined by what it does to the basis vectors. So to build a matrix, ask: where do e_x = (1,0) and e_y = (0,1) land? Stack those two landing places as the columns of the matrix, and the matrix is built. Then for any input v, the output is the same weighted combination of the columns:

A·v = v1·(column 1) + v2·(column 2)The product is a linear combination of A's columns, weighted by the components of v. This is the "column picture."

Concretely, for a 2x2 matrix the rule is row-dotted-into-vector:

[ a b ; c d ] · [ x ; y ] = [ a·x + b·y ; c·x + d·y ]Each output component is a dot product of one row with the input vector.

Three transformations show up everywhere. A scaling matrix diag(2, 3) doubles x and triples y. A rotation by angle theta keeps every length but spins the plane. A shear slides layers past each other, like a deck of cards pushed sideways:

R(theta) = [ cos theta, -sin theta ; sin theta, cos theta ]Its columns are where e_x and e_y land after rotating by theta counterclockwise — both still unit length, still perpendicular.

1.3Bases, Change of Basis, and Inner Products

A basis is a minimal set of vectors whose linear combinations reach every point in the space — a chosen "coordinate language." The standard basis {e_x, e_y} is convenient but not sacred. In quantum mechanics you constantly switch bases: position vs. momentum, or the energy eigenbasis vs. the spin-up/spin-down basis. The physical state never changes; only its component list does.

To change basis you express the new basis vectors in the old coordinates, stack them as columns of a matrix P, and then the same vector's new components are obtained by solving P·c_new = v_old (equivalently c_new = P-1·v_old). The geometry that makes all of this measurable is the inner product (dot product):

u . v = u1·v1 + u2·v2 + ... + un·vn = |u| |v| cos(angle)The inner product collapses two vectors into one number that encodes both length and the angle between them.

From it you get the norm (length) and the test for orthogonality:

|v| = sqrt(v . v)    u . v = 0 <=> u perpendicular to vA basis whose vectors are mutually orthogonal and unit-length is called orthonormal — the gold standard.

An orthonormal basis is the physicist's favorite because finding a vector's components becomes trivial: each component is just an inner product, c_i = e_i . v. In quantum mechanics this is exactly the rule that turns a state into measurement probabilities — the squared inner product with an eigenstate is the probability of that outcome (the Born rule).

Common mistakeDo not confuse a vector with its component list. Writing v = (3, 2) is meaningless until you say "in which basis." The same arrow becomes a different pair of numbers in a rotated basis — yet its length |v| and any angle it makes with another vector are basis-independent. If a quantity you compute changes when you merely rotate the axes, you have made an error somewhere.

1.4Matrix Multiplication Is Composition

Why is matrix multiplication defined by that strange "row times column" rule? Because a matrix is a transformation, and multiplying two matrices means doing one transformation, then the other — composition. If B acts first and A acts second, the combined transformation is the matrix product A·B, applied as (A·B)·v = A·(B·v).

(A·B)_ij = sum_k A_ik · B_kjEntry (i,j) of the product is row i of A dotted with column j of B. This is the only rule that makes (AB)v = A(Bv) hold.

Two consequences fall straight out of the "composition" view and are worth burning into memory. First, order matters: rotating then shearing is not the same as shearing then rotating, so in general A·B is not B·A. Second, for the product A·B to exist the inner dimensions must match: an (m x n) matrix times an (n x p) matrix gives an (m x p) matrix — the n's must agree, because the output of B must be a valid input to A.

✦ Worked Example — rotate, then read off geometry

Take v = (3, 1). Rotate it by theta = 90° counterclockwise, then verify the rotation preserved its length and find the angle between the original and a second vector w = (1, 2).

  1. Build the rotation matrix. At theta = 90°, cos theta = 0 and sin theta = 1, so R = [ 0, -1 ; 1, 0 ].
  2. Apply it (column picture / row-dot rule). R·v = [ 0·3 + (-1)·1 ; 1·3 + 0·1 ] = [ -1 ; 3 ]. So the rotated vector is (-1, 3).
  3. Check length is preserved. |v| = sqrt(3² + 1²) = sqrt(10) ≈ 3.162; |R·v| = sqrt((-1)² + 3²) = sqrt(10). Equal — rotations are length-preserving (orthogonal), as required.
  4. Inner product of v and w. v . w = 3·1 + 1·2 = 5, with |v| = sqrt(10) and |w| = sqrt(5).
  5. Angle between v and w. cos(angle) = (v . w)/(|v||w|) = 5 / (sqrt(10)·sqrt(5)) = 5/sqrt(50) = 1/sqrt(2) ≈ 0.707, so the angle = 45°. The dot product handed us the geometry directly — no protractor needed.

🛠️ Hands-On: vectors and matrices in NumPy

The free Python + NumPy/SciPy stack runs everything in this specialization. Week 1 setup: install it once (below), then reuse it every lecture and for the project.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy / SciPy / Matplotlib (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed (from python.org or your package manager), create an environment and install the scientific stack:

python3 -m venv quantum-env
source quantum-env/bin/activate       # macOS / Linux
quantum-env\Scripts\activate           # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import numpy, scipy; print(numpy.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. We use numpy.linalg for dense matrices and scipy.sparse.linalg for the large sparse systems that show up in later weeks.

Reproduce the worked example: build a rotation matrix, apply it, confirm length is preserved, and read off the angle between two vectors with the inner product.

# vectors_matrices.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

v = np.array([3.0, 1.0])
w = np.array([1.0, 2.0])

theta = np.pi / 2                       # 90 degrees
R = np.array([[np.cos(theta), -np.sin(theta)],
              [np.sin(theta),  np.cos(theta)]])

Rv = R @ v                              # @ is matrix-vector product
print("R @ v =", Rv)                    # [-1.  3.]
print("len v  =", np.linalg.norm(v))    # 3.1622...
print("len Rv =", np.linalg.norm(Rv))   # 3.1622... (preserved)

dot = np.dot(v, w)                       # inner product = 5.0
cos_angle = dot / (np.linalg.norm(v) * np.linalg.norm(w))
print("angle (deg) =", np.degrees(np.arccos(cos_angle)))   # 45.0

# Composition: do B (shear) first, then A (rotation)
B = np.array([[1.0, 1.0], [0.0, 1.0]])   # shear
print("A@B v:", R @ (B @ v), " (A@B)@v:", (R @ B) @ v)   # identical

# Solve a linear system A x = b  (a transformation 'run backwards')
A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0], [1.0, 3.0]])
b = np.array([5.0, 10.0])
x = np.linalg.solve(A, b)
print("solution x =", x, " check:", A @ x)   # A @ x recovers b

Try swapping the order: compare R @ B with B @ R and confirm they differ — matrix multiplication does not commute. Then change theta and verify np.linalg.norm(R @ v) never changes.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The columns of a matrix that represents a linear transformation are…
A. the eigenvalues of the transformation
B. where the standard basis vectors land after the transformation
C. always orthogonal to each other
D. the inverse of the rows
Show answer

B. A linear map is fixed by what it does to the basis; stacking the images of e_x, e_y, ... as columns builds the matrix. Eigenvalues (A) are scalars, not columns. Columns are orthogonal (C) only for special matrices like rotations. (D) is meaningless.

Q2. A 2x2 rotation matrix R(theta) acts on a vector v. What does it preserve?
A. the components of v but not its length
B. nothing — it changes everything
C. the length |v| and angles between vectors
D. only the x-component
Show answer

C. Rotations are orthogonal transformations: they preserve all lengths and angles (and hence inner products). The components (A) generally change; (D) is false since both components mix.

Q3. For matrices A (3x2) and B (2x4), which product is defined, and what is its shape?
A. A·B, shape 3x4
B. B·A, shape 4x3
C. both A·B and B·A, both 2x2
D. neither product is defined
Show answer

A. For A·B the inner dimensions must match: (3x2)(2x4) works and gives 3x4. B·A would need (2x4)(3x2), but 4 ≠ 3, so it is undefined — ruling out B, C, and D.

Q4. Two nonzero vectors u and v satisfy u . v = 0. This means…
A. u and v are parallel
B. u and v are perpendicular (orthogonal)
C. u and v have the same length
D. at least one of them is the zero vector
Show answer

B. Since u . v = |u||v|cos(angle), a zero dot product with nonzero lengths forces cos(angle) = 0, i.e. a 90° angle. Parallel vectors (A) have a maximal dot product; (C) is unrelated; (D) is excluded because both are nonzero.

Q5. Writing v = (3, 2) for a fixed physical arrow is only meaningful once you also specify…
A. the matrix that created it
B. its eigenvalues
C. the determinant of the space
D. the basis (coordinate system) the components are measured in
Show answer

D. Components are the vector's shadow on a chosen basis; rotate the basis and the same arrow gets different numbers. A vector has no eigenvalues (B), a "space" has no determinant (C), and no matrix is needed to name an existing vector (A).

✓ What you learned

  • A vector is a coordinate-free arrow; its component list is its shadow on a chosen basis, while a matrix is a linear transformation of space.
  • The matrix-vector product is a linear combination of the matrix's columns — the columns are where the basis vectors land (scaling, rotation, shear).
  • A basis is a coordinate language; change of basis relabels the same physical vector, and an orthonormal basis makes components into simple inner products.
  • The inner product gives length (norm), angle, and the orthogonality test u . v = 0 — all basis-independent.
  • Matrix multiplication is composition (order matters, inner dimensions must match), and NumPy's @, np.linalg.norm, and np.linalg.solve put it all into code.
