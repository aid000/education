Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

Hidden inside every matrix are a handful of special directions it cannot rotate — find them, and the hardest problems in physics fall apart into independent, solvable pieces.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define eigenvalues and eigenvectors as the invariant directions of a linear map and read them off a transformation geometrically.
  • Derive the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0 and use it to find eigenvalues by hand for small matrices.
  • Explain why symmetric and Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors — and why physics depends on it.
  • Connect the eigenvalue problem to the time-independent Schrödinger equation and to normal modes of vibration.
  • Compute eigenpairs reliably with np.linalg.eig and np.linalg.eigh, and know which to reach for.

2.1The Directions a Matrix Cannot Bend

A square matrix A is a linear transformation: feed it a vector, get a vector back, generally rotated and stretched. Most input directions come out pointing somewhere new. But almost every matrix hides a few special directions that come out pointing exactly the same way — only scaled. A vector v on such a direction satisfies:

A v = λ v  (v ≠ 0)v is an eigenvector; the scale factor λ is its eigenvalue. The matrix acts on this direction as pure stretching.

The word eigen is German for "own" or "characteristic" — these are the directions a matrix considers its own. Acting with A on an eigenvector never tilts it; it only lengthens (λ > 1), shrinks (0 < λ < 1), flips (λ < 0), or freezes it (λ = 1). Off the eigen-directions the matrix mixes things up; along them it is as simple as multiplying by a number. That collapse from "complicated transformation" to "just a number" is the entire payoff.

AnalogyThink of A as a current in a pond and vectors as twigs floating on it. A generic twig gets spun and carried off at an angle. But a twig laid along an eigen-direction is only pushed straight along its own length — faster or slower, forward or backward, never turned. Those special "no-spin" lines are the eigenvectors; how fast the current pushes along each is the eigenvalue.

2.2The Characteristic Equation

How do we find these directions? Rewrite A v = λ v by moving everything to one side, using the identity matrix I so the dimensions match:

(A − λI) v = 0We need a nonzero v in the null space of the matrix (A − λI).

A nonzero vector v can only be sent to 0 if the matrix (A − λI) is singular — it must squash some direction to nothing. A matrix is singular exactly when its determinant is zero. That gives the master condition, the characteristic equation:

det(A − λI) = 0A polynomial of degree n in λ for an n×n matrix. Its roots are the eigenvalues.

Expanding the determinant produces the characteristic polynomial, degree n in λ. Its n roots (real or complex, possibly repeated) are the eigenvalues. For each eigenvalue λ, you then solve (A − λI) v = 0 to find the eigenvector(s) spanning its direction. Two facts fall out for free and make excellent sanity checks: the eigenvalues sum to the trace (the diagonal sum) and multiply to the determinant:

λ₁ + λ₂ + ... + λn = tr(A)    λ₁·λ₂·...·λn = det(A)Cheap, powerful checks: if your eigenvalues don't sum to the trace, something is wrong.
Common mistakeForgetting the I. Writing det(A − λ) = 0 is meaningless — you cannot subtract a scalar λ from a matrix. You must subtract λ times the identity, which removes λ only from the diagonal entries. A second trap: assuming a real matrix has real eigenvalues. A plain rotation matrix has complex eigenvalues (it turns every real vector), which is exactly why the symmetric/Hermitian guarantee in §2.3 matters so much.

2.3Why Physics Loves Symmetric & Hermitian Matrices

A real matrix is symmetric if A = Aᵀ (it equals its own transpose). Its complex-valued cousin is Hermitian: A = A† , where A† is the conjugate transpose (transpose, then conjugate every entry). These are not arbitrary — they are the matrices that encode physical observables. And they come with a remarkable two-part guarantee (the spectral theorem):

A = A†  ⟹  every λ is real,  and  eigenvectors of distinct λ are orthogonalReal spectrum + orthogonal eigenbasis. This is the backbone of quantum mechanics and of vibration analysis.

Why real eigenvalues? In quantum mechanics, energy, momentum, and position are represented by Hermitian operators, and any measured value must be a real number — you never read 3 + 2i joules off a meter. The Hermitian property forces the spectrum to be real, so it can describe measurable quantities. The short proof: take Av = λv, hit it from the left with v† . Then v†Av = λ v†v. Taking the conjugate transpose of the whole scalar and using A† = A shows v†Av is real and v†v > 0, so λ = (v†Av)/(v†v) is real.

Why orthogonal eigenvectors? They form a clean coordinate system — perpendicular axes with no overlap. In quantum mechanics, eigenstates of different energy are mutually exclusive outcomes; orthogonality is the mathematical statement that measuring "state 1" rules out "state 2." Practically, an orthogonal eigenbasis lets you decompose any state or any motion into independent pieces that evolve on their own.

AnalogySpin a lump of clay on a potter's wheel and a symmetric "stress" matrix squashes the round ball into an ellipsoid. Its longest and shortest axes are always at right angles — never a skewed parallelogram. Those perpendicular principal axes are the eigenvectors; the amount of squash along each is its (real) eigenvalue. Nature builds observables out of exactly this shape.

2.4Eigenproblems Are Everywhere in Physics

Once you recognize the pattern A v = λ v, you start seeing it everywhere. The two pillars of computational physics are both eigenvalue problems in disguise.

Quantum mechanics: the time-independent Schrödinger equation

The allowed energies of a quantum system are the eigenvalues of its Hamiltonian operator H — a Hermitian operator. Discretize space on a grid and H becomes a (large, Hermitian) matrix; the equation

H ψ = E ψEnergy levels E are eigenvalues; the wavefunctions ψ (orbitals, bound states) are the eigenvectors.

is literally an eigenvalue problem. The discrete energy levels of an atom — why spectra are sharp lines and not a smear — are exactly the discrete eigenvalues of a Hermitian matrix. We solve a small version of this by hand and on the computer in Week 8.

Classical vibration: normal modes

A network of masses and springs obeys M ẍ = −K x. Seeking oscillating solutions x = v·eiωt turns this into the generalized eigenproblem K v = ω²M v: the eigenvalues are the squared resonant frequencies and the eigenvectors are the normal modes — the special shapes in which the whole structure vibrates in lock-step at one frequency. Because K and M are symmetric, those frequencies are real and the modes are orthogonal, which is why any complicated vibration decomposes cleanly into independent modes (Week 3).

The same structure governs the principal axes of a rotating rigid body (the inertia tensor), the stability of equilibria (signs of eigenvalues of the Hessian), and the principal components that compress data — all read off from one matrix's eigenpairs.

✦ Worked Example — eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 symmetric matrix

Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the symmetric matrix A = [[2, 1], [1, 2]], and verify the trace/determinant checks.

  1. Set up the characteristic equation: A − λI = [[2−λ, 1], [1, 2−λ]]. Its determinant is (2−λ)(2−λ) − (1)(1) = (2−λ)² − 1.
  2. Solve det = 0: (2−λ)² = 1, so 2−λ = ±1, giving λ₁ = 3 and λ₂ = 1. Both real, as the spectral theorem promised for a symmetric matrix.
  3. Sanity checks: trace = 2 + 2 = 4 and λ₁ + λ₂ = 3 + 1 = 4 ✓. det(A) = 2·2 − 1·1 = 3 and λ₁·λ₂ = 3·1 = 3 ✓.
  4. Eigenvector for λ₁ = 3: solve (A − 3I)v = [[−1, 1], [1, −1]]v = 0, i.e. −v₁ + v₂ = 0, so v₁ = v₂. Direction v₁ = (1, 1) (normalize to (1, 1)/√2).
  5. Eigenvector for λ₂ = 1: solve (A − I)v = [[1, 1], [1, 1]]v = 0, i.e. v₁ + v₂ = 0, so v₂ = −v₁. Direction v₂ = (1, −1)/√2.
  6. Orthogonality check: v₁ · v₂ = (1)(1) + (1)(−1) = 0 ✓ — perpendicular, exactly as guaranteed for distinct eigenvalues of a symmetric matrix.

First reproduce the worked example, then see why eigh (for symmetric/Hermitian matrices) is the right tool: it returns real, sorted eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors, while the general eig can hand back complex round-off noise on the same matrix.

# eigenvalues.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

A = np.array([[2.0, 1.0],
              [1.0, 2.0]])

# General solver: works for any square matrix
vals, vecs = np.linalg.eig(A)
print("eig  values:", vals)             # [3. 1.] (order not guaranteed)

# eigh: ONLY for symmetric/Hermitian — faster, real, sorted ascending
w, V = np.linalg.eigh(A)
print("eigh values:", w)                # [1. 3.]
print("eigh vectors:\n", V)             # columns are the eigenvectors

# Check the definition A v = lambda v for the first eigenpair
print("residual:", A @ V[:, 0] - w[0] * V[:, 0])   # ~0
# Check orthonormality: V^T V should be the identity
print(np.round(V.T @ V, 12))            # [[1 0],[0 1]]

Now a real eigenproblem: a particle in a 1-D box. Build the Hamiltonian as a tridiagonal (Hermitian) matrix and let eigh hand you the energy levels and wavefunctions.

# particle_in_a_box.py — discrete Schrodinger equation
import numpy as np

N = 500                       # grid points
x = np.linspace(0, 1, N)
h = x[1] - x[0]

# Kinetic energy ~ -1/2 d^2/dx^2 via the second-difference stencil
main = np.full(N, 2.0)
off  = np.full(N - 1, -1.0)
H = (np.diag(main) + np.diag(off, 1) + np.diag(off, -1)) / (2 * h**2)

E, psi = np.linalg.eigh(H)    # Hermitian -> real, sorted energies
print("lowest 4 energies:", E[:4])
# Ratios approach 1 : 4 : 9 : 16  (E_n proportional to n^2), the box spectrum
print("ratios:", np.round(E[:4] / E[0], 2))

Predict the energy ratios from En ∝ n² before you run it, then plot psi[:, 0] and psi[:, 1] with Matplotlib — the ground state and first excited state of the box.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A vector v satisfies A v = λ v with v ≠ 0. Geometrically, what does A do to the direction of v?
A. rotates it by 90 degrees
B. leaves the direction unchanged, only scaling it by λ
C. always shrinks it to the zero vector
D. projects it onto the x-axis
Show answer

B. An eigenvector keeps its line; A only rescales it by λ. (A) describes a rotation, which generally moves a direction; (C) would require v = 0 or λ = 0 for all of v, not the general case; (D) is a specific projection, not the eigen-relation.

Q2. Why do we solve det(A − λI) = 0 rather than det(A − λ) = 0 to find eigenvalues?
A. The two are identical; the I is just decoration.
B. Because λ must be subtracted from every entry of A.
C. Because you cannot subtract a scalar from a matrix; λI subtracts λ from the diagonal so (A − λI) is a valid matrix that must be singular.
D. Because the determinant is only defined for diagonal matrices.
Show answer

C. A − λ is undefined (matrix minus scalar). We need (A − λI) singular so a nonzero v lands in its null space. (A) is false — the I matters; (B) is wrong, λI touches only the diagonal; (D) is false, determinants are defined for any square matrix.

Q3. For a Hermitian matrix (A = A†), the spectral theorem guarantees that…
A. all eigenvalues are real and eigenvectors of distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal
B. all eigenvalues are complex with nonzero imaginary part
C. the matrix has no eigenvalues
D. every eigenvalue equals 1
Show answer

A. Real spectrum and an orthogonal eigenbasis — the reason Hermitian operators model observables. (B) is backward (the imaginary parts vanish); (C) is false (an n×n matrix always has n eigenvalues counted with multiplicity); (D) describes only the identity, not Hermitian matrices in general.

Q4. In the time-independent Schrödinger equation H ψ = E ψ, what are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors?
A. eigenvalues are wavefunctions; eigenvectors are forces
B. eigenvalues are the allowed energies E; eigenvectors are the wavefunctions ψ
C. eigenvalues are positions; eigenvectors are momenta
D. there is no eigenvalue structure; it is solved by integration only
Show answer

B. H ψ = E ψ is exactly A v = λ v: E are the (real, since H is Hermitian) energy eigenvalues and ψ are the eigenvectors. (A) swaps the roles; (C) misnames the quantities; (D) ignores that on a grid H is a Hermitian matrix whose eigenpairs we compute directly.

Q5. You have a real symmetric matrix and want its eigenvalues and eigenvectors in NumPy. The best choice is…
A. np.linalg.solve, since eigenvalues are just a linear system
B. np.linalg.inv, then read the diagonal
C. np.linalg.det in a loop over λ
D. np.linalg.eigh, which exploits symmetry to return real, sorted eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors
Show answer

D. eigh is built for symmetric/Hermitian matrices: faster and numerically cleaner than the general eig, with guaranteed-real sorted eigenvalues. (A) solves Ax = b, not the eigenproblem; (B) the inverse's diagonal is unrelated to eigenvalues; (C) scanning det(A − λI) by hand is hopeless numerically.

✓ What you learned

  • An eigenvector is an invariant direction of a linear map; A only scales it by its eigenvalue λ, via A v = λ v.
  • Eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0; they sum to the trace and multiply to the determinant.
  • Symmetric/Hermitian matrices have real eigenvalues and orthogonal eigenvectors — the spectral theorem that underpins physics.
  • Eigenproblems are physics: H ψ = E ψ gives quantum energy levels, and K v = ω²M v gives classical normal modes.
  • Compute with np.linalg.eig for general matrices and np.linalg.eigh for symmetric/Hermitian ones — sorted, real, orthonormal.
