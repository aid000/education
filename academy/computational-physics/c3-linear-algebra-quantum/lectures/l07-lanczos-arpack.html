Krylov Methods: Lanczos & ARPACK

When your matrix has a million rows but you only need the lowest few energies, you never build the whole thing — you let the matrix tell you its own best subspace.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a Krylov subspace and explain why repeated matrix–vector products reveal extremal eigenvalues first.
  • Derive the Lanczos three-term recurrence and see how it tridiagonalizes a symmetric matrix without ever forming a dense matrix.
  • Explain what Ritz values are and why outer eigenvalues converge long before the subspace is full.
  • Use scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh (ARPACK) to extract a handful of extremal eigenpairs of a huge sparse matrix.
  • Compare the cost of Lanczos/ARPACK against dense diagonalization and know when each is the right tool.

7.1The Only Thing a Huge Matrix Lets You Do: Multiply

Last week (Week 6) you met sparse matrices: a quantum Hamiltonian on a lattice, a graph Laplacian, a finite-difference operator — all huge (N can be 10⁶ or far more) but mostly zeros. Storing N² numbers is impossible, and a dense eigensolver that costs O(N³) flops is out of the question. But there is one operation a sparse matrix does cheaply: it multiplies a vector. A matrix–vector product Av costs only as many operations as there are nonzeros — often O(N), not O(N²).

So we are forced to ask: what can we learn about a matrix using only its action on vectors? Start with any nonzero vector v and keep applying A. The vectors you generate,

v,   Av,   A²v,   A³v,   ...,   A^(m-1)vThe span of these m vectors is the m-th Krylov subspace K_m(A, v).

span a space called the Krylov subspace K_m. The magic is that this little subspace — built from nothing but matrix–vector products — contains astonishingly good approximations to the extremal eigenvectors of A (the largest and smallest eigenvalues). The directions belonging to large-magnitude eigenvalues get amplified each time you multiply, so they emerge from the Krylov sequence first. This is the same instinct as the power method, but instead of throwing away every intermediate vector, Krylov methods keep all of them and search the whole subspace.

AnalogyThink of striking a bell. You do not need a blueprint of the bell to learn its tone — you hit it (one matrix–vector product), listen, hit again, and the dominant resonances stand out almost immediately. The Krylov sequence A^k v is exactly this repeated "striking"; the loudest, most extreme modes ring out first, and you read off the pitch (eigenvalue) without ever weighing the whole bell.

7.2The Lanczos Algorithm: Tridiagonalize Without the Matrix

The raw Krylov vectors A^k v become almost parallel (they all tilt toward the dominant eigenvector), so they are numerically useless as a basis. The Lanczos algorithm fixes this for symmetric (Hermitian) matrices by building an orthonormal basis q₁, q₂, ... of the same Krylov subspace, one vector at a time. Remarkably, when A is symmetric, orthogonalizing the new vector against the previous two is enough — every earlier vector is automatically orthogonal. That gives the famous three-term recurrence:

A q_k = beta_(k-1) q_(k-1) + alpha_k q_k + beta_k q_(k+1)alpha_k = q_k^T A q_k (diagonal), beta_k = ||r_k|| (off-diagonal). Only the two previous q's appear.

Stacking the basis vectors as columns of Q_m and collecting the alpha's and beta's, the projection of A onto the Krylov subspace is a small tridiagonal matrix T_m:

Q_m^T A Q_m = T_m  (m x m, tridiagonal)m is the number of Lanczos steps, typically a few dozen — vastly smaller than N.

Now diagonalize T_m. Because it is tiny and tridiagonal, that costs almost nothing (O(m²) or less). Its eigenvalues, called Ritz values, are the Krylov subspace's best estimates of A's eigenvalues — and the extremal ones converge to full accuracy after only a handful of steps, long before m reaches N. The whole point: we replaced an N×N eigenproblem with an m×m one, paying only m matrix–vector products to do it.

Common mistakePlain Lanczos loses orthogonality in floating point: round-off lets old directions creep back, producing ghost (spurious, duplicated) eigenvalues. Do not trust a hand-rolled three-term loop for production work, and do not "fix" it by just running more steps. Real solvers re-orthogonalize (full or selective) — which is exactly why you should call a battle-tested library like ARPACK instead of writing your own.

7.3ARPACK and eigsh: Lanczos You Can Actually Trust

ARPACK is the standard, free, time-tested library that implements a robust, re-orthogonalized, restarted variant of Lanczos (the Implicitly Restarted Lanczos Method). You reach it from Python through scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh — the "sh" stands for symmetric/Hermitian. You hand it a sparse matrix (or even just a function that computes Av via a LinearOperator), say how many eigenvalues you want and from which end of the spectrum, and it returns just those eigenpairs:

w, v = eigsh(A, k=6, which='SA')k = how many eigenpairs; which='SA' = smallest algebraic (ground state + low excited states). 'LA' = largest.

The "restarted" idea matters: rather than letting the Krylov basis grow without bound (which costs memory and re-orthogonalization time), ARPACK keeps a fixed-size workspace and periodically compresses it, discarding the directions that point at eigenvalues you do not want and keeping those that point at the ones you do. You control this with ncv (the workspace size, default about 2k+1 to min(N, max(2k+1,20))). A larger ncv converges in fewer restarts but uses more memory per step.

Interior eigenvalues and shift-invert

Krylov methods love the outside of the spectrum; eigenvalues buried in the middle converge painfully slowly. If you need states near a target energy sigma (say, levels near the Fermi energy of a solid), use shift-invert: feed ARPACK the operator (A − sigma·I)⁻¹, whose largest eigenvalues correspond to A's eigenvalues closest to sigma. In SciPy that is eigsh(A, k=6, sigma=target, which='LM'), which factorizes once and solves a linear system each iteration.

AnalogyAsking for outer eigenvalues is like spotting the tallest and shortest people in a stadium — easy from the rim. Asking for an interior eigenvalue is like finding the person of exactly average height in the crowd: hopeless from a distance. Shift-invert is handing everyone a ruler measured from your target height, so the person closest to it now towers over the rest — the interior problem becomes an outer one.

7.4Why It Is So Much Cheaper Than Dense Diagonalization

Dense diagonalization (numpy.linalg.eigh, Week 4) returns all N eigenpairs and costs about O(N³) time and O(N²) memory. If N = 10⁶, that is 10¹⁸ flops and 8 terabytes just to store the matrix — completely infeasible. Lanczos/ARPACK costs, very roughly:

cost ~ (number of iterations) x (cost of one A·v) + O(m³)A·v is O(nnz) ~ O(N) for a sparse matrix; m (workspace) is tens, not millions.

The dominant cost is matrix–vector products, each O(nnz). To get k eigenvalues you typically need a few dozen to a few hundred such products — so the total scales like O(N) (times a modest constant), not O(N³). The trade is fundamental: dense methods give you everything; Krylov methods give you a few extremal eigenpairs for a tiny fraction of the cost. In physics you almost always want the latter — the ground state and a few low-lying excitations of a Hamiltonian, the slowest modes of a relaxation operator, the leading vibrational frequencies.

✦ Worked Example — Lanczos by hand on a 3x3 matrix

Run two Lanczos steps on the symmetric matrix A = [[2,1,0],[1,2,1],[0,1,2]] starting from q₁ = (1,0,0)ᵀ, and read off the first Ritz value.

  1. Step 1 — alpha₁: A q₁ = (2,1,0)ᵀ. Then alpha₁ = q₁ᵀ(A q₁) = 2. The 1x1 projection T₁ = [2] already gives a Ritz value of 2 — exactly the middle eigenvalue of A.
  2. Residual: r₁ = A q₁ − alpha₁ q₁ = (2,1,0) − 2(1,0,0) = (0,1,0). Its norm is beta₁ = 1.
  3. New basis vector: q₂ = r₁ / beta₁ = (0,1,0)ᵀ. (Orthogonal to q₁, as required.)
  4. Step 2 — alpha₂: A q₂ = (1,2,1)ᵀ, so alpha₂ = q₂ᵀ(A q₂) = 2. The tridiagonal block is now T₂ = [[2,1],[1,2]].
  5. Ritz values of T₂: eigenvalues of [[2,1],[1,2]] are 2 ± 1 = 1 and 3. After just two of three steps Lanczos has already captured the extreme eigenvalues 1 and 3 (A's true spectrum is {2 − √2, 2, 2 + √2} ≈ {0.586, 2, 3.414}). The outer Ritz values 1 and 3 bracket the true extremes 0.586 and 3.414 and tighten as we take the third step — the hallmark of Krylov convergence from the outside in.

🛠️ Hands-On: ground state of a sparse Hamiltonian with eigsh

Uses the same free Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the one-time install details lives in the early lectures) — no new tools today; scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh ships with SciPy.

Build a 1-D tight-binding / discrete-Laplacian Hamiltonian as a sparse matrix, then pull just the lowest few eigenvalues with ARPACK and compare timing against dense eigh.

# lanczos_arpack.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
from scipy.sparse.linalg import eigsh
from numpy.linalg import eigh
import time

N = 5000                                  # try 200000 for eigsh — eigh will choke
# 1-D discrete Laplacian: -1 on off-diagonals, 2 on diagonal (tridiagonal, sparse)
diag    = 2.0 * np.ones(N)
offdiag = -1.0 * np.ones(N - 1)
H = sp.diags([offdiag, diag, offdiag], offsets=[-1, 0, 1], format="csr")

# --- ARPACK / Lanczos: just the 6 SMALLEST eigenvalues ---
t0 = time.perf_counter()
w_small, v_small = eigsh(H, k=6, which="SA")   # SA = smallest algebraic
t_arpack = time.perf_counter() - t0
print("lowest 6 (eigsh):", np.round(w_small, 5))
print(f"eigsh time: {t_arpack:.3f} s")

# exact eigenvalues of this matrix: 2 - 2 cos(k*pi/(N+1)), k=1..N
k = np.arange(1, 7)
exact = 2 - 2 * np.cos(k * np.pi / (N + 1))
print("exact lowest 6:   ", np.round(exact, 5))

# --- Dense for comparison (only feasible because N is modest here) ---
t0 = time.perf_counter()
w_dense = eigh(H.toarray(), eigvals_only=True)  # O(N^3): ALL eigenvalues
print(f"dense eigh time: {time.perf_counter() - t0:.3f} s  (and it built an N*N array!)")
# eigsh is dramatically faster AND uses far less memory for the same 6 numbers

Now bump N to 200000 and comment out the dense block: eigsh still returns in well under a second, while H.toarray() alone would need ~320 GB. To probe states near a target energy sigma, swap in eigsh(H, k=6, sigma=1.0, which='LM') (shift-invert) and watch it lock onto the interior levels.

Q1. The m-th Krylov subspace K_m(A, v) is the span of…
A. the m largest eigenvectors of A
B. the vectors v, Av, A²v, …, A^(m−1)v
C. the m columns of A with the most nonzeros
D. all vectors orthogonal to v
Show answer

B. A Krylov subspace is built purely from repeated matrix–vector products on a starting vector. It is not the eigenvectors themselves (A) — those are what we approximate from the subspace; (C) and (D) are unrelated to the Krylov construction.

Q2. For a symmetric matrix, the Lanczos algorithm projects A onto the Krylov subspace, producing a small matrix that is…
A. diagonal
B. dense and nonsymmetric
C. tridiagonal
D. upper triangular
Show answer

C. The three-term recurrence makes T_m tridiagonal (Qᵀ A Q = T). It is not already diagonal (A) — you still diagonalize the small T. Arnoldi (the nonsymmetric case) gives an upper-Hessenberg matrix, not triangular (D); dense/nonsymmetric (B) is wrong because symmetry is exactly what gives the tridiagonal short recurrence.

Q3. You have a sparse symmetric Hamiltonian with N = 1,000,000 and only need the ground state and 5 lowest excited states. The right tool is…
A. numpy.linalg.eigh on the dense form
B. scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh(H, k=6, which='SA')
C. inverting H explicitly
D. computing the determinant of H
Show answer

B. eigsh (ARPACK/Lanczos) extracts just the k extremal eigenpairs at roughly O(N) cost. Dense eigh (A) needs O(N²) memory (~8 TB here) and O(N³) time; explicitly inverting (C) or taking a determinant (D) is even more expensive and does not give eigenpairs.

Q4. Why do Krylov methods like Lanczos converge to the extremal eigenvalues fastest?
A. Repeated multiplication by A amplifies large-magnitude eigencomponents, so outer modes dominate the subspace first
B. Interior eigenvalues do not exist for sparse matrices
C. ARPACK sorts the matrix entries before solving
D. The tridiagonal matrix has no interior entries
Show answer

A. Each application of A scales each eigendirection by its eigenvalue, so the largest-magnitude (extremal) components grow relative to the rest — the same mechanism as the power method. Interior eigenvalues certainly exist (B) and are simply slow to converge, which is why shift-invert is used; (C) and (D) are fabricated.

Q5. You need eigenvalues near an interior target energy sigma (deep inside the spectrum). The standard remedy in eigsh is…
A. request more eigenvalues with a huge k until you reach the middle
B. switch to dense eigh always
C. increase the matrix size N
D. shift-invert: pass sigma=target so eigenvalues nearest sigma become the easy outer ones of (A − sigma·I)⁻¹
Show answer

D. Shift-invert maps the eigenvalues closest to sigma to the largest-magnitude eigenvalues of (A − sigma·I)⁻¹, which Lanczos finds quickly. Brute-forcing a huge k (A) wastes enormous work; dense (B) defeats the purpose for large N; changing N (C) changes the problem entirely.

✓ What you learned

  • A Krylov subspace K_m is the span of v, Av, …, A^(m−1)v — built using only cheap matrix–vector products.
  • Lanczos builds an orthonormal basis via a three-term recurrence, projecting a symmetric A onto a tiny tridiagonal T_m whose eigenvalues (Ritz values) approximate A's extremes.
  • ARPACK via scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh gives a robust, re-orthogonalized, restarted Lanczos; use k, which, and ncv to control it.
  • Shift-invert (sigma=) turns hard interior eigenvalues into easy outer ones.
  • Krylov cost scales like O(N) matrix–vector products versus dense O(N³) — the right choice when you need only a few extremal states.
