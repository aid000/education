Week 9 · Lecture 9

Tight-Binding & Band Structure

Bring a million atoms together and their sharp, isolated energy levels blur into continuous bands — the single idea behind why some solids conduct and others don't.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the two physical ingredients of the tight-binding model: on-site energies and nearest-neighbor hopping.
  • Assemble the tight-binding Hamiltonian as a sparse matrix for a 1D chain of atoms.
  • Derive the dispersion relation E(k) for the infinite 1D chain and read off its bandwidth.
  • Explain how N discrete molecular levels broaden into a continuous band as N grows.
  • Diagonalize a finite tight-binding chain in NumPy/SciPy and compare the eigenvalues to the analytic band.

9.1Two Atoms, Then Many: the Tight-Binding Idea

Take a single isolated atom. An electron sitting on it has some well-defined energy — call it the on-site energy, ε. Now bring a second identical atom close. The electron can no longer tell which atom it lives on; it can tunnel from one to the other. That quantum-mechanical leakage of an electron between neighboring atoms is the single new ingredient of the tight-binding model, and we summarize its strength with one number, the hopping integral t.

"Tight-binding" means we assume electrons are tightly bound to their atoms — each electron mostly stays put on one site, with only a small amplitude to hop to a neighbor. That is the opposite limit from the free-electron picture, and it is astonishingly accurate for the electrons that control chemistry and conduction in real materials.

For two atoms, the Hamiltonian in the basis of "electron on atom 1" and "electron on atom 2" is a 2×2 matrix:

H = [[ ε , -t ], [ -t , ε ]]  →   E± = ε − t , ε + tThe diagonal is the on-site energy; the off-diagonal -t is the hopping. The two atomic levels split by 2t into a bonding (lower) and antibonding (upper) pair.

This is exactly the molecular bonding/antibonding splitting from chemistry, written as an eigenvalue problem. Everything that follows is just this same matrix, grown to many atoms.

AnalogyThink of each atom as a pendulum and hopping t as a weak spring coupling neighbors. One pendulum has a single frequency. Couple two and you get two modes (in-phase and out-of-phase). Couple a long chain of N pendulums and the N modes pack densely into a continuous band of frequencies. Electrons in a crystal do exactly this — the "frequencies" are energies, and the band is the band structure.

9.2Building the Lattice Hamiltonian

Now line up N identical atoms in a 1D chain, labeled n = 0, 1, …, N−1, with spacing a. Each site has on-site energy ε, and an electron can hop only to its immediate neighbors with amplitude −t. In the basis {|n⟩} of one orbital per site, the Hamiltonian is tridiagonal: ε on the diagonal, −t on the two off-diagonals.

Written out, the matrix element between sites m and n is:

H(m,n) = eps if m = n;  -t if |m - n| = 1;  0 otherwiseA real symmetric tridiagonal matrix. Its size grows with N, but it stays sparse: only ~3N nonzero entries, not N-squared.

Because H is real and symmetric, its eigenvalues are real (they are physical energies) and its eigenvectors are orthogonal — exactly the structure we exploited in earlier weeks. The sparsity is what lets us push to enormous N: a million-site chain has a million eigenvalues but only ~3 million nonzeros, so sparse solvers (Week 4) handle it easily.

9.3The Dispersion Relation E(k)

An infinite periodic chain has translational symmetry, so its eigenstates are Bloch waves: amplitudes of the form ψn = e^(i k n a), where k is the wavevector and a the lattice spacing. Plug this trial state into the eigenvalue equation Hψ = Eψ at site n. The diagonal gives εψn; the two hoppings bring in the neighbors n−1 and n+1:

eps*psi_n - t*psi_(n-1) - t*psi_(n+1) = E*psi_nSubstitute psi_n = e^(i k n a). The two neighbor terms contribute -t(e^(-i k a) + e^(+i k a)) = -2t cos(k a).

The phase factors collapse using e^(ix) + e^(−ix) = 2cos x, and ψn cancels from both sides. What remains is the celebrated 1D tight-binding dispersion relation:

E(k) = eps - 2t cos(k a)One continuous band. As k runs over the Brillouin zone -pi/a < k ≤ pi/a, the energy sweeps a band of total width 4t.

Read it carefully. The discrete index n is gone — replaced by a continuous label k. The single atomic level ε has spread into a continuous band running from ε−2t (at k=0) up to ε+2t (at k=±π/a). The bandwidth is therefore 4t: stronger hopping (bigger t) means a wider band and more mobile electrons. Near the band bottom, expanding cos gives E ≈ (ε−2t) + t a² k² — a parabola, just like a free particle, which is why electrons in a crystal behave as if they have an effective mass.

9.4From Discrete Levels to Bands

A finite chain of N atoms does not give a continuum — it gives exactly N discrete eigenvalues. For open boundary conditions the standard tridiagonal result is:

E_m = eps - 2t cos( m*pi / (N+1) ),  m = 1, 2, ..., NN discrete levels, all lying on the same cosine curve E(k) with the allowed k-values quantized by the boundaries.

This is the punchline of the whole lecture. The N levels are samples of the very same cosine band. For N = 2 you recover the bonding/antibonding pair (ε±t). As N grows, the levels crowd together; for N in the thousands they are so dense — spacing ~4t/N — that they look continuous. A macroscopic crystal has N ~ 10²³, so the band is a true continuum for all practical purposes.

Whether the material conducts depends on how electrons fill these bands. If a band is only partly filled, electrons near the top can be nudged into nearby empty states by a field — a metal. If a band is completely full and the next empty band is far above (a band gap), no low-energy excitations exist and the material is an insulator or semiconductor. Tight-binding, extended to more orbitals per site, predicts these gaps directly.

Common mistakeDon't confuse the number of atoms N with the bandwidth 4t. Adding more atoms does not make the band wider — it puts more levels into the same 4t-wide window, so they pack denser. The bandwidth is set by the hopping t (how strongly neighbors couple), not by the system size. Likewise, in E(k) = ε − 2t cos(ka), the coefficient is 2t (two neighbors), so the full top-to-bottom span is 4t, not 2t.
✦ Worked Example — eigenvalues of a 4-site chain

A 4-atom chain (open ends) has on-site energy ε = 0 and hopping t = 1 (in eV). Find all four energy levels two ways and confirm they agree.

  1. Set up H: the 4×4 tridiagonal matrix has 0 on the diagonal and −1 on the off-diagonals.
  2. Use the analytic formula: E_m = −2t·cos(mπ/(N+1)) with N = 4, so the angles are mπ/5 for m = 1,2,3,4.
  3. m = 1: cos(36°) = 0.809 → E₁ = −2(0.809) = −1.618 eV.
  4. m = 2: cos(72°) = 0.309 → E₂ = −0.618 eV. m = 3: cos(108°) = −0.309 → E₃ = +0.618 eV. m = 4: cos(144°) = −0.809 → E₄ = +1.618 eV.
  5. Check: the spectrum {−1.618, −0.618, +0.618, +1.618} is symmetric about ε = 0, spans nearly the full 4t = 4 eV window, and the eigenvalues of the explicit matrix (next section) match to machine precision. The lowest two are "bonding-like," the highest two "antibonding-like."

🛠️ Build a chain and watch the band appear

The free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack runs everything here (one-time setup is in the Week 1/2 lectures — reuse that environment). We build the tridiagonal Hamiltonian as a sparse matrix, diagonalize it, and overlay the eigenvalues on the analytic cosine band.

# tight_binding_chain.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
import scipy.sparse as sp
import scipy.sparse.linalg as spla
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

N   = 200          # number of atoms
eps = 0.0          # on-site energy (eV)
t   = 1.0          # hopping integral (eV)

# tridiagonal H: eps on diagonal, -t on the two off-diagonals (sparse)
main = eps * np.ones(N)
off  = -t  * np.ones(N - 1)
H = sp.diags([off, main, off], offsets=[-1, 0, 1], format="csr")

# lowest-k eigenvalues with a sparse solver (Week 4 toolset)
vals = spla.eigsh(H, k=6, which="SA", return_eigenvectors=False)
print("lowest 6 energies (eV):", np.sort(vals))

# analytic comparison: E_m = eps - 2t cos(m*pi/(N+1))
m = np.arange(1, N + 1)
E_analytic = eps - 2 * t * np.cos(m * np.pi / (N + 1))

# plot the band: energy vs. level index lands on a cosine
plt.plot(m, np.sort(E_analytic), '.', label="E_m (analytic)")
plt.axhline(eps - 2*t, ls="--"); plt.axhline(eps + 2*t, ls="--")  # band edges
plt.xlabel("level index m"); plt.ylabel("energy (eV)")
plt.title(f"1D tight-binding band, N={N}, width 4t={4*t} eV")
plt.legend(); plt.show()

Try it: rerun with N = 6, 50, 500. The dots are always trapped in the same [−2t, +2t] window and trace the same cosine — they just pack denser as N grows, exactly the discrete-to-band crossover. Then change t and watch the whole band widen or narrow by 4t.

💬 Discussion

A single carbon atom has sharply defined electron energy levels. A diamond crystal — astronomically many of the same carbon atoms — is a transparent insulator with a large band gap, while graphite (also pure carbon) conducts. Using the tight-binding picture, explain how identical atoms can yield such different electronic behavior. In your answer, address: (1) what physically sets the bandwidth, (2) why "more atoms" does not simply mean "more energy," and (3) what extra ingredient beyond a single 1D chain you would need to predict a band gap.

Show a model response

The key realization is that electronic behavior is governed by hopping geometry, not by the number of atoms. (1) The bandwidth is set by the hopping integral t — how much an electron's orbital on one atom overlaps its neighbor's. In our 1D chain the band is 4t wide regardless of N; carbon's bonds in diamond vs. graphite have different geometries and orbital overlaps, so their effective t's and band shapes differ. (2) Adding atoms keeps the band within the same energy window (set by t) but packs more discrete levels into it — N atoms give N levels of spacing ~4t/N, which is why a macroscopic crystal has a continuum, not higher energies. The energy span is fixed by coupling strength, not system size. (3) A single one-orbital 1D chain produces just one band and therefore no gap. To get a gap you need either more than one orbital (or atom) per unit cell — e.g. a two-site basis with alternating hoppings, which splits the single band into two with a gap between them — or higher dimensionality. Diamond's gap arises from its multi-orbital, 3D bonding; graphite's conduction comes from bands that touch at special points so there is no gap. So "same atoms, different solids" reduces to "different lattices and orbital overlaps, hence different t's and band structures."

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (a) ties bandwidth to hopping t, not to N; (b) explains that more atoms means denser levels in a fixed window, not larger energies; (c) identifies that a gap requires more than one band — i.e. a multi-site/multi-orbital unit cell or higher dimensionality — rather than just a longer chain.

🔗 Where this is heading

You now hold the workhorse model of computational condensed-matter physics. Next week's capstone reuses this exact tridiagonal-matrix machinery to solve the full 1D Schrodinger equation, where on-site energies become a discretized potential and hopping becomes the kinetic-energy stencil — tight-binding and finite differences are the same matrix in disguise. Q1. In the tight-binding model of a 1D chain, the off-diagonal matrix element −t physically represents…
A. the energy of an electron sitting on a single isolated atom
B. the amplitude for an electron to hop between neighboring atoms
C. the total number of atoms in the chain
D. the lattice spacing a
Show answer

B. −t is the hopping integral — the amplitude for tunneling between adjacent sites. A is the on-site energy ε (the diagonal). C and D are unrelated structural quantities, not Hamiltonian matrix elements.

Q2. Substituting a Bloch wave ψn = e^(ikna) into the 1D chain gives the dispersion relation…
A. E(k) = ε − 2t cos(ka)
B. E(k) = ε + t k²
C. E(k) = −t / (ka)
D. E(k) = ε sin(ka)
Show answer

A. The two neighbor terms combine via e^(ika)+e^(−ika)=2cos(ka), giving E = ε − 2t cos(ka). B is only the small-k approximation near the band bottom; C and D are not solutions of the eigenvalue equation.

Q3. For the 1D tight-binding band, the total bandwidth (top minus bottom) is…
A. t
B. 2t
C. 4t
D. proportional to the number of atoms N
Show answer

C. cos(ka) ranges from −1 to +1, so −2t cos(ka) ranges over ±2t — a span of 4t. B (2t) forgets the factor of two from having two neighbors. D is the central misconception: more atoms add more levels in the same window, not a wider band.

Q4. A finite chain of N atoms (one orbital each) produces how many distinct energy eigenvalues?
A. always exactly 2, regardless of N
B. a true continuum of energies
C. N²
D. N discrete levels, sampled from the cosine band
Show answer

D. One orbital per site gives an N×N Hamiltonian with N eigenvalues, all lying on the E(k) cosine. A is only the N=2 case; B is the N→∞ limit, never exact for finite N; C confuses level count with matrix size (N×N has N eigenvalues).

Q5. Why is it efficient to store the tight-binding Hamiltonian as a sparse matrix for large N?
A. because it has only about 3N nonzero entries instead of N²
B. because sparse matrices are always more accurate
C. because it removes all round-off error
D. because the eigenvalues become exactly integers
Show answer

A. Tridiagonal H has ~3N nonzeros (diagonal plus two off-diagonals), so sparse storage and solvers scale far better than dense N². B and C are false — sparsity is about storage/speed, not accuracy or error removal; D is unrelated to the storage format.

✓ What you learned

  • The tight-binding model needs just two numbers: on-site energy ε and nearest-neighbor hopping t.
  • The 1D chain Hamiltonian is a real, symmetric, tridiagonal matrix — sparse with ~3N nonzeros.
  • A Bloch wave gives the dispersion relation E(k) = ε − 2t cos(ka), a single band of width 4t.
  • N atoms give N discrete levels sampled from that band; as N grows they merge into a continuous band.
  • Building H sparsely and diagonalizing it in NumPy/SciPy reproduces the analytic band exactly.
