Diagonalization & Normal Modes

Every coupled, tangled-up oscillating system secretly hides a set of simple, independent vibrations — diagonalization is the change of glasses that lets you see them.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State what diagonalization A = P D P⁻¹ means and why the eigenbasis is the "natural coordinate system" of a matrix.
  • Explain how a similarity transform decouples a system of coupled differential equations.
  • Set up the equations of motion for a mass-spring chain and write them as M ẍ = −K x.
  • Identify eigenfrequencies (ω) and mode shapes from the eigenpairs of M⁻¹K, and interpret them physically.
  • Use NumPy/SciPy to diagonalize a stiffness matrix, recover the normal modes, and animate or plot a mode shape.

3.1Diagonalization: Working in the Eigenbasis

In Lecture 2 you learned that an eigenvector v of a matrix A is a special direction that A merely stretches: Av = λv. A general matrix mixes coordinates together — multiplying by A rotates and shears vectors in complicated ways. Diagonalization is the discovery that, in the right coordinate system, that same matrix does nothing more interesting than scale each axis independently.

Suppose an n×n matrix A has n linearly independent eigenvectors v₁,…,vₙ with eigenvalues λ₁,…,λₙ. Stack the eigenvectors as the columns of a matrix P, and put the eigenvalues on the diagonal of D. Then:

A = P D P⁻¹,   with  P = [ v₁ v₂ … vₙ ],  D = diag(λ₁,…,λₙ)The columns of P are the eigenvectors; D holds the eigenvalues. Equivalently P⁻¹ A P = D.

Read this right-to-left as a recipe applied to a vector x. First P⁻¹ rewrites x in the eigenbasis (it answers "how much of each eigenvector is in x?"). Then D scales each of those components by its eigenvalue — no mixing. Finally P converts back to the original coordinates. The hard work of A is just n independent multiplications hiding behind a change of basis.

AnalogyThink of a matrix as a foreign-language instruction that says "do this complicated dance." Diagonalization is hiring a translator (P⁻¹) who rewrites the instruction in your language, where it turns out to read simply "make the first axis 3× longer and the second axis half as long." You do the easy thing (D), then the translator (P) hands the result back in the original language. The dance was never complicated — you just didn't have the right words for it.

The eigenbasis is powerful because functions of A become trivial there. Since A = P D P⁻¹, you get A² = P D² P⁻¹ and, in general, any power or matrix exponential acts only on the diagonal:

Aᵏ = P Dᵏ P⁻¹,   e^(At) = P · diag(e^(λ₁ t),…,e^(λₙ t)) · P⁻¹Hard matrix operations reduce to scalar operations on the eigenvalues. This is the engine behind solving linear ODE systems.

3.2Decoupling a Coupled System

Why do physicists care so much about diagonalization? Because nature is full of coupled systems — atoms tugging on neighbors, circuits sharing currents, populations interacting — and coupling is exactly what makes equations hard to solve. Diagonalization untangles them.

Consider a linear system of differential equations ẍ = −Ax, where x is a vector of positions and A couples them (the acceleration of coordinate 1 depends on coordinate 2, and so on). The equations are tangled because A is not diagonal. Now substitute x = Pq, defining new coordinates q = P⁻¹x aligned with the eigenvectors:

P q̈ = −A P q   ⟹   q̈ = −(P⁻¹ A P) q = −D qMultiply on the left by P⁻¹. The coupling matrix A becomes the diagonal D.

In the new coordinates the system falls apart into n independent one-dimensional problems, one per eigenvalue:

q̈ₖ = −λₖ qₖ   for each k = 1,…,nEach q-coordinate obeys a simple harmonic oscillator equation with its own frequency. No coordinate talks to any other.

If every λₖ > 0 the solution of each decoupled equation is a pure sinusoid qₖ(t) = aₖ cos(√λₖ · t + φₖ). Each independent coordinate qₖ is called a normal coordinate, and its oscillation is a normal mode. The original messy motion x(t) = Pq(t) is just a superposition of these clean modes — that is the whole trick.

3.3The Mass-Spring Chain

The canonical coupled system is a chain of masses connected by springs — a one-dimensional model of everything from a vibrating molecule to a crystal lattice to the discretized string in a PDE solver. Take three identical masses m connected by springs of stiffness k, with the two end masses also tied to fixed walls.

Newton's second law for each mass collects the spring forces from its neighbors. For the middle mass, springs pull it toward both sides; for the end masses, one spring connects to a wall. Writing m ẍᵢ = (net spring force) for all three gives a coupled linear system. In matrix form M ẍ = −K x, with the mass matrix M = m·I and the stiffness matrix K:

K = k · [ [ 2, -1, 0 ], [ -1, 2, -1 ], [ 0, -1, 2 ] ]Each diagonal "2" = two springs on that mass; each off-diagonal "-1" = the coupling to a neighbor. K is symmetric.

Because M = m·I here, M⁻¹K = (k/m)·(the bracketed matrix). The normal modes are the eigenvectors of M⁻¹K, and the eigenfrequencies come from its eigenvalues. The pattern "2 on the diagonal, −1 off-diagonal" is one of the most important matrices in computational physics — it is also exactly what you get when you discretize the second-derivative operator in a PDE.

Common mistakeDo not set up the eigenproblem on K alone when the masses differ. The correct equation of motion gives the generalized eigenproblem K v = ω² M v, i.e. you diagonalize M⁻¹K, not K. When M = m·I the distinction collapses to a factor of m, but for unequal masses solving "K v = λ v" gives the wrong frequencies and wrong mode shapes. Use scipy.linalg.eigh(K, M) for the generalized problem.

3.4Eigenfrequencies & Mode Shapes

Plug a single-frequency trial motion x(t) = v cos(ωt) into M ẍ = −K x. The ẍ brings down a factor −ω², and after cancelling cos(ωt) you get an eigenvalue problem:

K v = ω² M v   ⟹   (M⁻¹K) v = ω² vThe eigenvalues of M⁻¹K are the squared eigenfrequencies ω²; the eigenvectors v are the mode shapes.

Each eigenpair is one normal mode. The eigenfrequency ω = √λ is how fast that mode oscillates; the mode shape v is the fixed pattern of relative displacements — which masses move together, which move opposite, which stay still. In a normal mode every mass oscillates at the same frequency, in lockstep, scaled by the entries of v. Because K is symmetric, the modes are real and mutually orthogonal — a guarantee from the spectral theorem you met in Lecture 2.

The intuition: a mode where neighbors move against each other stretches the springs more for the same amplitude, so it is "stiffer" and oscillates faster. A mode where everyone moves together stretches the internal springs least, so it is the slowest. This monotonic "more wiggles = higher frequency" rule is the discrete cousin of the harmonics on a guitar string, and it is exactly why a crystal's vibrational spectrum (its phonons) looks the way it does.

✦ Worked Example — the three eigenfrequencies of the chain

Find the normal-mode frequencies of three equal masses m and four equal springs k (the figure above). Work with the matrix A = M⁻¹K = (k/m)·T, where T is the tridiagonal "2, −1" matrix.

  1. Characteristic equation: the eigenvalues μ of T = [[2,−1,0],[−1,2,−1],[0,−1,2]] satisfy det(T − μI) = 0, which expands to (2−μ)[(2−μ)² − 1] − (2−μ) = 0, i.e. (2−μ)[(2−μ)² − 2] = 0.
  2. Solve: either 2−μ = 0 → μ = 2, or (2−μ)² = 2 → 2−μ = ±√2 → μ = 2 ∓ √2. The three eigenvalues are μ = 2 − √2 ≈ 0.586, μ = 2, μ = 2 + √2 ≈ 3.414.
  3. Eigenfrequencies: ω = √(μ·k/m). So ω₁ = √(0.586 k/m), ω₂ = √(2 k/m), ω₃ = √(3.414 k/m). Their ratio is ω₁ : ω₂ : ω₃ ≈ 1 : 1.85 : 2.41.
  4. Mode shapes: for μ = 2 the eigenvector is (1, 0, −1) — the middle mass stays put. For μ = 2 − √2 it is (1, √2, 1) — all same sign, ends smaller than the middle. For μ = 2 + √2 it is (1, −√2, 1) — neighbors alternate sign.
  5. Sanity check: the sign-change count grows with frequency (0, 1, 2 sign changes), and the trace of T equals the sum of eigenvalues: 2+2+2 = 6 = (2−√2) + 2 + (2+√2). ✓ Both confirm the answer.

🛠️ Hands-On: diagonalize the chain and read off its modes

The free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack you installed in Week 1 (Course 1) is all you need — no new tools this week. We'll build the stiffness matrix, get its eigenpairs with numpy.linalg.eigh (the symmetric-matrix solver, which returns sorted eigenvalues and orthonormal eigenvectors), and confirm the frequencies and mode shapes from the worked example.

# normal_modes.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

n = 3
k, m = 1.0, 1.0

# Build the tridiagonal stiffness matrix K (2 on diag, -1 off-diag)
K = k * (2*np.eye(n) - np.eye(n, k=1) - np.eye(n, k=-1))
M = m * np.eye(n)

# Symmetric eigenproblem: (M^-1 K) v = omega^2 v.
# For M = m*I this is just eigh(K)/m; eigh sorts eigenvalues ascending.
eigvals, eigvecs = np.linalg.eigh(K / m)
omega = np.sqrt(eigvals)                 # eigenfrequencies

print("omega^2 =", np.round(eigvals, 4)) # [0.586  2.0  3.414]
print("omega   =", np.round(omega, 4))   # [0.765  1.414 1.848]
for i in range(n):
    print(f"mode {i}: shape =", np.round(eigvecs[:, i], 3))

# Verify orthogonality of mode shapes (spectral theorem):
print("V^T V =\n", np.round(eigvecs.T @ eigvecs, 6))   # identity

For a long chain or for unequal masses, use the generalized solver scipy.linalg.eigh(K, M) directly. And for a huge sparse chain where you only want the few softest modes, scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh(K, k=4, sigma=0, which='LM') finds them without forming the full dense matrix — a preview of the iterative methods in Week 4.

# Animate-ready: reconstruct motion x(t) = sum_i a_i * v_i * cos(omega_i t)
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
t = np.linspace(0, 20, 400)
a = np.array([1.0, 0.0, 0.0])            # excite only the lowest mode
x = np.array([sum(a[i]*eigvecs[:, i]*np.cos(omega[i]*ti) for i in range(n)) for ti in t])
plt.plot(t, x); plt.xlabel("t"); plt.ylabel("displacement"); plt.legend(["m1","m2","m3"])
plt.title("Pure mode 1: all masses move in lockstep"); plt.show()

🔗 Where this is heading

Diagonalization is the spine of the rest of this course and the whole specialization. Next week (Week 4) the power method shows how to extract a dominant eigenpair iteratively when the matrix is too big to diagonalize directly — the workhorse behind ranking algorithms and large sparse eigensolvers. In quantum mechanics (later in C3) the time-independent Schrödinger equation Ĥψ = Eψ is an eigenvalue problem: energy levels are eigenvalues, stationary states are eigenvectors, and "diagonalizing the Hamiltonian" is the central numerical task. The same "2, −1" tridiagonal matrix you built here reappears as the discretized kinetic-energy operator in the C4 PDE course, and the normal-mode decomposition is exactly how molecular-dynamics codes compute infrared and Raman spectra. Master the change of basis once and these later subjects become variations on a single theme.

💬 Discussion

A real guitar string, a vibrating drumhead, and a crystal lattice are all "many coupled oscillators," yet only some of them have a tidy ladder of harmonics (frequencies in ratios 1 : 2 : 3 : …) while others do not. Our three-mass chain gave frequency ratios of roughly 1 : 1.85 : 2.41 — not a clean harmonic ladder. Why does the discrete chain's spectrum bend away from the simple integer ratios of an ideal continuous string, and what would have to change for the two to agree? Argue from the mode shapes and the role of the number of masses.

Show a model response

An ideal continuous string supports infinitely many modes whose frequencies are exactly proportional to the number of half-wavelengths: ωₙ ∝ n, giving the perfect 1 : 2 : 3 ladder. The discrete chain is a sampling of that string with only n points, and that finite sampling introduces dispersion: the chain's exact frequencies follow ωₖ ∝ sin(kπ / 2(n+1)), not ωₖ ∝ k. For small k (long-wavelength, smooth modes) the sine is nearly linear in k, so the lowest modes do approach the integer ratios — but the highest modes, where neighbors slam against each other, "feel" the discreteness and their frequencies bunch up below the linear trend. That is exactly why our ratios 1 : 1.85 : 2.41 fall short of 1 : 2 : 3: the top modes are flattened by dispersion.

For the two spectra to agree we would need to take the continuum limit: let the number of masses n → ∞ while shrinking the spacing and scaling k and m so the chain models the same physical string. Then the few lowest modes — the only ones a real instrument excites strongly — converge to the harmonic ladder, and the discreteness only matters at wavelengths comparable to the atomic spacing. This is also the reason a finite-difference PDE solver (the same "2, −1" matrix) gets the low-frequency, smooth solution accurately but mis-estimates the sharpest, highest-frequency features unless the grid is refined.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) identifies that the continuous string has ωₙ ∝ n exactly; (2) explains the discrete chain's dispersion / sine relation and why high modes flatten; (3) connects the deviation to the finite number of masses; and (4) invokes the continuum limit (n → ∞) as the condition for agreement. Bonus: tie it to grid refinement in PDE solvers.

Q1. In the diagonalization A = P D P⁻¹, what are the columns of P and the diagonal entries of D?
A. Columns of P are the eigenvectors of A; diagonal of D holds the eigenvalues
B. Columns of P are the eigenvalues; D holds the eigenvectors
C. P is always the identity; D is A reordered
D. Both P and D contain the eigenvalues
Show answer

A. By construction P = [v₁ … vₙ] stacks eigenvectors as columns and D = diag(λ₁,…,λₙ). B and D confuse vectors with scalars; C is false in general (P = I only if A is already diagonal).

Q2. Substituting x = Pq into the coupled system ẍ = −Ax accomplishes what?
A. It makes the matrix larger so the problem is easier to see
B. It turns the coupled equations into n independent equations q̈ₖ = −λₖqₖ
C. It removes all the eigenvalues from the problem
D. It is only a relabeling and changes nothing about the dynamics' difficulty
Show answer

B. Left-multiplying by P⁻¹ gives q̈ = −(P⁻¹AP)q = −Dq, which decouples into one independent oscillator per eigenvalue. A is false (size is unchanged), C is false (eigenvalues become the frequencies), D misses the entire point — decoupling makes it vastly easier.

Q3. For the mass-spring chain, the normal-mode frequencies and shapes come from the eigenproblem…
A. Kv = ωv (eigenvalues are the frequencies themselves)
B. Mv = ω²Kv
C. Kv = ω²Mv, i.e. diagonalizing M⁻¹K (eigenvalues are ω²)
D. det(K) = 0
Show answer

C. Trial motion vcos(ωt) in M ẍ = −Kx gives Kv = ω²Mv; the eigenvalues of M⁻¹K are ω² (not ω, ruling out A). B swaps K and M. D (a single determinant being zero) just says K is singular, which is not the mode condition.

Q4. Physically, what distinguishes the highest-frequency normal mode of the three-mass chain?
A. All three masses move together in the same direction
B. Neighboring masses move in opposite directions (most sign changes), stretching the springs most
C. Only the middle mass moves
D. No masses move at all
Show answer

B. More sign changes across the chain means more spring stretch per unit amplitude, hence higher stiffness and frequency — the mode (1, −√2, 1). A describes the lowest mode; C describes the middle mode (1, 0, −1); D is not a mode (zero displacement is the equilibrium, not an oscillation).

Q5. Why are the mode shapes of this chain guaranteed to be real and mutually orthogonal?
A. Because the chain has an odd number of masses
B. Because all the springs are identical
C. It is a numerical coincidence with no deeper reason
D. Because the stiffness matrix K is real symmetric, so the spectral theorem applies
Show answer

D. A real symmetric matrix has real eigenvalues and an orthogonal eigenbasis (the spectral theorem from Lecture 2). A and B are irrelevant — orthogonality holds for any number of masses and any positive spring constants, as long as K is symmetric. C is wrong: it is a theorem, not luck.

  • The mass-spring chain obeys M ẍ = −Kx with the tridiagonal "2, −1" stiffness matrix K.
