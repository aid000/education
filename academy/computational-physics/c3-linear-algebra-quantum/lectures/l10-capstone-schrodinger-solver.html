Capstone: A 1D Schrödinger Solver

Everything in this course — matrices, eigenvalues, diagonalization, sparsity — collapses into one elegant program that finds the energy levels and wavefunctions of any 1D quantum system you can draw.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Turn the time-independent Schrödinger equation into a matrix eigenvalue problem with a finite-difference Laplacian.
  • Build and diagonalize the Hamiltonian matrix for any 1D potential V(x) using NumPy/SciPy.
  • Reproduce the textbook square-well and harmonic-oscillator spectra and verify the equally spaced ladder E_n = (n + ½)ħω.
  • Model a symmetric double well and measure the tunneling splitting between the lowest two states.
  • Frame and ship the capstone deliverable: a validated solver, three test cases, and a written error analysis.

10.1The Schrödinger Equation Is an Eigenvalue Problem

The time-independent Schrödinger equation in one dimension is, at heart, the same statement we met in Lecture 2: a linear operator acting on a function returns that same function scaled by a number. The function is the stationary wavefunction ψ(x), the operator is the Hamiltonian Ĥ, and the scale factor is the energy E:

Ĥ ψ(x) = E ψ(x),   Ĥ = −(ħ²/2m) d²/dx² + V(x)Kinetic term (the second derivative) plus a potential term. ψ is an eigenfunction; E is its eigenvalue.

The whole strategy of computational quantum mechanics is to discretize this continuous operator onto a grid so that Ĥ becomes a finite matrix. Once it is a matrix, "find the allowed energies and states" is exactly the eigenvalue problem we have solved all course: the eigenvalues are the bound-state energies, and the eigenvectors are the wavefunctions sampled at the grid points.

Choose a grid of N points xj spaced by h on the interval [a, b]. The kinetic operator needs the second derivative, which we approximate with the second-order central finite difference from Course 1:

d²ψ/dx² |j ≈ (ψj+1 − 2ψj + ψj−1) / h²Couples each grid point to its two neighbours — a tridiagonal stencil.
AnalogyThink of the wavefunction as the shape of a vibrating guitar string, and the grid points as beads strung along it. Each bead is pulled by its two neighbours (the kinetic coupling) and sits in a landscape of hills and valleys (the potential V). The "natural shapes" of this beaded string — its standing-wave modes and their frequencies — are precisely the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of the Hamiltonian matrix. We literally reused the normal-modes machinery from Lecture 3; only the labels changed.

10.2Assembling the Hamiltonian Matrix

The finite-difference stencil makes the kinetic operator a tridiagonal matrix: −2 on the diagonal, +1 on each off-diagonal, scaled by −ħ²/(2m h²). The potential is even simpler — it is diagonal, because V(x) just multiplies ψ(x) point by point. Adding them gives the full Hamiltonian:

Hjj = ħ²/(m h²) + V(xj),   Hj,j±1 = −ħ²/(2m h²)Diagonal = kinetic "+2" term plus the local potential; off-diagonals = the "−1" neighbour coupling.

Because each row touches only three columns, H is sparse — exactly the structure Lecture 6 prepared us to exploit. For a clear derivation we will work in natural units ħ = m = 1, so the prefactors reduce to 1/h² and the matrix is purely geometric. The figure shows the resulting band structure of the matrix.

Common mistakeForgetting to divide the kinetic stencil by h², or mixing up which factor carries the units. A frequent symptom is energies that scale wrongly when you change the grid spacing. Also: the boundary rows have no neighbour on one side, which silently imposes ψ = 0 at the walls (Dirichlet boundaries). That is fine for a box, but if your domain is too narrow the wavefunction gets squeezed and every energy comes out too high — always check that ψ has decayed to ~0 before the edges.

10.3Three Test Cases — and Their Analytic Answers

A capstone is only as good as its validation. We choose three potentials whose exact spectra are known, so every computed number has a target to be checked against.

Infinite square well

A particle confined to a box of width L (V = 0 inside, infinite outside) has the classic ladder of energies that grow as n²:

En = n² π² ħ² / (2 m L²),   n = 1, 2, 3, ...In natural units with L = 1: E_n = n² pi²/2 ≈ 4.93, 19.7, 44.4, ...

Harmonic oscillator

For V(x) = ½ m ω² x², the levels are equally spaced — the single most-tested fact in quantum mechanics, and the basis of phonons, photons, and quantized fields:

En = (n + ½) ħω,   n = 0, 1, 2, ...With ħ = m = ω = 1 the levels are exactly 0.5, 1.5, 2.5, 3.5, ... — a constant gap of ħω = 1.

Symmetric double well

Two wells separated by a barrier, e.g. V(x) = λ(x² − a²)². Classically a particle sits in one well forever; quantum mechanically it can tunnel through the barrier. The two lowest states are a symmetric and an antisymmetric combination, split by a tiny tunneling gap ΔE that shrinks exponentially as the barrier grows:

ΔE = E1 − E0 ∝ e−S/ħS is the action under the barrier. A taller/wider barrier means a smaller splitting and slower tunneling between wells.

10.4Framing the Deliverable

This is the payoff week: your project is due now. The deliverable is a single, well-documented solver plus evidence that it works. A strong submission has four parts:

  • The solver. A function solve(V, x) that takes any potential array on a grid and returns the lowest k energies and wavefunctions. Reuse the sparse eigensolver from Lecture 6 (scipy.sparse.linalg.eigsh) so it scales to large grids.
  • Three validated test cases. Square well, harmonic oscillator, double well — each with a plot of V(x), the lowest few levels drawn as horizontal lines, and the wavefunctions. Tabulate computed vs analytic energies.
  • A convergence study. Show the energy error falling like h² (second-order) as you refine the grid — the Course 1 error budget, applied to your own code.
  • A short write-up. One page: the method, the three results, the tunneling splitting you measured, and the one thing that surprised you.

The grader is checking physics fidelity: does your harmonic ladder come out equally spaced? Do your square-well energies follow n²? Does the double-well splitting shrink when you raise the barrier? Getting those three qualitative signatures right is worth more than any single decimal place.

AnalogyShipping the capstone is like a flight test, not a thought experiment. You do not just claim the wing will fly — you instrument it, fly it against three known conditions, and plot how the error behaves. "It ran without crashing" is not validation; "it reproduced the analytic spectrum to 0.1% and converged as h²" is.
✦ Worked Example — harmonic-oscillator ground state by hand-checking the grid

In natural units (ħ = m = ω = 1) take V(x) = ½ x² on the domain [−6, 6] with N = 600 interior points. Predict the lowest two energies and confirm the gap, then estimate the grid error.

  1. Grid spacing: domain length 12 over N + 1 = 601 intervals gives h = 12 / 601 ≈ 0.0200.
  2. Kinetic prefactor: 1/h² ≈ 2500, so the diagonal kinetic term is 1/h² ≈ 2500 and the off-diagonals are −1/(2h²) ≈ −1250. These dwarf the potential at small x, as they should (kinetic energy dominates fine structure).
  3. Analytic targets: En = (n + ½), so E0 = 0.5 and E1 = 1.5; the level spacing is exactly ħω = 1.0.
  4. What the solver returns: diagonalizing H gives E0 ≈ 0.49999, E1 ≈ 1.49998 — both within ~10⁻⁵ of the exact values; the gap is 1.00000 to five digits.
  5. Error scaling: the finite-difference Laplacian is second-order, so halving h (doubling N) should cut the energy error by ~4. Going from N = 600 to N = 1200 takes the E0 error from ~10⁻⁵ to ~2.5×10⁻⁶ — a factor of 4, confirming p = 2.
  6. Takeaway: the equally spaced ladder (gap = 1) is the signature to look for; if your gaps drift, your domain is too small or your h too coarse.

🛠️ Hands-On: the complete capstone solver

This is the whole engine in ~30 lines, using the free Python + NumPy/SciPy/Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (see the install details in Lecture 1 if you need to recreate the environment). It assembles a sparse Hamiltonian, diagonalizes it with eigsh, and runs all three test cases.

# schrodinger1d.py — Capstone, C3 Lecture 10  (natural units hbar = m = 1)
import numpy as np
from scipy.sparse import diags
from scipy.sparse.linalg import eigsh

def solve(V, x, k=5):
    # V = potential sampled on grid x (uniform spacing); returns lowest k (E, psi)
    h = x[1] - x[0]
    N = len(x)
    main = 1.0 / h**2 + V                 # diagonal: -1/2 * (-2)/h^2 = 1/h^2, plus V
    off  = -0.5 / h**2 * np.ones(N - 1)   # neighbour coupling -1/(2h^2)
    H = diags([off, main, off], [-1, 0, 1])
    E, psi = eigsh(H, k=k, which='SM')    # k smallest eigenvalues (bound states)
    order = np.argsort(E)
    return E[order], psi[:, order]

# --- Test 1: infinite square well, width L = 1  ->  E_n = n^2 pi^2 / 2 ---
x = np.linspace(0, 1, 1002)[1:-1]         # interior points, psi = 0 at walls
E, _ = solve(np.zeros_like(x), x, k=4)
print("well   :", np.round(E, 2), " exact:", [round(n**2*np.pi**2/2,2) for n in (1,2,3,4)])

# --- Test 2: harmonic oscillator V = x^2/2  ->  E_n = n + 1/2 ---
x = np.linspace(-6, 6, 602)[1:-1]
E, _ = solve(0.5 * x**2, x, k=4)
print("SHO    :", np.round(E, 4), " gaps:", np.round(np.diff(E), 4))   # gaps ~ 1.0

# --- Test 3: double well V = 0.5*(x^2 - 4)^2  ->  measure tunneling split ---
x = np.linspace(-5, 5, 1002)[1:-1]
E, _ = solve(0.5 * (x**2 - 4.0)**2, x, k=4)
print("double :", np.round(E, 5), " split dE =", round(E[1] - E[0], 6))

Now experiment: raise the double-well barrier by changing 4.0 to 9.0 (deeper, wider wells) and watch dE collapse by orders of magnitude — direct numerical evidence of exponential tunneling suppression. Then add a Matplotlib panel plotting V(x) with each En as a horizontal line and the wavefunctions stacked on top; that figure is the centrepiece of your write-up.

Q1. After discretizing the 1D Schrödinger equation on a grid, finding the bound-state energies and wavefunctions becomes…
A. a matrix eigenvalue problem: eigenvalues are energies, eigenvectors are wavefunctions
B. a root-finding problem on a single scalar function
C. a Monte-Carlo integration over phase space
D. a least-squares curve fit to the potential
Show answer

A. The discrete Hamiltonian is a matrix; Ĥψ = Eψ is literally an eigenproblem. B/C/D describe unrelated numerical tasks — root finding solves f(x)=0, Monte-Carlo samples randomly, and fitting minimizes residuals; none yield quantized energy levels here.

Q2. Why is the discrete Hamiltonian for a 1D problem tridiagonal (and thus sparse)?
A. because the potential V(x) couples every point to every other point
B. because the second-derivative stencil couples each grid point only to its two nearest neighbours, and V is diagonal
C. because we throw away small matrix entries to save memory
D. because eigsh requires tridiagonal input
Show answer

B. The central second difference touches j−1, j, j+1, and V(x) is a pure diagonal (point-by-point) operator. A is backwards (V is local, not all-to-all); C describes lossy thresholding, which we do not do; D is false — eigsh accepts any sparse symmetric matrix.

Q3. The defining signature of the quantum harmonic oscillator spectrum is that the energy levels are…
A. spaced proportionally to n²
B. all degenerate (equal)
C. equally spaced, E_n = (n + ½)ħω, with a constant gap ħω
D. spaced like 1/n²
Show answer

C. The oscillator gives a uniform ladder with gap ħω. A is the square-well pattern (E_n ∝ n²); D is the hydrogen-atom pattern; B would mean no spectrum at all.

Q4. In a symmetric double well, what happens to the tunneling splitting ΔE between the lowest two states as you make the central barrier taller and wider?
A. it grows linearly with barrier height
B. it stays constant — barriers don't affect splittings
C. it oscillates with no clear trend
D. it shrinks exponentially, since ΔE ∝ e^(−S/ħ)
Show answer

D. A higher/wider barrier increases the action S under it, so the splitting falls off exponentially — tunneling between wells becomes exponentially slower. A, B, and C all contradict the e^(−S/ħ) dependence you can measure directly in the solver.

Q5. Your harmonic-oscillator energies come out systematically too high and the level gaps drift away from ħω. The most likely cause is…
A. the computational domain is too narrow, so the wavefunction is squeezed against the Dirichlet walls (and/or h is too coarse)
B. floating-point round-off in a single subtraction
C. using a sparse matrix instead of a dense one
D. requesting too few eigenvalues from eigsh
Show answer

A. If ψ hasn't decayed to ~0 before the box edges, confinement raises every energy and distorts the gaps; a coarse grid adds h² truncation error on top. B is far too small to explain a systematic shift; C doesn't change the eigenvalues (only how they're computed); D affects how many states you get, not their accuracy.
