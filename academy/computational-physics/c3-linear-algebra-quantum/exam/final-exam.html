Vectors, Matrices & Eigenvalue Basics · Weeks 1–2
1. The columns of a matrix that represents a linear transformation are…
Answer: A. A linear map is fixed entirely by what it does to the basis; stacking the images of e_x, e_y, … as columns builds the matrix. Eigenvalues are scalars, not columns; orthogonal columns describe only special matrices like rotations.
2. A 2×2 rotation matrix R(θ) applied to a vector v preserves…
Answer: C. Rotations are orthogonal transformations: they preserve all lengths and the angles (and hence inner products) between vectors — the components themselves generally change.
3. For a Hermitian (or real symmetric) matrix A = A†, the spectral theorem guarantees…
Answer: B. Hermiticity forces a real spectrum (needed since measured physical quantities are real) and an orthogonal eigenbasis for distinct eigenvalues — the backbone of why physics represents observables this way.
4. For an n×n matrix A, the eigenvalues are the roots of the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0. As a sanity check, the eigenvalues must…
Answer: D. These two cheap checks — eigenvalues summing to the trace and multiplying to the determinant — fall straight out of expanding the characteristic polynomial and catch most hand-computation errors.
Diagonalization, Normal Modes & the Power Method · Weeks 3–4
5. In the diagonalization A = PDP⁻¹…
Answer: B. By construction P = [v₁ … vₙ] stacks the eigenvectors as columns and D = diag(λ₁,…,λₙ). P equals the identity only in the special case A is already diagonal — it is not a general requirement.
6. For a mass-spring chain M ẍ = −K x with unequal masses, the normal-mode frequencies and shapes come from…
Answer: A. Trial motion v·cos(ωt) in M ẍ = −K x gives K v = ω²M v; solving the eigenproblem on K alone gives the wrong frequencies whenever the masses differ.
7. Substituting x = P q into a coupled system ẍ = −A x, where P's columns are A's eigenvectors, accomplishes what?
Answer: D. Left-multiplying by P⁻¹ gives q̈ = −Dq, so each normal coordinate qₖ obeys its own independent q̈ₖ = −λₖqₖ — the entire point of diagonalizing before solving.
8. Power iteration (repeatedly multiplying a vector by A and renormalizing) converges to…
Answer: C. Every eigencomponent except the dominant one decays like (λᵢ/λ₁)ᵏ, so the iterate aligns with the dominant eigenvector; the ratio of the two largest eigenvalues sets how fast.
9. To target the eigenvalue closest to some value μ (not necessarily the dominant one), the standard technique is…
Answer: A. This is inverse iteration with a shift: (A − μI)⁻¹ amplifies 1/(λᵢ − μ), largest for whichever λᵢ is nearest μ, and you solve a linear system each step rather than forming the inverse.
Singular Value Decomposition · Week 5
10. In the SVD A = UΣVᵗ, the diagonal entries of Σ (the singular values) are…
Answer: D. Singular values are lengths — the semi-axes of the image ellipsoid — so they are always real and ≥ 0, equal to √λᵢ(AᵗA), never A's own (possibly negative/complex) eigenvalues.
11. By the Eckart–Young theorem, truncating the SVD after k terms to form the best rank-k approximation A_k leaves a spectral-norm error ‖A − A_k‖₂ equal to…
Answer: C. Truncating after k terms leaves the dropped rank-one pieces; the largest of those, σ_(k+1)u_(k+1)v_(k+1)ᵗ, sets the 2-norm error exactly.
12. A 50×50 matrix has exactly 12 nonzero singular values. Its rank is…
Answer: B. The rank of a matrix equals the number of nonzero singular values — the SVD reveals rank exactly, which is why it is the numerically trustworthy way to compute it.
Sparse Matrices & Krylov Methods · Weeks 6–7
13. Matrices arising from discretized physical operators (Laplacians, lattice Hamiltonians, stiffness matrices) are sparse mainly because…
Answer: D. Locality — a site coupling only to its neighbors — makes most off-diagonal entries structurally zero, not a round-off artifact; the matrix is sparse but not generally diagonal (a tridiagonal Laplacian still has off-diagonal bands).
14. In Compressed Sparse Row (CSR) format, the array indptr stores…
Answer: A. indptr has length N+1; row i's nonzeros are data[indptr[i]:indptr[i+1]]. The values live in data (D), the column indices in indices (B), and explicit row/column pairs describe COO, not CSR (C).
15. For a sparse matrix with nnz nonzero entries, one matrix–vector product A x costs about…
Answer: B. SpMV touches each nonzero once with a multiply-add, costing ~2·nnz — the single cheap operation every iterative solver and eigensolver is built around.
16. The m-th Krylov subspace K_m(A, v) is defined as the span of…
Answer: C. A Krylov subspace is built purely from repeated matrix–vector products on a starting vector — it is not the eigenvectors themselves, which are what the subspace approximates.
17. For a symmetric matrix, the Lanczos algorithm projects A onto its Krylov subspace, producing…
Answer: C. The three-term recurrence that symmetry allows makes T_m exactly tridiagonal; diagonalizing the tiny T_m is cheap, and its outer eigenvalues (Ritz values) converge to A's extremal eigenvalues long before m reaches N.
18. You need eigenvalues of a large sparse Hamiltonian near an interior target energy σ (deep inside the spectrum). The standard remedy with ARPACK/eigsh is…
Answer: B. Shift-invert maps the eigenvalues closest to σ onto the largest-magnitude eigenvalues of (A − σI)⁻¹, which Lanczos finds quickly — brute-forcing a huge k wastes enormous work, and dense solves defeat the purpose at this scale.
The Schrödinger Equation & Tight-Binding · Weeks 8–9
19. After discretizing the 1D time-independent Schrödinger equation on a grid, the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of the resulting Hamiltonian matrix represent…
Answer: A. Hψ = Eψ is literally an eigenvalue problem: each eigenvalue is an allowed energy level, and its matching eigenvector is ψ sampled on the grid.
20. The finite-difference kinetic-energy matrix for the 1D Schrödinger equation is tridiagonal…
Answer: D. The stencil (ψ_(j-1) − 2ψ_j + ψ_(j+1))/h² touches only j and j±1, filling exactly three bands; V(x) is a multiplication operator and so enters only on the diagonal.
21. For the infinite square well of width L, the exact energy levels scale with the quantum number n as…
Answer: B. E_n = n²π²ℏ²/(2mL²), so the levels go 1, 4, 9, 16, … — the classic benchmark used to validate any finite-difference Schrödinger solver.
22. Substituting a Bloch wave into the 1D tight-binding chain (on-site energy ε, hopping t) gives the dispersion relation…
Answer: D. The two neighbor-hopping terms combine via e^(ika)+e^(−ika) = 2cos(ka), giving E(k) = ε − 2t·cos(ka); as k sweeps the Brillouin zone the energy spans a band of width 4t.
23. Adding more atoms to a 1D tight-binding chain (same ε, same hopping t) mainly does what to its spectrum?
Answer: A. The bandwidth is set by the hopping t, not by the number of atoms; N atoms give N discrete levels E_m = ε − 2t·cos(mπ/(N+1)), sampling the same band more densely as N grows.
Capstone: The 1D Schrödinger Solver · Week 10
24. The defining spectral signature your capstone solver should reproduce for the quantum harmonic oscillator is…
Answer: C. The oscillator's signature is a uniform ladder with gap ħω; option D is the square-well pattern, not the oscillator's, and the other two describe no real bound-state spectrum here.
25. As you raise and widen the barrier of a symmetric double well, the tunneling splitting ΔE between the two lowest states…
Answer: A. A taller/wider barrier increases the action S under it, so the tunneling splitting falls off exponentially — directly measurable by raising the barrier parameter in the capstone solver and watching ΔE collapse.
