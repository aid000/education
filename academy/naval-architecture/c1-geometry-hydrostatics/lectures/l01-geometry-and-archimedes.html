Hull Geometry, Offsets & Archimedes

Before you can analyze a ship you have to describe its shape with numbers — then Archimedes tells you whether it floats.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Read a lines plan and an offset table, and define stations, waterlines, and half-breadths.
  • Name the principal dimensions of a hull (L, B, T, D).
  • State Archimedes' principle and relate buoyancy to displacement.
  • Compute displacement from underwater volume and the block coefficient Cb.
  • Integrate an offset table in Python to get a waterplane area.

1.1Describing a Hull: Lines & Offsets

A ship's hull is a smooth 3-D surface, but we work with it as a table of numbers. The hull is sliced three ways: stations (cross-sections along the length), waterlines (horizontal slices at various heights), and buttocks (vertical fore-aft slices). Drawn together these are the lines plan.

The numerical version is the offset table: at each station and waterline it records the half-breadth — the horizontal distance from the centerline out to the hull. (We use half-breadths because a ship is symmetric about its centerplane; you compute one side and double it.)

The principal dimensions are the headline numbers: length L (between perpendiculars, LBP, or overall, LOA), beam B (max width), draft T (keel to waterline), and depth D (keel to deck).

1.2Archimedes & Displacement

Why does a steel ship float? Archimedes' principle: a submerged body is pushed up by a buoyant force equal to the weight of the fluid it displaces. A floating ship sinks until the weight of water pushed aside exactly equals its own weight:

Buoyant force = ρ · g · ∇    and at flotation    W = ρ · g · ∇ρ = water density, g = gravity, ∇ = volume of the underwater hull (displaced volume).

The mass of water displaced is the ship's displacement Δ — what the ship actually "weighs" in the water:

Δ = ρ · ∇   [seawater ρ ≈ 1025 kg/m³]Displacement is a mass (often quoted in tonnes), set entirely by the underwater volume ∇.
AnalogyPicture the hull "digging a hole" in the sea. Fill that hole back up with water and weigh it — that weight is the buoyant force. The ship floats at exactly the draft where the hole it digs holds a weight of water equal to the ship's own weight. Load cargo and the ship digs a deeper hole (more draft) until balance is restored.
Common mistakeDisplacement (the ship's total weight afloat) is not the same as deadweight (the cargo + fuel + stores it can carry) or lightship (the empty hull). Displacement = lightship + deadweight. Mixing these up is a classic error — and they're each quoted in tonnes, which makes it easy to do.

1.3Form Coefficients: the Block Coefficient

How "full" or "fine" is a hull? Compare its underwater volume to the smallest box that would enclose it — a block of length L, beam B, and draft T. The ratio is the block coefficient:

Cb = ∇ / (L · B · T)Underwater hull volume ÷ circumscribing box. Always between 0 and 1.

A bulk carrier or tanker is "full" (Cb ≈ 0.80 — box-like, lots of cargo). A fast container ship or warship is "fine" (Cb ≈ 0.50 — slender, less resistance). The block coefficient is the single most telling shape number, and the first of several form coefficients we develop in Week 4.

✦ Worked Example — displacement from the block coefficient

A cargo ship has L = 120 m, B = 18 m, T = 7 m, and block coefficient Cb = 0.65, floating in seawater (ρ = 1025 kg/m³). Find its underwater volume and displacement.

  1. Circumscribing box volume: L·B·T = 120 × 18 × 7 = 15,120 m³.
  2. Underwater volume: ∇ = Cb · (L·B·T) = 0.65 × 15,120 = 9,828 m³.
  3. Displacement: Δ = ρ·∇ = 1025 × 9,828 ≈ 1.007 × 10⁷ kg = ≈ 10,074 tonnes.
  4. Sanity check: Cb = 0.65 is typical for a general cargo ship — full enough to carry well, fine enough to move at moderate speed. The hull "weighs" about ten thousand tonnes afloat.

🛠️ Hands-On: a waterplane area from offsets

We use the free Python + NumPy/SciPy stack all course (with NavalToolbox/PyNAT later for cross-checks). Week 1 setup: install once (below), then reuse it every lecture and for the project.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy/SciPy (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the scientific stack:

python3 -m venv naval-env
source naval-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux
naval-env\Scripts\activate         # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import scipy; print(scipy.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. (We add NavalToolbox/PyNAT from GitHub in a later week to check our results.)

The waterplane is the shape of the hull sliced at the waterline. Given half-breadths at evenly spaced stations, its area is twice the integral of the half-breadth curve along the length. SciPy's simpson applies Simpson's rule (our Week-2 workhorse) — here's a preview:

# waterplane.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import simpson      # older SciPy: 'simps'

# half-breadths (m) at 7 evenly spaced stations over L = 120 m
half_breadths = np.array([0.5, 6.0, 8.5, 9.0, 8.5, 6.0, 1.0])
L = 120.0
h = L / (len(half_breadths) - 1)         # station spacing = 20 m

area_half = simpson(half_breadths, dx=h) # integrate the half-breadth curve
A_wp = 2 * area_half                      # both sides of the hull
print("waterplane area:", round(A_wp, 1), "m^2")     # ~1593 m^2

B = 18.0
Cwp = A_wp / (L * B)                       # waterplane coefficient
print("waterplane coeff Cwp:", round(Cwp, 3))        # ~0.74

You just integrated a real hull curve. Next week we'll see why Simpson's rule (not the trapezoidal rule) is the naval architect's default, and extend this to sectional areas, volumes, and centroids.

🔗 Where this is heading

Everything in hydrostatics is an integral of the offset table. Integrate half-breadths along the length and you get the waterplane area (today); integrate section areas and you get displacement and the centre of buoyancy (Weeks 5–7); take the second moment of the waterplane and you get the metacentric radius BM that decides whether the ship is stable (Week 8, and all of Course 2). Resistance and powering in Course 4 then build directly on the form coefficients you met here. Master the offset table and the integration rules, and the rest of naval architecture is bookkeeping done well.

Q1. In an offset table, a "half-breadth" is…
A. half the draft of the ship
B. the distance from the centerline to the hull at a given station and waterline
C. half the length between perpendiculars
D. the depth from keel to deck
Show answer

B. Half-breadths are measured from the centerline out to the hull surface. Because the hull is symmetric, we tabulate one side and double it.

Q2. Archimedes' principle says the buoyant force on a floating ship equals…
A. the weight of the ship's steel only
B. the weight of the water displaced by the underwater hull
C. the volume of the whole hull times gravity
D. atmospheric pressure times the deck area
Show answer

B. Buoyant force = ρg∇, the weight of displaced water. At flotation it balances the ship's total weight.

Q3. A hull has underwater volume ∇ = 9,000 m³ in seawater (ρ = 1025 kg/m³). Its displacement is about…
A. 9,000 tonnes
B. 8,780 tonnes
C. 9,225 tonnes
D. 1,025 tonnes
Show answer

C. Δ = ρ∇ = 1025 × 9,000 = 9,225,000 kg = 9,225 tonnes. (In fresh water, ρ = 1000, it would be exactly 9,000 t — the same hull floats slightly deeper in fresh water.)

Q4. The block coefficient Cb = ∇ / (L·B·T) of a full, box-like tanker compared with a fine, fast warship is…
A. higher (closer to 0.8)
B. lower (closer to 0.4)
C. exactly 1.0
D. the same — Cb doesn't depend on hull form
Show answer

A. A fuller hull fills more of its L×B×T box, so Cb is higher (≈0.80 for tankers). Fine, fast hulls are slender and run lower (≈0.50).

Q5. Why does a naval architect compute a waterplane area by integrating half-breadths and doubling, rather than measuring directly?
A. Because the hull is symmetric, so one side is tabulated and the result doubled
B. Because half-breadths are easier to invent than to measure
C. Because the waterplane is not a real area
D. Because integration is only an approximation and direct measurement is impossible
Show answer

A. The hull is symmetric about the centerline, so the offset table gives half-breadths for one side; the integral of that curve is the half-area, doubled to get the full waterplane area.
