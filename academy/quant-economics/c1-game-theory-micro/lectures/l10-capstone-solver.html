10.1From Ten Lectures to One Tool

For nine weeks you solved games by hand and checked each with a few lines of code. The capstone reverses the relationship: you now write the tool that does the solving, and the by-hand work becomes your test oracle. A good equilibrium solver is just a clean pipeline — take a game, normalize it, dispatch to the right algorithm, and return labeled results.

solve( game ) -> { pure_NE, mixed_NE, dominated, value }The capstone API: one entry point that accepts a normal-form (or oligopoly) game and returns every equilibrium plus the strategies that never survive.

Notice that everything from the course slots into one of those outputs. Dominance (Week 2) prunes strategies before you search. Pure Nash (Week 1) is the cell-by-cell best-response check. Mixed Nash (Week 3) is what you compute when no pure equilibrium exists, via support enumeration. Sequential games (Weeks 5–6) need backward induction on a tree rather than a matrix. And oligopoly (Week 7) is a continuous game whose equilibrium you find by iterating best responses instead of enumerating cells.

The deliverable is therefore not one clever function but a small, tested module. The grading rubric below rewards the same discipline a research or trading group would: correct math, readable code, and a report that interprets the numbers.

10.2The Solver Core: nashpy Behind a Clean API

For finite normal-form games, nashpy already implements the hard algorithms — support enumeration, vertex enumeration, and the Lemke–Howson pivoting method. Your job is not to reinvent them but to wrap them so the output is trustworthy and self-documenting. The two pieces you add are pre-processing (eliminate dominated strategies first, which shrinks the search) and post-processing (label each equilibrium and tag whether it is pure or mixed).

A mixed strategy is just a probability vector over a player's pure strategies. The defining condition for a mixed Nash equilibrium is the indifference principle: every pure strategy played with positive probability must earn the same expected payoff against the opponent's mix.

u_i(s, sigma_-i) = u_i(s', sigma_-i) for all s, s' in support(sigma_i)Indifference principle: a player only mixes among strategies that are equally good given the opponent's mixture — the equation your solver implicitly satisfies.

Support enumeration solves this directly: for each candidate pair of supports, it sets up the indifference equations as a linear system and checks the solution is a valid probability distribution. That is why it returns all equilibria, including mixed ones, while a naive cell-scan finds only pure equilibria.

AnalogyThink of your solver like a calculator with a clear display, not a black box. A bare nashpy call is like a slide rule — it gives the number but you must remember what it means. Your wrapper is the labeled scientific calculator: it returns "(Defect, Defect), pure, payoff (1,1)" so a reader six months later — or a grader today — knows exactly what each array stands for without re-deriving it.

10.3The Oligopoly Sim: Best-Response Dynamics in NumPy

Oligopoly is a continuous game — firms choose quantities on the real line, so there are infinitely many "cells" and enumeration is impossible. Instead we exploit the structure of the Cournot model, where each firm picks output to maximize profit given rivals' outputs. With linear inverse demand and constant marginal cost,

P(Q) = a - b * Q , Q = q1 + q2 , profit_i = (P - c) * q_iSymmetric Cournot duopoly: price falls linearly in total quantity Q; each firm's profit is its markup times its own output.

Maximizing firm 1's profit over q1 (take the derivative, set it to zero) yields the best-response function — the optimal output as a function of the rival's output:

BR1(q2) = (a - c - b * q2) / (2 * b)Firm 1's profit-maximizing reply to firm 2's quantity. Symmetric for firm 2 with q1 in place of q2.

The simulation simply iterates this: start anywhere, let each firm jump to its best response, and watch the quantities converge to the fixed point where q1 = BR1(q2) and q2 = BR2(q1) simultaneously. That fixed point is the Nash (Cournot) equilibrium. The symmetric solution is q* = (a - c) / (3b) per firm.

Common mistakeDo not assume best-response iteration always converges. It converges for the standard linear Cournot game because the best-response slopes are shallow (a contraction mapping), but in games with steep or non-monotone reactions the iterates can oscillate or diverge forever. Always plot the trajectory and check it settles — a "result" from a diverging loop is meaningless, and pasting an unconverged number into your report is the classic capstone failure.

A solver you cannot test is a solver you cannot trust.

3 — The report

One to two pages: state each game, show the solver's answer, and interpret it economically — why the prisoner's dilemma equilibrium is inefficient, why mixing makes a player unpredictable, and how the Cournot outcome sits between monopoly and perfect competition. The report is where you prove you understand the numbers, not just that you can produce them.

✦ Worked Example — Cournot duopoly, by hand then by sim

Take inverse demand P = 100 − Q (so a = 100, b = 1) and marginal cost c = 40 for both firms. Find the equilibrium quantities, price, and per-firm profit, then confirm your sim would reproduce them.

  1. Best response of firm 1: BR1(q2) = (a − c − b·q2)/(2b) = (100 − 40 − q2)/2 = (60 − q2)/2.
  2. Symmetry: at equilibrium q1 = q2 = q*. Substitute: q* = (60 − q*)/2 → 2q* = 60 − q* → 3q* = 60.
  3. Equilibrium quantity: q* = 20 per firm; this matches q* = (a−c)/(3b) = 60/3 = 20.
  4. Total output and price: Q = 40, so P = 100 − 40 = 60.
  5. Per-firm profit: (P − c)·q* = (60 − 40)·20 = 400 each.
  6. Sanity check vs. extremes: a monopoly would produce Q = (a−c)/(2b) = 30 (less, higher price); perfect competition drives P to c = 40 with Q = 60. The duopoly's Q = 40 sits between — exactly where the sim's best-response staircase lands.

🛠️ Hands-On: the capstone in two functions

Both functions use the free course stack — Python, NumPy, and nashpy from the Week 1 setup (the install details in Lecture 1). No new packages are needed for the capstone; everything below runs in that same environment.

# capstone_solver.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np
import nashpy as nash

def solve_normal_form(A, B):
    # A, B are the row/column players' payoff matrices
    game = nash.Game(A, B)
    results = []
    for sigma1, sigma2 in game.support_enumeration():
        pure = np.all((sigma1 == 0) | (sigma1 == 1)) and \
               np.all((sigma2 == 0) | (sigma2 == 1))
        payoffs = game[sigma1, sigma2]          # expected (u1, u2)
        results.append({
            "row_mix": np.round(sigma1, 3),
            "col_mix": np.round(sigma2, 3),
            "type": "pure" if pure else "mixed",
            "payoff": np.round(payoffs, 3),
        })
    return results

def cournot_sim(a, b, c, n=2, iters=50, q0=None):
    # symmetric Cournot: P = a - b*Q ; best-response iteration
    q = np.full(n, 1.0) if q0 is None else np.array(q0, float)
    for _ in range(iters):
        others = q.sum() - q                    # rivals' total per firm
        q = np.maximum((a - c - b * others) / (2 * b), 0)
    Q = q.sum(); price = a - b * Q
    return {"q": np.round(q, 3), "price": round(price, 3),
            "profit": np.round((price - c) * q, 3)}

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Matching Pennies -> unique MIXED equilibrium at 50/50
    A = np.array([[1, -1], [-1, 1]]); B = -A
    for eq in solve_normal_form(A, B):
        print(eq)
    # Cournot from the worked example -> q=20 each, price=60, profit=400
    print(cournot_sim(a=100, b=1, c=40, n=2))

Running it prints a single mixed equilibrium with row/col mixes [0.5, 0.5] for matching pennies, and {'q': [20., 20.], 'price': 60.0, 'profit': [400., 400.]} for the Cournot game — matching the by-hand answers exactly. Q1. Why does the capstone solver use support_enumeration rather than a simple cell-by-cell best-response scan?
A. A cell scan is faster on every game
B. Support enumeration also finds mixed-strategy equilibria, which a pure cell scan misses
C. A cell scan cannot handle symmetric games
D. Support enumeration guarantees a unique equilibrium
Show answer

B. Many games (e.g. matching pennies) have no pure equilibrium, so a pure cell scan returns nothing; support enumeration solves the indifference conditions and recovers mixed equilibria. A is false (enumeration can be slower but more complete), C is irrelevant, and D is wrong — enumeration can return several equilibria.

Q2. In a symmetric linear Cournot duopoly with P = a − bQ and marginal cost c, the per-firm equilibrium quantity is…
A. (a − c)/(3b)
B. (a − c)/(2b)
C. (a − c)/b
D. a/(3b)
Show answer

A. Solving q = (a − c − bq)/(2b) gives 3q = (a − c)/b, so q* = (a − c)/(3b). B is the monopoly total, C is the competitive total, and D ignores cost entirely.

Q3. The indifference principle for a mixed Nash equilibrium says that…
A. a player is indifferent among all of their pure strategies
B. both players must earn equal payoffs
C. every pure strategy played with positive probability earns the same expected payoff
D. the equilibrium maximizes total welfare
Show answer

C. A player only mixes among strategies that are equally good given the opponent's mix; strategies outside the support can be worse. A overstates it (only the support, not all strategies), B is false (payoffs need not be equal across players), and D confuses equilibrium with efficiency.

Q4. Your Cournot sim's quantities keep oscillating and never settle. The most appropriate response is to…
A. report the value from the last iteration anyway
B. increase the number of iterations until the print statement scrolls off-screen
C. conclude the game has no Nash equilibrium
D. check for non-convergence, since best-response iteration only converges when the dynamics form a contraction
Show answer

D. Oscillation means the iteration map is not contracting toward the fixed point; you must detect that rather than trust an arbitrary iterate. A and B paste a meaningless number into the report, and C is wrong — an equilibrium can exist even when this particular dynamic fails to reach it.

Q5. In the capstone grading split (0.4 correctness, 0.3 code quality, 0.3 report), the report exists primarily to…
A. demonstrate you can interpret the equilibria economically, not just compute them
B. replace the need for a test suite
C. pad the deliverable so it looks longer
D. prove the code runs without errors
Show answer

A. Interpretation — why a result is (in)efficient, what mixing means, where Cournot sits between monopoly and competition — is the point of the write-up. B is false (the test suite is its own artifact), C misreads the rubric, and D is what the tests, not the prose, establish.
