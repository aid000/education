Week 8 · Lecture 8

Stackelberg & Market Power

When one firm can commit to its output before its rival even moves, the order of play itself becomes a weapon.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up the Stackelberg leader–follower model as a sequential quantity game.
  • Solve it by backward induction: derive the follower's reaction function, then the leader's optimum.
  • Quantify the first-mover advantage and explain why credible commitment creates it.
  • Compare the Stackelberg outcome to the simultaneous Cournot equilibrium on quantity, price, and profit.
  • Connect output, the Lerner markup, and deadweight loss to draw welfare conclusions about market power.

8.1From Simultaneous to Sequential

In Week 7 both Cournot firms picked quantities at the same time, each guessing the other's move. But many real markets are not symmetric in timing: an incumbent builds a factory, publishes a capacity plan, or floods a channel with inventory before a rival can react. That difference — who moves first, and whether the move is observable and irreversible — is the heart of the Stackelberg model.

There are two firms producing a homogeneous good with linear inverse demand and constant marginal cost c:

P(Q) = a - b·Q,   Q = q1 + q2,   marginal cost = cThe leader (firm 1) chooses q1 first; the follower (firm 2) observes q1, then chooses q2.

The leader does not get to dictate the price — demand still does. What the leader controls is the strategic environment the follower walks into. Because the leader's choice is locked in and visible, the follower's only sensible response is its best reply to that specific q1. The leader, anticipating this, picks the q1 that steers the whole game to its own advantage.

AnalogyThink of merging onto a packed highway. If you ease in tentatively and simultaneously with the car beside you, you both brake and hesitate — that's Cournot. But if you commit hard and early — signal, accelerate, and clearly claim the gap first — the other driver's best response is to yield. You didn't change the road; you changed the order of decisions, and that handed you the lane. Stackelberg leadership is buying the lane by moving first and credibly.

8.2Backward Induction: Solve the Follower First

A sequential game is solved from the end backwards (the subgame-perfect logic from earlier weeks). So we start with the follower, who already sees q1 as a fixed number and simply maximizes its own profit:

profit2 = ( a - b(q1 + q2) - c ) · q2Take the derivative with respect to q2 and set it to zero.

The first-order condition gives the follower's reaction function — its best output for any leader quantity:

q2*(q1) = ( a - c - b·q1 ) / (2b)Exactly the Cournot best response: every extra unit the leader makes shaves the follower's reply by half a unit.

Now the leader moves first knowing this formula. It substitutes the follower's reaction back into its own profit, so its output already accounts for how the rival will retreat:

profit1 = ( a - b·q1 - b·q2*(q1) - c ) · q1 = [ (a - c)/2 - (b/2)·q1 ] · q1Maximizing this over q1 is the leader's whole problem — the follower is already "baked in."

Setting d(profit1)/dq1 = 0 yields the Stackelberg equilibrium quantities:

q1 = (a - c)/(2b)  (leader),   q2 = (a - c)/(4b)  (follower)The leader produces exactly twice the follower. Total Q = 3(a - c)/(4b).

Notice the leader's output equals the full monopoly quantity, (a−c)/(2b). By committing to it, the leader forces the follower to accept the leftover demand. That commitment is the entire source of its edge.

8.3The First-Mover Advantage

Plug the equilibrium quantities back into demand. Total output 3(a−c)/(4b) means the price is P = a − b·Q = (a + 3c)/4. Compare the two firms' profits with the symmetric Cournot benchmark, where each made (a−c)²/(9b):

profit1 = (a - c)² / (8b)  >   Cournot (a - c)² / (9b)  >   profit2 = (a - c)² / (16b)Leader beats both Cournot firms; the follower earns strictly less than it would have under simultaneous play.

This is the first-mover advantage: by committing to a large output, the leader credibly tells the follower "there is not much room left — produce little." The follower's rational reply leaves the leader with the larger share. Critically, the advantage exists only because the commitment is irreversible and observed. If the leader could secretly cut back after the follower committed, the threat would evaporate and we would slide back to Cournot.

Common mistakeDo not assume the leader sets the price or that "moving first always wins." Stackelberg leadership pays off in a quantity game where output is the strategic variable and commitment is credible. In a price-competition (Bertrand) setting with identical goods, moving first can actually be a disadvantage — your rival simply undercuts the price you publicly committed to. First-mover advantage is a property of the game's structure, not a universal law.

8.4Market Power, Markups & Welfare

Market power is the ability to price above marginal cost. The standard scalar for it is the Lerner index, the fractional markup:

L = (P - MC) / P = 1 / |elasticity of demand|L = 0 under perfect competition (price = MC); L rises toward 1 as a firm gains power.

Compare the three market structures on total output (using a = 120, b = 1, c = 0, so the competitive ideal is Q = 120 at P = 0):

The welfare story is subtle. The leader gains, the follower loses, and total industry profit falls relative to Cournot — but consumers win. Because total output (90) exceeds the Cournot output (80), the price is lower and the deadweight loss shrinks. So Stackelberg leadership is privately good for the leader and, somewhat counterintuitively, socially better than Cournot even though it is more "unequal." Still, with P > MC the market remains short of the competitive ideal — positive market power, positive deadweight loss.

✦ Worked Example — leader vs. follower with real numbers

Demand is P = 120 - Q (so a = 120, b = 1), Q = q1 + q2, and both firms have marginal cost c = 0. Firm 1 leads. Find the equilibrium quantities, price, and profits, and compare to Cournot.

  1. Follower reaction: maximize (120 − q1 − q2)q2 → FOC 120 − q1 − 2q2 = 0 → q2 = (120 − q1)/2.
  2. Leader's profit: substitute → profit1 = (120 − q1 − (120−q1)/2)·q1 = (60 − q1/2)·q1.
  3. Leader FOC: 60 − q1 = 0 → q1 = 60. Then q2 = (120 − 60)/2 = 30.
  4. Price: Q = 90, so P = 120 − 90 = 30. Profits: leader = 30×60 = 1800; follower = 30×30 = 900.
  5. Cournot benchmark: each firm produces 40, Q = 80, P = 40, profit each = 40×40 = 1600. So leading lifts firm 1 from 1600 to 1800 (+200), while the follower drops from 1600 to 900 (−700).
  6. Welfare check: total profit falls (2700 < 3200), but output rises (90 > 80) and price falls (30 < 40) — consumers are better off, and deadweight loss versus the competitive Q = 120 is smaller.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve Stackelberg in Python

We reuse the free QuantEcon + NumPy + nashpy stack from the Week 1 setup (the install details lives in Lecture 1 — no new tools needed here). Because Stackelberg is solved by backward induction, we code it directly: optimize the leader's profit, with the follower's reaction substituted in.

# stackelberg.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

a, b, c = 120.0, 1.0, 0.0   # P = a - b*Q, marginal cost c

def follower_best_response(q1):
    # firm 2 maximizes (a - b(q1+q2) - c)*q2 given q1
    return max((a - c - b * q1) / (2 * b), 0.0)

def leader_profit(q1):
    q2 = follower_best_response(q1)
    P  = a - b * (q1 + q2)
    return (P - c) * q1            # firm 1's profit

# maximize leader profit = minimize its negative
res = minimize_scalar(lambda q: -leader_profit(q), bounds=(0, a / b), method="bounded")
q1 = res.x
q2 = follower_best_response(q1)
P  = a - b * (q1 + q2)

print(f"leader  q1 = {q1:.1f}, profit = {(P-c)*q1:.0f}")   # 60.0, 1800
print(f"follower q2 = {q2:.1f}, profit = {(P-c)*q2:.0f}")  # 30.0,  900
print(f"price P = {P:.1f}, total Q = {q1+q2:.1f}")          # 30.0, 90.0

Run it and you reproduce the worked example: (60, 30), P = 30. Now set both firms to move simultaneously by solving q1 = q2 = (a−c)/(3b) and confirm the Cournot benchmark (40, 40), P = 40 — the price the leader's commitment beat. scipy ships with the Week-1 stack, so nothing new to install.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. What distinguishes the Stackelberg model from Cournot?
A. One firm chooses quantity first and the other observes it before responding
B. Firms choose prices instead of quantities
C. The goods are differentiated rather than homogeneous
D. There are many firms instead of two
Show answer

A. Stackelberg is sequential quantity competition: the leader commits first and the follower observes. Both models use quantities (so B is wrong) and homogeneous goods with two firms (C, D wrong).

Q2. The correct way to solve the Stackelberg game is to…
A. set both firms' first-order conditions equal simultaneously
B. assume the leader sets the monopoly price
C. solve the follower's reaction function first, then maximize the leader's profit with it substituted in (backward induction)
D. let the follower move first
Show answer

C. Sequential games are solved backward: derive the follower's best response, then the leader optimizes anticipating it. Solving both FOCs at once (A) is the Cournot method; B and D misstate the structure.

Q3. With P = 120 − Q and c = 0, the Stackelberg leader's quantity is…
A. 30
B. 40
C. 60
D. 90
Show answer

C. q1 = (a−c)/(2b) = 120/2 = 60 — equal to the monopoly output. 30 is the follower's output, 40 is the Cournot per-firm output, and 90 is total industry output.

Q4. Why does the leader earn more than it would under Cournot?
A. It produces less, raising the price
B. Its observable, irreversible commitment to a large output forces the follower to cut back
C. It colludes with the follower
D. It faces a lower marginal cost
Show answer

B. The first-mover advantage comes entirely from credible commitment: a large, irreversible, observed q1 shifts the follower down its reaction curve. The leader produces more, not less (A); there is no collusion (C) or cost asymmetry (D) in the model.

Q5. Compared with the Cournot outcome, the Stackelberg outcome has…
A. lower total output and a higher price
B. the same output but higher total profit
C. zero market power (price equals marginal cost)
D. higher total output, a lower price, and smaller deadweight loss
Show answer

D. Stackelberg output (90) exceeds Cournot (80), so price is lower and deadweight loss is smaller — consumers gain even though total profit falls. A reverses it; B is false (output differs, profit falls); C is wrong because P (=30) still exceeds MC (=0), so market power persists.
