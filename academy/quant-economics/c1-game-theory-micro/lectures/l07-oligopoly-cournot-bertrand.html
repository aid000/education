Oligopoly: Cournot & Bertrand

A handful of firms, one market — and the price they all charge falls out of a Nash equilibrium, not a planner's wish.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up the Cournot model where firms compete by choosing quantities.
  • Derive a firm's reaction (best-response) function from its profit-maximization problem.
  • Solve for the Cournot–Nash equilibrium as the intersection of reaction functions.
  • Set up Bertrand price competition and explain the Bertrand paradox.
  • Rank monopoly, Cournot, and perfect competition by price, quantity, and welfare.

7.1Quantity Competition: the Cournot Model

An oligopoly is a market with a few firms whose decisions affect each other. Back in Lecture 1 the prisoner's dilemma hinted that firms behave strategically; now we build the workhorse model. In the Cournot model, each firm chooses how much to produce — a quantity — and the market price is whatever clears the total output through a downward-sloping demand curve.

Take a linear inverse-demand curve and constant marginal cost. With two firms producing q1 and q2, total output is Q = q1 + q2, and the price every unit sells for is:

P(Q) = a - b(q1 + q2),   cost = c per unita = choke price (demand intercept), b = slope, c = constant marginal cost, with a > c > 0.

The crucial twist versus monopoly: when firm 1 expands output it pushes the price down on every unit — including firm 2's. Each firm's profit therefore depends on the rival's quantity, so we are squarely in a strategic game. The players are the firms, the strategies are quantities, and the payoffs are profits:

profit_i = (P(Q) - c) · q_i = (a - b(q1 + q2) - c) · q_iFirm i's profit is its per-unit margin times its own output — but the margin shrinks as the rival produces more.
AnalogyTwo farmers share one well that recharges slowly. Each decides how many buckets to draw. Drawing more water today is privately tempting, but it lowers the water level — and the "price" of water — for both. Neither internalizes the harm done to the other, so together they over-draw. Cournot quantity competition is exactly this tension between private incentive and shared market price.

7.2Reaction Functions & the Cournot–Nash Equilibrium

To find firm 1's best response, treat q2 as fixed and maximize profit over q1. Expanding profit_1 = (a - c)q1 - b q1² - b q1 q2 and setting the derivative with respect to q1 to zero gives the first-order condition:

d(profit_1)/dq1 = (a - c) - 2 b q1 - b q2 = 0Marginal revenue (which now includes the price drop on firm 1's own units) equals marginal cost.

Solving for q1 gives firm 1's reaction function — its profit-maximizing output as a function of what the rival does:

q1*(q2) = (a - c)/(2b) - q2/2The more the rival produces, the less firm 1 wants to produce — the lines slope downward. Outputs are "strategic substitutes."

By symmetry firm 2 has q2*(q1) = (a - c)/(2b) - q1/2. A Cournot–Nash equilibrium is a pair (q1, q2) where each firm is simultaneously best-responding — the point where the two reaction lines cross. Setting q1 = q2 = q* and solving:

q* = (a - c)/(3b)  per firm,   Q* = 2(a - c)/(3b)Each duopolist produces one-third of the competitive quantity; together they make two-thirds of it.

7.3Price Competition: Bertrand & the Paradox

Cournot assumed firms set quantities. But many firms post a price and then sell whatever quantity buyers demand at it. That is the Bertrand model. With identical products and constant marginal cost c, consumers buy entirely from whoever is cheapest; if both post the same price they split the market.

Now reason about best responses. Suppose your rival posts price p > c. By undercutting to p - ε you capture the entire market at a still-profitable price. So any price above marginal cost is unstable — both firms keep undercutting. The undercutting stops only when price hits marginal cost:

p1 = p2 = c,   profit = 0The unique Nash equilibrium: price equals marginal cost, just like perfect competition — with only two firms.

This is the Bertrand paradox: just two firms competing on price drive the outcome all the way to the competitive result, with zero economic profit. The number of competitors barely matters — what matters is the strategic variable. Switch from quantity (Cournot) to price (Bertrand) and the prediction lurches from comfortable markups to cut-throat marginal-cost pricing.

Common mistakeDo not conclude that "two firms always means competitive prices." The stark Bertrand result hinges on identical products, constant marginal cost, no capacity limits, and a one-shot game. Relax any one — differentiate the products, add capacity constraints, or repeat the game (Week 6) — and prices rise back above marginal cost. The paradox is a knife-edge benchmark, not a law of nature.

7.4Ranking the Market Structures

Put the three benchmarks side by side using the same linear demand P = a - bQ and marginal cost c. Monopoly sets MR = MC and produces Q_m = (a-c)/(2b). Perfect competition drives price to marginal cost, so P = c and Q_c = (a-c)/b. Cournot duopoly lands in between, at Q = 2(a-c)/(3b).

Q_monopoly < Q_cournot < Q_competition
P_monopoly > P_cournot > P_competition(a-c)/(2b) < 2(a-c)/(3b) < (a-c)/b — Cournot output is two-thirds of competitive, above monopoly's half.

More firms means more total output, lower prices, and higher consumer surplus — Cournot output rises toward the competitive level as the number of firms n grows (each produces (a-c)/[(n+1)b], so Q = n/(n+1) of the competitive amount). Bertrand, by contrast, jumps straight to the competitive corner with just two firms. The lesson the field cares about: market power is about strategy and structure, not merely headcount.

✦ Worked Example — solve a Cournot duopoly end to end

Market demand is P = 100 - Q, where Q = q1 + q2. Both firms have constant marginal cost c = 20. Find each firm's reaction function, the Cournot–Nash quantities and price, and compare with monopoly and competition.

  1. Firm 1's profit: profit_1 = (100 - q1 - q2 - 20)q1 = (80 - q2)q1 - q1².
  2. First-order condition: d(profit_1)/dq1 = 80 - q2 - 2q1 = 0, so the reaction function is q1 = (80 - q2)/2 = 40 - q2/2.
  3. Symmetry: firm 2 has q2 = 40 - q1/2. Set q1 = q2 = q*: q* = 40 - q*/2, so (3/2)q* = 40 and q* = 26.67 each.
  4. Market outcome: Q = 2q* = 53.33, price P = 100 - 53.33 = 46.67. Each firm's profit = (46.67 - 20)(26.67) = 711; industry profit ≈ 1422.
  5. Monopoly check: MR = 100 - 2Q = 20 -> Q_m = 40, P_m = 60, profit = (60-20)(40) = 1600. Cournot output (53.33) exceeds monopoly (40); price (46.67) is below monopoly (60). Industry profit falls from 1600 to 1422.
  6. Competition / Bertrand check: P = c = 20, Q = 80, profit = 0. Cournot sits neatly between monopoly and competition — exactly as the ranking in 7.4 predicts.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve Cournot in Python

We keep using the free QuantEcon + NumPy + nashpy stack from Week 1 (the one-time install lives in Lecture 1's setup box — reuse that virtual environment). Cournot has a continuum of quantities, so instead of a payoff matrix we either solve the linear best-response system directly or discretize the quantity grid and hand it to nashpy.

# cournot.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
import nashpy as nash

a, b, c = 100.0, 1.0, 20.0          # P = a - b*Q,  MC = c

# --- (1) closed-form Cournot-Nash via reaction functions ---
# q1 = (a-c)/(2b) - q2/2 ; symmetric solution q* = (a-c)/(3b)
q_star = (a - c) / (3 * b)
Q = 2 * q_star
price = a - b * Q
profit = (price - c) * q_star
print(f"q* per firm = {q_star:.2f},  P = {price:.2f},  profit each = {profit:.1f}")
# q* per firm = 26.67,  P = 46.67,  profit each = 711.1

# --- (2) discretize quantities and let nashpy find the pure NE ---
grid = np.arange(0, 51)             # candidate outputs 0..50
def payoff(qi, qj):
    P = max(a - b * (qi + qj), 0)
    return (P - c) * qi
A = np.array([[payoff(qi, qj) for qj in grid] for qi in grid])   # firm 1
B = A.T                                                          # symmetric firm 2
game = nash.Game(A, B)
for eq in game.support_enumeration():
    i = int(np.argmax(eq[0])); j = int(np.argmax(eq[1]))
    print("pure NE quantities:", grid[i], grid[j])               # -> 27 27 (nearest grid pt)

The closed-form and the grid search agree (27 ≈ 26.67). Try setting B for a Bertrand price grid where the best response is "rival's price minus one cent" and watch both prices collapse to c — the Bertrand paradox, simulated.

Q1. In the Cournot model, the strategic variable each firm chooses is its…
A. price
B. quantity produced
C. marginal cost
D. advertising budget
Show answer

B. Cournot is quantity competition; the market price is determined afterward by demand. Choosing price (A) is the Bertrand model. Marginal cost (C) is a given parameter, and advertising (D) is not in this model.

Q2. A firm's reaction function in Cournot competition shows…
A. total industry profit as a function of price
B. the market price as a function of total quantity
C. consumer surplus as a function of the number of firms
D. its profit-maximizing output as a function of the rival's output
Show answer

D. The reaction (best-response) function maps the rival's chosen quantity to this firm's optimal quantity. The price-vs-quantity relation (B) is the demand curve, not a reaction function; A and C are unrelated objects.

Q3. With demand P = 100 - Q and marginal cost c = 20, the symmetric Cournot duopoly output per firm is…
A. 40
B. 20
C. about 26.7
D. 80
Show answer

C. q* = (a-c)/(3b) = 80/3 ≈ 26.7 per firm. 40 (A) is the monopoly output; 80 (D) is the competitive total quantity; 20 is the marginal cost, not a quantity.

Q4. The Bertrand paradox states that, with identical products and constant marginal cost, two price-setting firms…
A. split the monopoly profit equally
B. price at marginal cost and earn zero profit, like perfect competition
C. each charge the monopoly price
D. cannot reach any equilibrium at all
Show answer

B. Each firm has an incentive to undercut any price above MC, so price collapses to c with zero profit — even with only two firms. The monopoly-profit/price answers (A, C) are exactly what undercutting destroys, and a unique equilibrium does exist (D is false).

Q5. Comparing the three structures on the same linear demand, the correct ranking of total output is…
A. Q_monopoly < Q_cournot < Q_competition
B. Q_competition < Q_cournot < Q_monopoly
C. Q_cournot < Q_monopoly < Q_competition
D. all three produce the same quantity
Show answer

A. Monopoly restricts output most ((a-c)/2b), competition produces most ((a-c)/b), and Cournot sits in between (2(a-c)/3b). B reverses the order, C wrongly puts Cournot below monopoly, and D ignores that market power restricts output.
