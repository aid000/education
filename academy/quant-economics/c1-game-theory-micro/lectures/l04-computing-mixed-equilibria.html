Computing Mixed-Strategy Equilibria

Last week we learned that randomizing can be optimal — this week we actually solve for the exact dice the rational player rolls.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Solve any 2×2 game for its mixed-strategy equilibrium using the indifference condition.
  • Draw and read the best-response correspondence, and locate equilibria as its intersections.
  • Use support enumeration to find equilibria in games larger than 2×2.
  • Compute equilibria in Python with nashpy and interpret the output.
  • Recognize and handle games with multiple equilibria (pure and mixed together).

4.1The Indifference Trick

Last week you saw why a player might randomize: in a game with no pure-strategy equilibrium (matching pennies, a penalty kick, a bluff), being predictable is fatal. But "randomize somehow" isn't an answer — we need the exact probabilities. The key insight is almost paradoxical:

If you mix between two strategies, both must give you the SAME expected payoffOtherwise you'd shift all your weight onto the better one — and stop mixing. So a genuine mix requires indifference.

This flips the problem around. Your own probabilities don't make you indifferent — they make your opponent indifferent. You choose your mix so that the other player can't exploit you by leaning toward either of their options. Concretely, in a 2×2 game let the row player play Top with probability p and the column player play Left with probability q. To pin down q, write the row player's expected payoff from Top and from Bottom as functions of q, then set them equal:

E[Top](q) = E[Bottom](q) -> solve for q
E[Left](p) = E[Right](p) -> solve for pRow's indifference equation pins down the COLUMN player's mix q; column's indifference pins down ROW's mix p.

Notice the cross-over: the row player's payoffs determine q (the column player's mix), and the column player's payoffs determine p. That cross-over is the single most important thing to internalize this week.

AnalogyA good penalty taker doesn't aim left because left is "better" — if it were, the keeper would always dive left and stop it. The taker mixes precisely so the keeper is indifferent between diving left or right. The taker's randomization is engineered around the keeper's payoffs, not the taker's. That is the indifference trick in one sentence.

4.2The Best-Response Correspondence

The indifference trick is algebra; the best-response correspondence is its picture. For each value of the opponent's mix, plot your own best response. In a 2×2 game both are functions on the unit square [0,1] × [0,1], and an equilibrium is any point where the two curves cross.

Take matching pennies, payoffs to the row player (column player gets the negative): matching gives row +1, mismatching gives row −1. Row plays Heads with probability p, column plays Heads with probability q. Row's best response as a function of q is a step:

if q > 1/2: row prefers Heads (p = 1)
if q < 1/2: row prefers Tails (p = 0)
if q = 1/2: row is indifferent (any p works)The vertical jump at q = 1/2 is exactly where mixing becomes rational — the correspondence is "set-valued" there.

Column, who wants to mismatch, has the mirror-image best response. The two step functions cross only at (p, q) = (1/2, 1/2) — the unique mixed equilibrium. The figure below shows both correspondences on the unit square.

4.3Support Enumeration for Larger Games

The indifference trick scales, but in a game with more than two strategies per player we don't know in advance which strategies a player will actually use with positive probability. The set of strategies played with positive probability is called the support. Support enumeration is the brute-force-but-finite recipe behind nashpy:

  1. Guess a support for each player (a subset of their strategies).
  2. Impose indifference across the strategies inside each support, and solve the resulting linear system for the probabilities.
  3. Check the candidate is valid: all probabilities are non-negative and sum to 1, and no strategy outside the support would do strictly better.
  4. Repeat over every pair of supports; keep all that pass.

For an m × n game there are (2m−1)(2n−1) support pairs, so this is exhaustive but exponential — fine for the small games in this course, and the reason large games need cleverer solvers (the Lemke–Howson algorithm, also in nashpy).

Common mistakeThe indifference equations only hold across strategies inside the support. A frequent error is forcing a player to be indifferent across all their strategies — including ones they never play. In equilibrium an unused strategy must yield a payoff that is less than or equal to the support payoff, not equal to it. Skipping the step-3 "no outside strategy is better" check produces phantom equilibria that aren't real.

4.4Games with Multiple Equilibria

Many strategically interesting games have more than one equilibrium — and often a mix of pure and mixed ones. The classic case is a coordination game like Battle of the Sexes: two players prefer different things but both prefer being together to being apart.

Two pure equilibria (both coordinate) + one mixed equilibriumA general result: a "generic" finite game has an ODD number of equilibria (the Oddness Theorem) — so two pure equilibria almost always come with a third, mixed one.

The mixed equilibrium is usually the worst of the three by expected payoff — players sometimes miscoordinate — yet it is the one selected when there's no way to signal or agree in advance. When a model has multiple equilibria, computation gives you the full menu; equilibrium selection (which one actually gets played) is a separate question driven by focal points, communication, or dynamics. For applications — say predicting whether two firms enter the same market — you must report all equilibria, not just your favorite.

AnalogyThink of two friends whose phones die mid-call. Each can call back or wait. If both call, the line is busy; if both wait, silence forever. There are two good pure outcomes (one calls, one waits) and one tense mixed outcome where each randomizes and they sometimes both wait. Real life resolves it with a convention ("the one who called first calls back") — that convention is an equilibrium-selection device, not part of the payoff math.
✦ Worked Example — solve a 2×2 by hand

A penalty kicker chooses Left or Right; the keeper dives Left or Right. The kicker scores if they mismatch the keeper. Payoffs are (kicker, keeper). Kicker plays Left with probability p; keeper dives Left with probability q.

Kicker payoffs: (L,L) = 0 · score-prob, kicker is stopped → 0; (L,R) = kicker scores → 1; (R,L) = 1; (R,R) = 0. Keeper gets the opposite: keeper saves (+1) when they match, 0 when they mismatch.

  1. Find q (keeper's mix) from the KICKER's indifference. Kicker's expected payoff from Left = (keeper dives L) · 0 + (keeper dives R) · 1 = (1 − q). From Right = q · 1 + (1 − q) · 0 = q.
  2. Set equal: 1 − q = q → q = 1/2. The keeper dives Left half the time.
  3. Find p (kicker's mix) from the KEEPER's indifference. Keeper saves by matching: payoff from diving Left = p · 1 + (1 − p) · 0 = p. From diving Right = (1 − p) · 1 = 1 − p.
  4. Set equal: p = 1 − p → p = 1/2. The kicker shoots Left half the time.
  5. Equilibrium: (p, q) = (1/2, 1/2). The kicker's scoring probability is 1/2 — and crucially, neither side can do better by deviating, because the other is perfectly balanced. If real keepers dive left 60% of the time, a kicker should shoot right more — exactly the kind of exploit sports analytics teams hunt for.

🛠️ Hands-On: support enumeration in Python

We reuse the same free QuantEcon + nashpy + NumPy stack from the Week 1 setup (the install details lives in Lecture 1). Nothing new to install this week. Below, support_enumeration() returns every equilibrium — pure and mixed — which is exactly why it's the right tool when a game may have several.

# battle_of_the_sexes.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np
import nashpy as nash

# rows/cols = (Ballet, Boxing). Both prefer being together.
# Row (player A) likes Ballet; Column (player B) likes Boxing.
A = np.array([[3, 0],      # row player's payoffs
              [0, 2]])
B = np.array([[2, 0],      # column player's payoffs
              [0, 3]])

game = nash.Game(A, B)
for eq in game.support_enumeration():
    print(np.round(eq[0], 3), np.round(eq[1], 3))

Expected output — three equilibria:

[1. 0.] [1. 0.]          # pure: both choose Ballet
[0. 1.] [0. 1.]          # pure: both choose Boxing
[0.6 0.4] [0.4 0.6]      # mixed: row plays Ballet 60%, column Boxing 60%

Check the mixed numbers by hand with the indifference trick: row is indifferent when 3q = 2(1 − q), giving q = 0.4 for the column player's Ballet weight; symmetric algebra gives p = 0.6. The code agrees — confirm it, then swap in the matching-pennies payoffs and verify you recover the single (1/2, 1/2) equilibrium.

Q1. In a 2×2 game, the row player's mixing probability p is pinned down by…
A. the row player's own indifference condition
B. the column player's indifference condition
C. maximizing the row player's payoff directly
D. the sum of both players' payoffs
Show answer

B. The cross-over rule: a player chooses their mix to make the opponent indifferent. So p comes from the column player's indifference equation, and q from the row player's. (A) reverses the cross-over; (C) would just push all weight to one pure strategy; (D) is an efficiency notion, irrelevant here.

Q2. Why must both pure strategies in a mixed-strategy support yield equal expected payoff?
A. Because the game is symmetric
B. Because payoffs are always equal at any Nash equilibrium
C. Because if one paid more, the player would shift all weight to it and stop mixing
D. Because mixed strategies maximize total welfare
Show answer

C. A genuine mix requires indifference: any payoff gap would make the player abandon the worse action. (A) symmetry isn't required; (B) payoffs need not be equal across players or across unused strategies; (D) mixing has nothing to do with welfare.

Q3. On the unit square, a mixed-strategy Nash equilibrium of a 2×2 game appears as…
A. the highest point of the row player's payoff curve
B. an intersection of the two players' best-response correspondences
C. the midpoint of the diagonal
D. any point on the boundary of the square
Show answer

B. Equilibria are exactly the points where each player is best-responding to the other — i.e., where the two BR correspondences cross. (A) ignores the opponent; (C) is only coincidentally true for symmetric games; (D) the boundary is just where pure best responses sit.

Q4. When you guess a support and solve the indifference equations, what extra check keeps you from reporting a phantom equilibrium?
A. Confirm the payoff matrix is square
B. Confirm both players use the same support
C. Confirm the equilibrium is Pareto-efficient
D. Confirm probabilities are non-negative and no strategy outside the support pays strictly more
Show answer

D. A valid candidate needs legal probabilities and no profitable deviation to an unused strategy. (A) games needn't be square; (B) supports can differ in size across players; (C) Nash equilibria are frequently inefficient (the prisoner's dilemma).

Q5. A coordination game like Battle of the Sexes has two pure equilibria. What does the Oddness Theorem lead you to expect?
A. A third equilibrium — typically a mixed one
B. Exactly two equilibria and no more
C. No equilibrium at all
D. Infinitely many equilibria
Show answer

A. Generic finite games have an odd number of equilibria, so two pure ones almost always come with a third mixed equilibrium — which support_enumeration() will list. (B) contradicts oddness; (C) every finite game has at least one equilibrium (Nash's theorem); (D) a continuum arises only in non-generic, knife-edge cases.
