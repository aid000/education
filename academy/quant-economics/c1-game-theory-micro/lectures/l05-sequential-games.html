Sequential Games & Backward Induction

When players move in turns, the future is written first — solve the game from its last move backward, and only credible plans survive.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Represent a turn-based game in extensive form as a game tree of decision nodes, branches, and payoffs.
  • Recognize perfect information and explain why it lets us solve the game cleanly.
  • Apply backward induction to fold a game tree from its leaves to its root.
  • Define a subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE) and distinguish credible from non-credible threats.
  • Explain the strategic value of commitment and first-mover considerations, and verify a solution in Python.

5.1The Extensive Form: Games as Trees

Lectures 1–4 lived in the normal form — a payoff matrix where both players choose simultaneously, each blind to the other's move. But many real strategic situations are sequential: one party moves, the other observes, then responds. An incumbent sets a price before a rival decides whether to enter; a market maker posts a quote before a trader hits it; a central bank announces policy before firms set wages. To capture timing, we need a richer picture — the extensive form, drawn as a game tree.

A game tree has four ingredients. Decision nodes mark whose turn it is. Branches are the actions available at a node. Terminal nodes (leaves) end the game and carry a payoff vector — one number per player, listed in a fixed order. A strategy is now a complete contingent plan: it specifies what a player would do at every node where they might move, even nodes that never get reached on the equilibrium path.

strategy = a full plan: an action at every decision node you ownNot just "what I do," but "what I would do in every situation that could arise" — including ones we expect never to occur.

That "even off the path" clause looks pedantic but is the heart of this lecture: threats and promises live at nodes you hope never to visit, and whether they are believable decides the outcome.

5.2Perfect Information & Backward Induction

A game has perfect information when every player, whenever they move, knows exactly where they are in the tree — they have observed every prior action. Chess and tic-tac-toe have perfect information; poker (hidden cards) does not. With perfect information and finitely many moves, there is a clean recipe to solve the game: backward induction.

The idea is to reason from the end. Look at the last decision a player makes. Since nothing follows it, that player simply picks the action with the highest payoff — no strategic guessing needed. Replace that node with its winning payoff. Now the second-to-last mover faces a problem whose consequences are known, and can optimize the same way. Keep "folding" the tree, node by node, until you reach the root. The chosen action at each node forms the solution.

solve last move -> fold its payoff up -> solve the move before -> ... -> reach the rootZermelo's theorem: a finite perfect-information game can always be solved this way, yielding a well-defined outcome.

Below, Player 1 moves first (choosing L or R), then Player 2 responds. Payoffs are written (Player 1, Player 2). Backward induction starts at Player 2's nodes.

AnalogyBackward induction is how a good chess player thinks: "If I move here, what is my opponent's best reply? And given that reply, what is my best reply after that?" You plan the endgame first and let it dictate the opening. The same logic powers the Bellman equation in dynamic programming — solve the last stage, then recurse backward — which is why these tools reappear across reinforcement learning and optimal-control trading.

5.3Subgame-Perfect Equilibrium & Credible Threats

Every sequential game also has a normal form, and that normal form can have extra Nash equilibria that rely on threats a player would never actually carry out. Backward induction throws those away. The equilibrium it produces is called a subgame-perfect equilibrium (SPE).

A subgame is any node together with everything that follows it — a self-contained game-within-the-game. A strategy profile is subgame perfect if it prescribes a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, not just along the path actually played.

SPE: the strategy is a Nash equilibrium in every subgameEquivalently (finite, perfect information): the profile that survives backward induction. It forbids plans that are not optimal once you actually arrive at a node.

The payoff of SPE is the concept of a credible threat. Consider market entry: a potential entrant decides whether to Enter; the incumbent then chooses to Fight (a price war that hurts both) or Accommodate (share the market). The incumbent loudly threatens, "Enter and I will fight!" In the normal form, (Stay Out, Fight) is a Nash equilibrium — if the entrant believes the threat, staying out is their best response, and the incumbent's threat is never tested.

But is the threat credible? Backward induction asks: if entry actually happens, what will the incumbent really do? If accommodating yields a higher payoff than fighting, the incumbent will accommodate — fighting is a money-losing bluff. The entrant, anticipating this, enters anyway. Only (Enter, Accommodate) is subgame perfect; the threat to fight is non-credible and gets pruned.

Common mistakeDo not treat every Nash equilibrium of a sequential game as a valid prediction. (Stay Out, Fight) is a perfectly good Nash equilibrium, but it rests on a threat the incumbent would never execute once entry occurs — so it is not subgame perfect. Forgetting to check off-path behavior makes you believe in bluffs. SPE is exactly the refinement that filters out non-credible threats.

5.4Commitment & the First-Mover Edge

If a threat is only believed when it is in your interest to carry it out, then the way to make a threat work is to change your own incentives — to commit. Commitment sounds like throwing away options, yet in strategic settings it can raise your payoff, precisely because it changes what rivals expect.

Suppose the incumbent, before the entrant moves, irreversibly invests in capacity that makes fighting cheap (or signs a contract that penalizes accommodating). Now fighting really is the incumbent's best response after entry — the threat becomes credible, the entrant stays out, and the incumbent keeps the whole market. By removing the soft option, the incumbent improved its outcome. This is the strategic logic behind capacity investment, "scorched-earth" debt covenants, and a general's burning the bridges behind their own army.

commitment value = payoff(committed SPE) − payoff(uncommitted SPE)Moving first and locking in a choice can be worth more than flexibility, because it reshapes the rival's best response.

This also clarifies the first-mover question. Moving first helps when your move is observable and irreversible, so it shifts the follower's incentives (as in quantity competition — the Stackelberg leader of Week 7). Moving first hurts when it merely reveals information the rival exploits. The advantage is not in the timing itself but in the commitment that observable, irreversible early moves create.

✦ Worked Example — the entry game, solved by backward induction

An Entrant first chooses Out or In. If Out, payoffs are (Entrant 0, Incumbent 10). If In, the Incumbent chooses Fight → (−2, −1) or Accommodate → (3, 4). Payoffs are (Entrant, Incumbent). Find the subgame-perfect equilibrium and check whether "I will fight entry" is credible.

  1. Start at the last move (Incumbent, after In): Fight gives the incumbent −1; Accommodate gives 4. Since 4 > −1, the incumbent chooses Accommodate.
  2. Fold that payoff up: the entrant knows that choosing In leads to Accommodate, i.e. the entrant gets 3 (not −2).
  3. Solve the Entrant's move: Out gives 0; In gives 3. Since 3 > 0, the entrant chooses In.
  4. SPE: (Entrant: In; Incumbent: Accommodate-if-In), with outcome payoff (3, 4).
  5. Is "Fight" credible? No. (Out, Fight) is a Nash equilibrium of the normal form — but Fight gives the incumbent −1 versus 4 from accommodating, so the incumbent would never actually fight. The threat is non-credible and is pruned by subgame perfection.
  6. Commitment twist: if the incumbent could pre-commit (e.g. sink a cost that flips Fight's payoff to +5), then Fight would beat Accommodate, the entrant would foresee a real fight, and choosing Out (0) over In (−2) would keep the entrant away — the incumbent secures 10. Commitment turned a bluff into a winning strategy.

🛠️ Hands-On: backward induction in Python

We keep using the free QuantEcon stack with nashpy and NumPy from the Week 1 setup (the one-time install lives in Lecture 1). Game trees are small and recursive, so we will first solve the entry game by hand-coding backward induction, then cross-check with nashpy on the equivalent normal form.

# entry_game.py — Lecture 5: backward induction on a perfect-info tree
# Tree: Entrant picks Out/In; if In, Incumbent picks Fight/Accommodate.
# Payoffs are (entrant, incumbent).

leaves = {
    "Out":              (0, 10),
    ("In", "Fight"):    (-2, -1),
    ("In", "Accommodate"): (3, 4),
}

# Step 1: incumbent's last move — maximize the incumbent's payoff (index 1)
incumbent_choice = max(["Fight", "Accommodate"],
                       key=lambda act: leaves[("In", act)][1])
in_payoff = leaves[("In", incumbent_choice)]
print("Incumbent will:", incumbent_choice, "-> payoff", in_payoff)

# Step 2: entrant compares Out vs the folded-up value of In (entrant payoff = index 0)
entrant_choice = "In" if in_payoff[0] > leaves["Out"][0] else "Out"
spe_payoff = in_payoff if entrant_choice == "In" else leaves["Out"]
print("Entrant will:", entrant_choice, "-> SPE payoff", spe_payoff)
# Incumbent will: Accommodate -> payoff (3, 4)
# Entrant will: In -> SPE payoff (3, 4)

Now confirm that the normal form has the extra, non-credible Nash equilibrium that backward induction discarded. Strategies: Entrant ∈ {Out, In}; Incumbent ∈ {Fight, Accommodate} (its plan if In occurs).

# normal form: rows = Entrant (Out, In); cols = Incumbent (Fight, Accommodate)
import numpy as np
import nashpy as nash

# If Entrant plays Out, the incumbent's plan is off-path: payoff stays (0,10).
Ent = np.array([[ 0,  0],     # Out vs (Fight, Accommodate)
                [-2,  3]])    # In  vs (Fight, Accommodate)
Inc = np.array([[10, 10],
                [-1,  4]])

g = nash.Game(Ent, Inc)
for eq in g.support_enumeration():
    print(eq)
# Two pure NE appear: (Out, Fight) and (In, Accommodate).
# Only (In, Accommodate) survives backward induction => it is the SPE.

The lesson: nashpy lists all Nash equilibria of the matrix, including the bluff (Out, Fight). Subgame perfection — the backward-induction pass above — is the extra filter that keeps only the credible one. Try editing the Fight payoff for the incumbent from −1 to +5 (the commitment twist) and watch the SPE flip to (Out, Fight).

🔗 Where this is heading

Backward induction needs a known end. Q1. In the extensive form, a player's strategy is…
A. the single action taken on the equilibrium path
B. a complete plan specifying an action at every decision node the player owns
C. the payoff the player receives at a terminal node
D. the probability of reaching the root
Show answer

B. A strategy is a full contingent plan — what you would do at every node you might move at, including off-path ones. (A) is just the realized action; (C) is a payoff, not a plan; (D) is meaningless since the root is always reached.

Q2. Backward induction solves a finite perfect-information game by…
A. averaging the payoffs across all terminal nodes
B. finding the move that maximizes total welfare at the root
C. starting at the last decisions and folding optimal payoffs up toward the root
D. randomizing over all strategies until payoffs stabilize
Show answer

C. You solve the last mover first, replace each node with its optimal payoff, then work backward to the root. (A) and (D) are not backward induction; (B) optimizes joint welfare, but each player optimizes only their own payoff.

Q3. A subgame-perfect equilibrium requires that the strategy profile…
A. maximizes the sum of both players' payoffs
B. be a Nash equilibrium only along the path actually played
C. uses a strictly dominant strategy at the root
D. be a Nash equilibrium in every subgame, including off the equilibrium path
Show answer

D. SPE demands Nash play in every subgame — that is exactly what rules out non-credible threats. (B) describes plain Nash and is the error SPE corrects; (A) is efficiency, not equilibrium; (C) need not hold.

Q4. In the entry game where Fight gives the incumbent −1 and Accommodate gives 4, the threat "I will fight entry" is…
A. non-credible, because once entry happens the incumbent prefers to accommodate
B. credible, because it is announced before the entrant moves
C. credible, because (Out, Fight) is a Nash equilibrium
D. irrelevant, since the entrant cannot observe it
Show answer

A. After entry, fighting (−1) is worse than accommodating (4), so the incumbent won't carry it out — the threat is non-credible. (B) announcing changes nothing without commitment; (C) being a Nash equilibrium is precisely why we need the SPE refinement; (D) the issue is credibility, not observability.

Q5. Why can a credible commitment raise a player's payoff even though it removes options?
A. Fewer options always lower payoffs, so it cannot
B. It changes the rival's best response by making a previously empty threat genuinely optimal to carry out
C. It forces both players to cooperate
D. It converts the game to one of imperfect information
Show answer

B. By altering its own incentives (e.g. sinking a cost), the committed player makes the threat credible, which shifts what the rival expects and does. (A) is the naive intuition the example refutes; (C) commitment is competitive, not cooperative; (D) commitment doesn't hide information.
