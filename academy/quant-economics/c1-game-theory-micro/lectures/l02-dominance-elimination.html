Dominance & Iterated Elimination

If a strategy is never your best move, throw it away — then watch the game shrink, one ruled-out choice at a time, until the rational answer is all that's left.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish strictly from weakly dominated strategies and write each definition formally.
  • Explain from first principles why a rational player never plays a strictly dominated strategy.
  • Run iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS) on a payoff matrix.
  • State why IESDS is order-independent for strict dominance — but not for weak dominance.
  • Connect surviving strategies to rationalizability and use elimination to locate Nash equilibria, then verify with nashpy.

2.1What It Means to Be Dominated

In Lecture 1 a dominant strategy was one you'd always want to play. Now flip it around: a dominated strategy is one you'd never want to play, because some other strategy does at least as well — and sometimes better — against everything the opponent might do. Spotting these is the cheapest tool in game theory: you don't need to find the equilibrium, you just delete the choices a rational player would never touch.

Let player i choose strategy s while everyone else plays the profile s-i, giving payoff ui(s, s-i). Strategy s is strictly dominated by an alternative s' when:

u_i(s', s_-i) > u_i(s, s_-i)  for EVERY s_-is' pays strictly more than s against every possible choice of the opponents — s is strictly dominated.

The keyword is every. It is not enough for s' to win on average or in the likely case; it must win in all columns. That strong requirement is exactly what makes the conclusion airtight — a rational, payoff-maximizing player can rule out s without knowing anything about what the opponent will do. No beliefs, no probabilities, no equilibrium needed: a strictly dominated strategy is simply garbage.

AnalogyThink of two commuting routes where one is faster no matter the traffic — rain or shine, rush hour or midnight, Route A always beats Route B by some margin. You don't need a weather forecast to delete Route B; it loses in every state of the world. A strictly dominated strategy is the route you can cross off your map before you even check the conditions.

2.2Strict vs Weak Dominance

The dangerous cousin of strict dominance is weak dominance. Strategy s is weakly dominated by s' when s' does at least as well everywhere, and strictly better in at least one case:

u_i(s', s_-i) >= u_i(s, s_-i) for all s_-i,  and > for at least one s_-iWeak dominance: s' never does worse, and sometimes does better. The ties are what make it slippery.

The difference looks small but matters enormously. With strict dominance, a rational player certainly abandons s — there is never any reason to keep it. With weak dominance, s sometimes ties s', so a player who happens to expect the opponent to play one of those tying columns is not strictly hurt by keeping s. That single "tie" is why weak dominance behaves badly under iteration, as we'll see in 2.4.

In practice: use strict dominance to delete with confidence; treat weak dominance as a softer hint that can throw away genuine equilibria if you lean on it too hard.

2.3Iterated Elimination (IESDS)

One deletion rarely solves a game. The power comes from iteration: delete a dominated strategy, and the smaller game may reveal a new strategy that is dominated only now that an opponent's option is gone. Repeat until nothing more can be removed. This is iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS).

The logic chains together cleanly: I am rational, so I drop my dominated strategy. I also know you are rational, so you drop yours. I know that you know that I'm rational, and so on. Each layer of "common knowledge of rationality" justifies one more round of deletion.

Three facts make IESDS safe and useful: (1) a strictly dominated strategy is never part of any Nash equilibrium, so deleting it throws away nothing real; (2) every Nash equilibrium of the original game survives all the way to the end; and (3) if the game collapses to a single cell, that cell must be the unique Nash equilibrium.

Common mistakeDeleting a strategy because it's dominated in some columns is wrong — that throws away strategies that are perfectly good best responses elsewhere. Strict dominance requires the alternative to win in every column. If you can find even one opponent choice where s ties or beats s', then s is not strictly dominated by s' and you may not delete it.

2.4Order-Independence & Rationalizability

A natural worry: if there are several dominated strategies, does the order in which I delete them change the answer? For strict dominance the answer is a reassuring no — the final surviving set is the same regardless of the order you eliminate in. The reason is structural: if s is strictly dominated in the full game, it stays strictly dominated in any subgame you reach by deleting other strategies, because removing columns can only remove constraints, never reverse a strict inequality.

Strict dominance → order-independent (unique survivor set)You can eliminate strictly dominated strategies in any sequence and reach the same reduced game.

For weak dominance this breaks. Deleting a weakly dominated strategy can erase a column that was the very reason another strategy looked weakly dominated — so different orders give different survivors, and you can accidentally delete a real Nash equilibrium. This is the single most important caveat in the topic, and the reason theorists default to strict dominance.

The strategies that survive IESDS have a name: they are (closely related to) the rationalizable strategies. Informally, a strategy is rationalizable if it is a best response to some reasonable belief about the opponents, where those beliefs themselves only put weight on rationalizable strategies. Rationalizability is the largest set of choices consistent with common knowledge of rationality — it never assumes players coordinate on an equilibrium, only that nobody plays something indefensible.

AnalogyRationalizability is like a debate where every claim must be backed up: a strategy "survives" only if you can give a story — a belief about the opponent — under which it's your best move, and that belief must itself be defensible by the same standard. Nash equilibrium demands more (the beliefs must be correct); rationalizability just demands that you never play something you couldn't justify to anyone.
✦ Worked Example — solve a game by IESDS

Row picks U, M, or D; Column picks L, C, or R. Payoffs (Row, Column):

          L         C         R
   U   (4, 3)    (5, 1)    (6, 2)
   M   (2, 1)    (8, 4)    (3, 6)
   D   (3, 0)    (9, 6)    (2, 8)
  1. Check Column's strategy C vs R. Column's payoffs — C: (1, 4, 6); R: (2, 6, 8). For every row, R > C (2>1, 6>4, 8>6). So C is strictly dominated by R for Column. Delete column C.
  2. Now compare Row's M vs U in the reduced 3×2 game (columns L, R only). Row M: (2, 3); Row U: (4, 6). U beats M in both (4>2, 6>3). So M is strictly dominated by U. Delete row M.
  3. Compare Row's D vs U (columns L, R). Row D: (3, 2); Row U: (4, 6). U beats D in both (4>3, 6>2). So D is strictly dominated by U. Delete row D. Only row U remains.
  4. Column now chooses between L and R given Row = U. Column gets 3 from L vs 2 from R. So Column plays L.
  5. Survivor: (U, L) = (4, 3). The game is dominance-solvable, so (U, L) is the unique Nash equilibrium — confirm by noting U is Row's best response to L, and L is Column's best response to U.

🛠️ Hands-On: automate the elimination in Python

Verify: run python -c "import nashpy, quantecon; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

We'll write a tiny strict-dominance reducer for the row player, then let nashpy confirm the equilibrium of the worked example. Rows are (U, M, D); columns are (L, C, R).

# iesds.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import nashpy as nash

# Row payoffs A, Column payoffs B. Order: rows U,M,D | cols L,C,R
A = np.array([[4, 5, 6],
              [2, 8, 3],
              [3, 9, 2]])
B = np.array([[3, 1, 2],
              [1, 4, 6],
              [0, 6, 8]])

def strictly_dominated_rows(M):
    # return indices of rows strictly dominated by another row
    dom = []
    for i in range(M.shape[0]):
        for k in range(M.shape[0]):
            if k != i and np.all(M[k] > M[i]):
                dom.append(i); break
    return dom

print("Row strictly-dominated indices:", strictly_dominated_rows(A))
# Column dominance: compare COLUMNS of B, so transpose it
print("Col strictly-dominated indices:", strictly_dominated_rows(B.T))

# Let nashpy enumerate the equilibrium of the full game
game = nash.Game(A, B)
for eq in game.support_enumeration():
    print(eq)              # ([1,0,0],[1,0,0]) -> Row U, Column L

The reducer flags column C (index 1) as strictly dominated, kicking off the chain that leaves (U, L); nashpy independently returns the same pure equilibrium. Try editing A and B so that no strategy is strictly dominated — you'll see the reducer return an empty list, a reminder that dominance only solves some games.

🔗 Where this is heading

Dominance is the warm-up. Many real games — including the oligopoly models in Week 7 — have no dominated strategies at all, so we'll need the heavier machinery of mixed strategies (Week 3), where players randomize and we hunt for equilibria in probabilities rather than pure choices. Iterated reasoning also reappears in extensive-form games as backward induction, and the "everyone shaves toward the rational answer" logic powers the famous beauty-contest and Cournot adjustment stories. In the Quant Finance track, the same elimination-of-indefensible-actions idea underlies how traders prune strategies that can never be optimal before committing capital.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Strategy s is strictly dominated by s' when s' pays more than s…
A. against every possible choice of the opponents
B. on average across the opponents' choices
C. against at least one choice of the opponents
D. only when the opponents are also rational
Show answer

A. Strict dominance requires s' > s in every column. "On average" (B) or "at least one" (C) is far too weak and would delete good strategies; rationality of opponents (D) isn't part of the definition of dominance itself.

Q2. What separates weak dominance from strict dominance?
A. Weak dominance requires a strictly higher payoff in every column
B. Under weak dominance s' is never worse and sometimes strictly better, allowing ties
C. Weak dominance only applies to the column player
D. Weak dominance guarantees a unique Nash equilibrium
Show answer

B. Weak dominance allows ties (≥ everywhere, > somewhere). A is the definition of strict dominance; dominance applies to both players (C); and weak dominance gives no uniqueness guarantee — it can even destroy equilibria (D).

Q3. Why is it safe to delete a strictly dominated strategy when hunting for Nash equilibria?
A. Because it lowers the total payoff of the game
B. Because deleting it always produces a single surviving cell
C. Because a strictly dominated strategy is never part of any Nash equilibrium
D. Because it is the same as a mixed strategy
Show answer

C. No Nash equilibrium ever uses a strictly dominated strategy, so removing it loses no equilibria. Deletion doesn't always reduce to one cell (B) — only dominance-solvable games do — and A and D are unrelated.

Q4. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies is order-independent. This means…
A. the surviving set of strategies is the same no matter the sequence of deletions
B. you must always delete rows before columns
C. the order changes the answer, so you must try all orders
D. it only works in symmetric games
Show answer

A. Strict dominance is preserved as columns are removed, so any deletion order yields the identical reduced game. There's no required row-then-column rule (B); order does not change the answer (C); and symmetry is irrelevant (D). Note this fails for weak dominance.

Q5. A strategy is rationalizable if it…
A. is dominated by every other strategy
B. maximizes the combined payoff of both players
C. is chosen with positive probability in every game
D. is a best response to some belief that itself puts weight only on rationalizable strategies
Show answer

D. Rationalizability is exactly "defensible as a best response under beliefs that are themselves defensible." A describes the opposite (a dominated strategy); B is joint efficiency, not individual rationality; C is simply false — many strategies are never used.
