Week 6 · Lecture 6

Repeated Games & Cooperation

When today's rivals will meet again tomorrow, the shadow of the future can turn a hopeless dilemma into a stable partnership.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish finitely from infinitely repeated games and explain why the endpoint matters.
  • Define the discount factor δ and use it to sum a stream of future payoffs.
  • Describe trigger strategies and tit-for-tat as ways to punish defection.
  • Derive the threshold on δ that makes cooperation a subgame-perfect equilibrium of the repeated prisoner's dilemma.
  • State the folk theorem intuitively: patience makes almost any "reasonable" outcome sustainable.

6.1Playing the Same Game Over and Over

In Lecture 1 the prisoner's dilemma had a grim verdict: both players defect, even though both would prefer mutual cooperation. That analysis assumed the game is played once. But most real strategic situations recur — two suppliers quote prices to the same buyers every quarter, neighbors share a fence year after year, market makers face each other across thousands of trades a day. When a game is played repeatedly, players can condition today's move on the history of past moves, and that single change reopens the door to cooperation.

A repeated game takes a one-shot game — called the stage game — and plays it in periods t = 0, 1, 2, … Before each period, both players observe everything that happened before. A strategy in the repeated game is therefore not a single action but a plan: a rule mapping any possible history to the action you will take next.

strategy : (history of past play) -> action this periodThe power of repetition comes entirely from making your current action depend on what the opponent did before.

The decisive distinction is whether the game has a known last period. A finitely repeated game ends after T periods; an infinitely repeated game (or one that ends randomly with some probability each period) has no fixed final round. As we will see, that difference completely changes what cooperation is possible.

AnalogyA single transaction with a stranger you'll never see again is a one-shot game — cheat and walk away. A standing relationship with a regular trading partner is a repeated game: the value of every future deal hangs on your reputation today, so cheating now forfeits a whole stream of tomorrows. Repetition turns "what can I grab right now?" into "what is this relationship worth?"

6.2The Discount Factor: Valuing the Future

To compare a payoff today against a payoff next period, we need an exchange rate between present and future. That rate is the discount factor δ (delta), a number between 0 and 1. A payoff of 1 received next period is worth only δ today; a payoff two periods out is worth δ², and so on. Patient players have δ close to 1; impatient players have δ close to 0.

Where does δ come from? Two sources blend into it: a genuine time preference (a dollar now beats a dollar later, captured by an interest rate r so that δ = 1/(1+r)), and the probability p that the relationship simply continues to the next period. If a game ends with probability 1 − p each period, the effective discount factor is the product of survival and impatience. Either way, δ measures how much the shadow of the future weighs on today's choice.

The lifetime value of receiving a constant payoff c in every period is a geometric series:

V = c + δc + δ²c + δ³c + ... = c / (1 - δ)Sum of an infinite geometric series with ratio δ < 1. Cooperating forever at per-period payoff c is worth c/(1 − δ) today.

This single formula is the engine of everything that follows. A one-time gain looks big in the period you grab it, but a permanent change of c per period is worth a factor of 1/(1 − δ) more — and when δ is near 1, that factor is enormous. Patience magnifies the value of an ongoing relationship.

Common mistakeDon't read δ as "how much I care about other people." It is purely about how much I value my own future payoffs — time preference plus the odds the game keeps going. A perfectly selfish player can still cooperate if δ is high, precisely because their own future is valuable. Cooperation here is enlightened self-interest, not altruism.

6.3Trigger Strategies & Tit-for-Tat

If today's action can depend on the past, players can make a credible threat of punishment. The simplest such plan is the grim trigger: cooperate as long as everyone has always cooperated, but the instant the opponent defects, switch to defect forever. One betrayal triggers permanent retaliation — there is no forgiveness.

A gentler alternative is tit-for-tat: start by cooperating, then in each period simply copy whatever the opponent did last period. Defect once and tit-for-tat punishes you once; return to cooperation and it forgives immediately. Tit-for-tat is famous for being nice (it never defects first), retaliatory (it punishes defection), forgiving (it restores cooperation), and clear (the opponent can read it instantly).

The point of either threat is the same: it makes a one-time defection unprofitable. You might grab a bigger payoff this period by defecting, but you forfeit the stream of cooperative payoffs you'd have earned otherwise. Whether that trade is worth it depends entirely on δ — which is exactly what we compute next.

6.4When Does Cooperation Survive? The Folk Theorem

Take the prisoner's dilemma from Lecture 1, with stage payoffs: mutual cooperation = 3 each, mutual defection = 1 each, and the temptation/sucker split = (5, 0). Suppose both players use grim trigger. A player weighing whether to defect this period compares two futures.

If you keep cooperating, you collect 3 every period forever:

V_coop = 3 / (1 - δ)Geometric sum of 3 per period.

If you defect now, you grab the temptation payoff 5 this period, but trigger permanent punishment — 1 in every period thereafter:

V_defect = 5 + δ·1/(1 - δ) = 5 + δ/(1 - δ)One-time gain of 5, then the punishment payoff 1 forever, discounted.

Cooperation is sustainable as a Nash (indeed subgame-perfect) equilibrium when staying loyal beats deviating, V_coop ≥ V_defect. Solving:

3/(1 - δ) ≥ 5 + δ/(1 - δ) => δ ≥ 1/2The temptation must be outweighed by the discounted loss of future cooperation. Here patience above one-half does it.

So if players are patient enough (δ ≥ ½ for these numbers), grim trigger makes mutual cooperation a self-enforcing equilibrium. The threat never has to be carried out on the equilibrium path — its mere credibility keeps everyone honest. This is the deep insight: repetition does not change the stage game, it changes the strategy space, and that is enough to rescue cooperation.

Finite horizon: the unraveling problem

Now suppose everyone knows the game ends at period T. In the last period there is no future to protect, so both defect — it's just the one-shot dilemma. But then period T−1 has no credible future punishment either (everyone already knows T will be all-defect), so they defect there too. This logic, called backward unraveling, marches all the way back: with a known finite end and a unique stage equilibrium, the only subgame-perfect outcome is to defect every period. Cooperation needs either an infinite horizon or genuine uncertainty about when the game ends.

The folk theorem generalizes the δ ≥ ½ result. Informally: in an infinitely repeated game with patient enough players, essentially any outcome that gives every player more than their guaranteed (minmax) payoff can be sustained as an equilibrium. The set of achievable average payoffs balloons from the single one-shot equilibrium to a whole region. Repetition is powerful — perhaps too powerful, since it predicts a vast multiplicity of equilibria rather than one sharp answer.

✦ Worked Example — does cooperation hold?

Two suppliers repeatedly choose to keep a high price (Cooperate) or undercut (Defect). Per-period profits: both High = 10; both undercut = 4; the lone undercutter gets 16 while the loyal firm gets 2. Both use grim trigger. For what discount factor δ is collusion at the high price sustainable?

  1. Value of always cooperating: 10 every period -> V_coop = 10/(1 − δ).
  2. Value of defecting now: grab 16 this period, then trigger forever-punishment at 4 -> V_defect = 16 + δ·4/(1 − δ).
  3. No-deviation condition: 10/(1 − δ) ≥ 16 + 4δ/(1 − δ).
  4. Clear the denominator (multiply by 1 − δ > 0): 10 ≥ 16(1 − δ) + 4δ = 16 − 12δ.
  5. Solve: 12δ ≥ 6 -> δ ≥ 1/2. If both suppliers value the future at least half as much as the present, high-price collusion is a self-enforcing equilibrium.
  6. Interpretation: raise the temptation payoff (say 16 -> 30) and the required δ rises — bigger short-run gains from cheating demand more patience to resist. This is exactly why fragile cartels watch for any sign that a rival is becoming short-sighted.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate a repeated tournament

We reuse the free QuantEcon + nashpy + NumPy stack from the Week 1 setup (the one-time install details lives in Lecture 1). No new packages are needed here — we'll write a tiny strategy simulator in plain NumPy to watch trigger strategies enforce cooperation.

# repeated_pd.py — Lecture 6: do trigger strategies sustain cooperation?
import numpy as np

# stage payoffs for the ROW player, rows/cols = (C, D)
PAY = np.array([[3, 0],     # C vs (C, D)
                [5, 1]])    # D vs (C, D)

def grim(history_opp):
    # cooperate (0) until opponent has ever defected (1), then defect forever
    return 1 if (history_opp == 1).any() else 0

def tit_for_tat(history_opp):
    return 0 if len(history_opp) == 0 else history_opp[-1]

def play(stratA, stratB, periods, delta):
    histA, histB, total = [], [], 0.0
    for t in range(periods):
        a = stratA(np.array(histB, dtype=int))   # A reacts to B's past
        b = stratB(np.array(histA, dtype=int))
        total += (delta ** t) * PAY[a, b]         # A's discounted payoff
        histA.append(a); histB.append(b)
    return total

delta = 0.6
print("both grim, A's value:", round(play(grim, grim, 50, delta), 3))
print("A defects (always-D) vs grim B:",
      round(play(lambda h: 1, grim, 50, delta), 3))

With δ = 0.6 (above the ½ threshold) loyal cooperation beats the always-defect deviation — defecting once collects 5 but then earns only 1 forever after. Lower delta below 0.5 and the deviation wins, reproducing the breakdown of cooperation by hand. Swap in tit_for_tat to see forgiveness restore cooperation after a single slip.

💬 Discussion

Real-world relationships rarely have a literally infinite horizon, yet cooperation between long-standing partners is everywhere. If the backward-unraveling argument says any known finite end destroys cooperation, why do we observe so much sustained cooperation in practice? Pick a concrete setting (suppliers, neighbors, repeat trading counterparties) and argue what feature of the real situation lets cooperation survive — and what could make it suddenly collapse.

Show a model response

The unraveling argument needs two strong ingredients: a commonly known last period and a unique stage equilibrium. Real relationships usually violate the first. Consider two suppliers who deal every quarter: neither knows exactly when the relationship will end — a contract might renew, a buyer might stay, the firm might keep operating. That open-endedness acts like an infinite horizon with a per-period continuation probability p, giving an effective discount factor δ_eff = δ·p. As long as δ_eff clears the threshold (½ in our numbers), grim-trigger cooperation is an equilibrium even though the relationship is, in expectation, finite. The mere possibility of a tomorrow is enough; certainty about the end is what kills it.

Cooperation collapses precisely when that future shrinks: news that a buyer is leaving, a partner nearing retirement, or a looming bankruptcy all push p (and δ_eff) down. Once δ_eff falls below the threshold, the one-time temptation outweighs the discounted value of the relationship, and rational partners defect — often pre-emptively, racing to cheat first. This is why behavior frequently sours in the final, observable rounds of a known-to-be-ending relationship, exactly as the model predicts.

A second realistic escape valve is uncertainty about the opponent's type: if there's a chance the other side is a committed cooperator, even fully rational players may cooperate to protect a valuable reputation, delaying defection well past where pure unraveling would bite.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) identifies that unraveling requires a known finite end, (2) ties continuation probability p to an effective discount factor and the δ-threshold, (3) names a concrete trigger that lowers p and breaks cooperation, and (4) connects the prediction to observed end-game behavior or reputation effects.

Q1. The discount factor δ in a repeated game primarily measures…
A. how much a player values future payoffs relative to present ones
B. how much a player cares about the opponent's welfare
C. the number of times the stage game is played
D. the probability that a player makes a mistake
Show answer

A. δ blends time preference with the chance the game continues — purely about the value of one's own future. (B) is wrong because cooperation here is self-interest, not altruism; (C) describes the horizon, not δ; (D) describes trembling-hand noise, a different concept.

Q2. In a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma with a commonly known last period and a unique stage equilibrium, the only subgame-perfect outcome is…
A. cooperate in every period
B. cooperate until the last period, then defect once
C. defect in every period
D. randomize 50–50 each period
Show answer

C. Backward unraveling: the last period is the one-shot dilemma (defect), which removes any credible future punishment in T−1, and so on back to period 1. (A) and (B) fail because no future threat is credible once the end is known; (D) has no basis here.

Q3. The lifetime value of receiving a constant payoff c every period, discounted by δ, equals…
A. c · δ
B. c / (1 − δ)
C. c · (1 − δ)
D. c / δ
Show answer

B. The geometric sum c + δc + δ²c + … = c/(1 − δ). (A) and (C) are single-term scalings; (D) inverts the relationship and isn't the series sum.

Q4. A grim-trigger player who observes a single defection by the opponent will…
A. forgive after punishing exactly once
B. ignore it and keep cooperating
C. defect for exactly two periods
D. defect in every remaining period
Show answer

D. Grim trigger is unforgiving — one defection triggers permanent retaliation. (A) describes tit-for-tat's one-period punishment; (B) is no punishment at all; (C) is an arbitrary fixed-length penalty, not the grim rule.

Q5. Using the Lecture-1 payoffs (cooperate = 3, temptation = 5, mutual defection = 1), cooperation under grim trigger is sustainable when…
A. δ ≥ 1/2
B. δ ≤ 1/2
C. δ = 0
D. for any δ between 0 and 1
Show answer

A. Setting 3/(1−δ) ≥ 5 + δ/(1−δ) gives δ ≥ ½: players must be patient enough that lost future cooperation outweighs the one-time temptation. (B) and (C) describe impatient players who defect; (D) is false because low δ breaks the threat.

✓ What you learned

  • A repeated game lets strategies condition on history, which reopens the door to cooperation a one-shot game forecloses.
  • The discount factor δ values the future; a constant payoff c per period is worth c/(1 − δ).
  • Trigger strategies (grim) and tit-for-tat make defection unprofitable by threatening future punishment.
  • Cooperation is a subgame-perfect equilibrium when δ clears a threshold (δ ≥ ½ for the Lecture-1 payoffs); a known finite end causes backward unraveling.
  • The folk theorem: with patient players and an infinite horizon, almost any individually rational outcome can be sustained.
