Mixed Strategies

When being predictable is fatal, the smartest move is to roll your own dice — and Nash proved a stable way to do it always exists.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a mixed strategy as a probability distribution over a player's pure actions.
  • Compute the expected payoff of a mixed strategy against an opponent's mix.
  • State and apply the indifference principle: a player randomizes only across actions that are all best responses.
  • Explain why matching pennies has no pure equilibrium but a unique mixed one.
  • State Nash's existence theorem and verify a mixed equilibrium with nashpy.

3.1When Predictability Loses

In Weeks 1 and 2 we hunted for pure-strategy Nash equilibria — each player commits to one action. But some games punish you the moment your behavior becomes predictable. A penalty taker who always shoots left is easy to stop; a poker player who only bets with strong hands is easy to fold against. In these games the rational thing to do is to deliberately randomize.

A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over your pure actions. If you have two actions, a mixed strategy is a single number: the probability p you play the first action (and 1 − p the second). A pure strategy is just the special case where one action gets probability 1.

sigma = (p, 1 - p),  with  0 <= p <= 1A mixed strategy over two actions: play action 1 with probability p, action 2 with probability 1 - p.

Randomizing is not "playing badly" or hedging out of confusion. It is a precise, optimal response to an opponent who is also trying to read you. The randomness is the point: it makes you impossible to exploit.

AnalogyThink of a tax auditor and a taxpayer. If the auditor always audits, honesty is forced but auditing is expensive; if the auditor never audits, everyone cheats. The auditor's stable policy is to audit with some probability — just high enough to keep cheating unprofitable. Tax-enforcement agencies really do randomize audits for exactly this reason.

3.2Expected Payoff of a Mix

To evaluate a mixed strategy we average payoffs by their probabilities — an expected payoff. Suppose the row player mixes with probability p on action Up and the column player's choice is fixed. The row player's expected payoff is just the probability-weighted average of the payoffs Up and Down would give:

E[u_row] = p · u(Up) + (1 - p) · u(Down)Expected payoff is linear in p — a straight line as p runs from 0 to 1.

Because expected payoff is linear in your own probability p, a key fact follows: if Up and Down give you different expected payoffs, you should pile all your probability onto the better one (p = 0 or p = 1). You only have a reason to genuinely mix (0 < p < 1) when the two actions give you exactly the same expected payoff. Hold onto that — it is the engine of the whole theory.

3.3The Indifference Principle

Here is the central idea, and it surprises everyone the first time. In a mixed equilibrium, you do not choose your own probabilities to make yourself happy. You choose them to make your opponent indifferent — because only an indifferent opponent is willing to randomize, which is what your own equilibrium mix requires of them.

A player mixes over several actions <=> all of those actions are best responsesThe indifference principle: any action played with positive probability must yield the maximum expected payoff. Inferior actions get probability 0.

Read it both directions. If an action gives strictly less expected payoff than another, a rational player puts zero weight on it. So every action that gets positive weight must tie for the best expected payoff. That gives us a clean recipe: to find player A's equilibrium mix, write down player B's expected payoffs for each of B's actions as functions of A's probability, then set those expressions equal and solve.

Common mistakeStudents set a player's own mix to equalize their own payoffs. Wrong direction. Your mix is pinned down by making the other player indifferent; the other player's mix is what makes you indifferent. Each player's randomization probabilities are solved from the opponent's payoff numbers, not their own. Mixing up whose payoffs go into which equation is the number-one error on this topic.

3.4Matching Pennies & Nash's Theorem

The cleanest example is matching pennies. Each player secretly shows Heads or Tails. The "matcher" wins if the coins agree; the "mismatcher" wins if they differ. One player's gain is exactly the other's loss — a zero-sum game.

Chase the best responses around the matrix and you never stop: wherever you land, one player wants to move. There is no pure equilibrium. By symmetry, the unique mixed equilibrium has each player choosing Heads with probability ½ — perfectly unpredictable, so neither can be exploited. We will derive that ½ formally in the worked example.

Does randomizing always rescue us? Yes. This is the crown jewel of the field:

Nash's Existence TheoremEvery finite game (finitely many players, each with finitely many actions) has at least one Nash equilibrium — possibly in mixed strategies.

The proof uses a fixed-point theorem: the best-response map on the (compact, convex) space of mixed-strategy profiles must have a fixed point, and that fixed point is an equilibrium. The practical upshot is enormous — we never have to worry that a sensible model of competition simply "has no answer." It always has one; our job is to compute it (Week 4).

✦ Worked Example — solve matching pennies

Use the matrix above. Let the matcher play Heads with probability p and the mismatcher play Heads with probability q. Find the equilibrium (p*, q*).

  1. Pin down p* via the mismatcher's indifference. The matcher's mix p must make the mismatcher indifferent between Heads and Tails.
  2. Mismatcher plays Heads: payoff is −1 when matcher shows Heads (prob p) and +1 when matcher shows Tails (prob 1−p) → E = −p + (1−p) = 1 − 2p.
  3. Mismatcher plays Tails: payoff is +1 when matcher shows Heads and −1 otherwise → E = p − (1−p) = 2p − 1.
  4. Set equal: 1 − 2p = 2p − 1 → 4p = 2 → p* = ½.
  5. By the symmetric argument (the matcher's payoffs are the negatives), q* = ½ makes the matcher indifferent too. So q* = ½.
  6. Equilibrium: each player plays Heads and Tails with probability ½. Each player's expected payoff is 0 — fair, and unexploitable. Check: at p = ½ the mismatcher gets 1 − 2(½) = 0 either way, confirming indifference.

🛠️ Hands-On: find the mixed equilibrium in Python

Same free QuantEcon + nashpy + NumPy stack from the Week 1 setup (the install details lives in Lecture 1 — reuse that environment; nothing new to install here). Support enumeration returns mixed equilibria automatically, as probability vectors.

# matching_pennies.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
import nashpy as nash

# rows/cols = (Heads, Tails); row = matcher, col = mismatcher
A = np.array([[ 1, -1],      # matcher's payoffs
              [-1,  1]])
B = np.array([[-1,  1],      # mismatcher's payoffs (= -A, zero-sum)
              [ 1, -1]])

game = nash.Game(A, B)
for eq in game.support_enumeration():
    print(eq)                # (array([0.5, 0.5]), array([0.5, 0.5]))

# expected payoff to the matcher under the equilibrium
sigma_r, sigma_c = next(game.support_enumeration())
print("matcher E[payoff] =", sigma_r @ A @ sigma_c)   # 0.0

The output ([0.5, 0.5], [0.5, 0.5]) is exactly our hand calculation: both players mix 50/50, expected payoff 0. Try skewing the win values (say the matcher wins +2 on Heads-Heads) and watch p* move away from ½ — the indifference equation rebalances it.

💬 Discussion

Real penalty takers, poker players, and tax authorities clearly do not pull out a random-number generator before each decision — yet game theory predicts they should randomize. Is the mixed-strategy prediction actually realistic? What real-world mechanism could let a person (or an institution) behave "as if" they were randomizing with the equilibrium probabilities, even without consciously flipping a coin? And when might the prediction fail?

Show a model response

A mixed equilibrium need not describe a single person literally randomizing. Three readings make it realistic:

(1) Population / frequency interpretation. If a large population of takers faces a population of keepers, "shoot left with probability 0.4" can mean 40% of takers shoot left. No individual randomizes, yet the aggregate frequencies match the equilibrium mix — and empirical studies of penalty kicks and tennis serves find frequencies strikingly close to the theoretical values.

(2) Unpredictability from many hidden factors. A skilled player conditions on fatigue, the score, a hunch, the last few rounds — a private state the opponent cannot observe. From the opponent's side this looks like a draw from a distribution, which is exactly what the equilibrium needs.

(3) Learning / no-regret dynamics. Players who adjust toward whatever has worked recently tend to converge, on average, to the equilibrium frequencies even without knowing the theory — the mix emerges from adaptation.

When it fails: humans are notoriously bad at being random (we under-produce repeats), the game may not be one-shot (reputation and signaling change it), or one side has an exploitable tell. Then observed play drifts from the 50/50-style prediction — and exploiting that drift is itself a strategic edge.

A strong answer: (a) distinguishes literal randomization from a frequency/population reading; (b) names a concrete mechanism (hidden private state or learning dynamics) that produces "as-if" randomness; (c) ties the equilibrium probabilities back to the indifference principle; and (d) gives at least one realistic condition under which the prediction breaks down.

❓ Lecture 3 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A mixed strategy is best described as…
A. a probability distribution over a player's pure actions
B. a strategy that mixes two players' payoffs together
C. the average of all players' best responses
D. a strategy guaranteed to win
Show answer

A. A mixed strategy assigns probabilities to your own pure actions. B and C confuse it with combining players; D is impossible — no strategy guarantees a win against a rational opponent.

Q2. Why is matching pennies the classic example of needing mixed strategies?
A. Both players have a dominant strategy
B. It has two pure-strategy Nash equilibria
C. It has no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium, only a mixed one
D. Its only equilibrium is (Cooperate, Cooperate)
Show answer

C. Best responses chase each other endlessly, so no cell is stable — the only equilibrium is the 50/50 mix. There is no dominant strategy (A) and no pure equilibrium at all (B); cooperation (D) is from a different game.

Q3. The indifference principle says that in a mixed equilibrium, every action you play with positive probability must…
A. have the lowest expected payoff
B. yield the same (maximal) expected payoff as your other played actions
C. be a dominant strategy
D. be played with equal probability
Show answer

B. Any action with positive weight must tie for the best expected payoff; otherwise you'd shift weight off it. A is backwards, C isn't required, and equal probabilities (D) only happen in special symmetric games like matching pennies.

Q4. When solving for the row player's equilibrium mixing probability, you set equal the expected payoffs of…
A. the row player's own two actions, using the row player's payoffs
B. the column player's two actions, using the column player's payoffs
C. both players' total payoffs
D. the row player's actions across two different games
Show answer

B. The row player's mix is chosen to make the column player indifferent, so you equate the column player's expected payoffs (using the column player's numbers). Choice A is the most common mistake — solving with the wrong player's payoffs.

Q5. Nash's existence theorem guarantees that…
A. every game has a unique equilibrium
B. every game has a pure-strategy equilibrium
C. only zero-sum games have equilibria
D. every finite game has at least one equilibrium, possibly in mixed strategies
Show answer

D. Existence is guaranteed for any finite game once randomization is allowed — but it need not be unique (A), need not be pure (B), and applies far beyond zero-sum games (C).

✓ What you learned

  • A mixed strategy is a probability distribution over your pure actions; pure play is the p = 1 special case.
  • Expected payoff is linear in your own probability — so you only mix when actions tie.
  • The indifference principle: you randomize only over best responses, and your mix is set to make the opponent indifferent.
  • Matching pennies has no pure equilibrium but a unique 50/50 mixed one — provably unexploitable.
  • Nash's existence theorem guarantees every finite game has an equilibrium, which we verified with nashpy.
