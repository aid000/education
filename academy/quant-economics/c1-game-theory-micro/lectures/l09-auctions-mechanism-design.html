Auctions & Mechanism Design

When the seller cannot see what a bidder is truly willing to pay, the right rules can make honesty the winning strategy.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish the four classic auction formats: first-price sealed-bid, second-price (Vickrey), English, and Dutch.
  • Derive the equilibrium bid in a first-price auction and explain bid shading.
  • Prove that truthful bidding is a (weakly) dominant strategy in a second-price auction.
  • State the Revenue Equivalence Theorem and the assumptions it rests on.
  • Define a mechanism and incentive compatibility, and verify the second-price rule with Python.

9.1Four Ways to Sell One Object

An auction is a game where the rules of the sale — not a posted price — decide who wins and what they pay. It is the purest stage for strategic reasoning: each bidder knows their own value for the object but only has beliefs about everyone else's. The seller designs the rules; the bidders best-respond. Four formats dominate practice:

  • First-price sealed-bid: everyone submits one secret bid; highest bid wins and pays its own bid.
  • Second-price sealed-bid (Vickrey): highest bid wins but pays the second-highest bid.
  • English (ascending): price rises openly; bidders drop out until one remains, paying roughly the last drop-out's value.
  • Dutch (descending): price falls from a high start; the first bidder to accept wins and pays that price.

These pair up by strategic equivalence. The Dutch auction is identical to the first-price sealed-bid: in both, the only thing you control is the single price at which you'd commit, and you learn nothing before committing. The English auction maps onto the second-price rule: staying in until the price reaches your value, then dropping, means the winner pays essentially the runner-up's value.

Dutch ≡ First-price   |   English ≈ Second-priceTwo open (dynamic) formats collapse onto the two sealed-bid formats once you ask "what do I actually decide, and what do I know when I decide it?"

We model the standard independent private values (IPV) setting: each bidder i draws a value vi independently from a common, known distribution, and values their own object only (no resale, no common unknown quality). This is the workhorse case for online ad auctions, spectrum sales, and procurement.

9.2The Second-Price Auction Is Truthful

The Vickrey auction has a remarkable property: bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy — it is at least as good as any other bid no matter what the others do. The reason is that your bid never sets your price; it only decides whether you win. The price you pay, if you win, is the highest competing bid.

Let your true value be v and let b = the highest bid among everyone else. Compare bidding truthfully (your bid = v) against shading down or up:

If you win you pay b, not your bid -> only winning vs losing is at stakeBid = v wins exactly when v > b, and then earns surplus v - b > 0; it loses exactly when v < b, where winning would mean paying more than v.

Walk the cases. If you raise your bid above v, the only new outcomes you create are wins in the range b between v and your inflated bid — but there you pay b > v and book a loss. If you shade below v, the only new outcomes are losses in the range b between your bid and v — wins you forfeit that would have paid you positive surplus v − b. Either deviation can only hurt or do nothing. So truth-telling is weakly dominant, and you never need to know or guess the others' values.

AnalogyThink of the price tag as written by your rivals, not by you. In a second-price auction your bid is just an instruction — "buy it for me as long as the price stays at or below this number." Naming your honest reservation price is exactly the instruction that buys whenever the deal is good and walks away whenever it isn't. Shading the number can only make you miss a good deal or chase a bad one.

9.3First-Price Equilibrium & Bid Shading

In a first-price auction you pay your own bid, so bidding your true value guarantees zero surplus — you must shade your bid below v. But how much? Shade too little and you overpay; shade too much and you lose winnable auctions. The equilibrium balances these at the margin.

Take the symmetric IPV benchmark: n bidders, each value drawn independently and uniformly on [0, 1]. Suppose every rival uses the same increasing bidding function b(v). A standard equilibrium analysis (maximize expected surplus, then solve the resulting differential equation) yields a clean linear rule:

b(v) = ( (n - 1) / n ) · vWith n bidders and uniform[0,1] values, each bidder shades by the factor (n-1)/n. Example: n = 2 -> bid v/2; n = 5 -> bid 0.8v.

The intuition is sharp. With only n = 2 bidders, you bid half your value — competition is weak, so you grab a big margin. As n grows, (n−1)/n → 1: the more rivals you face, the closer you must bid to your true value, because the threat of being outbid squeezes your shading toward zero. Competition, not the format, is what drives prices up.

Common mistakeDo not assume the second-price auction earns the seller less just because the winner "pays the lower bid." In a first-price auction bidders shade down; in a second-price auction they bid their full value but pay only the runner-up's. These two effects cancel on average — the expected revenue is the same. Reading off one realized sale and concluding one format is cheaper confuses a single draw with the expectation.

9.4Revenue Equivalence & Mechanism Design

The two analyses above meet in one of the field's landmark results, the Revenue Equivalence Theorem:

All standard auctions yield the same expected revenue...when values are independent and private, bidders are risk-neutral and symmetric, the object always goes to the highest valuer, and a zero-value bidder expects zero surplus.

First-price, second-price, English, and Dutch all hand the seller the same expected revenue. They differ only in how they extract it — through shading or through pay-the-runner-up. This is why a seller's real choice between formats hinges on the fine print the theorem assumes away: risk-averse bidders favor the first-price format, collusion is easier to police in sealed bids, and asymmetric bidders break the tie.

Stepping back, an auction is just one mechanism — a rule that maps the bidders' reported information into an outcome (who wins) and payments. Mechanism design is "game theory in reverse": instead of taking the rules as given and predicting play, the designer chooses the rules so that the equilibrium play produces the outcome they want. The key constraint is incentive compatibility (IC):

A mechanism is incentive-compatible if truth-telling is each player's best responseUnder dominant-strategy IC (DSIC), honesty is optimal regardless of others — exactly the second-price property. The Revelation Principle says any achievable outcome can be implemented this way.

The second-price auction is the textbook DSIC mechanism: its rules make honesty a dominant strategy, so the designer learns everyone's true value "for free." This idea — design the payment rule so that lying never pays — underlies online ad auctions, public-spectrum sales, congestion pricing, and matching systems for schools and organ exchange. It is the bridge from describing strategic behavior to engineering it.

✦ Worked Example — three bidders, two formats

Three bidders have private values v = (90, 60, 40) for one item. (a) Who wins and what do they pay in a second-price (Vickrey) auction if everyone bids truthfully? (b) Suppose instead values are uniform on [0, 100] and there are n = 3 bidders in a first-price auction — what does the bidder with v = 90 bid in the symmetric equilibrium, and what is their surplus if they win?

  1. Vickrey winner: truthful bids are exactly (90, 60, 40). The highest bid, 90, wins.
  2. Vickrey price: the winner pays the second-highest bid = 60. Surplus = 90 − 60 = 30.
  3. Truthfulness check: could the v = 90 bidder do better by bidding 70? No — they still win and still pay 60. Bidding below 60 would forfeit a profitable win. Truth is weakly dominant.
  4. First-price bid: with uniform[0,100] and n = 3, the rule is b(v) = ((n−1)/n)·v = (2/3)·v. For v = 90: b = (2/3)(90) = 60.
  5. First-price surplus (if they win): value minus own bid = 90 − 60 = 30. The two formats deliver the same winner and, here, the same realized surplus — a concrete glimpse of revenue equivalence.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate revenue equivalence

We reuse the free QuantEcon + NumPy stack from Week 1 (the one-time install lives in the Lecture 1 setup box — no new tools needed here). The script below runs a Monte-Carlo tournament: it draws many auctions, simulates truthful second-price bidding and equilibrium first-price shading, and compares average seller revenue.

# revenue_equivalence.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n, trials = 4, 200_000          # 4 bidders, uniform[0,1] values
V = rng.random((trials, n))     # each row = one auction's values

# Second-price: truthful bids -> seller gets the 2nd-highest value
sorted_V = np.sort(V, axis=1)            # ascending
second_price_rev = sorted_V[:, -2]       # runner-up value

# First-price: symmetric eqm bid b(v) = (n-1)/n * v ; winner is top value
shade = (n - 1) / n
bids = shade * V
first_price_rev = bids.max(axis=1)       # winner pays own bid

print(f"2nd-price avg revenue: {second_price_rev.mean():.4f}")
print(f"1st-price avg revenue: {first_price_rev.mean():.4f}")
# Both ~ 0.6000 = (n-1)/(n+1) for uniform[0,1] -> Revenue Equivalence!

Both averages land near the theoretical (n−1)/(n+1) = 3/5 = 0.6. Try changing n: as bidders increase, both revenues rise toward the top value's expectation — and they stay equal. That equality, holding across a random tournament, is the Revenue Equivalence Theorem made visible.

💬 Discussion

The Revenue Equivalence Theorem says first-price and second-price auctions raise the same expected revenue, yet most large online advertising exchanges spent years using second-price rules and then publicly switched many auctions to first-price. If the expected revenue is identical, why would a real-world seller deliberately choose one format over the other? Argue which considerations outside the theorem's assumptions could tip the decision, and what a bidder should change about their behavior after such a switch.

Show a model response

Revenue equivalence holds only under a tight set of assumptions — independent private values, risk-neutral and symmetric bidders, no collusion, no reserve subtleties, and a well-defined value distribution. Real markets violate these, and the violations break the tie:

  • Transparency and trust: a first-price rule is simple to audit — the winner pays what they bid. Second-price rules require the seller to honestly report a "second price" the bidder can't observe, creating an incentive for the seller to inflate it. First-price removes that suspicion.
  • Risk aversion: when bidders are risk-averse, first-price auctions raise more expected revenue, because anxious bidders shade less to avoid losing.
  • Asymmetry and thin competition: with few or unequal bidders, the formats diverge and the seller may strictly prefer one.

For a bidder, the switch is consequential: under the old second-price rule the dominant strategy was to bid true value, but under first-price that guarantees zero surplus. Post-switch a bidder must shade by roughly (n−1)/n of their value, and crucially must now estimate the number and strength of competitors, since the optimal shade depends on them. Truth-telling stops being a safe default.

Q1. In a second-price (Vickrey) sealed-bid auction with independent private values, a bidder's weakly dominant strategy is to bid…
A. exactly their true value
B. slightly above their true value to be safe
C. the (n−1)/n fraction of their value
D. whatever they expect the second-highest rival to bid
Show answer

A. Your bid only decides whether you win, not what you pay (you pay the runner-up's bid), so bidding true value is weakly dominant. B can cause you to win and overpay (a loss); C is the first-price shading rule; D would require knowing others' bids, which the dominant strategy never needs.

Q2. In the symmetric first-price auction with values uniform on [0,1] and n bidders, the equilibrium bid is b(v) = ((n−1)/n)·v. As n grows large, equilibrium bids…
A. approach zero
B. approach the bidder's true value v
C. exceed the bidder's true value
D. stay fixed at half the value
Show answer

B. (n−1)/n → 1 as n grows, so bids approach v — more competition forces less shading. A reverses the direction; C never happens in equilibrium (you'd risk negative surplus); D is only the n = 2 case.

Q3. Which auction is strategically equivalent to the first-price sealed-bid auction?
A. the English (ascending) auction
B. the second-price (Vickrey) auction
C. a posted-price sale
D. the Dutch (descending) auction
Show answer

D. In the Dutch auction your only decision is the single price at which you'd commit, with no information revealed first — identical to choosing one sealed first-price bid. The English auction (A) maps to the second-price rule (B); a posted price (C) involves no strategic bidding.

Q4. The Revenue Equivalence Theorem states that the standard auction formats yield…
A. the same expected revenue under its assumptions (IPV, risk-neutral symmetric bidders, item to the highest valuer)
B. the same revenue in every single auction, guaranteed
C. the highest revenue always from the first-price format
D. zero revenue when bidders are rational
Show answer

A. The theorem equates expected revenue under specific assumptions. B confuses the expectation with each realized draw (revenues differ auction-by-auction); C is false under the symmetric benchmark (and only risk-aversion tips toward first-price); D is nonsense — rational bidders still pay positive prices.

Q5. A mechanism is "incentive-compatible" when…
A. it always selects the most efficient outcome regardless of reports
B. it charges every participant the same fee
C. truthful reporting of private information is a best response for each player
D. it requires no payments from any player
Show answer

C. Incentive compatibility means the rules make honesty optimal — under DSIC, regardless of others (the second-price property). A describes efficiency, a separate goal; B and D are unrelated to the truth-telling condition.
