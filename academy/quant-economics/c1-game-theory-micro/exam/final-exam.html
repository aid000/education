Nash Equilibrium & Dominance · Weeks 1–2
1. At a Nash equilibrium…
Answer: C. That mutual best-response, no-regret condition is the definition. It does not require efficiency (A) — the prisoner's dilemma is the standard counterexample — nor identical strategies (B) or cooperation (D).
2. Strategy s is strictly dominated by s' when s' pays strictly more than s…
Answer: A. Strict dominance requires s' to beat s in every column, with no exceptions — that airtight requirement is what lets a rational player delete s without knowing anything about the opponent's beliefs.
3. Iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies (IESDS) is order-independent, meaning…
Answer: D. Removing a strictly dominated strategy never un-does another strategy's strict dominance, so any order of deletion reaches the identical reduced game. (B) describes the failure mode of weak dominance, not strict.
4. If iterated elimination of strictly dominated strategies reduces a game to a single surviving cell, that cell…
Answer: B. A game that collapses to one cell under IESDS is "dominance-solvable," and that surviving cell is the unique Nash equilibrium. Dominance-solvability says nothing about Pareto efficiency (C) — a dominance-solvable game can still land on an inefficient outcome, as the prisoner's dilemma shows.
Mixed Strategies · Weeks 3–4
5. A mixed strategy is best described as…
Answer: A. A mixed strategy assigns probabilities to your own actions; a pure strategy is the special case with probability 1 on a single action. No strategy guarantees a win (C) against a rational opponent, and mixing is used far beyond zero-sum games (D).
6. Matching pennies has no pure-strategy Nash equilibrium because…
Answer: C. From any cell, the losing player always wants to switch, so no cell is stable. Matching pennies is exactly zero-sum (B is false), has no dominant strategy (A is false), and "cooperate" (D) belongs to a different game entirely.
7. In a mixed-strategy equilibrium, a player's own mixing probabilities are chosen to make…
Answer: B. This is the cross-over rule at the heart of the indifference principle: your mix is engineered around the opponent's payoffs so that they cannot exploit you by leaning toward either option. (A) reverses the direction, which is the single most common error on this topic.
8. In a 2×2 game, the row player's equilibrium mixing probability p is pinned down by…
Answer: D. p is set to make the column player indifferent, so it is solved from the column player's expected-payoff equations — not the row player's own payoffs (A), which is the classic reversed-cross-over mistake.
9. When using support enumeration to find a mixed equilibrium, a candidate solution is valid only if…
Answer: C. A valid candidate needs legal probabilities and no profitable deviation to an unused strategy. Supports can differ in size across players (A is false), symmetry isn't required (B), and Nash equilibria are frequently inefficient (D).
Sequential & Repeated Games · Weeks 5–6
10. Backward induction solves a finite perfect-information game by…
Answer: A. Solve the last mover first, replace that node with its optimal payoff, then work backward to the root. (C) maximizes joint welfare, but each player optimizes only their own payoff.
11. A subgame-perfect equilibrium requires the strategy profile to be a Nash equilibrium…
Answer: B. SPE demands Nash play in every subgame — exactly what prunes non-credible threats like "I will fight entry" when accommodating is actually better once entry occurs. (A) describes plain Nash equilibrium, the weaker standard SPE corrects.
12. The discount factor δ in a repeated game primarily measures…
Answer: D. δ blends time preference with the odds the relationship continues — purely about the value of one's own future. Cooperation sustained by a high δ is enlightened self-interest, not altruism (B).
13. A player using grim trigger who observes a single defection by the opponent will…
Answer: A. Grim trigger is unforgiving by design — one defection triggers permanent retaliation. (B) describes tit-for-tat's one-period punishment instead.
14. In a finitely repeated prisoner's dilemma with a commonly known final period, the unique subgame-perfect outcome is to…
Answer: C. The known last period is a plain one-shot dilemma (defect), which removes any credible threat in the second-to-last period, and so on back to period one — backward unraveling destroys cooperation whenever the horizon is finite and commonly known.
Oligopoly: Cournot, Bertrand & Stackelberg · Weeks 7–8
15. In the Cournot model, the variable each firm strategically chooses is its…
Answer: D. Cournot is quantity competition — the market price clears afterward via demand. Choosing price (A) instead is the Bertrand model.
16. With linear demand P = a − bQ and constant marginal cost c, the symmetric Cournot–Nash output per firm is…
Answer: B. Solving the symmetric reaction functions q = (a−c)/(2b) − q/2 gives q* = (a−c)/(3b) per firm — one-third of the competitive quantity, between monopoly (A) and competition (C).
17. The Bertrand paradox says that, with identical products and constant marginal cost, two price-setting firms…
Answer: C. Any price above marginal cost invites undercutting, so the unique equilibrium drives price to c with zero economic profit — even with only two firms. A unique equilibrium does exist (D is false).
18. Ranking total output on the same linear demand curve, the correct order is…
Answer: A. Monopoly restricts output most ((a−c)/2b), competition produces most ((a−c)/b), and Cournot sits in between (2(a−c)/3b). Market power — not headcount alone — is what restricts output.
19. What distinguishes the Stackelberg model from Cournot?
Answer: D. Stackelberg is sequential quantity competition — the leader moves first and the follower's best response is computed by backward induction. Both models still use quantities and two homogeneous-good firms, so A, B, and C are wrong.
20. The Stackelberg leader earns more than it would under Cournot because…
Answer: B. The first-mover advantage comes entirely from credible commitment: a large, observed, irreversible q1 shifts the follower down its reaction curve. The leader produces more, not less (A), and there is no collusion (C) or cost asymmetry (D) in the model.
Auctions, Mechanism Design & Capstone · Weeks 9–10
21. In a second-price (Vickrey) sealed-bid auction, a bidder's weakly dominant strategy is to bid…
Answer: A. Your bid only decides whether you win, not what you pay (you pay the runner-up's bid), so bidding your true value is weakly dominant. (C) is the first-price shading rule, not second-price.
22. In the symmetric first-price auction with values uniform on [0,1] and n bidders, equilibrium bids b(v) = ((n − 1)/n)·v behave such that, as n grows large…
Answer: D. As n grows, (n−1)/n → 1, so bids approach v — more competition squeezes shading toward zero. Bids never exceed v in equilibrium (B), since that risks a loss-making win.
23. The Revenue Equivalence Theorem states that, under its assumptions (independent private values, risk-neutral symmetric bidders, item to the highest valuer), the standard auction formats…
Answer: C. The theorem equates expected revenue under its assumptions — bid shading in first-price and pay-the-runner-up in second-price cancel on average. (B) confuses the expectation with a single realized draw, which can differ auction to auction.
24. A mechanism is "incentive-compatible" when…
Answer: B. Incentive compatibility means the rules make honesty optimal — under dominant-strategy IC, regardless of what others report, exactly the second-price auction's property. (A) describes efficiency, a separate design goal.
25. A Cournot best-response simulation's quantities keep oscillating and never settle. The appropriate response is to…
Answer: D. Oscillation means the best-response map is not contracting toward the fixed point; the honest move is to detect and report that, not paste an arbitrary iterate into a report (A). An equilibrium can still exist even when this particular dynamic fails to find it (B is false).
