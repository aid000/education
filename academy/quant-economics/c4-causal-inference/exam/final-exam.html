Potential Outcomes, Experiments & Confounding · Weeks 1–3
1. The individual treatment effect τi = Yi(1) − Yi(0) cannot be computed from data because…
Answer: C. A unit is either treated or not, so we see Y(1) or Y(0) but never both — the fundamental problem of causal inference. The other options are either false or irrelevant to why τi itself is unobservable.
2. Under random assignment, the simple difference in group means is unbiased for the ATE because randomization makes treatment…
Answer: A. With {Y(1),Y(0)} ⊥ D, each group's mean equals the population's, so the observable difference in means equals the ATE. B is nonsensical, C describes selection on observables (a weaker, conditional case), and D is the opposite of random assignment.
3. In the decomposition E[Y|D=1] − E[Y|D=0] = ATT + selection bias, the selection-bias term equals…
Answer: D. Selection bias is the difference in the untreated-state outcomes between the two groups — whether they'd have differed even absent treatment. A is the ATT itself, not the bias; B is unrelated; C has it backwards — the bias is generically nonzero exactly in observational data.
4. A confounder X that opens a back-door path D ← X → Y is best addressed by…
Answer: B. Conditioning on a confounder on an open back-door path blocks the leak between D and Y. Conditioning on a mediator (C) throws away part of the real effect, and conditioning on a collider (D) opens a new spurious path — both create bias rather than remove it.
Regression Adjustment & Matching · Weeks 4–5
5. The assumption {Y(0),Y(1)} ⊥ D | X, nicknamed "selection on observables," says that…
Answer: A. Within each stratum of the observed X, treatment is "as good as random." B describes an actual experiment, a sufficient but stronger condition than what this assumption claims. C is false — confounders may exist, they just must all be measured. D is a modeling convenience, not an identification requirement.
6. The defining limitation of regression adjustment is that it…
Answer: C. Adjustment closes back-door paths only through measured X; a confounder you never recorded leaves residual bias the regression output cannot reveal. A, B, and D are all simply false.
7. The common-support (overlap) condition for matching and propensity-score methods requires that…
Answer: D. Overlap means every unit has a positive chance of either treatment status, so a counterfactual exists everywhere. A is actually the opposite — a near-perfect classifier destroys overlap. B and C are irrelevant to the condition.
8. Compared with regression adjustment, nearest-neighbor matching…
Answer: B. Matching is nonparametric and declines to pair units with no comparable counterpart; regression extrapolates via its functional form instead. A and D are false, and C is wrong — both methods rest on the same selection-on-observables assumption.
Instrumental Variables · Week 6
9. The exclusion restriction for an instrument Z — that Cov(Z, u) = 0 — is hard to test directly because…
Answer: D. Because u is never observed, Cov(Z,u)=0 cannot be checked with a statistic — it has to be defended with a narrative about the instrument's source. A, B, and C describe unrelated or false conditions.
10. Because the exclusion restriction cannot be tested, researchers typically defend it by…
Answer: A. A credible defense is a narrative argument grounded in how the instrument's variation was generated. C (the F-stat) tests relevance, not exclusion — a common confusion. B and D do nothing to an assumption about an unobserved error.
11. The IV estimate identifies the Local Average Treatment Effect, the average effect for…
Answer: B. Only compliers supply instrument-driven variation, so IV identifies their average effect. Always-takers (A) and never-takers (C) contribute no such variation, and IV does not generally recover the full-population ATE (D) under heterogeneous effects.
12. A weak instrument (first-stage F well below 10) is dangerous mainly because…
Answer: C. Because βIV divides by the first-stage covariance, a near-zero first stage explodes the variance and re-introduces OLS-like bias. A is a separate (unrelated) condition, and B and D are simply false.
Difference-in-Differences & Regression Discontinuity · Weeks 7–8
13. The parallel-trends assumption behind difference-in-differences requires that…
Answer: D. Parallel trends is about counterfactual changes, not levels. A is wrong because DiD differences away any fixed level gap; B describes an RCT, not DiD; C is backwards — DiD relies on the control absorbing whatever common trend exists.
14. In the regression Y = β0 + β1·Treat + β2·Post + β3·(Treat×Post), the difference-in-differences estimate is…
Answer: A. The double difference cancels everything but the interaction, so β3 is the treatment effect. β1 is just the fixed level gap and β2 is the common time trend — neither is the causal estimate.
15. In an event-study plot, pre-treatment lead coefficients that drift away from zero before the policy date most likely indicate…
Answer: C. Non-zero leads signal a pre-trend: the groups were moving apart before the policy, so any post-period "effect" may just be that divergence continuing. A confuses leads with lags; B and D are unrelated to what leads measure.
16. Which of the following is a genuine threat to the validity of a DiD estimate?
Answer: B. Anticipation contaminates the "before" period itself, violating the design — a real pitfall. A and C are irrelevant to identification, and D (a level gap) is harmless since DiD differences it away.
17. Regression discontinuity is credible at the cutoff only if…
Answer: D. Continuity of the potential outcomes at the cutoff is the design's identifying assumption. The running variable need not be random (A); global linearity (B) is never required since RDD fits locally; and sample sizes (C) are irrelevant.
18. The causal effect in a sharp RDD is estimated as…
Answer: C. The effect is the discontinuous jump at X = c, estimated from two separate local fits. A pooled line (A) confounds the jump with the overall trend, a correlation (B) is not causal here, and a full-range mean difference (D) compares units far from the cutoff.
19. The continuity assumption in an RDD would be violated if…
Answer: A. Manipulation of the running variable makes units just above and below the cutoff systematically different, breaking continuity. B, C, and D are routine design choices or incidental facts, not threats to the assumption.
20. You re-estimate an RDD effect at half, the optimal, and double the bandwidth, and the estimate swings from 0.1 to 0.9. The right conclusion is…
Answer: D. A credible RDD is roughly stable across reasonable bandwidths; wild swings are a red flag, not reassurance. Cherry-picking the largest number (B) is exactly what sensitivity checks guard against, and bandwidth genuinely does shift estimates through the bias-variance trade-off (ruling out C).
Panel Fixed Effects & Capstone Policy Design · Weeks 9–10
21. Entity fixed effects remove bias from a confounder only if that confounder is…
Answer: B. The within transformation subtracts each entity's own mean, which cancels only a confounder that never changes over the panel. A confounder's strength (A), measurement error (C), and its distribution (D) are all beside the point.
22. A state adopts a job-training program precisely because its labor market was already starting to recover. A two-way fixed-effects regression of employment on the program indicator will…
Answer: A. Entity effects absorb only time-invariant traits and time effects absorb only shocks common to every unit; a confounder that is both state-specific and changing over time (the recovery coinciding with adoption) passes straight through both. B and C each describe only one half of what fixed effects can do, and clustering (D) fixes inference, not bias.
23. Standard errors in a panel regression should usually be clustered by entity because…
Answer: C. Serial correlation within an entity violates the independence assumption behind classical standard errors, so ignoring it makes inference overconfident. A confuses inference with bias — fixed effects, not clustering, address bias — and B and D are simply false.
24. What should determine the choice of identification strategy (DiD, RDD, or IV) for a policy-evaluation capstone?
Answer: D. The assignment mechanism dictates which identifying assumption is plausible, so it should drive the design choice. A and C describe specification-shopping, which invalidates inference, and B ignores the actual source of identification entirely.
25. A capstone using instrumental variables reports a point estimate without noting that it applies only to compliers, and uses classical (non-clustered) standard errors despite state-level treatment assignment. The report is best described as…
Answer: B. Honest reporting states whose effect was estimated (compliers, not everyone) and clusters at the level treatment was assigned (here, state) to avoid overstated significance. A and D are false claims about the mechanics, and C ignores that uncertainty is part of any real finding.
