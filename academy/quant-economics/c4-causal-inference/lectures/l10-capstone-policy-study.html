Capstone: A Causal Study of a Real Policy

Nine weeks of tools converge here: pick a real policy, choose a credible identification strategy, run it on public data, and report an honest effect — caveats and all.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Translate a real policy question into a precise causal estimand with a defined treatment, outcome, and target population.
  • Choose among DiD, RDD, and IV by reasoning about how the policy assigned treatment.
  • Implement the chosen design on public data and read the estimate off the coefficient.
  • Test the identifying assumption your design relies on — and report what happens when it bends.
  • Write up the effect with honest caveats: a reproducible, falsifiable capstone deliverable.

10.1From a Policy Question to an Estimand

This is the week everything pays off. Over nine weeks you built a toolkit — potential outcomes, randomized experiments, regression adjustment, matching, instrumental variables, regression discontinuity, difference-in-differences, and panel fixed effects. The capstone asks you to do what a working policy analyst does: take one real policy, estimate its causal effect on public data, and defend the answer. Before touching a regression you must pin down the estimand — the exact number you are trying to learn.

A good estimand names four things: the treatment (what the policy did), the outcome (what you measure), the population (who the effect is for), and the contrast (treated versus what counterfactual). Recall the potential-outcomes definition from Week 1: the individual treatment effect is the difference between the two worlds, and the policy-relevant quantity is usually an average over a population.

ATT = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) | D = 1 ]Average Treatment effect on the Treated: the mean effect among the units that actually received the policy. Most policy evaluations target the ATT, not the ATE.

The fundamental problem of causal inference is that for each unit you observe only one of Y(1) and Y(0). Every design in this course is a different answer to the same question: how do we build a credible stand-in for the missing counterfactual Y(0) among the treated? Your capstone choice is really a choice of which credible counterfactual you can defend with the data you have.

AnalogyChoosing an identification strategy is like choosing a witness in a trial. You can't replay the crime, so you find the most credible stand-in for "what would have happened anyway": a near-identical neighbor (matching), an applicant who just missed the cutoff (RDD), a parallel-trending control group (DiD), or a coin-flip-like nudge from outside (IV). The whole case rests on whether that witness is actually comparable — and a good analyst spends most of their effort cross-examining their own witness.

10.2Choosing the Strategy: DiD, RDD, or IV

You do not pick a method because it is fashionable — you pick it because of how the policy assigned treatment. The assignment mechanism dictates which assumption you can plausibly defend. Three questions sort almost any policy into a design:

Did the policy switch on at a date for some groups but not others?

Then use difference-in-differences (DiD) (Week 8). Compare the before-to-after change in a treated group to the same change in an untreated comparison group. The estimate is the 2x2 difference of means, or equivalently the interaction coefficient in a two-way fixed-effects regression (Week 9):

Y_it = a + b*Treat_i + g*Post_t + d*(Treat_i x Post_t) + e_itd (the DiD estimate) is the extra change in the treated group beyond what the control group experienced. Identifying assumption: parallel trends.

Was treatment assigned by crossing a sharp threshold of a running variable?

Then use regression discontinuity (RDD) (Week 7). Units just above and just below the cutoff are nearly identical except for treatment, so the jump in the outcome at the cutoff is the local effect. Identification rests on smoothness: everything else must vary continuously through the threshold.

Was there an outside nudge that shifted treatment but not the outcome directly?

Then use instrumental variables (IV) (Week 6). A valid instrument is relevant (it moves treatment) and satisfies the exclusion restriction (it touches the outcome only through treatment). The IV estimate is a Local Average Treatment Effect for compliers.

Common mistakeRunning every method and reporting the one with the biggest, "most significant" effect. This is specification-shopping, and it invalidates your inference: if you tried five designs and kept the prettiest, your reported p-value is meaningless. Commit to one design based on the assignment mechanism before you look at the treatment effect, and pre-register that choice in your write-up.

10.3Checking the Identifying Assumption

An estimate is only as good as the assumption it rides on, and you can never prove that assumption — but you can stress-test it. A capstone that reports a number without probing its assumption is incomplete. Each design has a signature diagnostic:

DiD — parallel trends

The treated and control groups need not be at the same level; they need to have moved in parallel absent treatment. Test it with a pre-trend (event-study) plot: estimate the treated-control gap in each period before treatment. If those pre-period coefficients are flat and near zero, parallel trends is credible; if they already diverge before the policy, your DiD is contaminated.

Y_it = a_i + l_t + sum_k b_k * D_i * 1{t = t0 + k} + e_itEvent-study form. The b_k for k < 0 (pre-treatment) should be ~0; a jump at k = 0 is the dynamic treatment effect.

RDD — no manipulation, smooth covariates

Run a density test on the running variable (no suspicious bunching just past the cutoff, which would signal gaming) and check that predetermined covariates do not jump at the threshold. A placebo cutoff away from the real one should yield no effect.

IV — instrument strength and exclusion

Report the first-stage F-statistic; a rule of thumb is F > 10 to avoid the weak-instrument bias that can make IV worse than OLS. Exclusion cannot be tested directly, so defend it with a narrative argument and, where possible, an over-identification test.

AnalogyAssumption checks are the smoke detectors of a causal study. A flat pre-trend plot doesn't prove the house won't burn down — parallel trends is fundamentally untestable in the post-period — but a detector that's already beeping (diverging pre-trends, bunching at the cutoff, a weak first stage) tells you to evacuate the design before you publish a number you'll have to retract.

10.4Reporting the Effect — Honestly

The deliverable is not a coefficient; it is a persuasive, falsifiable argument that the coefficient is causal. A strong capstone report follows a fixed skeleton so a reader can audit every claim:

(1) Question and estimand — the policy, the treatment/outcome/population, and whether you target ATT or LATE. (2) Data — public source, sample, time span, and how treatment is coded. (3) Design and why — the assignment mechanism and the single assumption you stake the result on. (4) Results — the point estimate with a confidence interval and clustered standard errors (cluster at the level treatment was assigned — Week 9). (5) Assumption checks — pre-trends / density / first-stage F. (6) Caveats and external validity — for whom and where this effect holds.

report: d_hat = -0.083 (95% CI: -0.146 to -0.020), SE clustered by stateAlways pair the point estimate with its uncertainty. A coefficient without a clustered CI is not a finding — it is a number.

The honesty section is what separates a real study from a press release. State the LATE-vs-ATE gap if you used IV (your effect is for compliers, not everyone). Acknowledge that parallel trends is untestable in the post-period. Report whether the result survives reasonable bandwidth or specification changes. A confidently caveated null is a perfectly good capstone — finding "no detectable effect, tightly bounded" is real knowledge.

Common mistakeReporting classical (homoskedastic) standard errors when treatment is assigned at the group level. With state-level policies and individual-level data, errors are correlated within state; classical SEs can be several times too small, turning noise into spurious "significance." Cluster at the level of treatment assignment, and if you have few clusters, say so.
✦ Worked Example — a minimum-wage DiD, end to end

A state raises its minimum wage in 2018; a neighboring state does not. Outcome: log teen employment rate. You have two periods (2016 = pre, 2020 = post) and the four group means below. Estimate the policy's effect via the 2x2 DiD.

  1. Group means (log employment): Treated-Pre = 3.90, Treated-Post = 3.82; Control-Pre = 3.86, Control-Post = 3.84.
  2. Change in treated group: 3.82 - 3.90 = -0.08.
  3. Change in control group: 3.84 - 3.86 = -0.02 (a common shock both states felt).
  4. DiD estimate: d = (-0.08) - (-0.02) = -0.06. The policy is associated with a 6% larger drop in teen employment than the common trend would predict.
  5. Check the assumption: using a 2014 vs 2016 pre-period, the treated-control gap moved by only -0.005 — essentially flat, so parallel trends is credible.
  6. Report honestly: with state-clustered SEs the 95% CI is (-0.118, -0.002); the effect is negative but imprecise, the estimand is the ATT for this one treated state, and it may not generalize to states with different labor markets.

🛠️ Hands-On: run the capstone DiD in Python

We use the course's free stack — pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, and linearmodels. The full one-time install lives in the Week 1/2 setup lecture; reuse that environment here. The snippet below loads a public-style panel, runs the two-way fixed-effects DiD with state-clustered errors, and prints the event-study pre-trend coefficients for your assumption check.

# capstone_did.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# df: public panel with columns state, year, log_emp, treat (1 if state ever treated)
df = pd.read_csv("state_teen_employment.csv")
df["post"]  = (df["year"] >= 2018).astype(int)
df["did"]   = df["treat"] * df["post"]

# --- Two-way fixed effects DiD, clustered by state ---
m = smf.ols("log_emp ~ did + C(state) + C(year)", data=df).fit(
        cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["state"]})
print(m.params["did"], m.bse["did"])      # DiD point estimate + clustered SE
print(m.conf_int().loc["did"])            # 95% confidence interval

# --- Event-study pre-trend check: leads/lags relative to 2018 ---
df["k"] = df["year"] - 2018
ev = smf.ols("log_emp ~ C(k, Treatment(reference=-2)):treat + C(state) + C(year)",
             data=df).fit(cov_type="cluster", cov_kwds={"groups": df["state"]})
# pre-period (k < 0) coefficients should be ~0 -> parallel trends credible
print(ev.params.filter(like="treat"))

Swap the formula for linearmodels.iv.IV2SLS if your capstone uses an instrument, or fit a local linear regression around the cutoff for an RDD. The pattern is identical: estimate, cluster, then plot the diagnostic that interrogates your one key assumption.

Q1. What should drive your choice of identification strategy for the capstone?
A. Whichever design gives the largest, most significant effect
B. How the policy actually assigned treatment to units
C. Whichever method has the most Python library support
D. Alphabetical order: always try DiD first
Show answer

B. The assignment mechanism determines which identifying assumption is plausible, so it dictates the design. (A) is specification-shopping and invalidates inference; (C) and (D) ignore the actual source of identification.

Q2. A policy switched on in 2018 for some states but not others, and you have panel data. The natural design is…
A. regression discontinuity
B. matching on the propensity score
C. difference-in-differences
D. a simple cross-sectional regression
Show answer

C. Treatment turning on at a date for some groups but not others is the textbook DiD setup. RDD (A) needs a running-variable cutoff; matching (B) and a plain cross-section (D) can't difference out the common time shock or fixed group differences.

Q3. The pre-trend (event-study) plot is used to check the key assumption behind…
A. difference-in-differences (parallel trends)
B. instrumental variables (exclusion restriction)
C. regression discontinuity (smoothness)
D. randomized experiments (random assignment)
Show answer

A. Flat, near-zero pre-period coefficients support parallel trends, the DiD assumption. Exclusion (B) can't be tested this way; RDD uses density/covariate-smoothness tests (C); an RCT's randomization (D) is checked with covariate balance.

Q4. Your capstone uses IV. The estimate you report is best described as the…
A. average treatment effect for the whole population (ATE)
B. average effect on everyone the policy could reach
C. effect on units assigned to control
D. Local Average Treatment Effect for compliers
Show answer

D. IV identifies the LATE — the effect for compliers, those moved by the instrument. It is generally not the full-population ATE (A, B), and certainly not an effect defined on the control group (C). Honest reporting flags this LATE-vs-ATE gap.

Q5. You evaluate a state-level policy using individual-level data. The most important standard-error correction is to…
A. cluster the standard errors at the state (treatment-assignment) level
B. use classical homoskedastic standard errors for simplicity
C. drop standard errors and report only the point estimate
D. bootstrap individual observations as if independent
Show answer

A. Treatment is assigned at the state level, so errors are correlated within state; clustering there avoids badly understated SEs. Classical SEs (B) and individual-level resampling (D) wrongly assume independence; reporting no uncertainty (C) is not a finding at all.
