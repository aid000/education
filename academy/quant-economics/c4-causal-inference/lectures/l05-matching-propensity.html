Matching & Propensity Scores

If you can't randomize, the next best thing is to build a fair comparison by hand — pairing each treated unit with the control it could have been.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why matching treated and control units on covariates removes confounding under selection-on-observables.
  • Perform nearest-neighbor matching and reason about distance metrics and the curse of dimensionality.
  • Define the propensity score and use it for matching and inverse-probability weighting.
  • State the common-support / overlap requirement and diagnose violations.
  • Check covariate balance after matching and explain how matching relates to regression adjustment.

5.1Matching: Building the Counterfactual by Hand

Last week (Lecture 4) you adjusted for confounders with a regression that models the outcome as a smooth function of covariates. Matching takes a more direct, model-light route: for every treated unit, find an untreated unit that looks the same on the observed covariates X and compare their outcomes. If two job-training participants and non-participants are identical in age, education, prior earnings, and region, then the difference in their later earnings is plausibly the effect of training rather than a difference in who they were to begin with.

This works only under the same identifying assumption from Week 3 — unconfoundedness (a.k.a. selection on observables): once we condition on X, treatment is as good as randomly assigned.

{ Y(1), Y(0) } ⊥ D | XConditional on covariates X, the potential outcomes are independent of treatment D. Within a cell of identical X, who got treated is effectively random.

Given that, the treatment effect for the treated population (the ATT) is the average, over treated units, of the difference between each treated unit's outcome and the outcome of its matched control:

ATT = (1/N_t) * sum over treated i of [ Y_i - Y_(j(i)) ]j(i) is the control matched to treated unit i. We impute each treated unit's missing Y(0) with a comparable control's observed outcome.
AnalogyMatching is like a dating app for data. Each treated unit "swipes" for the control that most resembles it — same age bracket, same background, same starting point. A randomized trial hands you perfect matches for free (randomization balances everything). With observational data you have to search for each unit's near-twin, and the quality of your answer is only as good as the quality of those pairings.

5.2Nearest-Neighbor Matching & the Curse of Dimensionality

"Looks the same" needs a definition. With a single covariate it is just the closest value. With several covariates we need a distance metric on the covariate vector. The standard choice is the Mahalanobis distance, which rescales each covariate by its spread and accounts for correlations so that no single high-variance variable dominates:

d(X_i, X_j) = sqrt[ (X_i - X_j)' * S^(-1) * (X_i - X_j) ]S is the covariance matrix of X. Setting S = I gives ordinary (Euclidean) distance; using S standardizes the scales.

Nearest-neighbor (NN) matching pairs each treated unit with the control of smallest distance. Variants include matching with replacement (a good control can be reused — lowers bias, raises variance), k-nearest-neighbor matching (average over the k closest controls), and caliper matching (refuse any match farther than a threshold, to avoid forcing bad pairs).

The trouble is dimension. As the number of covariates grows, the data spread out and even the "nearest" neighbor can be far away — there is simply no control that closely resembles a given treated unit on all of X at once. This curse of dimensionality is exactly the problem the propensity score was invented to solve.

5.3The Propensity Score: One Number Instead of Many

The propensity score is the probability of receiving treatment given the covariates:

e(X) = P( D = 1 | X )Typically estimated with a logistic regression of treatment D on covariates X.

The remarkable Rosenbaum–Rubin theorem (1983) says that if treatment is unconfounded given the full vector X, then it is also unconfounded given the single scalar e(X). The propensity score is a balancing score: among units with the same e(X), the distribution of X is the same for treated and control. So you can collapse a high-dimensional matching problem onto one dimension and match (or stratify) on that.

Two workhorse uses follow. Propensity-score matching pairs treated and control units with similar e(X). Inverse-probability weighting (IPW) instead reweights everyone so the treated and control groups become comparable — upweighting controls that look "treatment-like" and treated units that look "control-like":

ATE = E[ D*Y / e(X) ] - E[ (1-D)*Y / (1 - e(X)) ]Each unit is weighted by the inverse of its probability of the treatment it actually received, creating a pseudo-population in which D is independent of X.
Common mistakeThe propensity score is not meant to predict treatment well — a near-perfect classifier is a disaster, not a triumph. If e(X) for some treated units is close to 1, the IPW weight 1/e(X) is fine, but the controls' weight 1/(1−e(X)) explodes and a handful of observations dominate the estimate. The goal is balance, not predictive accuracy. Extreme propensity scores signal an overlap problem, covered next.

5.4Overlap, Balance, and the Link to Regression

Matching and weighting only make sense where treated and control units actually coexist. The common-support / overlap condition requires that for every covariate value the propensity score is strictly between 0 and 1:

0 < e(X) < 1 for all XEvery unit has a positive chance of being either treated or control. Regions where one group is absent offer no valid comparison.

If a slice of the treated group has no comparable controls (e.g. everyone over a certain prior-earnings level was treated), there is no counterfactual to estimate there — you must either trim those units or restrict your claim to the region of overlap.

After matching you must check balance: did the procedure actually equalize the covariates? The standard diagnostic is the standardized mean difference (SMD) for each covariate, before vs. after matching:

SMD = ( mean_treated(X) - mean_control(X) ) / sd_pooled(X)A common rule of thumb: |SMD| below 0.1 after matching indicates acceptable balance. Compare a "Love plot" of SMDs before and after.

How does this relate to regression? Both rely on the same unconfoundedness assumption and both estimate the same kind of adjusted effect. Regression adjustment (Lecture 4) extrapolates with a parametric model and implicitly weights observations by treatment variance; matching is nonparametric and refuses to extrapolate beyond the data's support. The modern best practice combines them: match first to enforce overlap and balance, then run a regression on the matched sample. This doubly robust idea (a regression model and a propensity model) is consistent if either model is correct — a recurring theme in the rest of this course.

✦ Worked Example — nearest-neighbor match by hand

A small job-training study has 2 treated and 3 control workers. We match on one covariate, prior annual earnings (in $1000s). Outcome Y is later earnings ($1000s).

Treated: T1 (prior=20, Y=34), T2 (prior=30, Y=41).   Control: C1 (prior=18, Y=29), C2 (prior=28, Y=33), C3 (prior=42, Y=40).

  1. Match T1 (prior=20): distances to controls are |20−18|=2, |20−28|=8, |20−42|=22. Nearest is C1 (Y=29).
  2. Match T2 (prior=30): distances are |30−18|=12, |30−28|=2, |30−42|=12. Nearest is C2 (Y=33).
  3. Per-pair effects: T1: 34 − 29 = +5. T2: 41 − 33 = +8.
  4. Estimated ATT: (5 + 8) / 2 = +6.5 thousand dollars — the imputed effect of training on the treated.
  5. Sanity check: a naive difference in group means is (34+41)/2 − (29+33+40)/3 = 37.5 − 34 = 3.5. The naive number is smaller because C3 (prior=42) inflates the control mean — yet C3 is so dissimilar it was correctly never matched. That is matching doing its job: discarding the bad comparison.

🛠️ Hands-On: propensity scores & IPW in Python

We use the course's free stack — pandas, NumPy, and statsmodels — from the Week 1/2 setup (the install details in Lectures 1–2). The only new tool here is scikit-learn's NearestNeighbors for the matching step.

⬇ One extra package for this lecture: scikit-learn

source econ-env/bin/activate       # macOS / Linux

Verify: run python -c "import sklearn; print('ok')".

Estimate a propensity score by logistic regression, check overlap, then compute the ATE by inverse-probability weighting.

# propensity.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
from sklearn.neighbors import NearestNeighbors

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 2000
age   = rng.normal(40, 10, n)
educ  = rng.normal(12, 3, n)
# treatment depends on covariates -> confounding
lin   = -6 + 0.10*age + 0.20*educ
p     = 1/(1 + np.exp(-lin))
D     = rng.binomial(1, p)
# true treatment effect = 5; outcome also depends on covariates
Y     = 10 + 5*D + 0.8*age + 1.2*educ + rng.normal(0, 5, n)
df = pd.DataFrame(dict(Y=Y, D=D, age=age, educ=educ))

# 1) estimate propensity score e(X) by logistic regression
X = sm.add_constant(df[['age','educ']])
ps_model = sm.Logit(df['D'], X).fit(disp=0)
df['e'] = ps_model.predict(X)

# 2) check overlap: trim units outside common support
lo, hi = df.loc[df.D==1,'e'].min(), df.loc[df.D==0,'e'].max()
df = df[(df.e >= lo) & (df.e <= hi)].copy()

# 3) inverse-probability weighting estimate of the ATE
# weight each unit by 1/P(treatment it actually got)
mu1 = np.average(df.loc[df.D==1,'Y'], weights=1/df.loc[df.D==1,'e'])
mu0 = np.average(df.loc[df.D==0,'Y'], weights=1/(1-df.loc[df.D==0,'e']))
print(f"naive diff : {df[df.D==1].Y.mean() - df[df.D==0].Y.mean():.2f}")
print(f"IPW ATE    : {mu1 - mu0:.2f}")   # ~5, recovering the truth

# 4) nearest-neighbor match on the propensity score (ATT)
treat, ctrl = df[df.D==1], df[df.D==0]
nn = NearestNeighbors(n_neighbors=1).fit(ctrl[['e']])
_, idx = nn.kneighbors(treat[['e']])
matched_y0 = ctrl['Y'].iloc[idx.flatten()].to_numpy()
att = (treat['Y'].to_numpy() - matched_y0).mean()
print(f"NN-matched ATT: {att:.2f}")

The naive difference is biased upward (treated workers have more education); IPW and propensity-matching both recover the true effect of about 5. Try printing an SMD table before and after matching to confirm balance improved.

🔗 Where this is heading

Matching and weighting buy you a credible answer only when unconfoundedness holds — when every confounder is observed. That is a strong, untestable assumption. Next week (Lecture 6) we relax it with instrumental variables, which can identify effects even with unobserved confounders. Later, propensity scores reappear inside doubly robust and machine-learning estimators (double/debiased ML), and the overlap logic returns in regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences. The same balance-checking discipline underpins how policy evaluators and quantitative researchers defend a causal claim to a skeptical audience.

Q1. Matching estimates a credible causal effect under which key assumption?
A. Treatment was randomly assigned by a coin flip
B. Unconfoundedness — treatment is as good as random given the observed covariates X
C. The outcome is normally distributed
D. There are more controls than treated units
Show answer

B. Matching needs selection-on-observables: { Y(1),Y(0) } independent of D given X. (A) describes an experiment, where matching is unnecessary. (C) and (D) are neither required nor sufficient.

Q2. The propensity score e(X) is defined as…
A. the probability of treatment given the covariates, P(D=1 | X)
B. the probability of the outcome given treatment, P(Y=1 | D)
C. the correlation between treatment and outcome
D. the average treatment effect
Show answer

A. The propensity score is the conditional probability of treatment given X. (B) confuses it with an outcome model; (C) and (D) are different quantities entirely.

Q3. The common-support (overlap) condition requires that…
A. the treated and control groups have equal sample sizes
B. every covariate is normally distributed
C. 0 < e(X) < 1 for all X, so both groups exist at every covariate value
D. the propensity score perfectly predicts treatment
Show answer

C. Overlap means each unit has a positive chance of either treatment status, so a counterfactual exists everywhere. (D) is the opposite — perfect prediction means e(X) hits 0 or 1, destroying overlap.

Q4. After propensity-score matching you compute standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each covariate. Why?
A. To test whether the treatment effect is statistically significant
B. To estimate the propensity score more precisely
C. To predict which units will be treated
D. To check covariate balance — whether matching made the groups comparable on X
Show answer

D. SMDs (often shown in a Love plot, target |SMD| < 0.1) verify the match actually equalized covariates. They are a balance diagnostic, not a significance test (A) or a prediction tool (C).

Q5. A key contrast between matching and regression adjustment is that…
A. matching does not extrapolate beyond the data's support, while regression can
B. matching requires randomized data but regression does not
C. regression cannot adjust for any confounders
D. matching and regression rely on completely different identifying assumptions
Show answer

A. Matching is nonparametric and refuses to compare units with no counterpart, whereas regression extrapolates via its model. (D) is wrong — both rest on unconfoundedness. (B) and (C) are simply false.

✓ What you learned

  • Matching builds counterfactuals by pairing treated units with similar controls under unconfoundedness.
  • Nearest-neighbor matching uses a distance metric (e.g. Mahalanobis), but suffers the curse of dimensionality.
  • The propensity score e(X)=P(D=1|X) is a balancing score; it powers PS-matching and inverse-probability weighting.
  • The common-support / overlap condition (0 < e(X) < 1) and a post-match balance check (SMD / Love plot) are mandatory diagnostics.
  • Matching and regression share the same assumption; combining them gives a doubly robust estimator.
