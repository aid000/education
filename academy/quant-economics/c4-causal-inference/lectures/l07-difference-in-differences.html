Week 7 · Lecture 7

Difference-in-Differences

When you can't randomize, let a policy's timing do the work — compare how two groups change, not just how they differ.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the difference-in-differences (DiD) design with treatment and control groups observed before and after a policy.
  • State the parallel-trends assumption and explain why it, not "no baseline difference," is what makes DiD credible.
  • Compute the 2×2 DiD estimator from four group-by-period means, and recover it as a regression coefficient.
  • Read and build an event-study plot with leads and lags to probe pre-trends and dynamic effects.
  • Spot the common pitfalls — anticipation, compositional change, and bad two-way fixed-effects comparisons.

7.1The Design: Compare Changes, Not Levels

Last week instrumental variables rescued us when treatment was endogenous but we had an external nudge. This week we exploit a different lever: timing. A policy switches on at a known date for some units (the treatment group) but not others (the control group), and we observe an outcome both before and after. The classic motivating question: did a minimum-wage hike in one region cost jobs, when a neighboring region with no hike serves as a comparison?

The naive answers both fail. Comparing treated units' after level to controls' after level confounds the policy with pre-existing differences (the regions were never identical). Comparing treated units' after to their own before confounds the policy with everything else that changed over time (a recession, seasonality). Difference-in-differences nets out both: it takes each group's change over time, then subtracts the control's change from the treated group's change.

DiD = (Y_treat,after − Y_treat,before) − (Y_ctrl,after − Y_ctrl,before)First difference removes any fixed group gap; second difference removes the common time shock. What's left is the treatment effect.

The first difference inside each parenthesis kills any time-invariant level gap between the groups. Subtracting one change from the other kills any shock that hit both groups equally. That double-differencing is the entire idea — and it is why the method needs panel or repeated cross-section data spanning the policy date.

7.2The Parallel-Trends Assumption

DiD does not require the two groups to be at the same level before treatment — it explicitly differences that away. What it does require is the parallel-trends assumption: in the absence of treatment, the treated group's average outcome would have moved by the same amount as the control group's. The control's observed change is our estimate of the counterfactual change the treated group would have experienced anyway.

E[ Y(0)_treat,after − Y(0)_treat,before ] = E[ Y_ctrl,after − Y_ctrl,before ]Y(0) is the untreated potential outcome. The treated group's counterfactual trend equals the control group's observed trend.

Visually, parallel trends means the treated group was on a line running parallel to the control's, and treatment knocks it onto a new path. The vertical gap between where the treated group actually lands and where the parallel counterfactual line predicted it would land is the treatment effect.

AnalogyTwo siblings grow at the same rate each year, even though one is taller. One starts a new diet on their birthday. To measure the diet's effect you don't compare their heights (one was always taller), and you don't just track the dieter's growth (kids grow anyway). You ask: did the dieter grow more or less than the sibling did over the same months? The sibling's growth is the "what would have happened anyway" yardstick — exactly the control's trend in DiD.

7.3The 2×2 Estimator as a Regression

With one treated group, one control group, and two periods, the four cell means give the estimate directly. But writing DiD as a regression generalizes it, delivers standard errors, and lets us add covariates. Define Treat = 1 for the treatment group, Post = 1 in the after period, and run:

Y = β0 + β1·Treat + β2·Post + β3·(Treat × Post) + εβ3, the coefficient on the interaction, IS the difference-in-differences estimate of the treatment effect.

Read off the four cell means: control-before is β0; control-after is β0 + β2; treated-before is β0 + β1; treated-after is β0 + β1 + β2 + β3. Now take the double difference and watch everything cancel except the interaction:

(treat,after − treat,before) − (ctrl,after − ctrl,before) = (β2 + β3) − (β2) = β3The level gap β1 and the common time shock β2 both drop out — only the interaction β3 survives.

So β1 absorbs the fixed group difference, β2 absorbs the common trend, and β3 is the causal estimate under parallel trends. In real panels with many groups and many periods you replace Treat and Post with group fixed effects and time fixed effects — the two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) regression — and cluster standard errors at the group level, since outcomes within a group are correlated over time.

Common mistakeDo not report a "significant" β1 or β2 as evidence the policy worked. β1 is just the pre-existing level gap and β2 is the economy-wide trend — neither is the treatment effect. Only the interaction β3 is the DiD estimate. A related error is forgetting to cluster: ignoring within-group serial correlation can shrink standard errors several-fold and manufacture false significance.

7.4Event Studies: Checking Pre-Trends

Parallel trends is an assumption about an unobserved counterfactual, so we can never prove it. But we can make it credible by checking that the groups moved in parallel before treatment. The event-study (dynamic DiD) regression estimates a separate treatment-vs-control gap for each period relative to the policy date, normalizing the period just before treatment (k = −1) to zero:

Y = α_g + γ_t + Σ_k βk · (Treat × 1[t − t0 = k]) + εβk is the treated-control gap at event time k; the omitted k = −1 is the baseline. Leads (k < 0) test pre-trends; lags (k ≥ 0) trace the dynamic effect.

Plot the βk against event time k with confidence bands. If the leads (the βk for k = −3, −2, …) hover around zero, the groups were trending together before the policy — parallel trends looks reasonable. If the leads drift away from zero, you have a pre-trend: the groups were already diverging, and any "effect" you find after may be that divergence continuing, not the policy. The lags (k = 0, 1, 2, …) then show whether the effect appears immediately, builds up, or fades.

✦ Worked Example — a 2×2 DiD by hand

A region raises its minimum wage; a neighboring region does not. We measure average teen employment per store (jobs). Cell means: Treated region — before = 20.0, after = 21.0. Control region — before = 23.0, after = 23.4. Estimate the policy's effect on employment.

  1. Treated change: 21.0 − 20.0 = +1.0 job per store.
  2. Control change: 23.4 − 23.0 = +0.4 jobs per store (the common time trend — both regions grew).
  3. Difference-in-differences: (+1.0) − (+0.4) = +0.6 jobs per store. The minimum-wage increase is associated with 0.6 more jobs than the control trend predicted — no job loss here.
  4. Regression check: β0 = 23.0 (control-before), β1 = 20.0 − 23.0 = −3.0 (level gap), β2 = 23.4 − 23.0 = +0.4 (time trend), and β3 = 0.6. The interaction reproduces the hand calculation exactly.
  5. Sanity on the trap: a naive after-only comparison gives 21.0 − 23.4 = −2.4, suggesting the policy destroyed jobs. That's just the pre-existing −3.0 level gap minus the policy gain — which is precisely why we difference it out.

🛠️ Hands-On: DiD in Python

We use the course's free stack — pandas, NumPy, and statsmodels — from the Week 1/2 setup (the install details in those lectures). We also add linearmodels for clean two-way fixed effects; it's a small, free add-on.

⬇ One extra package for this lecture: linearmodels

Inside the same Week-1 virtual environment, add the panel-data helper:

pip install linearmodels

Verify: run python -c "import linearmodels; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set. (statsmodels alone is enough for the 2×2 OLS below; linearmodels just makes many-group, many-period TWFE tidy.)

First, reproduce the worked example with the interaction regression, then sketch an event study with leads and lags.

# did_minwage.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# long panel: one row per (region, period)
df = pd.DataFrame({
    "treat": [1, 1, 0, 0],            # 1 = region that raised the wage
    "post":  [0, 1, 0, 1],            # 1 = after the policy date
    "jobs":  [20.0, 21.0, 23.0, 23.4],
})

# DiD = coefficient on the treat:post interaction
m = smf.ols("jobs ~ treat + post + treat:post", data=df).fit()
print(m.params["treat:post"])        # -> 0.6  (the DiD effect)

With a real multi-period panel you'd instead estimate an event study: regress the outcome on group and time fixed effects plus interactions of treat with each event-time dummy, omitting k = −1, then plot the coefficients.

# event study with two-way fixed effects (many groups, many years)
from linearmodels.panel import PanelOLS

panel = panel.set_index(["region", "year"])   # MultiIndex = entity, time
# k = event time; reltime dummies omit k == -1 as the baseline
res = PanelOLS.from_formula(
    "jobs ~ C(reltime) + EntityEffects + TimeEffects",
    data=panel,
).fit(cov_type="clustered", cluster_entity=True)

coefs = res.params.filter(like="reltime")      # plot these vs k
print(coefs)   # leads near 0 = parallel pre-trends; lags = dynamic effect

Clustering on entity (cluster_entity=True) handles the within-group serial correlation flagged in 7.3. Plot coefs against event time with their confidence intervals to get the figure above.

🔗 Where this is heading

DiD assumes a clean before/after with everyone treated at the same time. Real policies often roll out in staggered waves across regions — and recent econometrics shows that the naive two-way fixed-effects estimator can then be badly biased, because already-treated units get used as "controls" for later-treated ones. Modern staggered-adoption estimators fix this, and they build directly on the event-study logic here. Next week, Regression Discontinuity exploits a sharp eligibility cutoff instead of a date; later in the program, these designs feed into policy-evaluation work and the synthetic-control methods used when there is only one treated unit.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The key identifying assumption of difference-in-differences is that…
A. the treatment and control groups have identical outcome levels before treatment
B. in the absence of treatment, the two groups' outcomes would have changed by the same amount (parallel trends)
C. treatment was randomly assigned
D. the control group is never affected by any time trend
Show answer

B. Parallel trends is the assumption. A is wrong because DiD differences away level gaps — equal levels are not required. C describes a randomized experiment, not DiD. D is backwards: DiD relies on the control absorbing the common time trend.

Q2. In the regression Y = β0 + β1·Treat + β2·Post + β3·(Treat×Post), which coefficient is the DiD treatment-effect estimate?
A. β0, the intercept
B. β1, the coefficient on Treat
C. β2, the coefficient on Post
D. β3, the coefficient on the interaction Treat×Post
Show answer

D. The double difference cancels everything but the interaction, so β3 is the effect. β1 (B) is just the fixed level gap between groups, β2 (C) is the common time trend, and β0 (A) is the control-before mean — none of these is the treatment effect.

Q3. Treated region: before = 50, after = 58. Control region: before = 40, after = 43. The DiD estimate is…
A. +15
B. +8
C. +5
D. +18
Show answer

C. Treated change = 58 − 50 = 8; control change = 43 − 40 = 3; DiD = 8 − 3 = +5. B (+8) is only the treated group's own change (ignores the time trend). A (+15) is the after-period level difference; D (+18) is the before-period level difference plus the treated change — both confound levels with the effect.

Q4. In an event-study plot, what does it mean if the pre-treatment lead coefficients (k = −3, −2) are large and trending, not near zero?
A. The groups were already diverging before treatment, casting doubt on parallel trends
B. The treatment effect is unusually large
C. The standard errors must be clustered incorrectly
D. The control group received the treatment early
Show answer

A. Non-zero leads signal a pre-trend — the groups were diverging before the policy, so parallel trends is suspect and post-period "effects" may just be that divergence continuing. B confuses pre-period leads with the post-period effect; C and D are unrelated to what leads measure.

Q5. Which of the following is a genuine threat to a DiD estimate?
A. The treatment and control groups had different outcome levels in the pre-period
B. The outcome variable is measured in dollars
C. The control group is larger than the treatment group
D. Units anticipate the policy and change behavior before it takes effect
Show answer

D. Anticipation contaminates the pre-period (the "before" already reflects the policy), violating the design — a real pitfall. A is harmless: DiD differences out level gaps. B and C are irrelevant to identification; units and group sizes don't bias the estimator.
