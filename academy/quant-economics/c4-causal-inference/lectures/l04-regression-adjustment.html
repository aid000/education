Regression Adjustment & Selection on Observables

If you can measure every reason people sorted into treatment, a humble regression can recover the causal effect a randomized trial would have found.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the conditional-independence / unconfoundedness assumption and explain why it is called "selection on observables."
  • Show how including confounders as controls in a regression removes the bias from Lecture 3.
  • Recover the average treatment effect (ATE) by adjusting for, then averaging over, the covariate distribution.
  • Distinguish the regression coefficient on treatment from a properly weighted ATE, and know when they coincide.
  • Articulate the fatal limitation: regression adjustment cannot fix bias from unobserved confounders.

4.1The Problem We Inherited

Last week you saw that a naive comparison of treated and untreated units is contaminated by confounding: a variable that drives both who gets treated and the outcome makes the simple difference in means a mix of the true effect plus a selection term. Suppose we want the effect of a job-training program (T = 1) on later earnings (Y). People who enroll tend to be more motivated, and motivation also raises earnings on its own. The raw gap overstates the program.

The cleanest fix is randomization — flip a coin to assign T, and the treated and control groups are identical in expectation, confounders and all. But we usually cannot randomize policy. Regression adjustment asks a softer question: what if we cannot randomize, but we can measure the confounders? If motivation, prior earnings, age, and education are all in our data, maybe we can compare treated and untreated people who look the same on those variables and call the within-group difference causal.

Y = β0 + τ·T + β1·X1 + ... + βk·Xk + ePut the treatment T and the confounders X into one regression; the coefficient τ on T is the candidate causal effect, "holding X fixed."

The phrase "holding X fixed" is doing enormous work here. The next sections make precise the assumption that turns that coefficient into a causal effect — and the assumption it can never buy.

4.2Unconfoundedness: Selection on Observables

Recall the potential outcomes Y(1) and Y(0): what a unit would earn with and without training. The fundamental problem is we only ever see one of them per person. Confounding means treatment status T is correlated with the potential outcomes — motivated people (high Y(0) and Y(1)) are also more likely to have T = 1. The assumption that rescues us says: once we condition on the observed covariates X, that correlation disappears.

{ Y(0), Y(1) } ⊥ T | XConditional independence: within each stratum of X, treatment is "as good as random." Also called unconfoundedness or ignorability.

In words: among people with the same age, education, prior earnings, and motivation score, who actually entered the program is unrelated to what they would have earned either way. The covariates X soak up all the systematic reasons for selecting into treatment. That is why the assumption is nicknamed selection on observables — the selection happens, but only along dimensions we can see and control for.

We also need overlap (a.k.a. common support / positivity): at every value of X we care about, there must be some chance of being treated and some chance of not, 0 < P(T=1 | X) < 1. If every 22-year-old with a college degree was treated, there is no comparable control to learn the counterfactual from, and adjustment quietly extrapolates instead of comparing.

AnalogyThink of building a fair race out of an unfair one. Runners chose their own starting blocks — faster runners crept forward (that is the confounding). Unconfoundedness says: if I sort runners into groups by everything that determined their head start (training, age, shoe type), then within each group the remaining starting position is essentially random. Compare finish times within groups, average across groups, and you recover how much the actual race — the treatment — mattered.

4.3From Coefficient to Average Treatment Effect

Under unconfoundedness and overlap, the causal effect is identified. The clean, model-free statement is the adjustment formula: estimate the treated-minus-control gap within each X-stratum, then average those gaps over the population's X-distribution.

ATE = E_X[ E(Y | T=1, X) - E(Y | T=0, X) ]Inner bracket: causal effect at a fixed X. Outer E_X: average over how X is actually distributed in the population.

Linear regression is one way to estimate the inner conditional means. If we believe the outcome is linear in X with a constant treatment effect, then the coefficient τ in 4.1 equals the ATE directly. But beware: when the treatment effect varies with X (heterogeneity) and treated/control groups have different X-distributions, the single OLS coefficient becomes a variance-weighted blend of the per-stratum effects, not the population ATE. To target the ATE exactly without leaning on linearity, use the figure below's recipe: fit a flexible model, predict everyone as if treated and again as if untreated, and average the difference.

Common mistakeDo not control for a variable that lies on the causal pathway from treatment to outcome (a mediator), nor for a collider. If training raises earnings partly by boosting a "skill certification" you also include as a control, you regress away part of the very effect you want, biasing τ toward zero. "Add every variable you have" is wrong; you control for pre-treatment confounders, not consequences of treatment.

4.4The Limit: It Only Handles What You Can See

Everything above hinges on one untestable line: {Y(0),Y(1)} ⊥ T | X. If a confounder is unobserved — say "grit," which you never measured but which drives both enrollment and earnings — then conditioning on the X you do have leaves a residual correlation between T and the potential outcomes. The adjusted τ is still biased, and no amount of clever regression on the observed columns can detect or remove it.

This is the dividing line of the entire course. Selection-on-observables methods (regression, and the matching/propensity methods next week) are only as credible as the claim that you measured every relevant confounder. That claim is a judgment, defended with subject-matter knowledge, not a statistic you can read off the output.

We can probe its fragility with a sensitivity analysis: ask how strong an unobserved confounder would have to be to overturn the result. But to truly break free of the assumption, we need designs that exploit external variation — instrumental variables (Week 6), regression discontinuity, and difference-in-differences (Course C5) — which can identify effects even when unmeasured confounders exist.

observed X handled by adjustment | unobserved U → residual bias remainsRegression adjustment closes back-door paths through measured X only. Paths through unmeasured U stay open.
✦ Worked Example — adjusting away a confounder

A training program's effect on monthly earnings is confounded by education, which takes two levels: HS and College. Within each level the program's true effect is a constant +$200. Suppose the data give these stratum means:

  1. HS stratum: treated earn $1,500; control earn $1,300. Within-group effect = +$200.
  2. College stratum: treated earn $2,500; control earn $2,300. Within-group effect = +$200.
  3. Population mix: 60% HS, 40% College. Adjustment formula: ATE = 0.60(200) + 0.40(200) = +$200. Correct.
  4. Now the naive estimate. College workers select into the program: of the treated, 70% are College; of the controls, only 20% are College.
  5. Treated mean = 0.30($1,500) + 0.70($2,500) = $450 + $1,750 = $2,200.
  6. Control mean = 0.80($1,300) + 0.20($2,300) = $1,040 + $460 = $1,500.
  7. Naive difference = $2,200 - $1,500 = +$700 — over three times the truth, because the treated group is loaded with high-earning College workers. Adjusting for education recovers the real +$200.

🛠️ Hands-On: regression adjustment in Python

We use the free pandas + NumPy + statsmodels + linearmodels stack from the Week-1/2 setup (the one-time install details lives in Lecture 1/2 — reuse that environment). No new packages are needed here; we just call OLS and then do the two-prediction averaging by hand to target the ATE.

# regression_adjustment.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 4000
# education confounder: drives both treatment AND earnings
college = rng.binomial(1, 0.45, n)
# college workers are far more likely to enroll (selection on observables)
p_treat = 0.20 + 0.55 * college
T = rng.binomial(1, p_treat)
# true constant effect = +200; college adds +1000 baseline
Y = 1300 + 1000 * college + 200 * T + rng.normal(0, 150, n)
df = pd.DataFrame({"Y": Y, "T": T, "college": college})

# 1) NAIVE difference in means -> badly biased upward
naive = df.loc[df.T == 1, "Y"].mean() - df.loc[df.T == 0, "Y"].mean()

# 2) REGRESSION ADJUSTMENT: control for the confounder
adj = smf.ols("Y ~ T + college", data=df).fit()

# 3) Target ATE by predict-all-treated minus predict-all-control
d1 = df.assign(T=1); d0 = df.assign(T=0)
ate = (adj.predict(d1) - adj.predict(d0)).mean()

print(f"naive diff    = {naive:8.1f}")   # ~ 700-900, biased
print(f"adj coef on T = {adj.params['T']:8.1f}")  # ~ 200
print(f"ATE (g-comp)  = {ate:8.1f}")      # ~ 200, the truth

The naive gap is wildly inflated; adding college as a control snaps the estimate back to the true +200. Try deleting college from the data entirely (simulating an unobserved confounder) and re-running step 2 — the bias returns and nothing in the output warns you. That is the limitation of 4.4 in one experiment.

🔗 Where this is heading

Regression adjustment and matching / propensity scores (Week 5) are two faces of the same selection-on-observables coin — Week 5 reweights instead of modeling the outcome, and "doubly robust" estimators combine both for insurance. When you suspect an unobserved confounder you cannot measure, you graduate to design-based identification: instrumental variables (Week 6), and in Course C5 regression discontinuity and difference-in-differences. Project checkpoint (Week 4 milestone): lock in your capstone's causal question and write the explicit unconfoundedness assumption for your dataset — list every confounder you can measure and name at least one you cannot, since that honesty drives which method you ultimately choose.

❓ Lecture 4 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The conditional-independence (unconfoundedness) assumption states that…
A. treatment is independent of the potential outcomes once we condition on X
B. treatment is randomly assigned by the experimenter
C. the outcome is a linear function of the covariates
D. there are no confounders of any kind
Show answer

A. Within each stratum of the observed X, treatment is "as good as random." B describes an actual experiment (a sufficient but stronger condition, not what this assumption claims). C is a modeling choice unrelated to identification. D is false — confounders may exist, they just must all be observed and controlled.

Q2. Why is the assumption nicknamed "selection on observables"?
A. because outcomes are observed but treatment is not
B. because all selection into treatment operates through variables we have measured
C. because we only observe one potential outcome per unit
D. because the treatment effect is observable directly in the raw means
Show answer

B. Selection still happens, but only along dimensions in X that we can see and adjust for. A is backwards. C is the fundamental problem of causal inference, true of every method, not this nickname. D is exactly what's false — raw means are biased by confounding.

Q3. In the worked example, the naive difference was +$700 while the true effect was +$200. The +$500 gap came from…
A. measurement error in earnings
B. the treatment effect varying randomly
C. the treated group containing far more high-earning College workers than the control group
D. a violation of the overlap assumption
Show answer

C. Education confounds: 70% of treated vs 20% of controls were College, so the treated mean is pulled up by composition, not by the program. A and B are not what generated the gap (the effect was a constant +200). D is wrong — both strata had treated and control units, so overlap held; the issue was confounding, which adjustment fixes.

Q4. Which variable should you not include as a control in a regression for the causal effect of training on earnings?
A. age measured before the program
B. prior earnings (pre-treatment)
C. a job certification the training itself produced (a post-treatment mediator)
D. education level fixed before enrollment
Show answer

C. Controlling for a mediator on the treatment-to-outcome path absorbs part of the effect you want, biasing τ toward zero. A, B, and D are all legitimate pre-treatment confounders that should be controlled.

Q5. The defining limitation of regression adjustment is that it…
A. requires the treatment to be binary
B. only works with fewer than ten covariates
C. always assumes a linear outcome model
D. cannot remove bias from confounders that are unobserved
Show answer

D. Adjustment closes back-door paths only through the X you measured; an unmeasured confounder U leaves residual bias the output cannot reveal. A is false (treatment can be continuous). B is false (many covariates are fine). C is false — you can use flexible, non-linear models and still adjust; linearity is a convenience, not a requirement.

✓ What you learned

  • Stated unconfoundedness, {Y(0),Y(1)} ⊥ T | X, and why it is called selection on observables.
  • Used the adjustment formula — within-stratum gaps averaged over the X-distribution — to recover the ATE.
  • Saw a confounder inflate the naive difference to +$700 and adjustment restore the true +$200.
  • Avoided controlling for mediators and colliders, which would bias the treatment coefficient.
  • Internalized the hard limit: adjustment cannot fix unobserved confounding, and locked your capstone causal question + assumption at the Week-4 project checkpoint.
