Randomized Experiments

Flip a coin to decide who gets the treatment, and the confounders that haunt observational studies simply vanish — that is the quiet magic of randomization.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a randomized controlled trial (RCT) is the "gold standard" for causal claims.
  • Show how random assignment makes treatment statistically independent of the potential outcomes.
  • Prove that the difference-in-means estimator is unbiased for the average treatment effect (ATE).
  • Use covariate balance as a check that randomization "worked."
  • Distinguish internal from external validity, and explain why pure RCTs are often infeasible in economics.

2.1The Identification Problem & the Coin Flip

Last week we wrote each unit i as having two potential outcomes: Yi(1) if treated and Yi(0) if not. The unit-level causal effect is Yi(1) − Yi(0), but we never observe both — the fundamental problem of causal inference. We settle instead for the population average, the average treatment effect:

ATE = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) ] = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]The average causal effect of moving everyone from untreated to treated.

The trouble is the data. A naive analyst compares the average outcome of those who happened to receive the treatment with those who didn't — the naive comparison E[Y | D=1] − E[Y | D=0]. But people who take a job-training program, enroll in a class, or buy insurance differ systematically from those who don't. Those differences (motivation, health, wealth) drive the outcome on their own, so the comparison mixes the true effect with selection bias.

Randomization is the surgical fix. If a coin flip — not the person, the doctor, or the market — decides who is treated, then the treated and untreated groups are, on average, identical in every respect except treatment. The "apples vs. oranges" comparison becomes apples vs. apples.

AnalogyThink of two identical decks of cards shuffled from the same box. If you deal them into two piles at random, the piles will have nearly the same mix of suits, high cards, and aces — not because you arranged it, but because randomness spreads every trait evenly. An RCT shuffles people into treatment and control the same way: every hidden trait, measured or not, is dealt evenly across the two groups.

2.2Why Random Assignment Identifies the ATE

Let Di = 1 if unit i is treated and 0 otherwise. When treatment is assigned by a fair randomization device, the assignment cannot depend on anything about the person — including their (unobserved) potential outcomes. Formally, this is the independence (or "ignorability") assumption:

{ Y(1), Y(0) } ⊥ DThe pair of potential outcomes is statistically independent of treatment assignment.

This single line is the engine of the whole method. Independence lets us swap a conditional expectation for an unconditional one. Because D carries no information about Y(1) or Y(0):

E[Y(1) | D=1] = E[Y(1)] and E[Y(0) | D=0] = E[Y(0)]The treated group's E[Y(1)] equals the whole population's, and likewise for the control group.

Now use the consistency assumption — that an observed outcome equals the potential outcome under the treatment actually received: Yi = Di·Yi(1) + (1−Di)·Yi(0). So observed outcomes among the treated are draws of Y(1), and among controls are draws of Y(0). Stringing it together:

E[Y | D=1] - E[Y | D=0] = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)] = ATEUnder randomization, the observable difference in group means equals the causal ATE.

The quantity on the left is something we can estimate from data; the quantity on the right is what we want. Randomization is the bridge that makes them equal — it converts an unanswerable counterfactual question into a difference of two sample averages.

2.3The Difference-in-Means Estimator & Covariate Balance

The sample analog of the bridge above is the difference-in-means estimator. With N1 treated and N0 control units:

ATE-hat = (1/N1) ∑ Yi Di - (1/N0) ∑ Yi (1-Di)Average outcome among the treated minus average outcome among controls.

Under random assignment this estimator is unbiased for the ATE, and you can compute it — with a valid standard error — simply by regressing the outcome on the treatment dummy: Y = α + τ·D + ε. The OLS slope τ-hat is exactly the difference in means, and its t-statistic tells you whether the effect is distinguishable from zero.

Checking that randomization "worked"

Randomization balances all traits in expectation, but in any one finite sample the coin can land lopsided by chance. We diagnose this with a balance check: compare baseline (pre-treatment) covariates — age, prior income, test scores — across the two groups. If the groups look alike on these before treatment, we trust that the unobservables are balanced too. A balance table reporting each covariate's mean in each arm and the difference is the first table in nearly every credible experimental paper.

Common mistakeDo not control for post-treatment variables (anything measured after treatment is assigned) in your balance check or your regression. Treatment can affect those variables, so conditioning on them reintroduces bias — the very thing randomization eliminated. Balance checks belong to pre-treatment covariates only. Likewise, a single imbalanced covariate out of twenty is expected by chance and is not, by itself, evidence that randomization failed.

2.4Internal Validity, External Validity & Why Economists Can't Always Randomize

An RCT done right has superb internal validity: within the study, the estimated effect is genuinely causal because randomization closed every back door. But internal validity is local. External validity asks the harder question — will the same effect show up in a different population, time, or scale?

Several forces threaten external validity even when internal validity is airtight:

  • Non-representative samples: volunteers for an experiment may differ from the broader population the policy will reach.
  • Scale & general equilibrium effects: training 100 workers raises their wages; training a million might flood the labor market and depress wages — an effect invisible at small scale.
  • Hawthorne / placebo effects: people behave differently simply because they know they are being studied.

And often we cannot randomize at all. You cannot randomly assign people to be unemployed, to live through a recession, to smoke, or to receive a minimum-wage law. Ethics, law, politics, and cost rule out the coin flip for most of the big questions in economics. That is precisely why the rest of this course builds quasi-experimental tools — selection & confounding next week, then matching, instrumental variables, difference-in-differences, and regression discontinuity — each one a way to mimic a coin flip when nature or policy won't run the experiment for us.

✦ Worked Example — estimating the ATE of a job-training program

A pilot randomizes 200 unemployed workers: 100 into a training program (treatment), 100 into a control arm. After six months, average monthly earnings are $1,820 in the treatment group and $1,650 in the control group. Baseline (pre-program) earnings averaged $1,400 in treatment and $1,390 in control. Estimate the ATE and assess credibility.

  1. Difference in means: ATE-hat = $1,820 − $1,650 = $170 / month.
  2. Balance check: baseline earnings differ by only $1,400 − $1,390 = $10 — tiny relative to the $170 effect, so the groups looked alike before treatment. Randomization appears to have worked.
  3. Inference: suppose each group's earnings have standard deviation ≈ $600, so SE = sqrt(600²/100 + 600²/100) = sqrt(3600+3600) = sqrt(7200) ≈ $84.9.
  4. t-statistic: t = 170 / 84.9 ≈ 2.0 — right at the 5% significance threshold, so the effect is marginally distinguishable from zero. A 95% CI is roughly 170 ± 1.96·84.9 = [$3, $337].
  5. Interpretation: the program raised earnings by about $170/month in this sample (strong internal validity). Whether it would help a national rollout — where scale and general-equilibrium effects bite — is an external-validity question the pilot cannot answer.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate an RCT in Python

Here we build the ground truth by hand — we know the true ATE because we wrote it — then show that randomization recovers it while a naive comparison does not.

# rct_simulation.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
N = 2000

# a confounder: "ability" raises earnings AND self-selection into training
ability = rng.normal(0, 1, N)

# potential outcomes (in $): true ATE = 170 for everyone
Y0 = 1500 + 300 * ability + rng.normal(0, 200, N)
Y1 = Y0 + 170                               # constant treatment effect

# --- (a) RANDOMIZED assignment: a coin flip, independent of ability ---
D_rand = rng.integers(0, 2, N)
Y_rand = np.where(D_rand == 1, Y1, Y0)
df = pd.DataFrame({"Y": Y_rand, "D": D_rand, "ability": ability})
print(smf.ols("Y ~ D", data=df).fit().params["D"])   # ~170: unbiased!

# --- (b) SELF-SELECTION: high-ability people opt in (NOT random) ---
D_self = (ability + rng.normal(0, 0.5, N) > 0).astype(int)
Y_self = np.where(D_self == 1, Y1, Y0)
df2 = pd.DataFrame({"Y": Y_self, "D": D_self})
print(smf.ols("Y ~ D", data=df2).fit().params["D"])   # >>170: selection bias

Block (a) recovers ≈170 because the coin flip made D independent of ability. Block (b) returns a number far above 170: the treated are also higher-ability, so the comparison conflates the treatment effect with the confounder. Add ability back as a control in (b) and watch the estimate snap back toward 170 — a preview of next week's confounding.

❓ Lecture 2 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why does random assignment let the difference in group means identify the ATE?
A. It guarantees the treatment effect is positive
B. It makes treatment independent of the potential outcomes, so each group's mean equals the population's
C. It eliminates all sampling variability
D. It forces both potential outcomes to be observed for every unit
Show answer

B. Independence is the key: with {Y(1),Y(0)} ⊥ D, E[Y(1)|D=1]=E[Y(1)] and E[Y(0)|D=0]=E[Y(0)], so the observable mean difference equals the ATE. A is false (sign isn't guaranteed); C confuses bias with variance (randomization removes bias, not noise); D is impossible — the fundamental problem of causal inference still holds.

Q2. A balance table in an RCT primarily checks that…
A. the outcome differs between treatment and control
B. the sample is representative of the whole country
C. pre-treatment covariates are similar across the two arms
D. the treatment effect is statistically significant
Show answer

C. Balance checks compare baseline covariates across arms to confirm randomization produced comparable groups. A and D concern the outcome/effect, not balance; B is about external validity (representativeness), a separate concern.

Q3. An RCT shows a training program raises earnings for its participants, but the effect may not hold for a national rollout because of scale and general-equilibrium effects. This is a concern about…
A. external validity
B. internal validity
C. selection bias within the experiment
D. measurement error in the outcome
Show answer

A. Generalizing to a different population, scale, or setting is the definition of external validity. Internal validity (B) concerns whether the estimate is causal within the study, which randomization already secured; C and D are unrelated to the rollout question.

Q4. Which of these is the strongest reason economists often cannot run an RCT?
A. The difference-in-means estimator is biased
B. Potential outcomes don't exist outside experiments
C. OLS cannot estimate a treatment dummy
D. You cannot ethically or feasibly assign people to recessions, unemployment, or laws
Show answer

D. Many treatments of interest can't be randomly imposed for ethical, legal, political, or cost reasons. A is false (it's unbiased under randomization); B is false (potential outcomes are defined for any unit); C is false (regressing Y on D is exactly how we compute it).

Q5. In a randomized trial, you regress the outcome Y on the treatment dummy D and get a slope of 170. What does this slope represent?
A. The estimated average treatment effect (difference in means)
B. The correlation between Y and D
C. The selection bias term
D. The proportion of units that were treated
Show answer

A. Regressing Y on a single binary D gives a slope exactly equal to the difference in group means, which under randomization is the unbiased ATE estimate. B is a unitless quantity (not in dollars); C is zero because of randomization; D would be the mean of D, not the slope on D.

✓ What you learned

  • The RCT is the gold standard because a coin flip severs treatment from every confounder, measured or not.
  • Random assignment makes {Y(1),Y(0)} ⊥ D, which turns the unobservable ATE into an observable difference in means.
  • The difference-in-means estimator is unbiased for the ATE and is just the OLS slope of Y on the treatment dummy.
  • Covariate balance on pre-treatment variables is the diagnostic that randomization "worked."
  • Internal vs. external validity differ — and ethics, scale, and cost mean economists usually can't randomize, motivating quasi-experiments.
