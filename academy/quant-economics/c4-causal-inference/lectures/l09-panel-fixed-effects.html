Panel Methods & Fixed Effects

When you can't measure the confounder, sometimes you can subtract it away — by watching the same units change over time.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what panel (longitudinal) data is and how it differs from a cross-section or a single time series.
  • Define entity fixed effects and time fixed effects, and say exactly which confounders each one absorbs.
  • Derive the within transformation and show why it sweeps out every time-invariant unobserved confounder.
  • Set up a two-way fixed-effects model and state the assumption it still relies on.
  • Explain why standard errors must be clustered, and estimate a fixed-effects model with linearmodels.

9.1What Is Panel Data?

Up to now most of our data has been a cross-section: one snapshot of many units (people, firms, states) at a single moment. The trouble is that units differ in ways we can't see — a state's political culture, a worker's grit, a firm's management quality. If those hidden traits also drive the outcome, our regression coefficient is contaminated by omitted-variable bias.

Panel data (also called longitudinal data) fixes part of this by following the same units across multiple time periods. We index every observation twice: by entity i and by time t.

y(i,t) = b1 * x(i,t) + a(i) + lambda(t) + u(i,t)y for entity i in period t depends on regressor x, an entity-specific term a(i), a time-specific term lambda(t), and idiosyncratic noise u(i,t).

The magic is in the a(i) term. It is a fixed, unknown number for each entity — a bundle of everything stable about that unit that we never measured. Because we observe each entity more than once, we can let the data hold a(i) constant and look only at how an entity's outcome moves relative to its own average. That is the entire idea of fixed effects.

9.2Entity Fixed Effects & the Within Transformation

How do we make a(i) disappear without ever measuring it? We exploit a small algebraic trick. First, average the model over time within each entity. Let an overbar denote an entity's average across its periods:

ybar(i) = b1 * xbar(i) + a(i) + lambdabar + ubar(i)The entity average. Crucially a(i) is constant over time, so its average is just a(i) itself.

Now subtract this entity average from the original equation, period by period. This is the within transformation (also called demeaning):

y(i,t) - ybar(i) = b1 * ( x(i,t) - xbar(i) ) + ( u(i,t) - ubar(i) )The a(i) terms cancel exactly: a(i) - a(i) = 0. The fixed effect is gone.

Look at what happened: a(i) canceled itself out. Every time-invariant trait of the entity — measured or not, known or unknown — drops out of the equation. We are now regressing each entity's deviations from its own mean of y on its deviations from its own mean of x. Running OLS on these demeaned variables gives the fixed-effects (within) estimator of b1.

An equivalent route is the least-squares dummy variable (LSDV) approach: include one dummy variable per entity. With n entities that is n intercepts, and the slope you get on x is numerically identical to the within estimator. Demeaning is just the computationally cheap way to do the same thing.

AnalogyThink of weighing yourself on a scale that is permanently miscalibrated — it always reads 3 kg too high. You can never learn the calibration error, but you can still measure how much weight you gained or lost this month: the constant +3 cancels when you subtract last month's reading from this month's. Each entity's fixed effect is that personal "miscalibration," and the within transformation is the before-minus-after subtraction that erases it.
Common mistakeFixed effects only kill confounders that are constant over time. They do nothing about a confounder that itself changes over the panel — for example, a state simultaneously raising its minimum wage and launching a job-training program in the same years. Such time-varying confounders pass straight through the demeaning. "I used fixed effects, so I've handled confounding" is false: you've handled only the time-invariant part.

9.3Time Fixed Effects & Two-Way Fixed Effects

Entity fixed effects absorb everything stable about a unit. But there are also shocks that hit every unit in the same period — a national recession, a federal tax change, a pandemic. Those are captured by the time term lambda(t): a separate intercept for each period, common to all entities.

Including both sets of dummies gives the two-way fixed-effects (TWFE) model — by far the most common workhorse in applied policy work:

y(i,t) = b1 * x(i,t) + a(i) + lambda(t) + u(i,t)Entity effects a(i) absorb time-invariant cross-unit differences; time effects lambda(t) absorb common shocks each period.

Intuitively, TWFE asks: after stripping out each unit's baseline level and the average movement common to all units in each year, does an entity's extra change in x line up with an extra change in y? It compares units that changed their policy to units that didn't, in the same years. This is exactly the engine behind the difference-in-differences designs you will formalize next term.

The identifying assumption is strict exogeneity of x conditional on the fixed effects: once a(i) and lambda(t) are removed, the remaining variation in x must be uncorrelated with the idiosyncratic error u(i,t). Equivalently, treatment timing must not be driven by unit-specific shocks to the outcome.

9.4Why Standard Errors Must Be Clustered

Estimating b1 is only half the job; we also need an honest measure of its uncertainty. Default OLS standard errors assume every observation's error is independent. In a panel that is almost never true: an entity's shocks are correlated over time. A state that runs hot one year tends to run hot the next; the u(i,t) within an entity move together.

If you ignore this serial correlation, your standard errors are far too small, your t-statistics are inflated, and you will declare effects "significant" that are pure noise. The fix is to cluster the standard errors by entity — allowing arbitrary correlation of errors within each cluster while assuming independence across clusters:

Var_hat( b1 ) = ( X'X )^(-1) [ sum over clusters g of Xg' ug_hat ug_hat' Xg ] ( X'X )^(-1)The cluster-robust ("sandwich") variance: it sums the outer products of residuals within each cluster g rather than treating every row as independent.

The number of clusters matters: clustered standard errors are justified by having many clusters. With only a handful of entities (say, a dozen states), the cluster correction itself becomes unreliable and special small-sample adjustments are needed. As a rule of thumb, cluster at the level at which treatment is assigned — usually the entity.

Common mistakeAdding entity fixed effects does not automatically fix your standard errors. The fixed effects address bias in the point estimate; clustering addresses correlation in the errors. These are two separate problems. A textbook error is to report a fixed-effects coefficient with default (non-clustered) standard errors — the estimate may be fine while the inference is badly overconfident.
✦ Worked Example — demeaning a two-period panel by hand

Two cities, two years. We study whether a transit-subsidy intensity x raises ridership y. City A has a high baseline (busy transit culture); City B has a low baseline. Raw data:

  • City A: year 1 (x=2, y=20), year 2 (x=4, y=26).
  • City B: year 1 (x=1, y=8), year 2 (x=3, y=14).
  1. A naive pooled comparison is biased: City A has both higher x and higher y largely because of its unmeasured transit culture a(A). Comparing A to B confounds the subsidy effect with the culture gap.
  2. Compute entity means. City A: xbar = (2+4)/2 = 3, ybar = (20+26)/2 = 23. City B: xbar = (1+3)/2 = 2, ybar = (8+14)/2 = 11.
  3. Demean each observation. City A yr1: (x−3, y−23) = (−1, −3); yr2: (1, 3). City B yr1: (x−2, y−11) = (−1, −3); yr2: (1, 3).
  4. Run OLS on the demeaned points. Every point satisfies (demeaned y) = 3 × (demeaned x): the slope is b1 = 3. Each extra unit of subsidy intensity adds 3 units of ridership.
  5. The fixed effects canceled. The huge level gap (City A averages 23 vs City B's 11) vanished in step 3 — both cities' demeaned points landed on the same line. That gap was a(A) − a(B), the time-invariant confounder, and the within transformation erased it.

🛠️ Hands-On: fixed effects with linearmodels

We continue with the free course stack — Python + pandas + NumPy + statsmodels — and add linearmodels, whose PanelOLS makes entity and time effects (and clustered SEs) one-liners. Reuse your Week 1–2 environment; you only need the small install note below for this one new package.

⬇ Add linearmodels to your existing environment (one new package)

With your course virtual environment active, install just the panel package:

pip install linearmodels

Verify: run python -c "import linearmodels; print(linearmodels.__version__)" — a version number means you're set.

Below we build a small simulated panel where the true effect of x is 3 and each entity has its own hidden level. We then estimate it three ways and compare. A real study would load public state-year data instead, but the API is identical.

# panel_fixed_effects.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from linearmodels.panel import PanelOLS, PooledOLS

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
n_ent, n_yr = 50, 6
ent = np.repeat(np.arange(n_ent), n_yr)
yr  = np.tile(np.arange(n_yr), n_ent)

alpha = np.repeat(rng.normal(0, 5, n_ent), n_yr)   # hidden entity level a(i)
x = rng.normal(0, 1, n_ent*n_yr) + 0.4*alpha       # x correlated with a(i) -> confounding
y = 3.0*x + alpha + rng.normal(0, 1, n_ent*n_yr)   # true effect b1 = 3

df = pd.DataFrame({"entity": ent, "year": yr, "x": x, "y": y})
df = df.set_index(["entity", "year"])              # MultiIndex: (entity, time)

# 1) Pooled OLS ignores a(i) -> biased upward
print(PooledOLS(df.y, df[["x"]].assign(const=1)).fit().params["x"])

# 2) Entity fixed effects, clustered by entity
fe = PanelOLS(df.y, df[["x"]], entity_effects=True)
print(fe.fit(cov_type="clustered", cluster_entity=True).params["x"])

# 3) Two-way fixed effects (entity + time), clustered
twfe = PanelOLS(df.y, df[["x"]], entity_effects=True, time_effects=True)
res = twfe.fit(cov_type="clustered", cluster_entity=True)
print(res.summary)

Pooled OLS returns a slope well above 3 (the entity confounder leaks in), while both fixed-effects estimates recover roughly 3. Notice cluster_entity=True — without it the standard errors would be too small. Swap in real public panel data, set the index to (entity, time), and the workflow is unchanged.

🔗 Where this is heading

Two-way fixed effects is the bridge to difference-in-differences, where a sharp policy switch lets us interpret the residual jump as a treatment effect — and to the modern literature warning that TWFE can mislead when treatment rolls out at different times across units (staggered adoption). 💬 Discussion

A team evaluates a state job-training program using a two-way fixed-effects regression of employment on a "program active" indicator, with state and year effects and standard errors clustered by state. The coefficient is positive and significant. A reviewer objects: "Fixed effects don't make this causal." Is the reviewer right? What specific threat survives the fixed effects here, and what would you check or add to take the objection seriously?

Show a model response

The reviewer is partly right. State fixed effects remove every time-invariant difference between states (a permanently strong economy, stable political culture), and year fixed effects remove shocks common to all states in a given year (a national recession). What they do not remove is a time-varying, state-specific confounder — and that is exactly the worry for a program that states choose to launch. If a state adopted the training program precisely because its labor market was already starting to recover, that recovery — not the program — drives the employment gain. This is reverse-causal selection on trends, and it passes straight through both sets of fixed effects.

To take the objection seriously I would: (1) run an event study — plot the estimated effect in each period relative to adoption and check that pre-adoption coefficients are flat and near zero, which tests the parallel-trends assumption directly; (2) add state-specific linear time trends as a robustness check and see whether the effect survives; (3) confirm standard errors are clustered by state and worry about the number of clusters (a handful of states needs small-sample adjustments); and (4) if adoption is staggered across years, use a heterogeneity-robust diff-in-diff estimator rather than plain TWFE, since the simple two-way specification can be biased under staggered timing.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) states clearly that fixed effects remove only time-invariant (entity) and common-time confounders; (2) names a concrete time-varying, unit-specific threat such as selection on pre-trends / endogenous policy timing; and (3) proposes at least one concrete diagnostic — an event study / parallel-trends check, unit-specific trends, or a staggered-adoption-robust estimator. Bonus: mentions the number-of-clusters caveat for inference.

❓ Lecture 9 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. What does the within (demeaning) transformation accomplish in a fixed-effects model?
A. It removes any confounder that is constant over time for each entity
B. It removes all measurement error in the outcome
C. It makes the regressor uncorrelated with itself
D. It guarantees the estimate is causal
Show answer

A. Subtracting each entity's own mean cancels a(i), so every time-invariant trait drops out. B is wrong — demeaning does nothing to measurement error. C is meaningless. D is false: time-varying confounders survive, so causality is not guaranteed.

Q2. Which confounder does an entity fixed effect fail to control for?
A. A state's permanent geography
B. A firm's stable management culture
C. A worker's fixed innate ability
D. A time-varying local economic boom that coincides with the policy
Show answer

D. Entity effects absorb only things that are constant over time (A, B, C are all time-invariant). A confounder that changes over the panel — like an economic boom arriving with the policy — passes straight through demeaning.

Q3. A two-way fixed-effects model adds, beyond entity effects, a separate intercept for each…
A. regressor
B. time period, capturing shocks common to all entities that period
C. outcome category
D. standard-error cluster
Show answer

B. Time fixed effects are one intercept per period, absorbing shocks (recessions, national policy) hitting every unit at once. A and C misdescribe the model; D confuses inference (clustering) with the regression specification.

Q4. Why should standard errors in a panel regression usually be clustered by entity?
A. Clustering removes omitted-variable bias from the coefficient
B. Clustering increases the point estimate
C. An entity's errors are correlated over time, so default SEs are too small
D. It is required for the model to converge
Show answer

C. Serial correlation within an entity violates the independence assumption; ignoring it makes default SEs too small and t-stats inflated. A confuses inference with bias (fixed effects, not clustering, address bias); B and D are simply false.

Q5. In the worked example, City A averaged y = 23 and City B averaged y = 11, yet the estimated effect of x was the same b1 = 3 for both. Why did the large level gap not bias the estimate?
A. Because the cities had the same number of observations
B. The within transformation subtracted each city's own mean, removing the level gap a(A) − a(B)
C. Because y was measured without error
D. Because time fixed effects absorbed the gap
Show answer

B. Demeaning within each city erased its baseline level — the time-invariant confounder — leaving only within-city variation. A is irrelevant to the bias; C is unrelated; D is wrong because the gap is an entity-level difference, handled by entity (not time) demeaning.
