Instrumental Variables

When the treatment you care about is tangled up with the error term, you need a lever from outside the model to pry causation loose from correlation.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the endogeneity problem: why OLS is biased when a regressor correlates with the error.
  • Define a valid instrument through its two conditions — relevance and the exclusion restriction.
  • Carry out two-stage least squares (2SLS) and explain what each stage buys you.
  • Interpret the IV estimate as a Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) for compliers.
  • Diagnose weak instruments and explain why they make IV worse than OLS, then run IV in Python.

6.1The Endogeneity Problem

Suppose we want the causal effect of an extra year of schooling on wages, and we run the simple regression

wagei = α + β · schooli + uiWe want β to be the causal return to a year of education — but only if school is "clean."

Ordinary least squares only recovers the true β if the regressor is exogenous — uncorrelated with the error term u. But u soaks up everything we left out: innate ability, family background, ambition. People with more ability tend to get more schooling and earn more for reasons unrelated to school. That makes school correlate with u: the regressor is endogenous.

When a regressor is correlated with the error, OLS is biased and inconsistent — more data does not save you. The estimate is contaminated by every back-door path that runs through the omitted variable:

Cov(school, u) ≠ 0  ⇒  plim β̂OLS = β + Cov(school, u) / Var(school)The bias term does not shrink as the sample grows; it is baked into the population moments.

Endogeneity has three classic sources, all of which break exogeneity in the same way: omitted variables (ability above), simultaneity (price and quantity are set jointly by supply and demand), and measurement error in the regressor. The instrumental-variables method attacks all three with a single idea.

6.2What Makes a Valid Instrument

An instrument Z is a third variable that affects the outcome only through the endogenous regressor. Think of it as a randomizer we found in the wild: it shoves the treatment around for reasons that have nothing to do with the unobserved confounders. A valid instrument must satisfy two conditions.

1. Relevance. The instrument must actually move the treatment. Formally it must be correlated with the endogenous regressor:

Cov(Z, school) ≠ 0If the lever isn't connected to the treatment, pulling it does nothing — this is checkable from the data.

2. The exclusion restriction. The instrument must be uncorrelated with the error — it influences the outcome only through the treatment, never directly and never through a confounder:

Cov(Z, u) = 0This is an assumption about an unobserved error, so it cannot be tested directly — it must be argued from how Z was generated.

A causal diagram makes the logic vivid: Z points into the treatment, the treatment points into the outcome, and the confounder muddies the treatment-outcome link — but no arrow runs from Z to the outcome except the one passing through the treatment.

AnalogyAn instrument is a natural lottery. Imagine the only thing that nudged some students into an extra year of school was an arbitrary calendar rule — a birth-date cutoff that has nothing to do with their ability or ambition. That rule is "as good as random": it reshuffles schooling without touching the confounders, so any wage difference it traces out must run through schooling alone.

6.3Two-Stage Least Squares

How do we actually use the instrument? The mechanics are beautifully simple: we isolate the "clean" part of the treatment — the variation driven only by the instrument — and use only that to explain the outcome. This is two-stage least squares (2SLS).

Stage 1. Regress the endogenous treatment on the instrument (and any controls). The fitted values D̂ are the part of the treatment that the instrument predicts — exogenous by construction:

Di = π0 + π1 Zi + vi  →  D̂i = π̂0 + π̂1 Ziπ1 must be nonzero and strong — this is the relevance condition, and it is testable.

Stage 2. Regress the outcome on the fitted treatment D̂, not the original. Because D̂ is built only from the instrument, it is uncorrelated with the error, so its coefficient is a consistent estimate of the causal effect:

Yi = α + βIV D̂i + eiβIV uses only the instrument-driven variation in D, purging the confounded part.

For a single instrument and single endogenous regressor, the whole thing collapses to a memorable ratio — the Wald estimator — the reduced-form effect of Z on Y divided by the first-stage effect of Z on D:

βIV = Cov(Z, Y) / Cov(Z, D)"Effect of the lever on the outcome" divided by "effect of the lever on the treatment" — the units cancel to give per-unit-of-treatment effect.
Common mistakeDo not run the two regressions by hand and report the second-stage standard errors as-is. Manually plugging in fitted values gives the right coefficient but wrong standard errors, because the second stage ignores that D̂ was itself estimated. Always use a dedicated 2SLS routine (like linearmodels's IV2SLS), which corrects the standard errors for you.

6.4LATE and the Weak-Instrument Danger

Here is the subtle payoff. When treatment effects differ across people, IV does not recover the average effect for everyone. It recovers the Local Average Treatment Effect (LATE) — the average effect only for compliers: the people whose treatment status actually responds to the instrument.

Partition the population by how they react to the instrument: compliers take the treatment when the instrument nudges them and not otherwise; always-takers take it regardless; never-takers never take it; and we assume away defiers (who would do the opposite, ruled out by a monotonicity assumption). Always-takers and never-takers contribute no variation the instrument can exploit, so they drop out:

βIV = LATE = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) | complier ]The effect is "local" to the subpopulation the instrument moves — a different instrument can yield a different LATE.

This is why two valid instruments for the same treatment can give different numbers and both be correct: they identify the effect on different complier groups. LATE is honest about whose effect you measured.

Common mistakeA weak instrument — one with a tiny first-stage correlation — is dangerous, not merely imprecise. Because βIV divides by Cov(Z, D), a near-zero denominator blows up the variance and, worse, reintroduces bias toward OLS even when the exclusion restriction holds. A common rule of thumb: the first-stage F-statistic should exceed about 10. Below that, IV can be more biased than the OLS you were trying to fix.
✦ Worked Example — the Wald estimator end to end

A job-training program is offered by lottery. The instrument Z = 1 if you won a voucher, 0 if not. Treatment D = 1 if you actually completed training; Y = annual earnings (in $1,000s). From the data:

  1. Among voucher winners (Z = 1): 60% completed training, so E[D | Z=1] = 0.60; average earnings E[Y | Z=1] = 38.
  2. Among non-winners (Z = 0): 20% completed training anyway (always-takers), so E[D | Z=0] = 0.20; average earnings E[Y | Z=0] = 32.
  3. First stage (effect of Z on D): 0.60 − 0.20 = 0.40. The instrument is relevant — winning a voucher raises completion by 40 percentage points.
  4. Reduced form (effect of Z on Y): 38 − 32 = 6 ($6,000 more earnings for winners).
  5. Wald / IV estimate: βIV = 6 / 0.40 = 15. Completing training raises earnings by about $15,000.
  6. Interpretation (LATE): This $15k is the effect for compliers — the 40% who trained because they won the voucher and would not have otherwise. It says nothing about always-takers or never-takers, and a different recruitment lever could yield a different LATE.

🛠️ Hands-On: IV in Python

We use the course's free stack — Python with pandas, NumPy, and statsmodels (set up in the Week 1/2 install details) — plus linearmodels, which provides a clean 2SLS estimator with correct standard errors and a built-in weak-instrument test.

⬇ New tool this week: install linearmodels

Your Week 1/2 environment already has pandas, NumPy, and statsmodels. Add the one new package:

pip install linearmodels

Verify: run python -c "import linearmodels; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

We simulate the schooling-and-wages story with a known confounder (ability) and a clean instrument (a birth-date cutoff), so we can see IV recover the truth that OLS misses.

# iv_schooling.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
from linearmodels.iv import IV2SLS

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
n = 5000
ability = rng.normal(0, 1, n)            # unobserved confounder
Z       = rng.binomial(1, 0.5, n)        # instrument: birth-date cutoff

# school depends on the instrument AND on ability (endogeneity)
school = 10 + 1.5*Z + 0.8*ability + rng.normal(0, 1, n)
# TRUE causal return = 2.0 per year; ability also raises wages directly
wage   = 20 + 2.0*school + 3.0*ability + rng.normal(0, 2, n)

df = pd.DataFrame({"wage": wage, "school": school, "Z": Z})

# Naive OLS -- biased upward because ability is in the error
ols = sm.OLS(df["wage"], sm.add_constant(df["school"])).fit()
print("OLS return:", round(ols.params["school"], 3))   # ~2.4, too high

# 2SLS with Z as the instrument for school
iv = IV2SLS(df["wage"], sm.add_constant(df[[]]),
            df["school"], df["Z"]).fit()
print("IV  return:", round(iv.params["school"], 3))     # ~2.0, the truth
print(iv.first_stage)   # check first-stage F > 10 (relevance)

OLS overstates the return because ability inflates both schooling and wages. IV, leaning only on the instrument's exogenous push, lands on the true 2.0 — and the first-stage F confirms the instrument is strong. Try shrinking the 1.5*Z coefficient toward 0 to manufacture a weak instrument and watch the IV estimate destabilize.

💬 Discussion

Birth-date cutoffs, weather, distance to the nearest college, draft lotteries, and policy rollout dates have all been used as instruments for hard-to-randomize treatments. Pick one treatment you would love to estimate the causal effect of (say, the effect of military service on later earnings, or of attending a charter school on test scores). Propose a candidate instrument, then argue both sides: make the strongest case that your instrument satisfies relevance and the exclusion restriction — and then the strongest case that it secretly violates the exclusion restriction. Whose LATE would your instrument identify?

Show a model response

Treatment: the effect of serving in the military on later civilian earnings. Instrument: a draft-lottery number assigned by birth date.

Case FOR validity. Relevance: a low lottery number sharply raised the probability of serving — easily checkable as a strong first stage. Exclusion: the number is assigned by a public random draw on birth date, so it is plausibly unrelated to a person's ability, family wealth, or ambition; it should affect earnings only by changing whether they served.

Case AGAINST validity. The exclusion restriction can fail if the lottery number affects earnings through a channel other than service. For example, men with low numbers might stay in school longer to defer the draft, or change career and marriage timing in anticipation — so the number moves earnings even for some who never served. If so, Z is correlated with the error and IV is biased.

Whose LATE? The estimate is the effect for compliers — men who served because of a low number and would not have volunteered otherwise. It does not speak to career-military always-takers or to those exempt for other reasons.

❓ Lecture 6 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A regressor is "endogenous" when it…
A. is correlated with the error term
B. is measured without any error
C. has a large variance
D. is determined before the experiment
Show answer

A. Endogeneity means Cov(regressor, error) ≠ 0, which biases OLS. (B) describes the opposite of measurement-error problems; large variance (C) is unrelated; and being pre-determined (D) would tend to make a variable exogenous, not endogenous.

Q2. The two conditions a valid instrument must satisfy are…
A. normality and homoskedasticity
B. relevance (correlated with the treatment) and the exclusion restriction (uncorrelated with the error)
C. unbiasedness and consistency
D. linearity and large sample size
Show answer

B. A valid instrument must move the treatment (relevance) and affect the outcome only through the treatment (exclusion). (A) and (D) are regression assumptions, not instrument conditions; (C) describes estimator properties, not the instrument itself.

Q3. In two-stage least squares, the second stage regresses the outcome on…
A. the instrument directly
B. the raw (original) treatment variable
C. the residuals from the first stage
D. the fitted values of the treatment from the first stage
Show answer

D. Stage 2 uses the predicted treatment D̂ — the part driven only by the instrument, hence exogenous. Regressing on Z directly (A) gives the reduced form, not the structural effect; the raw treatment (B) is still endogenous; first-stage residuals (C) are the endogenous part you want to discard.

Q4. The IV estimate identifies the Local Average Treatment Effect, meaning the average effect for…
A. always-takers
B. never-takers
C. compliers — those whose treatment status responds to the instrument
D. the entire population
Show answer

C. LATE is the effect for compliers, the only group whose treatment the instrument actually moves. Always-takers (A) and never-takers (B) supply no instrument-driven variation, and IV does not generally recover the whole-population average (D) when effects are heterogeneous.

Q5. A "weak instrument" (first-stage F well below 10) is dangerous mainly because…
A. the IV estimate becomes high-variance and biased back toward OLS
B. it automatically violates the exclusion restriction
C. it forces the treatment effect to be zero
D. it makes OLS unbiased
Show answer

A. Because βIV divides by the first-stage covariance, a near-zero first stage explodes the variance and reintroduces OLS-like bias. A weak instrument can still satisfy exclusion (B is about a separate condition); it does not zero out the effect (C); and it has no bearing on whether OLS is unbiased (D).
