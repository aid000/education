Selection Bias & Confounding

The treated and the untreated were never the same people to begin with — and that hidden difference is exactly what a naive comparison mistakes for an effect.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a naive comparison of treated vs. untreated outcomes is generally biased.
  • Decompose the observed difference in means into the causal effect plus a selection-bias term.
  • Define a confounder and read a back-door path off a simple causal graph.
  • Connect confounding to omitted-variable bias and predict the sign of that bias.
  • Explain why "controlling for" the right variables removes bias — and why controlling for the wrong ones creates it.

3.1Why the Naive Comparison Lies

Week 2 showed that a randomized experiment makes the treated and control groups statistically identical, so a simple difference in average outcomes recovers the causal effect. But most policy questions can't be randomized: we observe who chose to take a training program, who chose to go to a hospital, who chose to adopt a new technology. The people who took the treatment are systematically different from those who didn't — and that is the whole problem.

Recall the potential-outcomes notation from Week 1. Each unit i has an outcome under treatment, Yi(1), and under control, Yi(0); the individual effect is their difference. We only ever see one of the two. The quantity we usually want is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) — how much treatment helped the people who actually took it.

ATT = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) | D = 1 ]The average effect of treatment among units that received it (D = 1 means "treated").

What we actually compute from data is the difference in observed group means: the average outcome of the treated minus the average outcome of the untreated. The danger is assuming that difference equals the ATT. In an observational setting it does not, because the untreated group is a flawed stand-in for what the treated group would have looked like without treatment.

3.2Decomposing Selection Bias

Let's write the naive comparison out and see exactly where it goes wrong. Add and subtract the missing counterfactual term E[Y(0) | D = 1] — the average control-state outcome of the treated people, which we never observe:

E[Y | D=1] - E[Y | D=0] = ATT + ( E[Y(0)|D=1] - E[Y(0)|D=0] )Observed gap = true effect on the treated + selection bias.

The second bracket is the selection bias: the difference in the untreated-state outcomes between the two groups. It asks: even with no treatment at all, would the treated group have had a different outcome than the control group? If yes, the comparison is contaminated before treatment ever acts.

Make it concrete. Suppose a job-training program is taken mostly by highly motivated workers. The motivated would have earned more even without training, so E[Y(0)|D=1] > E[Y(0)|D=0], and selection bias is positive — we overstate the program's value. Flip it: if a hospital's intensive-care unit treats the sickest patients, those patients would have fared worse anyway, so the bias is negative and the raw numbers make the care look harmful. Same algebra, opposite sign.

AnalogySelection bias is like judging an umbrella's effectiveness by comparing people who carry one to people who don't — on rainy days. Umbrella-carriers get wet less, but mostly because they're the kind of people who check the forecast and stay indoors. The umbrella (treatment) and the staying-dry (outcome) are both driven by a third thing: how cautious the person is. The raw comparison credits the umbrella for caution's work.

3.3Confounders & Back-Door Paths

That "third thing" has a name: a confounder — a common cause of both the treatment and the outcome. Causal graphs make this visible. We draw variables as nodes and direct causes as arrows. A confounder X sits upstream of both the treatment D and the outcome Y:

A back-door path is any path connecting D and Y that starts with an arrow pointing into D (here D ← X → Y). It is not a causal channel, yet it transmits statistical association: groups with different D differ in X, and X moves Y directly. An open back-door path is precisely what produces the selection-bias term from 3.2. The goal of causal inference is to block every back-door path while leaving the front-door causal arrow intact.

You block a back-door path by conditioning on (controlling for) a variable on that path — here, on X. Within each level of X, the treated and untreated no longer differ in X, so the leak is sealed. This is the graphical meaning of the back-door criterion: find a set of variables that closes all back-door paths and contains no "bad controls" (3.4).

AnalogyThink of D and Y as two rooms connected by two doors. The front door (D -> Y) is the real causal hallway you want to measure foot traffic through. The back door (D <- X -> Y) is a side passage that lets people slip between the rooms without using the hallway. To measure true hallway traffic, you don't brick up the front door — you just lock the back door by conditioning on X.

3.4Confounding as Omitted-Variable Bias

If you've taken econometrics, you already know this story under a different name. Suppose the true outcome model is linear in the treatment D and the confounder X:

Y = b0 + b1*D + b2*X + eb1 is the causal effect of D; X is a confounder that also affects Y.

If we omit X and regress Y on D alone, the estimated slope is not b1. The classic omitted-variable-bias formula tells you exactly how far off you'll be:

plim(b1_hat) = b1 + b2 * dwhere d is the slope of regressing the omitted X on the included D (how D and X move together).

The bias term is b2 · d: (effect of the confounder on the outcome) × (association between confounder and treatment). It is zero only when X doesn't affect Y (b2 = 0) or when X is uncorrelated with D (d = 0). Randomization works precisely because it forces d = 0 — treatment is assigned independently of every X. This is the same selection-bias term from 3.2, now in regression clothing, and its sign is fully predictable: motivation raises both training uptake (d > 0) and earnings (b2 > 0), so the omitted-variable bias is positive.

So "controlling for the right variables" means including, in your regression or matching, exactly the confounders that sit on open back-door paths. Do that and b1_hat becomes consistent for the causal effect.

Common mistakeMore controls are not always better. Never control for a variable that lies on the causal pathway after treatment (a mediator) — that throws away part of the effect you're trying to measure. Worse, never control for a collider: a variable caused by both D and Y (or by their causes). Conditioning on a collider opens a spurious path and creates bias where there was none. "Add every variable you have" is how careful-looking studies get the sign wrong.
✦ Worked Example — sign and size the selection bias

A voluntary job-training program. Among trainees (D=1), average post-program earnings are $32,000. Among non-trainees (D=0), average earnings are $28,000. An audit reveals that, had they not trained, the trainees would still have earned $30,000 on average (they were more motivated). Find the true ATT and the selection bias.

  1. Naive comparison: E[Y|D=1] - E[Y|D=0] = 32,000 - 28,000 = +$4,000.
  2. Counterfactual control outcome of the treated: E[Y(0)|D=1] = 30,000 (given by the audit).
  3. True ATT: E[Y(1)|D=1] - E[Y(0)|D=1] = 32,000 - 30,000 = +$2,000.
  4. Selection bias: E[Y(0)|D=1] - E[Y(0)|D=0] = 30,000 - 28,000 = +$2,000.
  5. Check the decomposition: ATT + bias = 2,000 + 2,000 = 4,000 = the naive gap. ✓ The naive estimate doubles the real effect — motivation, the confounder, did half the work the program got credit for.

🛠️ Hands-On: see confounding bias in Python

We use the same free stack from Weeks 1–2 — pandas, NumPy, statsmodels, and linearmodels. (The full one-time install lives in the Week 1 lecture's setup box; no new tools are needed here.) We'll simulate data where a confounder X drives both treatment and outcome, then watch the naive regression inherit omitted-variable bias and a controlled regression recover the truth.

# confounding_demo.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
n = 20_000

# X = motivation (the confounder). It raises BOTH treatment and outcome.
X = rng.normal(0, 1, n)
# Treatment depends on X: motivated people are likelier to train.
p = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-(0.0 + 1.2 * X)))
D = rng.binomial(1, p)

# TRUE causal effect of D is 2.0. X also adds 3.0 per unit to Y.
Y = 1.0 + 2.0 * D + 3.0 * X + rng.normal(0, 1, n)
df = pd.DataFrame({"Y": Y, "D": D, "X": X})

naive = smf.ols("Y ~ D", data=df).fit()
adj   = smf.ols("Y ~ D + X", data=df).fit()

print("Naive  (omits X):", round(naive.params["D"], 3))   # ~3.3 — biased UP
print("Adjusted (+ X)  :", round(adj.params["D"],   3))   # ~2.0 — recovers truth

The naive slope on D lands well above 2.0: it absorbs the b2·d bias because motivated people both train more and earn more. Adding X to the regression blocks the back-door path D ← X → Y, and the estimate snaps back to the true effect of 2.0. Try setting the 1.2 * X coefficient in p to 0.0 — that makes treatment independent of X (like randomization), and the naive estimate becomes unbiased on its own.

🔗 Where this is heading

Knowing which variables to control for is half the battle; the other half is how. Week 4 turns the back-door criterion into practice with regression adjustment and the conditional-independence (selection-on-observables) assumption. But controls only close back-doors you can measure — when confounders are unobserved, you need a different lever: instrumental variables exploit a front-door-style source of variation, difference-in-differences and panel methods difference out fixed confounders, and regression discontinuity finds a slice of data that is locally as-good-as-random. The same causal-graph reasoning underpins treatment-effect estimation across the Quant Finance and Policy tracks.

💬 Discussion

A widely cited observational study reports that people who drink one to two glasses of wine per day live longer than both teetotalers and heavy drinkers. A headline concludes: "Moderate drinking causes longer life." Using the language of this lecture — selection bias, confounders, back-door paths, and bad controls — argue why this causal claim is shaky. Name at least one plausible confounder, sketch its back-door path, predict the sign of the resulting bias, and propose one variable that would be a bad control. Then describe what evidence would actually change your mind.

Show a model response

The comparison is observational: moderate drinkers were never randomly assigned. The most plausible confounder is baseline health and socioeconomic status (SES). Healthier, wealthier people can afford moderate, social drinking and tend to live longer for unrelated reasons; the sickest people often stop drinking (becoming "teetotalers"), so the abstainer group is contaminated by former drinkers who quit due to illness — a "sick-quitter" effect.

Back-door path: Drinking ← Health/SES → Longevity. Health/SES causes both the moderate-drinking pattern and longevity, so this path is open and non-causal. Its bias is positive: it makes moderate drinking look protective because healthier people are over-represented among moderate drinkers. To support the causal claim we would need to block this path by conditioning on health and SES.

A bad control would be a downstream consequence of drinking, such as blood pressure or HDL cholesterol measured after years of drinking — these are mediators on the causal pathway, so conditioning on them removes part of the very effect we want. Conditioning on a collider like "hospitalized for a liver condition" (caused by both heavy drinking and poor health) would be worse, opening a spurious path.

What would change my mind: a design that breaks the back-door path without measuring every confounder — e.g., an instrumental-variable or natural-experiment approach (a genetic variant affecting alcohol metabolism, used as a Mendelian-randomization instrument), or a large randomized trial of a modest alcohol intervention with mortality follow-up. Convergent results across these designs would be persuasive in a way the raw observational gap is not.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) names a concrete confounder that plausibly causes both treatment and outcome; (2) draws its back-door path as D ← X → Y; (3) predicts the bias sign with a reason; (4) identifies a mediator or collider as a bad control; and (5) proposes a design (IV / natural experiment / RCT) that closes the back-door, not just "add more controls."

❓ Lecture 3 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The selection-bias term in the decomposition E[Y|D=1] - E[Y|D=0] = ATT + bias is equal to…
A. E[Y(0)|D=1] - E[Y(0)|D=0]
B. E[Y(1)|D=1] - E[Y(0)|D=1]
C. the variance of the treatment effect
D. always zero in observational data
Show answer

A. Selection bias is the difference in the untreated-state outcomes between the groups — whether they'd have differed even with no treatment. B is the ATT itself; C is unrelated; D is exactly backwards — bias is generally nonzero in observational data and zero under randomization.

Q2. A confounder is best defined as a variable that…
A. is caused by both the treatment and the outcome
B. lies on the causal pathway between treatment and outcome
C. is a common cause of both the treatment and the outcome
D. has no relationship to either treatment or outcome
Show answer

C. A confounder causes both D and Y, opening a back-door path. A describes a collider (conditioning on it creates bias); B describes a mediator (a bad control); D describes an irrelevant variable.

Q3. In the omitted-variable-bias formula plim(b1_hat) = b1 + b2·d, the bias vanishes when…
A. the sample size is small
B. the omitted variable is uncorrelated with the treatment (d = 0)
C. the treatment effect b1 is large
D. the error term has high variance
Show answer

B. The bias b2·d is zero if either the confounder doesn't affect Y (b2=0) or it's uncorrelated with D (d=0). Randomization guarantees d=0. Sample size (A), the size of b1 (C), and error variance (D) don't remove this asymptotic bias.

Q4. A naive study finds that patients sent to the ICU have higher death rates than other patients, concluding the ICU is harmful. The most likely explanation is…
A. positive selection bias inflating the ICU's apparent benefit
B. randomization failed to balance the groups
C. the ICU truly increases mortality
D. negative selection bias: the ICU treats the sickest patients, who would fare worse anyway
Show answer

D. Severity is a confounder that raises both ICU admission and mortality, so E[Y(0)|D=1] > E[Y(0)|D=0] and the bias is negative — making beneficial care look harmful. There's no randomization here (B), and the data don't support a true causal harm (C); A has the sign backwards.

Q5. Which variable is a bad control that you should NOT condition on when estimating the causal effect of D on Y?
A. a pre-treatment confounder that causes both D and Y
B. a baseline characteristic measured before treatment
C. a collider caused jointly by D and Y
D. a variable on an open back-door path
Show answer

C. Conditioning on a collider opens a spurious path and creates bias where there was none. A and D are exactly the confounders you should control for; B is generally safe because it precedes treatment.

✓ What you learned

  • A naive comparison of treated vs. untreated equals the true effect plus selection bias, because the groups differ before treatment acts.
  • Decomposed the observed gap into ATT + selection bias, and learned to read the bias's sign from who selects in.
  • A confounder is a common cause of D and Y; it opens a back-door path D ← X → Y that you block by conditioning on X.
  • Confounding is omitted-variable bias: the bias equals b2·d, the confounder's effect on Y times its association with D.
  • Controlling for the right variables closes back-doors; controlling for mediators or colliders creates new bias.
