Week 1 · Lecture 1

The Causal Question & Potential Outcomes

Before any regression runs, you must decide what you are even asking: would the world have been different had we acted otherwise?

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish a causal question from a predictive one, and say why correlation alone cannot answer it.
  • Define the potential outcomes Y(1) and Y(0) for a single unit under treatment and control.
  • Write the individual treatment effect and explain the fundamental problem of causal inference.
  • Define the average treatment effect (ATE) and the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT) as expectations.
  • Use counterfactual reasoning to see why a naive difference in group means is biased.

1.1Prediction vs. Causation

Most of the statistics you have met so far answers a predictive question: given what I observe, what is my best guess of an outcome? A model that predicts hospital readmission from a patient's chart is enormously useful — but it tells you nothing about what would happen if you changed the treatment plan. That is a different kind of question entirely.

A causal question asks about a hypothetical intervention: if we set the treatment to one value rather than another, for the same unit, how would the outcome differ? "Does this job-training program raise earnings?" is causal. "Which workers tend to have high earnings?" is predictive. The two can have opposite answers — trainees may earn less than non-trainees simply because struggling workers are the ones who enroll.

Prediction: E[Y | X = x]  vs.  Causation: E[Y if we set T = t]The left object conditions on what we passively see; the right object intervenes and asks "what if". They coincide only under special conditions.

This distinction is the entire foundation of policy evaluation. A minimum-wage study, a drug trial, an ad-spend ROI estimate — all are causal questions dressed in different clothes. To answer them precisely we need a language for "what would have happened otherwise." That language is potential outcomes.

1.2The Potential-Outcomes Framework

Fix a single unit i — a person, a firm, a county. Let the treatment be binary: Ti = 1 if the unit is treated, Ti = 0 if not. The key idea (the Neyman–Rubin model) is that unit i carries two potential outcomes, one for each version of the world:

Yi(1) = outcome if unit i is treated
Yi(0) = outcome if unit i is untreatedBoth are properties of the unit that exist before we choose anything. Treatment merely reveals one of them.

Crucially, Yi(1) and Yi(0) are defined for every unit, treated or not. A control unit still has a Y(1); we just never observe it. What we actually record is the outcome corresponding to the treatment the unit received — the observed outcome:

Yi = Ti · Yi(1) + (1 - Ti) · Yi(0)If T = 1 we see Y(1); if T = 0 we see Y(0). This "switching equation" links the two potential outcomes to the one number in the dataset.
AnalogyThink of each unit standing in front of two sealed envelopes: one says "your outcome if treated," the other "your outcome if not." Both envelopes were written before you decided. Assigning the treatment doesn't create an outcome — it only lets you open one envelope. The other is sealed forever. Causal inference is the art of reasoning about the envelope you never get to open.

1.3The Individual Effect & the Fundamental Problem

The thing we ultimately care about is the individual treatment effect — how much the treatment changed this unit's outcome:

τi = Yi(1) - Yi(0)The difference between the two envelopes for unit i. This is the cleanest possible definition of "the effect of the treatment."

Here is the catch that defines the whole field. To compute τi we would need both Yi(1) and Yi(0) for the same unit. But a unit is either treated or not — we observe exactly one of the two. The other is the counterfactual, and it is missing for every single unit. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference: the individual effect is never observed.

Common mistakeStudents often try to "recover" the missing counterfactual by using the same unit later (its before-treatment value) or a different but similar unit as if it were the same person. Both are substitutions, not observations — and they are only valid under explicit assumptions you must defend. Treating a control unit's outcome as if it were the treated unit's missing Y(0) is exactly the leap that causal inference has to justify, not assume.

Because we can never see τi directly, the field pivots to a humbler but achievable target: averages of the individual effects across many units. Averages can be identified from data even when no single effect can.

1.4ATE, ATT & Counterfactual Reasoning

If we average the individual effect over the whole population we get the average treatment effect (ATE) — the headline number most policy questions want:

ATE = E[ τi ] = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) ] = E[Y(1)] - E[Y(0)]The mean causal effect if we treated everyone vs. no one. Linearity of expectation lets us split the difference.

Sometimes we care only about those who actually got treated — for instance, the effect of a training program on the people who enrolled. That is the average treatment effect on the treated (ATT):

ATT = E[ Y(1) - Y(0) | T = 1 ]Conditions on the treated subgroup. Its second term E[Y(0) | T=1] is fully counterfactual -- the treated units' outcomes had they NOT been treated.

Now the temptation: just compare the average outcome of the treated with the average outcome of the controls. Call this the naive difference in means. Decompose it with the potential-outcomes algebra:

E[Y | T=1] - E[Y | T=0] = ATT + { E[Y(0) | T=1] - E[Y(0) | T=0] }The braced term is SELECTION BIAS: how the treated and control groups differ in their untreated potential outcome.

The naive comparison equals the ATT plus a bias term. That bias is nonzero whenever the kind of unit that gets treated would have had a different Y(0) anyway — sicker patients seek treatment, struggling firms take loans, poorer regions receive aid. Only when assignment is unrelated to potential outcomes (the job of Week 2's randomization) does the bias vanish and the difference in means recover the ATE.

✦ Worked Example — true ATE vs. the naive estimate

Suppose an oracle hands us both potential outcomes for the four units in the figure (in reality impossible — this is to build intuition). Earnings in $1,000s: A: Y(1)=8, Y(0)=6; B: Y(1)=7, Y(0)=5; C: Y(1)=6, Y(0)=4; D: Y(1)=5, Y(0)=3. Units A and C were treated; B and D were controls. Compute the true ATE, the true ATT, and the naive difference in means, then explain the gap.

  1. Individual effects: τ = Y(1) - Y(0) is 2 for every unit (8-6, 7-5, 6-4, 5-3). So the effect is a constant +2.
  2. True ATE: average of the τi = (2+2+2+2)/4 = +2 (i.e. $2,000).
  3. True ATT: average τ over the treated A and C = (2+2)/2 = +2. Same here because the effect is constant.
  4. Naive difference in means: treated observe Y(1) = {8, 6}, mean 7. Controls observe Y(0) = {5, 3}, mean 4. Difference = 7 - 4 = +3.
  5. The gap: the naive estimate (+3) overstates the true effect (+2) by +1. Source: the treated units (A, C) had higher untreated outcomes too — E[Y(0)|T=1] = (6+4)/2 = 5 vs. E[Y(0)|T=0] = (5+3)/2 = 4. That +1 selection bias is exactly the braced term in §1.4. The treated were "better off anyway," so the raw comparison flatters the program.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate potential outcomes in Python

All course we use a free, open stack: Python with NumPy, pandas, statsmodels, and linearmodels on public data. Because we can simulate a world, we get to peek at both potential outcomes — the only setting where you can check an estimator against ground truth. Week 1 setup: install once below, then reuse every lecture and for the project.

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the stack:

Verify: run python -c "import numpy, pandas, statsmodels, linearmodels; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

We build a population where the true effect is exactly +2 for everyone, but treated units have higher baseline outcomes (selection). We then compare the impossible-to-know true ATE against the naive difference in means.

# potential_outcomes.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
n = 10_000

# A latent "baseline ability" drives both Y(0) and who gets treated
ability = rng.normal(0, 1, n)

# Potential outcomes: constant treatment effect of +2 for EVERY unit
Y0 = 5 + 2.0 * ability + rng.normal(0, 1, n)   # outcome if untreated
Y1 = Y0 + 2.0                                   # outcome if treated

# Selection: higher-ability units are more likely to be treated
p_treat = 1 / (1 + np.exp(-1.5 * ability))
T = rng.binomial(1, p_treat)

# Switching equation: we observe only one potential outcome per unit
Y = T * Y1 + (1 - T) * Y0
df = pd.DataFrame({"T": T, "Y": Y, "Y1": Y1, "Y0": Y0})

true_ate  = (df.Y1 - df.Y0).mean()                       # oracle: +2
naive_diff = df.loc[df.T == 1, "Y"].mean() - df.loc[df.T == 0, "Y"].mean()
print(f"True ATE        = {true_ate:.3f}")               # ~2.000
print(f"Naive diff means = {naive_diff:.3f}")            # > 2: biased upward

The true ATE prints as ~2.000, but the naive difference comes out well above 2 — selection bias, because high-ability units both get treated more and have higher Y(0). In real data the Y1/Y0 columns simply don't exist; the rest of the course is about recovering the ATE without that oracle.

🔗 Where this is heading

Potential outcomes are the grammar of every method in this course. Week 2 shows how randomized experiments sever the link between treatment and potential outcomes, killing the selection-bias term so the difference in means is unbiased for the ATE. When randomization is impossible, later weeks rebuild a credible counterfactual another way: matching and regression adjustment on observed covariates, difference-in-differences using pre-treatment trends, instrumental variables exploiting quasi-random nudges, and regression discontinuity at policy cutoffs. Every one of them is just a different strategy for estimating the same E[Y(0)] you can never see — and the identification logic connects directly to the structural and time-series tools in the wider Quant Economics track.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Which of the following is a causal question rather than a predictive one?
A. Which patients are most likely to be readmitted?
B. Would prescribing this drug lower this patient's readmission risk?
C. What is the average readmission rate this year?
D. How correlated are age and readmission?
Show answer

B. It asks what would happen if we intervened (prescribe vs. not) for the same unit — a counterfactual. A and C are descriptive/predictive; D is a correlation, which cannot by itself answer a "what if we acted" question.

Q2. The fundamental problem of causal inference is that…
A. treatments are usually expensive to administer
B. outcomes are measured with error
C. for any unit we observe only one of its two potential outcomes
D. sample sizes are always too small
Show answer

C. A unit is either treated or not, so we see Y(1) or Y(0) but never both — the other is the missing counterfactual. A, B, and D are practical nuisances, not the conceptual problem that makes the individual effect unobservable.

Q3. The switching equation Y = T·Y(1) + (1−T)·Y(0) says that…
A. the observed outcome equals the potential outcome matching the assigned treatment
B. treatment always increases the outcome
C. Y(1) and Y(0) are always equal
D. the treatment effect is the sum of the two potential outcomes
Show answer

A. When T=1 the equation returns Y(1); when T=0 it returns Y(0) — it just selects the relevant envelope. B is false (effects can be zero or negative); C would make every effect zero; D confuses the effect (a difference) with a sum.

Q4. The ATT differs from the ATE because the ATT…
A. uses only the control group
B. averages the effect over only the treated units
C. ignores potential outcomes entirely
D. is always larger than the ATE
Show answer

B. ATT = E[Y(1) − Y(0) | T=1] averages the individual effect over the treated subgroup, whereas the ATE averages over everyone. A is wrong (the treated define it); C is wrong (it is built from potential outcomes); D is false — the ordering depends on who selects in.

Q5. A naive difference in means equals ATT + { E[Y(0)|T=1] − E[Y(0)|T=0] }. The braced term is zero when…
A. the treatment effect is large
B. the sample is very big
C. outcomes are normally distributed
D. treated and control units have the same average untreated outcome (no selection on Y(0))
Show answer

D. The braced term is selection bias: the difference in the groups' untreated potential outcomes. It vanishes precisely when assignment is unrelated to Y(0) — e.g. under randomization. Effect size (A), sample size (B), and the outcome's distribution (C) do not remove selection bias.

✓ What you learned

  • Separated causal from predictive questions — causation is about hypothetical interventions, not passive observation.
  • Defined the two potential outcomes Y(1) and Y(0) and the switching equation linking them to the observed Y.
  • Stated the fundamental problem of causal inference: the individual effect Y(1) − Y(0) is never observed.
  • Defined the ATE and ATT as expectations of the individual effect over different populations.
  • Decomposed the naive difference in means into ATT plus selection bias, and saw the bias numerically.
