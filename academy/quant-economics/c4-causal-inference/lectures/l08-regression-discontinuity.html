Regression Discontinuity Design

When a treatment switches on at an arbitrary cutoff, the lucky and the unlucky on either side are nearly identical — and that razor-thin border is your experiment.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recognize a regression discontinuity setting: a continuous running variable that triggers treatment at a known cutoff.
  • Distinguish a sharp RDD (treatment jumps from 0 to 1) from a fuzzy RDD (only the probability of treatment jumps).
  • Estimate the causal effect as the size of the jump at the cutoff using local linear regression.
  • State and defend the continuity assumption that makes the design credible.
  • Choose a bandwidth and probe the sensitivity of your estimate to it.

8.1A Running Variable and a Cutoff

Many policies are switched on by a hard threshold. A student is offered a scholarship if their test score is at least 80. A patient is admitted to intensive care if a severity index crosses 30. A county receives federal aid if its poverty rate exceeds 20%. In every case there is a continuous running variable (call it X) and a sharp cutoff c: cross it and the treatment turns on.

Here is the key insight. A unit that scored 79.9 and one that scored 80.1 are, for all practical purposes, the same kind of unit — same ability, same background, same luck on the day. Yet one gets treated and the other does not, decided by a sliver of measurement noise. Right at the cutoff, treatment is as good as randomly assigned. So if outcomes jump as we cross c, that jump can only be the treatment talking.

Di = 1 if Xi ≥ c,   else 0Sharp RDD: the treatment indicator D is a deterministic step function of the running variable X.

We never observe the same county both above and below the line, so we cannot difference within a unit (that was Week 7's trick). Instead we compare units just above versus just below, and use a regression to extrapolate each side cleanly up to the boundary.

AnalogyThink of a speed-limit camera that fines you at exactly 100 km/h. A driver at 99 and a driver at 101 are essentially the same careful-ish person, but one gets a ticket and one does not. If ticketed drivers later slow down far more than un-ticketed ones, that gap at the 100 line measures the ticket's causal effect — not the effect of being a fast versus a slow person, because at the line the two groups are indistinguishable.

8.2Estimating the Jump at the Cutoff

The estimand is the local average treatment effect at the cutoff: the difference between the limit of the outcome as we approach c from the right (treated) and from the left (untreated).

τ = limx → c+ E[Y | X=x] − limx → c- E[Y | X=x]The treatment effect is the vertical gap between the two regression curves exactly at the cutoff.

In practice we fit a regression of Y on X separately on each side of the cutoff and read off the two intercepts at X = c. Pooling everything into one straight line would smear the jump away — the whole point is to let the two sides have their own slopes and levels. The cleanest specification centers the running variable and interacts it with treatment:

Yi = α + τ Di + β(Xi − c) + γ Di(Xi − c) + εiτ is the coefficient on D: the jump in the intercept at X = c. β is the left slope, β+γ the right slope.

Because we only trust the design near the cutoff, we restrict the data to a window of width h on each side — the bandwidth — and fit a low-order (usually linear) line within it. That is local linear regression: a straight line is a fine approximation over a narrow window, and it avoids the wild tail behavior of high-degree polynomials.

Common mistakeDo not fit a single regression line through all the data and call its slope the treatment effect. The effect is the vertical jump at the cutoff, not a slope, and you must estimate the two sides separately. Fitting one pooled line — or one high-degree global polynomial — confounds the discontinuity with the natural trend of Y in X and is a classic way to manufacture a fake jump (or hide a real one).

8.3The Continuity Assumption & Sharp vs Fuzzy

RDD is credible only if one thing is true: everything else that affects the outcome varies smoothly through the cutoff. Formally, the conditional expectations of the potential outcomes E[Y(0)|X] and E[Y(1)|X] are continuous in X at c. If that holds, the only thing that can jump at c is the treatment, so the jump in observed Y is causal.

The assumption fails if units can manipulate the running variable to land on the favorable side — say, a teacher nudging a 79 up to an 80 for deserving students. Then those just above the cutoff differ systematically from those just below, and continuity breaks. We probe this two ways: a density test (is there suspicious bunching of units just above the cutoff?) and covariate balance (do pre-treatment characteristics jump at c? They shouldn't).

So far we assumed treatment switches cleanly from 0 to 1 — a sharp design. Often the cutoff only changes the probability of treatment: crossing the eligibility line makes you far more likely to enroll, but some eligible people decline and some ineligible ones find a way in. That is a fuzzy RDD, and we scale the outcome jump by the treatment-probability jump — exactly the instrumental-variables logic from Week 6, with cutoff-crossing as the instrument:

τfuzzy = ( jump in Y at c ) / ( jump in P(D=1) at c )A Wald / fuzzy-RDD ratio: the outcome jump divided by the first-stage jump in treatment probability gives the effect on compliers at the cutoff.

8.4Bandwidth Choice & Sensitivity

The bandwidth h sets a bias-variance trade-off. A wide window keeps many observations (low variance) but pulls in units far from the cutoff, where the linear approximation gets worse (high bias). A narrow window is locally honest (low bias) but throws away data, so the estimate is noisy (high variance).

MSE(h) = Bias(h)2 + Variance(h)Wider h: bias grows, variance shrinks. Narrower h: bias shrinks, variance grows. The optimal h minimizes their sum.

Modern practice picks h with a data-driven rule that targets minimum mean-squared error (the Imbens–Kalyanaraman / Calonico–Cattaneo–Titiunik selectors), often paired with a triangular kernel that weights points nearer the cutoff more heavily. You should never report a single number, though: the credibility of an RDD lives or dies on a sensitivity check. Re-estimate τ across a range of bandwidths (half the optimal, the optimal, double it) and plot the estimates with confidence bands. A trustworthy result is roughly flat across reasonable windows; a result that swings wildly with h is a red flag.

Common mistakeReaching for a high-order global polynomial (cubic, quartic) instead of local linear regression. High-degree polynomials chase noise in the tails and produce erratic, misleading values right at the boundary where you care most. Prefer local linear (or local quadratic) within a sensible bandwidth, and report sensitivity to h rather than the polynomial degree.
✦ Worked Example — a scholarship at score 80

A scholarship is awarded to any applicant scoring at least 80 on an entrance exam (running variable X = score, cutoff c = 80, sharp design). The outcome Y is first-year GPA. Within a bandwidth of h = 5 (scores 75–85) we fit a local linear regression with the centered specification from §8.2 and obtain:

  1. Intercept α = 2.60. This is the fitted GPA at X = 80 for the untreated (just-below) side — the left curve's value at the cutoff.
  2. Coefficient on D, τ = 0.35. The treated side's intercept at X = 80 is α + τ = 2.95. So GPA jumps from 2.60 to 2.95 right at the cutoff.
  3. Read the effect: the scholarship raises first-year GPA by τ = 0.35 grade points, for students near the 80 threshold.
  4. Interpret the slopes: β = 0.04 (GPA rises ~0.04 per point below the cutoff); β + γ is the slope just above. The separate slopes ensure the trend in score is not mistaken for the jump.
  5. Sensitivity: refit at h = 2.5 and τ-hat = 0.31; at h = 10, τ-hat = 0.38. All near 0.35, so the result is robust to bandwidth — the design is credible.
  6. Scope: this 0.35 is a local effect for students scoring near 80. It says nothing about a student scoring 50 or 95 — RDD only identifies the effect at the cutoff.

🛠️ Hands-On: estimate an RDD in Python

We use the same free stack from Week 1/2 setup (Python + pandas + NumPy + statsmodels; the install details lives in the early lectures). RDD needs no new package — a local linear regression is just OLS on the windowed, centered, interacted data.

# rdd_scholarship.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# --- simulate a sharp RDD: cutoff c = 80, true jump = 0.35 ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 2000
X = rng.uniform(60, 100, n)          # running variable: exam score
c = 80.0
D = (X >= c).astype(int)              # sharp treatment rule
Y = 2.6 + 0.04*(X - c) + 0.35*D + rng.normal(0, 0.5, n)
df = pd.DataFrame({"Y": Y, "X": X, "D": D})

# --- local linear RDD within a bandwidth h around the cutoff ---
def rdd(df, c, h):
    w = df[(df.X >= c - h) & (df.X <= c + h)].copy()
    w["xc"] = w.X - c                 # center the running variable
    m = smf.ols("Y ~ D + xc + D:xc", data=w).fit()
    return m.params["D"], m.bse["D"], len(w)

for h in [2.5, 5, 10]:
    tau, se, k = rdd(df, c, h)
    print(f"h={h:>4}: tau_hat={tau:.3f}  se={se:.3f}  n={k}")
# tau_hat hovers near 0.35 across bandwidths -> robust, credible jump

The coefficient on D is the estimated jump τ. Looping over h is the sensitivity check from §8.4. For a real study you would add a triangular-kernel weight and an MSE-optimal bandwidth (the rdrobust package automates both); for a fuzzy design, replace the OLS with a two-stage least squares from linearmodels, instrumenting D with the cutoff-crossing dummy.

❓ Lecture 8 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a regression discontinuity design, the causal effect is measured by…
A. the slope of a single line fit through all the data
B. the size of the vertical jump in the outcome at the cutoff
C. the average outcome above the cutoff minus the average below it, over the whole range
D. the correlation between the running variable and the outcome
Show answer

B. The effect is the discontinuous jump at X = c. A single global slope (A) confounds the jump with the trend; a full-range mean difference (C) compares dissimilar far-from-cutoff units; correlation (D) is not a causal quantity here.

Q2. A program raises the probability of treatment from about 0.3 to about 0.8 when the running variable crosses the cutoff, but treatment is not guaranteed. This is…
A. a sharp RDD
B. a randomized controlled trial
C. a difference-in-differences design
D. a fuzzy RDD
Show answer

D. Only the treatment probability jumps, not the treatment itself, so it is fuzzy. Sharp (A) requires a clean 0-to-1 jump; there is no randomization (B); and no before/after panel comparison (C) is involved.

Q3. The assumption that makes an RDD credible is that…
A. the running variable is randomly assigned to every unit
B. treatment and control groups have identical sample sizes
C. all confounders (and the potential outcomes) vary continuously through the cutoff
D. the outcome is a linear function of the running variable everywhere
Show answer

C. Continuity of potential outcomes at the cutoff means the only thing that jumps is the treatment. The running variable is not random (A); equal sample sizes are irrelevant (B); and global linearity (D) is never required — we only fit locally.

Q4. Estimating the jump with local linear regression instead of a high-order global polynomial is preferred because…
A. high-degree polynomials chase noise in the tails and give erratic values at the boundary
B. local linear regression always has zero bias
C. polynomials cannot be fit to discontinuous data
D. local linear regression uses every observation in the dataset
Show answer

A. Global high-order polynomials are notoriously unstable near the cutoff. Local linear is not bias-free (B) — it trades some bias for stability; polynomials can be fit fine (C); and local methods deliberately use only points within the bandwidth, not all data (D).

Q5. You re-estimate τ at half, the optimal, and double the bandwidth, and the estimate swings from 0.1 to 0.9. The best conclusion is…
A. the design is highly credible because you tried several bandwidths
B. the estimate is fragile and the RDD result should be treated with strong skepticism
C. you should report the largest estimate, since wider bandwidths use more data
D. bandwidth never affects an RDD estimate, so the numbers must be a coding error
Show answer

B. A credible RDD is roughly stable across reasonable bandwidths; wild swings signal the jump is not robust. Trying many bandwidths is good practice but instability is bad news, not reassurance (A); cherry-picking the largest (C) is exactly the abuse sensitivity checks guard against; and bandwidth genuinely does shift estimates via the bias-variance trade-off (D).

✓ What you learned

  • Identified an RDD by its running variable and a known treatment cutoff, where assignment near the line is as-good-as-random.
  • Estimated the effect as the vertical jump in the outcome at the cutoff using local linear regression with separate slopes on each side.
  • Distinguished sharp (0-to-1 treatment) from fuzzy (probability jump) designs, scaling by the first stage in the fuzzy case.
  • Stated the continuity assumption and learned to defend it with density and covariate-balance checks.
  • Navigated the bandwidth bias-variance trade-off and confirmed credibility via a sensitivity check across windows.
