Solving Equilibrium: Root-Finding & Fixed Points

An equilibrium price is just a number where two curves cross — and the whole game is teaching a computer to find that crossing fast and reliably.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Reframe a market-clearing condition (supply = demand) as a root-finding problem, f(p) = 0.
  • Explain and code the bisection method and reason about its guaranteed but slow convergence.
  • Derive Newton's method from a local linear approximation and contrast its quadratic speed with bisection.
  • Define a fixed point and a contraction mapping, and state when fixed-point iteration converges.
  • Solve for a market-clearing price in Python using scipy.optimize.brentq and a fixed-point iteration, and judge stability from the slope.

7.1Equilibrium as a Root-Finding Problem

So far in this course we have simulated and optimized. Now we ask the central question of computational economics: given a model, what price (or interest rate, or wage) makes the system consistent with itself? In a single market, "consistent" means markets clear: the quantity buyers want equals the quantity sellers offer. Let demand be D(p) and supply S(p). The equilibrium price p* solves:

D(p*) = S(p*)Market clearing: at the right price, every unit produced is bought and no buyer is left wanting.

That looks like an equation about two functions, but it is cleaner to fold it into one. Define the excess demand function f(p) = D(p) − S(p). The equilibrium is then exactly the price where excess demand is zero:

f(p*) = D(p*) - S(p*) = 0A zero of f is called a "root." Finding equilibrium = finding a root. This reframing is the heart of computational equilibrium.

Why does this help? Because the world has a deep, general-purpose toolbox for root-finding: numerical methods that, given any function, hunt down the input where it crosses zero. We rarely have a clean algebraic formula for p* — demand and supply can be messy nonlinear curves estimated from data. But a computer can locate the crossing to ten decimal places in microseconds. That is the bargain of this lecture: trade a formula you cannot write for an algorithm that always works.

AnalogyExcess demand is a thermostat reading. When f(p) > 0 there are more buyers than goods — a shortage — and the price gets bid up. When f(p) < 0 there is a glut and the price drifts down. The equilibrium price is the setting where the room is exactly comfortable: the reading is zero and nothing wants to move.

7.2Bisection: Slow but Sure

The simplest robust root-finder is bisection. It needs just one thing: two prices a and b that bracket the root, meaning f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs. If f is continuous, the Intermediate Value Theorem guarantees a root lives somewhere between them.

The algorithm: evaluate f at the midpoint m = (a+b)/2. The root must lie in whichever half still has a sign change — so keep that half and throw away the other. Repeat. Each step halves the interval.

m = (a + b) / 2; keep [a, m] if f(a)*f(m) < 0, else [m, b]The bracket width shrinks by exactly 1/2 every iteration: after n steps it is (b - a) / 2^n.

Because the interval halves each round, to reach an accuracy of e you need about log2((b−a)/e) steps. Cutting a starting interval of width 1 down to 10-6 takes only about 20 evaluations. That is linear convergence: you gain a fixed number of correct bits per step. It is not blazing fast, but it cannot fail once you have a valid bracket — a rare and precious guarantee in numerical work.

7.3Newton's Method & brentq

Bisection ignores everything about f except its sign. Newton's method exploits the slope. Near a guess pn, approximate f by its tangent line and ask where that line hits zero. The tangent is f(pn) + f'(pn)(p − pn); setting it to zero and solving gives the update:

p_(n+1) = p_n - f(p_n) / f'(p_n)Slide down the tangent line to where it crosses zero, then repeat. Needs the derivative f'(p).

When it works, Newton is quadratically convergent: the number of correct digits roughly doubles each step. Where bisection adds one bit per iteration, Newton can go from 3 correct digits to 6 to 12 in two steps. That is why it powers solvers deep inside option pricers, general-equilibrium models, and macro DSGE codes where a root must be found millions of times.

The catch: Newton needs the derivative, and it can diverge — if a tangent is nearly flat (f' ≈ 0) the next guess can fly off to infinity, and there is no bracket to catch it. In practice you want the best of both worlds: bisection's safety and Newton's speed. That is exactly what Brent's method delivers, and SciPy ships it as scipy.optimize.brentq.

brentq(f, a, b) -> p* with f(p*) = 0Give it a function and a bracket [a, b] that changes sign; it blends bisection, secant, and inverse-quadratic steps.

brentq keeps the root bracketed at all times (so it is guaranteed to converge like bisection) but takes fast secant/parabolic steps whenever they stay inside the bracket. It needs no derivative, only a sign-changing interval. For one-dimensional equilibrium problems it is the default workhorse — robust, derivative-free, and nearly as fast as Newton.

Common mistakePassing brentq a bracket where f(a) and f(b) have the same sign. The method assumes a sign change — without one it raises ValueError: f(a) and f(b) must have different signs. Always sanity-check that you straddle the root (e.g. a very low price gives excess demand > 0 and a very high price gives excess demand < 0) before calling it.

7.4Fixed Points & Contraction

Root-finding asks where f(p) = 0. A close cousin asks where a map sends a point back to itself. A fixed point of a function g is a value p* with:

g(p*) = p*The input that g leaves unchanged. Plot y = g(p) and y = p; the fixed point is where they cross.

This is the natural language for price adjustment. Economists often model how a price updates one period to the next: a tâtonnement ("groping") rule like pn+1 = pn + α·f(pn) raises the price when there is excess demand and lowers it when there is a glut. Define g(p) = p + α·f(p); the price stops moving exactly when g(p*) = p*, i.e. when f(p*) = 0. So a fixed point of the adjustment map is the market equilibrium. Just iterate:

p_(n+1) = g(p_n), starting from any p_0Feed the output back as the next input. The sequence may converge to p*, or it may not — that is the whole question.

When does this converge? The key idea is a contraction mapping: g is a contraction if it shrinks distances, |g(x) − g(y)| ≤ L·|x − y| with a Lipschitz constant L < 1. The Banach fixed-point theorem then guarantees a unique fixed point and that iteration from any start converges to it. Locally, the relevant L is the slope magnitude |g'(p*)|:

stable iff |g'(p*)| < 1Slope flatter than 45 degrees pulls you in (cobweb spirals inward). Steeper than 45 degrees pushes you out (diverges).
AnalogyFixed-point iteration is a "cobweb." Start at p₀, bounce up to the curve y = g(p), then sideways to the 45-degree line to get your next p, and repeat. If the curve is flatter than the diagonal at the crossing, the cobweb spirals inward to the fixed point — stable. If it is steeper, the cobweb spirals outward — the price oscillates away and the iteration explodes.
✦ Worked Example — clear a linear market by hand

A market has linear demand D(p) = 100 − 5p and supply S(p) = 20 + 3p. Find the equilibrium price two ways: (1) directly, and (2) by one bisection step plus a fixed-point check.

  1. Excess demand: f(p) = D(p) − S(p) = (100 − 5p) − (20 + 3p) = 80 − 8p.
  2. Direct root: set 80 − 8p = 0 → p* = 10. Quantity: D(10) = 100 − 50 = 50 = S(10) = 20 + 30. Markets clear. ✓
  3. Bracket it: f(0) = 80 > 0 (shortage at price 0) and f(20) = 80 − 160 = −80 < 0 (glut). Opposite signs → root in [0, 20]. ✓
  4. One bisection step: midpoint m = 10, f(10) = 0 — we landed exactly on the root. (For a nonlinear f you would test the sign and keep the changing half.)
  5. Fixed-point view: use g(p) = p + α·f(p) = p + α(80 − 8p) = (1 − 8α)p + 80α. The slope is g'(p) = 1 − 8α. Pick α = 0.1 → g'= 0.2, |0.2| < 1, so the iteration is stable and converges to p* = 10. Pick α = 0.3 → g' = 1 − 2.4 = −1.4, |−1.4| > 1, so it diverges. The step size α decides stability — that is the lesson.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the market in Python

We use the free scientific-Python stack from the Week 1/2 setup (Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib + QuantEcon — see the install drop-down in the early lectures). Root-finding lives in scipy.optimize; no new install is needed today. The script below clears a nonlinear market with brentq and then reproduces the answer with a fixed-point iteration so you can see them agree.

# market_clearing.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# A nonlinear market: demand falls, supply rises (both in p > 0)
def demand(p):   return 120.0 / (1.0 + p)      # constant-elasticity-ish demand
def supply(p):   return 2.0 * p                 # upward-sloping supply
def excess(p):   return demand(p) - supply(p)   # f(p) = D - S

# 1) Bracket the root: low price -> shortage, high price -> glut
a, b = 0.01, 50.0
assert excess(a) > 0 and excess(b) < 0, "bracket must change sign"

# 2) Robust derivative-free solve
p_star = brentq(excess, a, b)
print(f"brentq price   = {p_star:.6f},  excess = {excess(p_star):.2e}")

# 3) Fixed-point iteration (tatonnement): p_(n+1) = p_n + alpha * f(p_n)
def tatonnement(p0, alpha=0.05, tol=1e-8, itmax=10_000):
    p = p0
    for n in range(itmax):
        p_new = p + alpha * excess(p)
        if abs(p_new - p) < tol:
            return p_new, n
        p = p_new
    raise RuntimeError("did not converge -- alpha too large?")

p_fp, iters = tatonnement(p0=1.0, alpha=0.05)
print(f"fixed-point    = {p_fp:.6f}  in {iters} steps")
# Both print ~ the same p*. Try alpha=0.5 and watch it blow up (|g'|>1).

Run it: brentq nails the root in a handful of internal steps, and the tâtonnement converges to the same price for a small alpha. Then crank alpha up and watch the iteration diverge — a live demonstration of the |g'(p*)| < 1 stability rule. QuantEcon also bundles a compute_fixed_point helper if you prefer a ready-made iterator.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Writing market clearing as f(p) = D(p) − S(p) = 0 turns finding an equilibrium price into…
A. a root-finding problem
B. a sorting problem
C. a matrix inversion
D. a linear regression
Show answer

A. The equilibrium price is the zero (root) of excess demand. It is not sorting (B) or regression (D), which fit/order data, nor a matrix inversion (C) — that solves linear systems, not a single nonlinear equation.

Q2. Bisection requires that the two endpoints a and b satisfy…
A. f(a) and f(b) are both positive
B. f(a) and f(b) have opposite signs (a sign change)
C. the derivative f' is known at a and b
D. a and b are equal
Show answer

B. A sign change brackets a continuous root (Intermediate Value Theorem). Same signs (A) need not bracket a root; bisection uses no derivative (C); equal endpoints (D) leave no interval to halve.

Q3. Compared with bisection, Newton's method is typically faster because it…
A. never needs a starting guess
B. is guaranteed to converge from any start
C. uses the slope f'(p) and converges quadratically (digits roughly double per step)
D. halves the interval each step
Show answer

C. Newton uses the tangent (the derivative) and converges quadratically. It is not guaranteed to converge (B) — it can diverge when f' ≈ 0 — it still needs a guess (A), and halving the interval (D) is bisection, not Newton.

Q4. A fixed point of a map g is a value p* satisfying…
A. g'(p*) = 0
B. g(p*) = 0
C. g(p*) = 1
D. g(p*) = p*
Show answer

D. By definition a fixed point is left unchanged by the map: g(p*) = p*. Option B is a root of g (a different thing); A is a stationary point of g; C is arbitrary.

Q5. Fixed-point iteration pₙ₊₁ = g(pₙ) is locally stable (converges) when, at the fixed point…
A. |g'(p*)| < 1
B. |g'(p*)| > 1
C. g(p*) > 0
D. g'(p*) = 1 exactly
Show answer

A. A slope magnitude below 1 makes g a local contraction, so the cobweb spirals inward. |g'| > 1 (B) diverges; the level g(p*) (C) is irrelevant to stability; slope exactly 1 (D) is the borderline case where the test fails to guarantee convergence.
