Markov Chains & Dynamic Stochastic Models

When tomorrow depends only on today — not on the whole history — a single matrix captures the entire future of a stochastic economy.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the Markov property and build a transition matrix from real probabilities.
  • Propagate a distribution forward: compute where the system sits after n steps.
  • Define and compute a stationary distribution, and state when ergodicity makes it unique.
  • Simulate a Markov chain and connect long-run sample frequencies to the stationary law.
  • Model stochastic productivity or employment as a Markov process using QuantEcon's MarkovChain.

6.1The Markov Property & the Transition Matrix

In Week 5 you drove the stochastic growth model with a random productivity shock. But where does that randomness come from, period after period? The workhorse answer in computational economics is the Markov chain: a system that hops between a finite set of states, where the probability of the next state depends only on the current state — not on the entire path that led there.

P( Xt+1 = j | Xt = i, Xt-1, ... , X0 ) = P( Xt+1 = j | Xt = i )The Markov property: the present state is a sufficient summary of the past for predicting the future.

Collect those one-step probabilities into a square transition matrix P, where entry P[i, j] is the probability of moving from state i to state j. Each row is a probability distribution over next states, so every row must be non-negative and sum to one — a property called being row-stochastic.

P[i, j] = P( Xt+1 = j | Xt = i ),   with  Σj P[i, j] = 1 for every row iP fully specifies the dynamics: states plus this matrix is all you need.

This is exactly how economists model an economy that flips between, say, recession and growth, or a worker who flips between employed and unemployed. The state today sets the odds for tomorrow; nothing else matters.

6.2Propagating Distributions Forward

Often we don't know the exact state — we only know a distribution over states: a row vector ψ with ψ[i] = the probability of being in state i right now. To find the distribution one step later, multiply on the right by P:

ψt+1 = ψt P   →    ψt+n = ψt PnEach step is one matrix multiply; n steps is the matrix power P^n. (Row-vector convention.)

Why does this work? The probability of landing in state j tomorrow is the chance of being in each state i today times the chance i→j, summed over i — and that weighted sum is precisely the vector–matrix product. The n-step transition probabilities are simply the entries of Pn, which is one of the most useful facts in the whole subject: a single matrix power answers "where will the economy be in 10 quarters?"

AnalogyThink of a fixed number of probability "tokens" spread across buckets (the states). Each period, every bucket sends a fixed fraction of its tokens down labeled pipes (the transition probabilities) to the other buckets. No tokens are created or destroyed — the total stays 1 — they just redistribute. Multiplying by P is one round of pouring; the long-run resting pattern of tokens is the stationary distribution.
Common mistakeWith the row-vector convention ψP, the matrix P must be row-stochastic (rows sum to 1) and you multiply the distribution on the left. Many linear-algebra texts use column vectors and column-stochastic matrices, multiplying Pψ on the right. Mixing the two conventions silently transposes your dynamics. QuantEcon and this course use the row convention — match it, and double-check that every row of P sums to exactly 1.

6.3Stationary Distributions & Ergodicity

Push a distribution forward long enough and, for well-behaved chains, it stops moving. A stationary distribution π is a fixed point of the dynamics — applying P leaves it unchanged:

π = π P,   π[i] ≥ 0,   Σi π[i] = 1π is a left eigenvector of P with eigenvalue 1, normalized to a probability vector.

Existence is guaranteed for any finite chain. Uniqueness and the guarantee that every starting distribution converges to π require the chain to be irreducible (you can eventually reach any state from any other) and aperiodic (it doesn't cycle in lockstep). A chain with both properties is ergodic, and then a powerful equivalence holds:

(1/n) Σt=1n 1{Xt = i}  →  π[i]   as  n → ∞Ergodic theorem: the long-run fraction of time spent in state i equals its stationary probability π[i].

This is why π is the single most-used object in this field. The stationary distribution of an employment chain is the economy's long-run unemployment rate; of a business-cycle chain, the fraction of quarters spent in recession; of an asset-state chain, the long-run distribution of returns. You compute π once and read off a steady-state statistic that a naive simulation would take millions of periods to estimate.

6.4Simulating a Chain & Modeling Productivity

The matrix tells you the law; a simulation draws an actual path from it. Starting in state i, you draw the next state from row i of P (a single categorical draw), move there, and repeat. The resulting sequence X0, X1, X2, ... is one realized history of the economy.

This is the engine behind dynamic stochastic models. In Week 5's growth model, productivity zt wasn't truly continuous — for computation we discretize it into a handful of states (low, medium, high) with a transition matrix that captures its persistence. A worker's labor-market history is a chain over {employed, unemployed} whose stationary distribution is the unemployment rate. An income process becomes a chain over earnings brackets.

Discretizing an AR(1) shock

A continuous persistent shock zt+1 = ρ zt + εt+1 is the standard building block. Two classic algorithms — Tauchen's method and Rouwenhorst's method — turn it into a finite Markov chain that matches the persistence ρ and variance, so you can plug a clean matrix into a dynamic program. Both ship inside QuantEcon, so you rarely code them by hand.

zt+1 = ρ zt + εt+1  →   finite states {z1, ..., zk} + transition matrix PDiscretization converts a continuous AR(1) into a Markov chain a computer can iterate exactly.
✦ Worked Example — find the stationary distribution by hand

Use the two-state business-cycle chain from the figure: from Recession (state 0), stay 0.7 / go to Growth 0.3; from Growth (state 1), go to Recession 0.1 / stay 0.9. So P = [[0.7, 0.3], [0.1, 0.9]]. Find the stationary distribution π = (π0, π1).

  1. Write π = πP componentwise. Column 0 gives: π0 = 0.7π0 + 0.1π1.
  2. Simplify. 0.3π0 = 0.1π1, so π1 = 3π0.
  3. Apply the normalization π0 + π1 = 1: π0 + 3π0 = 1, hence 4π0 = 1.
  4. Solve. π0 = 0.25 and π1 = 0.75. So π = (0.25, 0.75).
  5. Interpret & verify. The economy spends 75% of quarters in Growth and 25% in Recession — matching the π=0.75 line in the convergence figure. Check the fixed point: πP = (0.25·0.7 + 0.75·0.1,  0.25·0.3 + 0.75·0.9) = (0.175 + 0.075, 0.075 + 0.675) = (0.25, 0.75) = π. ✓

🛠️ Hands-On: chains with QuantEcon

This uses the same free stack from Week 1/2 setup (Python + QuantEcon + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib) — no new install needed. QuantEcon's MarkovChain class wraps simulation, the stationary distribution, and automatic checks for irreducibility and aperiodicity, and its tauchen helper discretizes an AR(1) for you.

# markov_chains.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
import quantecon as qe

# rows/cols = (Recession=0, Growth=1); each row sums to 1
P = np.array([[0.7, 0.3],
              [0.1, 0.9]])
mc = qe.MarkovChain(P, state_values=("recession", "growth"))

# 1) stationary distribution (left eigenvector for eigenvalue 1)
print(mc.stationary_distributions)   # [[0.25 0.75]]
print(mc.is_irreducible, mc.is_aperiodic)  # True True -> ergodic, pi is unique

# 2) simulate one 100,000-step path and compare time-averages to pi
path = mc.simulate(ts_length=100_000, init="recession")
frac_growth = np.mean(path == "growth")
print(round(frac_growth, 3))         # ~0.75 (ergodic theorem at work)

# 3) discretize an AR(1) productivity shock z' = 0.9 z + eps
mc_z = qe.tauchen(n=5, rho=0.9, sigma=0.1)   # 5-state chain
print(mc_z.P.shape, mc_z.state_values.round(3))

Line 1 returns π without any simulation; line 2 shows the long-run time-average converging to that same π (the ergodic theorem); line 3 produces a ready-to-use 5-state productivity chain for a growth or income model. Try raising rho toward 0.99 and watch the chain become far more persistent.

💬 Discussion

The Markov property says the present state is a complete summary of the past — the recent path adds nothing. Pick one real economic process you care about (unemployment spells, credit ratings, recession/recovery, a household's income, a stock's "regime"). Is a first-order Markov chain a reasonable model for it, or does history beyond the current state genuinely matter? If it matters, how could you repair the model while staying Markov — and what does that cost you?

Show a model response

Take unemployment spells. A pure two-state {employed, unemployed} chain violates the spirit of the Markov property because of duration dependence: someone unemployed for two years exits at a lower rate than someone unemployed for two weeks, so the bare label "unemployed" is not a sufficient summary — the length of the current spell matters too. The standard repair keeps the model Markov by enlarging the state space: replace "unemployed" with "unemployed-short," "unemployed-medium," "unemployed-long." Now the current (richer) state again contains everything needed to predict tomorrow, so the Markov property holds on the bigger space. The cost is concrete: more states mean a larger transition matrix to estimate from data and more parameters that can overfit, and the discretization of duration is somewhat arbitrary. This "expand the state until the present is sufficient" trick is exactly how economists turn an AR(1) (where last period's value matters) into a Markov chain over (value) states, and it is the same move behind higher-order chains and hidden-state regime models.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer: (1) names a specific process and takes a clear stance on whether first-order Markov fits; (2) identifies a concrete violation (duration dependence, momentum, longer memory) rather than just asserting one; (3) proposes a Markov-preserving fix — usually enlarging the state space (extra states, higher order, or hidden states); (4) names a real cost of that fix (more parameters / data needed, harder estimation, arbitrary discretization).

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The Markov property states that the probability of the next state depends on…
A. the entire history of past states
B. only the current state
C. the stationary distribution
D. the number of states
Show answer

B. "Memoryless": the current state is a sufficient summary of the past. (A) is what the Markov property explicitly rules out; (C) is the long-run limit, not the one-step rule; (D) is irrelevant to which information matters.

Q2. A valid (row-stochastic) transition matrix must have…
A. columns that each sum to 1
B. all entries equal
C. non-negative entries with every row summing to 1
D. a determinant of 1
Show answer

C. Each row is a probability distribution over next states, so rows are non-negative and sum to 1. (A) describes the column-stochastic convention, not the row convention used here; (B) and (D) are not requirements at all.

Q3. Using the row-vector convention, the distribution after n steps starting from ψ0 is…
A. ψ0 Pn
B. Pn ψ0
C. n ψ0 P
D. ψ0 / n
Show answer

A. Each step is one right-multiply by P, so n steps is the matrix power: ψ0Pn. (B) is the column-vector convention; (C) multiplies by n instead of taking a power; (D) is meaningless here.

Q4. A stationary distribution π satisfies which equation?
A. π = P π with π summing to the number of states
B. π P = 0
C. π = π P with π non-negative and summing to 1
D. π = P only when P is diagonal
Show answer

C. π is a probability vector left unchanged by P. (A) has the wrong multiplication side and a normalization that should be 1, not the state count; (B) would force π=0, not a distribution; (D) is false.

Q5. For an ergodic (irreducible & aperiodic) finite chain, the long-run fraction of time spent in state i…
A. depends heavily on the starting state
B. oscillates forever and never settles
C. equals 1/(number of states) for every state
D. converges to π[i], the stationary probability of state i
Show answer

D. The ergodic theorem ties time-averages to the unique stationary distribution. (A) is false because ergodicity erases the starting point; (B) describes a periodic chain, which ergodicity excludes; (C) holds only for the special uniform case, not in general.
