Linear & Convex Programming with CVXPY

When the objective is a smooth bowl and the constraints carve out a convex region, the global optimum is no longer a search — it's a guarantee.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what makes an optimization problem convex and why convexity guarantees a global optimum.
  • Recognize the standard forms of a linear program (LP) and a quadratic program (QP).
  • Model an optimization problem declaratively in CVXPY by stating variables, objective, and constraints.
  • Set up and solve an optimal-allocation / transport problem end to end.
  • Read a solver's dual variables as shadow prices and interpret them economically.

8.1Convexity: Why a Whole Class of Problems Is "Easy"

In Week 7 you hunted for equilibria with root-finding and fixed-point iteration — methods that can get stuck on the wrong solution because the landscape has many bumps. Convex optimization is the opposite world. A problem is convex when you minimize a convex function over a convex set, and that single property buys you something extraordinary: every local minimum is the global minimum. There is no wrong valley to get trapped in.

A set is convex if the straight line between any two points in the set stays inside the set. A function is convex if its graph bends upward like a bowl — the chord between any two points on the graph lies on or above the graph:

f( t·x + (1-t)·y ) <= t·f(x) + (1-t)·f(y),  for all t in [0,1]The function value at a blend of two points never exceeds the blend of the two function values — the defining "no shortcuts above the chord" property of a convex function.

The reason economists care is that an enormous share of real allocation problems are convex: minimizing cost subject to linear supply/demand balance, choosing a portfolio to minimize variance, fitting a model by least squares, scheduling power generation. When a problem is convex, a solver returns the certified optimum in seconds, even with thousands of variables.

AnalogyConvex optimization is like dropping a marble into a smooth bowl. No matter where you release it, it rolls to the same bottom point. A non-convex problem is a bumpy mountain range: where the marble settles depends on where you dropped it, so you can never be sure you found the lowest valley. Convexity removes that anxiety entirely — the bottom is unique and certified.

8.2Recognizing LP and QP Forms

Two convex forms cover a huge fraction of applied work. The linear program (LP) has a linear objective and linear constraints:

minimize  cTx   subject to  Ax <= b,  x >= 0x is the vector of decisions, c the per-unit costs, and A, b encode resource limits. Everything is linear — the feasible region is a polytope (a many-faced solid).

Because the objective is a flat tilted plane and the feasible region is a polytope, the optimum always sits at a corner (vertex) of that region. This is the geometric heart of the simplex method and of every transportation and diet problem ever written.

The quadratic program (QP) adds a convex quadratic term to the objective while keeping the constraints linear:

minimize  (1/2)·xTQ·x + cTx   subject to  Ax <= bQ must be positive semidefinite (a "bowl-shaped" matrix) for the problem to be convex. Portfolio variance is exactly such a quadratic.

The classic QP in finance is mean-variance portfolio choice: minimize xTΣx (variance) subject to a target return and the budget sum(x) = 1. The classic LP in operations is the transport problem: ship goods from sources to sinks at minimum cost. Learning to spot which form you have is half the skill — once you name it, the solver does the rest.

Common mistakeNot every quadratic is convex. The QP is only convex when Q is positive semidefinite (all eigenvalues >= 0). If you write an objective like maximize xTQ·x with a covariance-style Q, you are maximizing a convex function — which is a non-convex problem the solver may reject or solve incorrectly. Always cast it as a minimization of a convex (or maximization of a concave) objective.

8.3Modeling Declaratively in CVXPY

The old way to solve an LP was to manually stuff your problem into the c, A, b matrices a raw solver expects — error-prone and unreadable. CVXPY flips this around. You declare variables, write the objective and constraints as ordinary algebra, and CVXPY compiles it into the matrix form a solver wants. You write what you mean; the library handles the bookkeeping.

This is disciplined convex programming (DCP): CVXPY knows the curvature of each building block (an affine expression, cp.norm, cp.quad_form, cp.sum) and refuses to build anything it cannot prove convex. If your model passes the DCP check, it is convex — and therefore globally solvable — by construction. That guarantee is the whole reason to model declaratively.

8.4Duality & Shadow Prices

Every convex program has a hidden twin called its dual. For each constraint in your problem, the solver computes a dual variable (a Lagrange multiplier). Economically, that number is a shadow price: it tells you how much the optimal objective would improve if you relaxed that constraint by one unit.

shadow price of constraint i  =  d(optimal value) / d(b_i)The rate at which the best-achievable objective changes as you loosen resource i by one unit — the marginal value of that resource at the optimum.

This is the deepest idea in the lecture. If a factory's labor constraint has a shadow price of $40, then one extra labor-hour is worth exactly $40 of additional profit at the optimum — that is precisely what the firm should be willing to pay for it. Shadow prices turn an optimization output into a market signal: in electricity markets the dual on the energy-balance constraint is the price of power, and in transport problems the duals are the implicit values of each location.

A constraint that is not binding (you have slack resource left over) has a shadow price of zero — loosening it further buys you nothing. This complementary slackness relationship (price > 0 only when the constraint is tight) is the bridge between the primal allocation and its dual prices, and it reappears as competitive-equilibrium prices in Week 9.

AnalogyThink of each constraint as a wall pressing against the optimum. The shadow price measures how hard that wall is pushing. A wall the solution isn't touching (slack constraint) pushes with zero force — its shadow price is 0. A wall the solution leans against hard (binding constraint) has a large shadow price: move it an inch and the whole solution shifts. The duals literally read off the pressure each constraint exerts.
✦ Worked Example — a transport LP with shadow prices

Two warehouses supply two stores. Warehouse W1 has 30 units, W2 has 20 units. Store S1 needs 25, S2 needs 25. Per-unit shipping costs are: W1→S1 = $4, W1→S2 = $6, W2→S1 = $5, W2→S2 = $3. Minimize total shipping cost, meeting all demand without exceeding supply.

  1. Variables: let x11, x12, x21, x22 >= 0 be units on each lane. This is an LP — linear cost, linear supply/demand constraints.
  2. Objective: minimize 4·x11 + 6·x12 + 5·x21 + 3·x22.
  3. Supply constraints: x11 + x12 <= 30 (W1); x21 + x22 <= 20 (W2).
  4. Demand constraints: x11 + x21 = 25 (S1); x12 + x22 = 25 (S2).
  5. Reasoning to the optimum: W2→S2 is cheapest ($3), so fill it: x22 = 20 (all of W2). That leaves S2 needing 5 more, which must come from W1: x12 = 5. S1's 25 units must all come from W1: x11 = 25. Check W1 usage: 25 + 5 = 30 ✓ (exactly its supply).
  6. Total cost: 4·25 + 6·5 + 5·0 + 3·20 = 100 + 30 + 0 + 60 = $190.
  7. Shadow price intuition: W1's supply constraint is binding (used all 30), so it has a positive shadow price — one extra unit at W1 lets us reroute a unit of S1 demand and would cut cost, so capacity at W1 has real marginal value. W2's supply is slack (used 20 of 20 — here also binding) — when a constraint has leftover room its shadow price is 0. CVXPY reports these duals automatically (next).

🛠️ Hands-On: model and solve it in CVXPY

We use the free QuantEcon stack from Week 1 plus CVXPY, the declarative convex-modeling library. CVXPY is the one new tool this week, so install just it into your existing environment (the full Python + QuantEcon + NumPy + SciPy setup lives in the Week 1/2 install note).

⬇ Install CVXPY (one-time, adds to your Week 1 environment)

With your econ-env activated, add CVXPY — it ships with free open-source solvers (ECOS, OSQP, SCS):

pip install cvxpy

Verify: run python -c "import cvxpy; print(cvxpy.__version__)" — a version number means you're set.

Now state the transport problem declaratively. Notice we write the math, not matrices — CVXPY compiles it and reports both the optimal plan and the dual (shadow) prices.

# transport_lp.py — Lecture 8
import cvxpy as cp
import numpy as np

cost   = np.array([[4., 6.],     # rows = warehouses W1,W2
                   [5., 3.]])    # cols = stores S1,S2
supply = np.array([30., 20.])
demand = np.array([25., 25.])

x = cp.Variable((2, 2), nonneg=True)         # units shipped on each lane

objective   = cp.Minimize(cp.sum(cp.multiply(cost, x)))
supply_con  = cp.sum(x, axis=1) <= supply     # each row's shipments <= supply
demand_con  = cp.sum(x, axis=0) == demand     # each col meets demand

prob = cp.Problem(objective, [supply_con, demand_con])
prob.solve()                                  # picks a free solver automatically

print("status      :", prob.status)           # optimal
print("total cost  :", round(prob.value, 2))  # 190.0
print("ship plan   :\n", np.round(x.value, 2))
print("supply duals:", np.round(supply_con.dual_value, 2))  # shadow prices
print("demand duals:", np.round(demand_con.dual_value, 2))

The plan matches our hand solution: 25 on W1→S1, 5 on W1→S2, 20 on W2→S2. The dual_value attached to each constraint is its shadow price — the marginal cost of one more unit of demand at a store, or the marginal value of one more unit of supply at a warehouse. Try bumping demand to [26., 25.] and watch the total cost rise by exactly the S1 demand dual.

❓ Lecture 8 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. What property of a convex optimization problem makes it "easy" to solve reliably?
A. It always has integer solutions
B. Any local minimum is also the global minimum
C. It never has any constraints
D. It can be solved without a computer
Show answer

B. Convexity guarantees a single bowl, so any local optimum is global — no wrong valley to get stuck in. A is false (convex problems are over the reals, not integers); C is false (constraints are fine as long as the feasible set is convex); D is irrelevant to convexity.

Q2. Which problem is a linear program (LP) in standard form?
A. minimize xTΣx subject to sum(x) = 1
B. minimize cos(x) subject to x >= 0
C. minimize cTx subject to Ax <= b, x >= 0
D. maximize xTQ·x with Q a covariance matrix
Show answer

C. A linear objective with linear inequality constraints is exactly the LP standard form. A is a QP (quadratic objective); B has a non-convex trigonometric objective; D maximizes a convex quadratic, which is non-convex.

Q3. In a quadratic program minimize (1/2)xTQ·x + cTx, what must be true of Q for the problem to be convex?
A. Q must be positive semidefinite
B. Q must be the identity matrix
C. Q must have all negative entries
D. Q must be invertible
Show answer

A. A positive semidefinite Q makes the quadratic a bowl (convex). The identity (B) is one special PSD case but far too restrictive; negative entries (C) say nothing about definiteness and can break convexity; invertibility (D) is neither necessary nor sufficient — a PSD matrix can be singular.

Q4. What does the dual variable (shadow price) of a binding constraint tell you?
A. The number of variables in the problem
B. How much the optimal objective changes per unit relaxation of that constraint
C. Whether the solver converged
D. The total runtime of the solve
Show answer

B. The shadow price is the marginal value of the resource — d(optimal value)/d(b_i). A, C, and D are solver/metadata facts unrelated to the dual's economic meaning.

Q5. By complementary slackness, a constraint that is not binding at the optimum (it has leftover slack) has a shadow price of…
A. infinity
B. exactly 1
C. the same as the objective value
D. zero
Show answer

D. If you have slack resource left over, loosening that constraint buys nothing, so its marginal value — and dual — is zero. A, B, and C all assign a nonzero value, contradicting the fact that an unused constraint has no marginal effect on the optimum.
