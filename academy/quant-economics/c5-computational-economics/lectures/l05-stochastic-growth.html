The Stochastic Growth & Optimal-Savings Model

How much should you consume today when tomorrow's income is a roll of the dice? The answer is a policy function — and we can compute it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the stochastic optimal-growth problem and explain each piece: utility, discounting, production, and the random shock.
  • Recast that infinite-horizon problem as a recursive dynamic program with a Bellman equation in state and policy.
  • Solve for the optimal consumption-savings policy with value-function iteration (VFI), building on Week 4.
  • Simulate optimal capital and consumption paths from a solved policy and read the ergodic distribution.
  • Distinguish the income-fluctuation problem and explain why a borrowing constraint generates precautionary saving.

5.1The Problem: Save, Invest, and Live With Luck

Imagine a single decision-maker — a farmer, a household, a whole economy in miniature — who each period observes how much wealth they have, then splits it between consuming now and saving (investing) for the future. Saving builds capital that produces output next period, but production is hit by a random shock: good weather, a productivity surprise, a demand swing. The shock is the "stochastic" in stochastic growth.

The decision-maker wants to maximize the discounted sum of expected utility from consumption over an infinite horizon. Three forces are in tension: we like consuming, we like consuming now more than later (discounting), and consuming today means less capital — hence less expected output — tomorrow.

max E[ Σt βt u(ct) ]  s.t.  kt+1 = zt f(kt) + (1−δ)kt − ctMaximize expected discounted utility; what you don't consume out of output plus undepreciated capital becomes next period's capital.

Here β ∈ (0,1) is the discount factor, u is a concave (diminishing-returns) utility like u(c) = ln c, f is the production function (say f(k) = kα), δ is depreciation, and zt is the productivity shock. Define output on hand as yt = zt f(kt) + (1−δ)kt — total resources available to split this period.

AnalogyThink of a reservoir. Rain (the shock zt) refills it unpredictably; you draw water to drink today (consumption) but every drop you leave behind raises the level — and tomorrow's evaporation-adjusted supply — for the future. The optimal "draw rule" tells you, for any reservoir level, exactly how much to use now. That rule is the policy function we are after.

5.2From Infinite Horizon to a Bellman Equation

An infinite sum of future decisions looks hopeless — until you notice the problem repeats. Tomorrow's sub-problem has the exact same shape as today's; only the state (wealth, shock) differs. That is the insight of dynamic programming: collapse the whole future into a single value function V(y) that records the best lifetime payoff achievable starting from output-on-hand y.

The optimal choice then satisfies the Bellman equation — today's payoff plus the discounted expected value of the state you roll into:

V(y) = max0 ≤ c ≤ y { u(c) + β E[ V(y') ] }where y' = z' f(y − c) + (1−δ)(y − c), and the expectation E is over next period's shock z'.

The thing you actually want is the policy function σ(y) = the consumption that attains the max for each y. Savings is then y − σ(y). The Bellman operator T — "plug a guess of V into the right-hand side, take the max, get a new V" — is a contraction mapping: iterating it converges to the unique fixed point V* regardless of where you start. That convergence guarantee is precisely what makes the Week-4 algorithm work here.

An equivalent, often faster, characterization comes from the first-order condition — the Euler equation. Set the marginal cost of saving one more unit equal to its discounted expected marginal benefit:

u'(ct) = β E[ u'(ct+1) · ( zt+1 f'(kt+1) + 1 − δ ) ]Marginal utility lost today equals discounted expected marginal utility gained tomorrow, scaled by the gross return on capital.

5.3Solving It: Value-Function Iteration

We rarely have a clean formula for V*, so we compute it. Value-function iteration (VFI), from Week 4, applies the Bellman operator repeatedly until the value function stops changing:

  1. Lay a grid of capital/wealth points and discretize the shock z into a few values with probabilities.
  2. Guess an initial value V0 (zeros are fine).
  3. For each grid point, search over feasible consumption to evaluate u(c) + β E[Vn(y')]; the max is Vn+1, the argmax is the policy.
  4. Repeat until max|Vn+1 − Vn| < ε. Convergence is guaranteed at geometric rate β.

The picture below shows what convergence looks like: the iterates climb monotonically toward the fixed point, with the gap between successive curves shrinking by a factor of about β each step.

Once V* is in hand, the recovered policy σ(y) tells you optimal consumption at every wealth level. Below it sits the savings policy y − σ(y), the engine that drives capital forward.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the value function with the policy function. VFI converges on V, but V is just a means to an end — you almost never report it. The object you simulate and interpret is the policy σ(y). A second trap: forgetting to take the expectation over the shock z' inside the Bellman update. Dropping E[·] silently solves a deterministic growth model and erases all the interesting risk behavior.

5.4The Income-Fluctuation Problem & Precautionary Saving

Now change the story slightly. Instead of capital that produces output, suppose a household receives a random labor income yt each period, can save at a fixed gross interest rate R = 1+r, but cannot borrow (assets at ≥ 0). This is the income-fluctuation problem — the workhorse of modern consumption and inequality research. The state is cash-on-hand; the Bellman equation is structurally identical to before, with a hard constraint:

V(a, y) = max0 ≤ c ≤ a+y { u(c) + β E[ V(a', y') ] }  s.t.  a' = R(a + y − c) ≥ 0Save out of cash-on-hand a + y, earn gross return R, never let assets go negative.

Here something new appears. If utility has a convex marginal utility (true for log and CRRA utility, where u'''>0, i.e. prudence), the household saves more than it would if future income were certain at its mean. That extra buffer is precautionary saving: a self-insurance stock built up to cushion bad income draws, precisely because hitting the borrowing constraint with zero assets is painful.

AnalogyPrecautionary saving is a financial seatbelt. You don't expect a crash, but because the downside (slamming into the a≥0 wall during a run of bad luck) is so much worse than the small comfort cost of buckling up, you carry a buffer of assets "just in case." Take away the risk — make income certain — and the seatbelt comes off: saving falls.

This single mechanism, aggregated across many heterogeneous households, generates realistic wealth distributions and explains why consumption is far smoother than income — the empirical backbone of incomplete-markets macro models you'll meet in later courses.

Common mistakePrecautionary saving is driven by the third derivative of utility (prudence, u'''>0), not merely risk aversion (u''<0). A quadratic utility is risk-averse yet has u'''=0 — it produces no precautionary motive (the classic "certainty-equivalence" result). If your simulated buffer doesn't appear, check that your utility is actually prudent, not just concave.
✦ Worked Example — one Bellman update by hand

Log utility u(c)=ln c, β=0.9, full depreciation (δ=1), production f(k)=k0.5, and a two-state shock: z=1.2 or z=0.8, each with probability 0.5. Suppose current capital k=4. Take a current value guess V(y)=ln(y). Evaluate the Bellman objective at the two candidate consumption choices c=3 and c=4, and pick the better one.

  1. Output on hand. With δ=1, y' = z'·(k')0.5 where k' = k − c = saving. (Today's y isn't needed for this comparison since we are given c directly.)
  2. Try c = 3: saving k' = 4 − 3 = 1. Next output: if z'=1.2, y'=1.2·10.5=1.2; if z'=0.8, y'=0.8. So E[V(y')] = 0.5·ln(1.2) + 0.5·ln(0.8) = 0.5(0.1823) + 0.5(−0.2231) = −0.0204.
  3. Objective: u(3) + β·E[V] = ln(3) + 0.9·(−0.0204) = 1.0986 − 0.0184 = 1.0802.
  4. Try c = 4: saving k' = 0, so next output is 0 in both states and ln(0) = −∞. The objective is −∞ — consuming everything is catastrophic because it leaves zero capital and zero future output.
  5. Decision. Between the two, c=3 (value 1.0802) crushes c=4 (value −∞). The optimizer would search a fine grid, but already we see the qualitative answer: save a strictly positive amount. The concavity of ln makes the all-consume corner infinitely bad — the engine behind a smooth interior policy.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the optimal-growth model in Python

We reuse the free QuantEcon stack from Week 1/2 setup (Python + NumPy + SciPy + QuantEcon + Matplotlib — see the install details box in the Week 1 lecture). No new packages are needed here; QuantEcon ships ready-made tools for exactly this model.

Below we build a grid, run value-function iteration to a fixed point, recover the consumption policy, and simulate a capital path. We use log utility and Cobb-Douglas production with a discretized log-normal shock.

# optimal_growth.py — Lecture 5 (Python + NumPy + SciPy + QuantEcon)
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

alpha, beta, delta = 0.4, 0.96, 0.1   # curvature, discount, depreciation
u      = np.log                        # log utility (prudent: u''' > 0)
f      = lambda k: k**alpha            # Cobb-Douglas production

# discretize a small productivity shock z around 1
z_vals = np.array([0.85, 1.0, 1.15])
z_prob = np.array([0.25, 0.50, 0.25])

grid = np.linspace(1e-4, 12.0, 200)    # capital grid

def bellman_update(V):
    # V is a callable over the grid (interpolated)
    V_new = np.empty_like(grid)
    policy = np.empty_like(grid)
    for i, k in enumerate(grid):
        y = np.dot(z_prob, z_vals) * f(k) + (1 - delta) * k  # mean resources view
        def neg_obj(c):
            kp = z_vals * f(k) + (1 - delta) * k - c          # next capital, per shock
            kp = np.clip(kp, 1e-8, None)
            EV = np.dot(z_prob, np.interp(kp, grid, V))       # expectation over z'
            return -(u(c) + beta * EV)
        res = minimize_scalar(neg_obj, bounds=(1e-8, y), method='bounded')
        V_new[i], policy[i] = -res.fun, res.x
    return V_new, policy

V = np.zeros_like(grid)
for it in range(400):                  # VFI loop
    V_new, policy = bellman_update(V)
    if np.max(np.abs(V_new - V)) < 1e-6:
        print(f"converged in {it} iterations"); break
    V = V_new

# simulate an optimal capital path from k0 = 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
k, path = 1.0, [1.0]
for t in range(60):
    c = np.interp(k, grid, policy)
    z = rng.choice(z_vals, p=z_prob)
    k = max(z * f(k) + (1 - delta) * k - c, 1e-8)
    path.append(k)
print("mean capital over path:", round(float(np.mean(path)), 3))

Swap np.log for a CRRA utility lambda c: (c**(1-g)-1)/(1-g) to dial risk aversion, or shrink/widen z_vals to see the policy flatten (more saving) as income risk rises — that's the precautionary motive showing up directly in the computed policy.

🔗 Where this is heading

You just solved a single-agent dynamic program — but the randomness lived in a discretized shock with fixed transition probabilities. Week 6 (Markov Chains) makes that shock process first-class: persistent productivity, stationary distributions, and ergodicity, so the income process itself becomes realistic. From there, the income-fluctuation problem scales into heterogeneous-agent models where millions of households each solve this exact Bellman equation and their savings aggregate into prices — the frontier of computational macro and the policy tools used to study inequality, taxation, and monetary transmission. The same VFI machinery also reappears in the Quant Finance track for optimal portfolio choice and option exercise.

Q1. In the stochastic optimal-growth model, what makes the infinite-horizon problem solvable as a Bellman equation?
A. The shock z is assumed to be zero
B. The problem is recursive — every future sub-problem has the same structure, captured by a value function of the state
C. Utility is linear, so there is nothing to optimize
D. The horizon is actually finite
Show answer

B. Recursive structure lets the whole future collapse into V(state). A is false (the shock stays); C is false (utility is concave, that's the point); D is false (the horizon is infinite — recursion is what tames it).

Q2. The Bellman operator T is a contraction mapping with modulus β. This guarantees that…
A. the policy is always to consume everything
B. value-function iteration diverges unless you start at the true V*
C. iterating T from any starting guess converges to a unique fixed point V*
D. the value function must be linear
Show answer

C. A contraction has a unique fixed point reached from any start (Banach). B is the opposite of the truth; A and D are unrelated to the contraction property.

Q3. After VFI converges, the object you actually simulate and interpret is…
A. the policy function σ(y), giving optimal consumption at each state
B. the value function V*, reported directly as the answer
C. the discount factor β
D. the depreciation rate δ
Show answer

A. V* is a means to an end; the policy σ drives every simulation and interpretation. β and δ are fixed parameters, not outputs.

Q4. Precautionary saving — building an extra buffer because future income is risky — arises specifically from…
A. risk neutrality (linear utility)
B. a negative discount factor
C. ignoring the borrowing constraint
D. prudence — a positive third derivative of utility (u''' > 0)
Show answer

D. Prudence (convex marginal utility) drives precautionary saving. Risk-neutral utility (A) has u'''=0 and no buffer; β is in (0,1) not negative (B); and the constraint a≥0 is the thing that makes the buffer valuable, not something you ignore (C).

Q5. A common bug in coding the Bellman update for this stochastic model is…
A. forgetting to take the expectation E[·] over next period's shock z', which silently solves the deterministic model
B. using a concave utility function
C. placing the choice variable on a grid
D. discounting future utility by β
Show answer

A. Dropping the expectation erases the stochastic content entirely. B (concave utility), C (gridding), and D (discounting) are all correct, intended parts of the method — not bugs.
