Dynamic Programming & the Bellman Equation

When today's choice changes tomorrow's options, you stop optimizing a number and start optimizing a function — the value of being in a state.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recognize a sequential decision problem and describe it with states, actions, transitions, and rewards.
  • Define the value function as the best total reward attainable from a given state.
  • State the Bellman equation and explain how it splits a problem into "now" plus "the rest."
  • Explain the principle of optimality and why it justifies the Bellman recursion.
  • Set up and solve the cake-eating problem — the canonical first dynamic program — analytically and in Python.

3.1Decisions That Unfold Over Time

In Lecture 2 you minimized a single objective with the optimizer's whole answer arriving at once: pick the vector that maximizes f. But many economic problems are not one-shot. You choose how much to consume today, and what is left over determines what you can do tomorrow. Today's action quietly rewrites tomorrow's menu of options. That feedback loop is the defining feature of a sequential decision problem.

To talk about such problems precisely we need four ingredients. They will recur in every dynamic program for the rest of the course:

state s  ·  action a  ·  transition s' = g(s, a)  ·  reward r(s, a)The state summarizes everything you need to know to act optimally; the action is your choice; the transition says how the action moves you to the next state; the reward is the payoff you collect this period.

The crucial idea is the state. A good state variable is a sufficient statistic for the future: once you know it, the entire history of how you arrived is irrelevant to what you should do next. In the savings problem the state is your current wealth; in inventory control it is stock on hand; in a job search it is the wage offer in front of you. Choosing the right state variable is the single most important modeling step.

We add one more piece. Future rewards are usually worth less than rewards today, so we apply a discount factor β with 0 < β < 1. The objective is the discounted sum of all current and future rewards:

max  r(s₀,a₀) + β·r(s₁,a₁) + β²·r(s₂,a₂) + ...A reward one period away counts for β of a reward now; two periods away, β²; and so on. Discounting keeps the infinite sum finite and reflects impatience or interest.
AnalogyThink of planning a road trip with a fixed tank of gas. The state is how much fuel you have left; the action is how far to drive today; the transition is the tank draining; the reward is the scenery you enjoy each day. You never plan the whole trip stop-by-stop in advance — you ask, at each town, "given the fuel I have now, what is the best I can still do?" That question is the value function.

3.2The Value Function and the Bellman Equation

A brute-force attack would optimize over the entire infinite sequence of actions at once — hopeless. Dynamic programming's breakthrough is to define a single object that hides all that future complexity. The value function V(s) is the best total discounted reward you can possibly achieve starting from state s and playing optimally forever after:

V(s) = max over all future plans of  [ total discounted reward starting from s ]V(s) does not depend on how you got to s — only on s itself. It is "the worth of being here."

The magic is that V obeys a self-referential equation. Whatever your optimal infinite plan is, it consists of one action now plus an optimal continuation from wherever that action lands you. So the value of being in s equals the best over actions of (reward now) plus (discounted value of the next state). This is the Bellman equation:

V(s) = max_a  {  r(s, a)  +  β·V( g(s, a) )  }"Now" = r(s, a). "The rest" = β·V of the next state g(s, a). The same unknown function V appears on both sides — it is a fixed-point equation.

Notice what just happened. We turned an optimization over an infinite stream of decisions into an optimization over a single action a, repeated. The price is that V is now unknown and appears on both sides — but that fixed-point structure is exactly what next week's value-function iteration will exploit to solve for V numerically.

The action that achieves the maximum, written a = π(s), is the policy: a rule mapping each state to the optimal action. Once you have V, reading off π is a one-line maximization at each state.

Common mistakeThe value function depends only on the state, never on the calendar time or the path taken to reach it (in a stationary, infinite-horizon problem). Students often try to carry along "how much I've already eaten" as an extra variable. If wealth today fully determines your future options, the past is sunk — adding history bloats the state and breaks the clean recursion. Keep the state as small as it can be while still being a sufficient statistic.

3.3The Principle of Optimality

Why is the Bellman equation true? It rests on a deceptively simple statement, the principle of optimality:

Any tail of an optimal plan is itself optimal for the state it starts from.If your full plan is best overall, then from period 1 onward it must also be the best possible plan starting from state s₁ — otherwise you could improve the tail and beat the original.

The argument is a proof by contradiction. Suppose the optimal plan from s₀ reaches state s₁ after one action, but the continuation from s₁ is not the best possible from s₁. Then you could swap in the better continuation, leaving the first action unchanged, and raise the total. That contradicts the original plan being optimal. Hence the tail must be optimal — which is precisely the β·V(s') term.

This is also the recipe behind the famous shortest-path logic: the shortest route from A to C that passes through B must use the shortest route from B to C. The figure below traces this "now plus the rest" decomposition.

3.4The Cake-Eating Problem

The canonical first dynamic program is wonderfully concrete. You have a cake of size x. Each period you eat some amount c, enjoy utility u(c), and save the rest for later. You never get more cake. How should you spread your eating over time? This is the savings problem stripped to its bare bones — wealth in, consumption out.

Here the state is the cake remaining x; the action is consumption c with 0 ≤ c ≤ x; the transition is x' = x − c; the reward is u(c). With a log utility u(c) = ln(c) and discount factor β, the Bellman equation is:

V(x) = max_{0 ≤ c ≤ x}  {  ln(c)  +  β·V(x − c)  }Eat a little now for utility ln(c); the leftover cake x − c carries its own optimal value V, discounted by β.

This problem has a clean closed form, which makes it the perfect benchmark for testing numerical methods next week. One can verify (by guessing V(x) = A·ln(x) + B and matching coefficients) that the optimal policy is to eat a constant fraction of whatever cake remains:

c* = (1 − β)·x    and    V(x) = ln(x)/(1 − β) + constantEat the fraction (1 − β) of remaining cake each period. A patient eater (β near 1) eats a tiny slice each period and stretches the cake out; an impatient eater (β near 0) devours most of it now.

Economically: more patience (higher β) means a smaller bite today and a flatter consumption path — exactly the intuition behind consumption smoothing in macro models. The geometry of that constant-fraction rule is shown below.

✦ Worked Example — solve a finite cake by backward induction

A cake of size x = 1 must be eaten over exactly two periods (periods 0 and 1), then it is gone. Utility is u(c) = ln(c) and β = 0.9. Find the optimal consumption in each period by working backward.

  1. Last period (period 1): whatever cake x₁ is left, there is no future, so eat it all: c₁ = x₁ and value V₁(x₁) = ln(x₁).
  2. Period 0 Bellman: choose c₀ to maximize ln(c₀) + β·ln(x₁) where x₁ = 1 − c₀. So maximize ln(c₀) + 0.9·ln(1 − c₀).
  3. First-order condition: differentiate and set to zero: 1/c₀ − 0.9/(1 − c₀) = 0, giving 1 − c₀ = 0.9·c₀.
  4. Solve: 1 = 1.9·c₀, so c₀ = 1/1.9 ≈ 0.526. Then x₁ = 1 − 0.526 = 0.474 and c₁ = 0.474.
  5. Read the pattern: the eater consumes about 53% now and saves 47% — patience (β=0.9) pulls consumption toward the future relative to eating everything immediately. With three or more periods you repeat the backward step; in the infinite-horizon limit this converges to the constant-fraction rule c* = (1 − β)x.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the cake by backward induction in Python

We use the free QuantEcon stack — Python + NumPy + SciPy (+ Matplotlib), pip-installable. The full one-time environment setup lives in the Week 1 install note; reuse that same econ-env here. This lecture needs nothing new beyond SciPy's minimize_scalar, which ships with SciPy.

We solve a finite-horizon cake by backward induction: start at the last period (eat everything) and step backward, at each period maximizing today's utility plus the discounted value already computed for tomorrow.

# cake_eating.py — Lecture 3: finite-horizon cake by backward induction
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

beta = 0.9
T    = 5                      # periods 0..4, cake gone after
x0   = 1.0

# V[t](x) approximated on a grid; here we instead solve analytically per step
# by exploiting that value is log-shaped. Simplest: simulate the policy.

def u(c):
    return np.log(c)

# Backward induction on a wealth grid
grid = np.linspace(1e-4, x0, 400)
V    = np.log(grid)                       # last period: eat all -> V = ln(x)
policy_fracs = []

for t in range(T - 1, 0, -1):             # step backward over periods
    Vnew = np.empty_like(grid)
    pol  = np.empty_like(grid)
    for i, x in enumerate(grid):
        # choose c in (0, x); next value interpolated from V
        def neg_obj(c):
            xnext = x - c
            Vnext = np.interp(xnext, grid, V)
            return -(u(c) + beta * Vnext)
        res = minimize_scalar(neg_obj, bounds=(1e-6, x - 1e-6), method='bounded')
        Vnew[i] = -res.fun
        pol[i]  = res.x
    V = Vnew
    policy_fracs.append(pol[-1] / grid[-1])   # fraction eaten at x = x0

print("eaten-fraction at first period (numeric):", round(policy_fracs[-1], 3))
print("infinite-horizon target  (1 - beta)    :", round(1 - beta, 3))
# As T grows, the first-period fraction approaches 1 - beta = 0.1

Run it and watch the first-period eating fraction creep toward 1 - beta as you raise T — the finite problem converging to the infinite-horizon constant-fraction rule. Next week we replace this hand-rolled backward loop with proper value-function iteration, which solves the fixed point directly.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Bellman equation is the workhorse of modern quantitative economics. Next week (Lecture 4) we solve it by value-function iteration — iterating the recursion until V stops changing, a contraction-mapping fixed point. From there the same machinery powers the stochastic growth model and income-fluctuation / consumption-savings problems where transitions are random, the McCall job-search model (when to accept a wage offer), and optimal-stopping problems throughout the Quant Finance track (American option exercise is a Bellman equation in disguise). The reinforcement-learning revolution in machine learning is, at its core, the same equation solved from data rather than from a known model.

💬 Discussion

The Bellman equation insists that the state is a sufficient statistic for the future — the past is sunk. Yet real people (and real firms) routinely let history matter: they "throw good money after bad," honor sunk costs, or feel loyalty to a path. Pick one real economic decision and argue both sides: what would the right state variable be, and is there a defensible reason the history might genuinely belong in the state rather than being a behavioral mistake?

Show a model response

Sample answer. Consider a firm deciding whether to keep funding a half-built factory. The clean dynamic-programming framing says the state is only the remaining cost to finish and the expected future revenue — money already spent is sunk and should not enter the decision. By that logic, "we've already spent so much, we can't stop now" is the classic sunk-cost fallacy.

But there is a defensible case for history entering the state, and it hinges on whether the past changes the future options or payoffs. If partial construction has lowered the remaining cost to completion, or built reputation/learning that raises future revenue, then those effects belong in the state — not as "history for its own sake," but because they are genuine, forward-looking determinants of value. The discipline of dynamic programming is precisely this test: include a variable in the state if and only if it changes the distribution of future rewards. History that fails that test (regret, pride, accounting optics) is a behavioral bias; history that passes it (accumulated capital, learned skill, a stock of inventory) is a legitimate state variable that was simply mislabeled as "the past."

✓ Self-check rubric. A strong answer: (1) names a concrete decision and proposes an explicit state variable; (2) correctly identifies the sunk-cost reasoning that the Bellman framing implies; (3) distinguishes history that alters future payoffs/options (legitimately part of the state) from history that does not (a behavioral bias); and (4) ties the test back to the definition of the state as a sufficient statistic for future rewards.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a dynamic program, the state variable is best described as…
A. a sufficient statistic that summarizes everything needed to act optimally going forward
B. the action the agent chooses each period
C. the discount factor applied to future rewards
D. the total reward collected so far
Show answer

A. The state is exactly the information you need to choose optimally from here on; history beyond it is irrelevant. B is the action, C is β, and D (accumulated past reward) is sunk and not part of the state.

Q2. The Bellman equation V(s) = max_a [ r(s,a) + βV(g(s,a)) ] works by…
A. optimizing the entire infinite sequence of actions in one step
B. splitting the problem into the reward now plus the discounted value of the next state
C. ignoring future rewards entirely
D. assuming the discount factor β is greater than 1
Show answer

B. That "now plus the rest" decomposition is the whole point — it reduces an infinite-horizon plan to a single-action maximization. A is the brute-force approach DP avoids; C contradicts the βV term; D would make the discounted sum diverge (β must be between 0 and 1).

Q3. The principle of optimality states that…
A. the first action of any plan is always the most important
B. optimal plans never use discounting
C. any tail of an optimal plan is itself optimal for the state where the tail begins
D. every problem has a unique optimal action regardless of state
Show answer

C. This is precisely what justifies the recursion: the continuation from the next state must itself be optimal, which is the βV(s') term. A and D are not implied by the principle, and B is false — discounting is standard.

Q4. In the cake-eating problem with u(c) = ln(c) and discount factor β, the optimal infinite-horizon policy is to eat…
A. the entire cake in the first period
B. a fixed absolute amount each period regardless of cake size
C. nothing until the very last period
D. a constant fraction (1 − β) of the remaining cake each period
Show answer

D. With log utility the optimal rule is c* = (1 − β)x — a constant fraction of what remains. A and C ignore smoothing/discounting; B is wrong because the optimal amount scales with the remaining cake, not a fixed absolute quantity.

Q5. Raising the discount factor β toward 1 (more patience) in the cake-eating problem causes the eater to…
A. consume a smaller slice today and stretch the cake out over more periods
B. consume the whole cake immediately
C. stop caring about future utility
D. violate the principle of optimality
Show answer

A. Since c* = (1 − β)x, a higher β shrinks the fraction eaten today, flattening the consumption path — the patient eater stretches the cake. B describes an impatient (low-β) eater; C is backwards (high β means future utility matters more); D is unrelated — the principle holds for any valid β.
