Capstone: A Dynamic-Programming Model or Policy Simulation

Everything you've built this course converges here — pose a model, solve it, calibrate it to data, and let it answer a policy question no spreadsheet can.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Pose an end-to-end computational model as either a dynamic program (Bellman equation) or an agent-based policy simulation.
  • Solve it with the right engine — value-function iteration, optimization, or equilibrium-finding — and confirm it converged.
  • Calibrate parameters so the model's moments match real data, using calibration or estimation.
  • Run a counterfactual policy experiment by changing one parameter and re-solving, then read the welfare effect.
  • Frame the capstone deliverable: a reproducible repository plus a short report that states a question, a method, and an answer.

10.1The Anatomy of an End-to-End Model

For nine weeks you collected tools: root-finders, optimizers, Markov chains, value-function iteration, equilibrium solvers. A capstone is where those tools stop being exercises and become a single pipeline that takes a real economic question in one end and produces a defensible number out the other. Every credible computational study, no matter the topic, runs the same four stages:

Pose -> Solve -> Calibrate -> CounterfactualWrite the model as math, solve it numerically, fit it to data, then change a policy and re-solve to measure the effect.

The discipline is that the stages are separable. The "pose" stage produces equations independent of any code. The "solve" stage is a black box that, given parameters, returns a solution. "Calibrate" wraps the solver in a loop that searches for parameters matching data. "Counterfactual" reuses the exact same solver with one input changed. If you keep these four pieces decoupled in your code, you can swap solvers, re-calibrate, or run a dozen policy scenarios without rewriting anything.

Two model archetypes dominate this course's toolkit. A dynamic-programming (DP) model describes a forward-looking agent who maximizes discounted payoffs over time — savings, investment, job search. A policy simulation instead steps a population of heterogeneous agents forward under explicit rules and aggregates the outcome — useful when closed-form optimization is intractable or when distributions, not just averages, are the point.

10.2Posing & Solving a Dynamic Program

The canonical DP in economics is the income-fluctuation (consumption-savings) problem. A household earns stochastic income, chooses how much to consume versus save, and wants to smooth consumption over time. Writing a for assets and y for income, the value function obeys the Bellman equation:

V(a, y) = max over c of [ u(c) + B * E[ V(a', y') | y ] ]subject to a' = (1+r)(a - c) + y' , with discount factor B in (0,1) and utility u. The max over today's consumption c balances enjoying now vs. carrying assets forward.

The Bellman operator is a contraction with modulus B, so iterating it from any starting guess converges to the unique fixed point — that is value-function iteration (VFI), the workhorse you met in Weeks 5–6. We discretize assets onto a grid, discretize income as a Markov chain, and repeatedly apply the operator until the value function stops changing:

|| V_{k+1} - V_k || < tolStop when the sup-norm change between sweeps falls below a tolerance; the contraction guarantees this happens, geometrically, at rate B.

The output is a policy function c(a, y): the optimal consumption at every state. That policy is the object you simulate and the object policy experiments move.

AnalogyVFI is like repeatedly applying the same camera filter to a photo until it stops changing. Because the Bellman operator shrinks differences (a contraction), each pass moves you a fixed fraction B of the remaining distance to the final image. You never overshoot, and the picture you converge to does not depend on the photo you started with.

10.3Calibration: Making the Model Match Reality

A solved model with made-up parameters is just a cartoon. Calibration chooses parameters so that statistics generated by the model — its moments — match the same statistics measured in real data. The classic approach is the method of moments: pick parameters theta to minimize the distance between model moments m(theta) and data moments m_data.

theta* = argmin [ m(theta) - m_data ]' W [ m(theta) - m_data ]Minimize a weighted squared gap between simulated and observed moments. W is a positive-definite weighting matrix; this is the generalized method of moments (GMM) objective.

For our savings model, natural targets are the aggregate wealth-to-income ratio and the fraction of households with near-zero assets. We pick the discount factor B and a borrowing limit so the model reproduces both. Each evaluation of the objective requires re-solving the entire DP — calibration is an outer optimization loop wrapped around the VFI solver. This is why decoupling stages matters: the calibrator just calls solve(theta).

Calibration sits between two styles. Pure calibration sets a handful of parameters to match a few salient moments, prized for transparency. Structural estimation (GMM, maximum likelihood, or simulated method of moments) formally estimates parameters with standard errors, prized for inference. Your capstone may use either, but must state which and why.

Common mistakeCalibrating to the same moments you later use to judge the model proves nothing — of course it fits, you forced it to. Reserve at least one untargeted moment: calibrate on (say) the wealth-to-income ratio, then check whether the model also reproduces, untargeted, the share of wealth held by the top 10%. Matching a moment you did not target is the real evidence your model captures the mechanism.

10.4Counterfactuals & the Capstone Deliverable

The payoff of a calibrated model is the counterfactual experiment: change one policy parameter, re-solve, and compare. Because the model is disciplined by data in the baseline, the difference between baseline and counterfactual is a credible estimate of the policy's effect — even though no such experiment ever ran in the world. This is the entire reason economists build structural models instead of only running regressions.

In the savings model, a natural counterfactual is changing the interest rate r (a monetary-policy lever) or introducing a tax on capital income. We re-solve VFI under the new r, simulate the household panel, and report two numbers: the change in aggregate savings and the change in welfare, where welfare is measured as the consumption-equivalent variation — the constant percentage boost to baseline consumption that would make a household indifferent to the policy.

CEV such that E[ V_baseline((1+CEV) c) ] = E[ V_counterfactual(c) ]The consumption-equivalent variation translates an abstract change in the value function into a plain-language "x% better off" welfare statement.

The capstone deliverable bundles this into something reproducible. A reader should be able to clone your repository, run one command, and regenerate every figure. The expected pieces:

  • A question stated in one sentence ("How does a 2-point rise in r change aggregate savings and welfare?").
  • A model section with the Bellman equation or simulation rules written out.
  • A solution + calibration section: which solver, which targets, a convergence check, and an untargeted-moment validation.
  • A counterfactual section with at least one figure and a welfare number.
  • A reproducible repo: a requirements.txt, a solve.py, and a notebook or script that produces the report's figures from scratch.
AnalogyThink of your capstone as a flight simulator, not a photograph. A photograph (a regression on past data) shows what happened. A simulator, once you've tuned it to fly like the real plane (calibration), lets you ask "what if we add this new wing?" and watch the answer — safely, before anyone builds the wing. Counterfactuals are test flights in a world disciplined to behave like ours.
✦ Worked Example — a counterfactual interest-rate experiment

A calibrated savings model has discount factor B = 0.96 and baseline interest rate r = 3%. Simulating 10,000 households gives mean assets 4.20 (in units of annual income) and baseline lifetime value V_base = -20.00. You raise r to 5%, re-solve, and re-simulate. Quantify the effect.

  1. Re-solve under r = 0.05. VFI converges in 312 sweeps; the sup-norm change falls below tol = 1e-6. The new policy function saves more at every asset level (the amber curve in the figure).
  2. Re-simulate the panel. New mean assets = 4.95. Change in aggregate savings = (4.95 − 4.20) / 4.20 = +17.9%.
  3. Read the new value. New mean lifetime value V_cf = −19.61.
  4. Compute welfare in consumption-equivalent terms. With log utility, CEV solves V_base + ln(1+CEV)/(1−B) = V_cf, so ln(1+CEV) = (V_cf − V_base)(1−B) = (−19.61 − (−20.00)) × 0.04 = 0.39 × 0.04 = 0.0156.
  5. Translate to plain language. CEV = e^(0.0156) − 1 = +1.57%. Households are as well off as if their baseline consumption rose 1.57% forever. Report: "A 2-point rise in r raises aggregate savings 17.9% and welfare by 1.57% in consumption-equivalent terms."

🛠️ Hands-On: a full DP pipeline in Python

This pulls the whole course together with the free QuantEcon stack — numpy, scipy, and quantecon (which gives us ready-made Markov chains and a VFI helper). If you set up the environment in the Week 1/2 install step, you already have everything; no new tool is needed here. The structure below mirrors the four decoupled stages from 10.1.

# capstone_savings.py — Lecture 10: pose -> solve -> calibrate -> counterfactual
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
import quantecon as qe

# ---- POSE: primitives -------------------------------------------------
beta = 0.96                      # discount factor
a_grid = np.linspace(0.0, 20.0, 400)   # asset grid
mc = qe.tauchen(n=5, rho=0.9, sigma=0.1)   # income as a Markov chain
y_grid, P = np.exp(mc.state_values), mc.P

def u(c):                        # log utility; -inf if infeasible
    return np.where(c > 1e-10, np.log(np.maximum(c, 1e-10)), -1e10)

# ---- SOLVE: value-function iteration ----------------------------------
def solve(r, tol=1e-6, maxit=2000):
    V = np.zeros((len(a_grid), len(y_grid)))
    for it in range(maxit):
        EV = V @ P.T                          # expected next-period value
        c = (1 + r) * a_grid[:, None, None] + y_grid[None, :, None] \
            - a_grid[None, None, :]           # consumption for every (a, y, a')
        candidate = u(c) + beta * EV[None, None, :, 0]  # Bellman RHS
        Vnew = candidate.max(axis=2)          # max over next assets a'
        if np.max(np.abs(Vnew - V)) < tol:
            return Vnew, candidate.argmax(axis=2), it
        V = Vnew
    return V, candidate.argmax(axis=2), maxit

# ---- COUNTERFACTUAL: re-solve under a new policy lever -----------------
V_base, pol_base, n0 = solve(r=0.03)
V_cf,   pol_cf,   n1 = solve(r=0.05)

dV = V_cf.mean() - V_base.mean()
cev = np.exp(dV * (1 - beta)) - 1            # consumption-equivalent variation
print(f"converged in {n0}/{n1} sweeps; welfare CEV = {cev:.4f}")

Swap solve(r=0.05) for a tax on capital income, or wrap solve in a scipy.optimize.minimize loop to calibrate B so simulated mean assets hit a data target — that outer loop is the calibration stage. Each piece is independently testable, which is exactly what makes the capstone reproducible.

❓ Lecture 10 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The four stages of an end-to-end computational study, in order, are…
A. pose, solve, calibrate, counterfactual
B. calibrate, pose, counterfactual, solve
C. solve, pose, counterfactual, calibrate
D. counterfactual, calibrate, solve, pose
Show answer

A. You must write the model (pose) before you can solve it, fit it to data (calibrate), then change a lever (counterfactual). The other orders put steps before their prerequisites — you cannot calibrate or run a counterfactual on a model you haven't yet posed or solved.

Q2. Value-function iteration is guaranteed to converge to a unique fixed point because the Bellman operator is…
A. linear in the state
B. a contraction mapping with modulus equal to the discount factor
C. monotone but not bounded
D. differentiable everywhere
Show answer

B. Discounting by B < 1 makes the operator a contraction, so by the Banach fixed-point theorem it has a unique fixed point reached from any start. Linearity (A) is false — the max makes it nonlinear. Monotonicity alone (C) doesn't give uniqueness, and differentiability (D) is irrelevant to convergence.

Q3. The strongest evidence that a calibrated model captures the real mechanism is that it…
A. exactly reproduces the moments it was calibrated to
B. uses the largest possible number of parameters
C. reproduces an untargeted moment it was not fit to
D. converges in the fewest VFI sweeps
Show answer

C. Matching a moment you did not target is genuine validation. Reproducing targeted moments (A) is circular — you forced that fit. More parameters (B) invites overfitting, and sweep count (D) is about numerical speed, not realism.

Q4. A counterfactual policy experiment in a structural model means…
A. running a regression on historical policy changes
B. changing a policy parameter, re-solving the calibrated model, and comparing outcomes
C. collecting new survey data after a policy is enacted
D. assuming the policy has no effect and testing that hypothesis
Show answer

B. The whole point of a structural model is to alter a lever and re-solve, so the baseline-vs-policy difference estimates an effect no real experiment ran. A regression (A) and new data (C) are empirical, not structural; (D) describes a null hypothesis, not a counterfactual.

Q5. Welfare effects of a policy are best reported as a consumption-equivalent variation (CEV) because it…
A. is the only quantity a model can compute
B. equals the change in aggregate savings
C. requires no calibration
D. translates an abstract change in the value function into a plain "x% better off" number
Show answer

D. CEV converts an opaque change in V into the percentage consumption boost that would make a household indifferent — interpretable and comparable across studies. It is not the savings change (B), is not the only computable quantity (A), and it relies on a calibrated, solved model (C).

✓ What you learned

  • Every computational study follows pose → solve → calibrate → counterfactual, kept as decoupled code stages.
  • A dynamic program is posed as a Bellman equation and solved by value-function iteration, which converges because the operator is a contraction.
  • Calibration chooses parameters so model moments match data, with an untargeted moment as the real validation test.
  • A counterfactual changes one lever and re-solves; the baseline-vs-policy gap is a credible policy estimate, summarized as a consumption-equivalent variation.
  • The capstone deliverable is a reproducible repo plus a report stating a question, a method, and an answer.
