Market Design & an Energy-Economics Application

When prices alone can't allocate scarce resources, you design the rules of the market itself — then let an algorithm clear it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what computational market design is and when a designed mechanism beats a free price signal.
  • Run the deferred-acceptance algorithm on a two-sided matching market and explain its stability guarantee.
  • Clear a single-item auction computationally and connect bids to the allocation and payment rules.
  • Formulate least-cost generation dispatch as a linear program and read off the market-clearing price.
  • Solve a small dispatch problem in CVXPY and interpret the dual variable as the energy price.

9.1What Is Computational Market Design?

Through most of this course you have taken markets as given: prices appear, agents optimize, supply meets demand. Market design flips the question around. Instead of asking "what is the equilibrium price?", it asks "what rules should govern who trades with whom, in what order, and at what payment, so that the outcome is efficient, stable, and hard to game?" When the good can't simply be sold at a posted price — because it is indivisible, because preferences are two-sided, or because the resource must be allocated under physical constraints — you engineer the mechanism and then compute the allocation.

Three settings recur in practice, and each is fundamentally a computation:

matching  |  auctions  |  constrained dispatchPairing agents to agents; selling scarce items to the highest values; allocating production across resources under hard constraints.

A good design is judged on three criteria you will see repeatedly: efficiency (the allocation maximizes total value), stability / incentive-compatibility (no agent or pair wants to break away or lie about preferences), and computational tractability (the clearing rule can actually be solved at scale). The art is that these goals trade off — and the algorithm you choose is the policy.

AnalogyA market designer is like the referee who writes the rulebook before the game, not a player inside it. In ordinary supply-and-demand the price is the referee. But for a kidney exchange, a school-seat assignment, or a power grid, there is no single price that respects all the constraints — so the designer specifies an algorithm that plays the role of the auctioneer, and the "price" emerges as an output of that computation.

9.2Two-Sided Matching & Deferred Acceptance

Consider a market with two disjoint sides — say, applicants and positions — where each agent ranks the agents on the other side. There is no money; we just want to pair them up well. The central solution concept is a stable matching: a pairing in which no applicant and position both prefer each other to their assigned partner. If such a "blocking pair" existed, they would defect and the matching would unravel.

A matching M is stable  <=>  no blocking pair (a, p) exists(a, p) blocks M if a prefers p to M(a) AND p prefers a to M(p) — both would rather pair with each other.

The deferred-acceptance (Gale–Shapley) algorithm always produces a stable matching, and it is the computational heart of real clearinghouses. With applicants proposing, it runs as follows:

  1. Each unmatched applicant proposes to their most-preferred position that hasn't yet rejected them.
  2. Each position tentatively holds its single most-preferred proposer so far and rejects the rest.
  3. Rejected applicants propose to their next choice; repeat until no proposals remain.

Because positions only ever tentatively accept — and trade up if a better applicant arrives later — the word "deferred." The algorithm terminates in at most n² proposal rounds and, remarkably, the version where one side proposes is strategy-proof for the proposing side: those agents can do no better than to report their true rankings.

Common mistakeA stable matching is not the one that maximizes total happiness, and it is usually not unique. The proposing side gets its best possible stable partner while the receiving side gets its worst; swap who proposes and you get a different stable matching. Do not assume "stable" means "fair" or "efficient in the utilitarian sense" — it only means no blocking pair exists.

9.3Running an Auction Computationally

When the good is sold for money and is scarce, an auction is the mechanism. The designer's two levers are the allocation rule (who wins) and the payment rule (what winners pay). For a single indivisible item with private values, two rules dominate the theory:

In a first-price sealed-bid auction the highest bidder wins and pays their own bid; bidders shade their bids below their true value to leave themselves a surplus. In a second-price (Vickrey) auction the highest bidder wins but pays the second-highest bid. The Vickrey rule has a beautiful property: bidding your true value is a weakly dominant strategy — you can never do better by lying, because your bid affects only whether you win, never how much you pay.

winner = argmax_i b_i    price = max_(j != winner) b_jVickrey (second-price): the top bidder wins and pays the highest losing bid — making truth-telling optimal.

Computationally, clearing a single-item auction is trivial: sort the bids, take the top one, charge the relevant price. The richness comes when items interact — in a uniform-price multi-unit auction (which is exactly how the energy market below clears) you stack supply offers from cheapest to most expensive, meet demand, and the marginal accepted offer sets the price paid to everyone. That single clearing price is the bridge from auctions to the dispatch problem in §9.4.

AnalogyA second-price auction is like a sealed silent auction where the winner only pays "one dollar more than the runner-up." Because what you write down can't raise your own bill — it only decides whether you take the prize — there is never a reason to write anything but your honest top value. That is incentive-compatibility made concrete.

9.4Energy Economics: Least-Cost Dispatch as an Optimization

Now the payoff. A power grid must, every few minutes, decide how much each generator should produce so that total demand is met at minimum cost, subject to each generator's capacity. This economic dispatch problem is a linear program — and its solution simultaneously delivers the efficient allocation and the market-clearing electricity price. With generators i, each producing g_i at marginal cost c_i up to capacity G_i, to serve total demand D:

minimize  sum_i c_i * g_i   s.t.  sum_i g_i = D,  0 <= g_i <= G_iLeast-cost dispatch: minimize total generation cost subject to a demand-balance equality and per-unit capacity limits.

The logic is the merit order: stack generators from cheapest to most expensive, and dispatch them in that order until demand is met. The last (marginal) unit you turn on sets the cost of the next megawatt-hour. By linear-programming duality, the dual variable on the demand-balance constraint equals that marginal cost — it is the locational/clearing price of energy, the "λ" the whole market pays. This is the same uniform clearing price from the multi-unit auction in §9.3, now produced by an optimizer.

This formulation scales: add transmission limits, ramp constraints, or a future-period capacity-planning layer (how much wind, solar, storage, and firm capacity to build) and dispatch becomes a large linear or mixed-integer program. Cheap, near-zero-marginal-cost renewables sit at the bottom of the merit order and push expensive fossil "peakers" off the top — which is precisely how clean-energy economics lowers the clearing price. The optimizer is the policy lab.

✦ Worked Example — clear a three-generator dispatch by hand

Three generators serve demand D = 90 MW. Costs and capacities: Gen A = $20/MWh, up to 50 MW; Gen B = $40/MWh, up to 50 MW; Gen C = $80/MWh, up to 50 MW. Find the least-cost dispatch, total cost, and the clearing price.

  1. Build the merit order (cheapest first): A ($20) → B ($40) → C ($80).
  2. Dispatch A fully: it's cheapest, so run it to its 50 MW cap. Remaining demand = 90 − 50 = 40 MW.
  3. Dispatch B next: at $40 it's the next cheapest. Use 40 of its 50 MW to finish demand. Remaining = 0. Gen C stays off.
  4. Allocation: g_A = 50, g_B = 40, g_C = 0 MW.
  5. Total cost: 50×$20 + 40×$40 + 0×$80 = $1000 + $1600 = $2,600.
  6. Clearing price: the marginal (last dispatched) unit is Gen B, so the uniform price is $40/MWh — the dual on the demand constraint. Every MWh, including A's cheap output, is paid $40; the gap (40 − 20 = $20/MWh on A's 50 MW) is A's inframarginal rent.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve dispatch with CVXPY and read the price off the dual

We reuse the free course stack — Python + NumPy + SciPy + CVXPY (the convex-optimization layer introduced in Week 8) — so there's nothing new to install. If you skipped the Week 1/2 setup, that install <details> lives in the early lectures; CVXPY itself was added in Lecture 8 via pip install cvxpy.

Below, the equality constraint's .dual_value is exactly the market-clearing price — no extra work needed.

# dispatch.py — Lecture 9: least-cost economic dispatch
import numpy as np
import cvxpy as cp

cost = np.array([20.0, 40.0, 80.0])   # $/MWh: A, B, C
cap  = np.array([50.0, 50.0, 50.0])   # MW capacity
D    = 90.0                            # MW demand

g = cp.Variable(3, nonneg=True)        # generation per unit
balance = cp.sum(g) == D               # demand must be met
prob = cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(cost @ g),
                  [balance, g <= cap])
prob.solve()

print("dispatch (MW):", np.round(g.value, 2))   # [50. 40.  0.]
print("total cost ($):", round(prob.value, 2))  # 2600.0
print("clearing price ($/MWh):", round(balance.dual_value, 2))  # 40.0

The dual value on balance recovers the $40 clearing price we found by hand. Now experiment: drop a near-zero-cost renewable block (cost ≈ 0) into the merit order and watch the clearing price fall as the expensive peaker is pushed out — clean-energy economics in three lines of code.

💬 Discussion

In the uniform-price energy market, every accepted generator is paid the single marginal clearing price — so a cheap generator earns far more per MWh than its own cost (inframarginal rent). As near-zero-marginal-cost renewables grow, the clearing price often collapses toward zero in sunny or windy hours. Is the marginal-pricing rule still the right market design once most capacity has near-zero marginal cost? What breaks, and what would you change — without abandoning efficiency or incentive-compatibility? Argue from the mechanisms in this lecture.

Show a model response

Marginal pricing is efficient in the short run: paying everyone the marginal cost gives generators the right signal to produce exactly when their cost is below the clearing price, and the dual-variable interpretation guarantees no cheaper feasible dispatch exists. The incentive-compatibility from the second-price logic (§9.3) carries over — bidding true marginal cost is near-optimal.

The break is on the investment / capacity side. If price = marginal cost and almost everyone's marginal cost is ~$0, the clearing price is ~$0 in most hours, so revenues never cover the large fixed build costs of renewables and storage — the "missing money" problem. Marginal pricing was designed for a world where fuel cost (not capital cost) was the binding scarcity; that assumption erodes.

A defensible redesign keeps short-run marginal pricing for dispatch (don't sacrifice efficiency) but layers on a separate market that prices the scarce thing that's now binding — firm capacity and flexibility. Concretely: a capacity-planning optimization (the §9.4 LP extended to build decisions) whose dual on a reliability/capacity constraint sets a capacity payment, plus scarcity pricing in the few tight hours. This preserves efficiency and truth-telling in dispatch while restoring the investment signal.

(3) Did your fix preserve efficiency and incentive-compatibility rather than just capping prices? If you hit all three, your answer is strong.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A matching is "stable" when…
A. it maximizes the total happiness of all agents
B. no applicant-position pair both prefer each other to their assigned partners
C. every agent gets their first choice
D. it is the only possible matching
Show answer

B. Stability means there is no blocking pair. It does not maximize total welfare (A), rarely gives everyone their top choice (C), and is usually not unique (D) — proposing-side vs receiving-side optimal matchings differ.

Q2. In the deferred-acceptance algorithm with applicants proposing, positions…
A. permanently accept the first applicant who proposes
B. propose to applicants in order of preference
C. tentatively hold their most-preferred proposer and may trade up later
D. accept all proposers and sort them out at the end
Show answer

C. Acceptances are deferred — held tentatively so a position can swap to a better applicant who proposes later. Positions don't propose (B), don't lock in the first arrival (A), and never hold more than their capacity (D).

Q3. In a second-price (Vickrey) auction for one item, the winner pays…
A. their own bid
B. the highest losing bid
C. the average of all bids
D. nothing
Show answer

B. The top bidder wins but pays the second-highest (highest losing) bid, which makes truthful bidding weakly dominant. Paying your own bid (A) is the first-price rule, where bidders shade below their value.

Q4. In least-cost economic dispatch, the uniform clearing price equals…
A. the marginal cost of the most expensive generator that is dispatched
B. the average cost across all dispatched generators
C. the cost of the cheapest generator
D. total cost divided by the number of generators
Show answer

A. The marginal (last dispatched) unit sets the price — equal to the dual variable on the demand-balance constraint. It is not an average (B/D), and cheaper inframarginal units earn rent above their own cost (so not C).

Q5. In the dispatch LP, the dual variable on the demand-balance equality constraint is best interpreted as…
A. the total quantity of energy produced
B. the capacity of the marginal generator
C. the number of binding constraints
D. the market-clearing price of one more MWh of demand
Show answer

D. A constraint's dual is the marginal change in the objective from relaxing it by one unit — here, the cost of serving one more MWh, i.e. the clearing price. It is a price, not a quantity (A/B) or a count (C).
