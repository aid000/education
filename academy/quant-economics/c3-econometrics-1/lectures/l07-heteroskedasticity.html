Week 7 · Lecture 7

Heteroskedasticity & Robust Standard Errors

Your coefficients are still honest — but if the noise fans out, the error bars around them are lying to you.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define heteroskedasticity and contrast it with the constant-variance (homoskedasticity) assumption.
  • Explain why heteroskedasticity leaves OLS coefficients unbiased but makes the usual standard errors wrong.
  • Detect it informally with a residual-vs-fitted plot and formally with the Breusch–Pagan and White tests.
  • Compute and use heteroskedasticity-robust (HC) standard errors, and know when to default to them.
  • Describe weighted least squares (WLS) and when it can recover efficiency over robust OLS.

7.1What Heteroskedasticity Is

Back in Week 4 the classical model assumed homoskedasticity: the error term has the same variance for every observation, no matter the values of the regressors. Formally, Var(u | x) = σ² — a single constant. Heteroskedasticity is simply the failure of that assumption: the spread of the errors changes with x.

Homoskedastic: Var(u | x) = sigma^2    Heteroskedastic: Var(u | x_i) = sigma_i^2Under homoskedasticity the error variance is one constant; under heteroskedasticity each observation can have its own variance sigma_i^2.

This is not some rare pathology — in cross-sectional economic data it is the norm. Model household spending against income and the residuals fan out: low-income households have little discretionary slack, so their spending hugs the line, while high-income households scatter widely. Model firm profits against size, test scores against class size, or savings against wealth, and the same flare appears. Whenever the "room to vary" grows with a regressor, you have heteroskedasticity.

7.2Why It Breaks Inference (but Not the Estimates)

Here is the subtle, crucial point. Heteroskedasticity violates one of the Gauss–Markov assumptions, but not the ones that deliver unbiasedness. The proof that E[β̂] = β only needs the regressors to be exogenous (E[u | x] = 0); it never touches the variance of u. So your coefficient estimates are still unbiased and consistent — they are pointing at the right targets.

What breaks is the variance of those estimates. The classical OLS standard-error formula bakes in the constant-variance assumption:

Classical: Var(beta_hat) = sigma^2 (X'X)^(-1)This formula is only valid under homoskedasticity. With unequal sigma_i^2 the true variance is the sandwich form below, and the classical one is simply the wrong number.

When the variances actually differ, the correct sandwich expression is Var(β̂) = (X'X)-1 [ X'ΩX ] (X'X)-1, where Ω is the diagonal matrix of the σi². The classical formula ignores the middle term and so reports a biased standard error — usually too small. The damage is concrete: every t-statistic, p-value, confidence interval, and F-test from Week 6 is computed from that wrong standard error, so they are all distorted. You can find "significant" effects that aren't there.

AnalogyThink of OLS as a marksman. With or without heteroskedasticity, the shots are still centered on the bullseye — the aim (the coefficient) is unbiased. Heteroskedasticity only changes how tightly the shots cluster, and crucially it means the official scorecard misreports the grouping. The classical standard error claims a tight 1-inch group when the real group is 3 inches wide. You'd over-trust a shooter you shouldn't.
Common mistakeStudents often "fix" heteroskedasticity by re-running OLS and reading the new coefficients, expecting them to change. They don't, and they shouldn't — heteroskedasticity never biased the coefficients. The disease is in the standard errors, so the cure (robust SEs or WLS) targets the inference, not the point estimates. Also: heteroskedasticity does not cause omitted-variable bias — keep the two problems separate.

7.3Detecting It: Plots, Breusch–Pagan, and White

The fastest diagnostic is the eyeball: plot the OLS residuals against the fitted values (or against a suspect regressor). A constant-width band suggests homoskedasticity; a fanning cone, a bow-tie, or any systematic change in spread flags heteroskedasticity. Plots are persuasive but informal — for a decision rule we use a formal test.

The Breusch–Pagan test

The logic: if the error variance depends on the regressors, then the squared residuals should be predictable from those regressors. So we regress the squared OLS residuals on the explanatory variables and ask whether they explain anything:

u_hat^2 = d0 + d1 x1 + ... + dk xk + errorAuxiliary regression. H0: d1 = ... = dk = 0 (homoskedasticity). The test statistic n*R^2 from this regression is approximately chi-square with k degrees of freedom.

Under the null of homoskedasticity, the regressors should have no explanatory power for û², so n·R² (using the auxiliary R²) is small. A large statistic — equivalently a small p-value — rejects homoskedasticity. Breusch–Pagan looks for variance that moves linearly with the regressors.

The White test

The White test is the more general cousin. It runs the same auxiliary regression but adds the squares and cross-products of the regressors, so it can catch nonlinear and interaction-driven heteroskedasticity that Breusch–Pagan misses. The price is degrees of freedom: with many regressors the term count explodes, so the test loses power. A common compromise is the "special case" White test that regresses û² on the fitted values ŷ and ŷ² alone.

Common mistakeDon't read a Breusch–Pagan or White rejection as proof your model is wrong. These tests are also sensitive to misspecification — an omitted nonlinear term or a missing variable can make residuals look heteroskedastic even when the true errors aren't. Always pair the test with a residual plot and a sanity check of functional form (Week 8) before concluding "it's just heteroskedasticity."

7.4The Fixes: Robust SEs and Weighted Least Squares

There are two responses, and they answer different questions.

Heteroskedasticity-robust (HC) standard errors

The modern default is to keep the OLS coefficients exactly as they are and simply recompute the standard errors using a formula that does not assume constant variance. These are the heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) or "robust" or "sandwich" standard errors. They estimate the middle of the sandwich using the squared residuals themselves:

Var_robust(beta_hat) = (X'X)^(-1) [ sum_i x_i x_i' u_hat_i^2 ] (X'X)^(-1)The squared residual u_hat_i^2 stands in for the unknown sigma_i^2. Valid for large samples whether or not heteroskedasticity is present.

The beauty is that robust SEs are valid whether or not heteroskedasticity exists — under homoskedasticity they just converge to the classical ones. With modern software they cost a single keyword, which is why in cross-sectional work it is now standard practice to report robust SEs by default rather than test first and patch later. Common finite-sample variants are labeled HC0 (the original), HC1 (a small degrees-of-freedom correction), and HC2/HC3 (better for small samples and high-leverage points).

Weighted least squares (WLS)

Robust SEs fix the inference but leave OLS slightly inefficient — under heteroskedasticity, OLS is no longer the minimum-variance estimator. Weighted least squares recovers that efficiency by down-weighting the noisy (high-variance) observations and up-weighting the precise ones. If you knew each observation's variance σi², you would weight by wi = 1/σi²:

minimize sum_i w_i ( y_i - b0 - b1 x_i )^2 ,  with w_i = 1 / sigma_i^2WLS minimizes a weighted sum of squared residuals. It is OLS applied to data rescaled so the transformed errors are homoskedastic.

The catch is that you almost never know σi² in advance. Feasible GLS (FGLS) estimates the variance pattern from the data first (e.g., model log û² on the regressors), then weights by the fitted variances. WLS/FGLS can beat robust OLS when you model the variance correctly — but if you guess the weights wrong, you can end up less efficient and with invalid inference. That fragility is exactly why robust-SE OLS is the safe everyday default and WLS is reserved for cases where the variance structure is well understood.

✦ Worked Example — Breusch–Pagan and a robust t-test end to end

You regress household monthly spending on income for n = 200 households and get a slope of 0.142. The Breusch–Pagan auxiliary regression of û² on income yields R² = 0.085. The classical SE on the slope is 0.018; the robust SE is 0.031.

  1. Breusch–Pagan statistic: LM = n·R² = 200 × 0.085 = 17.0.
  2. Reference distribution: with k = 1 regressor in the auxiliary model, compare 17.0 to χ² with 1 df. The 5% critical value is 3.84.
  3. Decision: 17.0 ≫ 3.84 (p ≈ 0.00004) → reject homoskedasticity. The variance grows with income, exactly as the spending-vs-income story predicts.
  4. Classical t-stat: t = 0.142 / 0.018 = 7.9 — looks overwhelmingly significant.
  5. Robust t-stat: t = 0.142 / 0.031 = 4.6. Still significant (|t| > 1.96), but notice the standard error nearly doubled — the classical version overstated our precision.
  6. Read it: the coefficient 0.142 is unchanged; only the error bar widened. Income still significantly predicts spending, but the honest confidence interval is far wider than OLS first claimed. Reporting the classical SE here would have been a real overstatement of certainty.

🛠️ Hands-On: detect and fix heteroskedasticity in statsmodels

We use the course's free stack — pandas, numpy, statsmodels, and pandas-datareader for FRED data. If you set up the environment in the Week 1–2 lectures (the install <details> there), you already have everything; just activate it.

Below we fit a model, run a residual plot's worth of diagnostics with the het_breuschpagan and het_white tests, then re-fit with robust standard errors and compare. The one-line robust fix is .fit(cov_type="HC3").

# heteroskedasticity.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import het_breuschpagan, het_white

# --- a cross-section where variance grows with x (simulated) ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n = 400
income = rng.uniform(20, 120, n)                 # thousands $/yr
# error std rises with income -> heteroskedastic by construction
u = rng.normal(0, 1, n) * (0.5 + 0.06 * income)
spend = 2.0 + 0.142 * income + u
df = pd.DataFrame({"spend": spend, "income": income})

# --- baseline OLS with classical (non-robust) SEs ---
ols = smf.ols("spend ~ income", data=df).fit()
print(ols.summary())                              # classical SEs

# --- formal tests on the residuals ---
bp = het_breuschpagan(ols.resid, ols.model.exog)  # (LM, LM_p, F, F_p)
wh = het_white(ols.resid, ols.model.exog)
print("Breusch-Pagan LM stat:", bp[0], " p:", bp[1])
print("White LM stat:", wh[0], " p:", wh[1])

# --- the one-line fix: robust (HC) standard errors ---
robust = smf.ols("spend ~ income", data=df).fit(cov_type="HC3")
print("classical SE(income):", ols.bse["income"])
print("robust   SE(income):", robust.bse["income"])
print("coef unchanged:", ols.params["income"], robust.params["income"])

# --- optional: weighted least squares if variance ~ income^2 ---
w = 1.0 / (income ** 2)                            # model-based weights
wls = smf.wls("spend ~ income", data=df, weights=w).fit()
print("WLS coef(income):", wls.params["income"])

Watch three things: the Breusch–Pagan and White p-values are tiny (heteroskedasticity confirmed); the coefficient on income is identical in ols and robust; only the standard error grows. Swap HC3 for HC1 to see how the variant changes the SE slightly.

🔗 Where this is heading

Robust standard errors are the entry point to a whole family of "robust covariance" tools. In Week 8 we add nonlinear terms and dummies — and a misspecified functional form is one of the things that can masquerade as heteroskedasticity, so today's diagnostics stay in your toolkit. With time-series and panel data (later in C3 and in C4), errors are not just heteroskedastic but autocorrelated, which calls for HAC (Newey–West) and clustered standard errors — the same sandwich idea, generalized. And in the Quant Finance track, heteroskedasticity is not a nuisance to patch but the main event: ARCH/GARCH models put time-varying variance at the center to forecast volatility and price risk.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Under heteroskedasticity, the OLS coefficient estimates are…
A. biased and inconsistent
B. still unbiased and consistent, but no longer minimum-variance
C. undefined
D. forced to zero
Show answer

B. Unbiasedness depends only on exogeneity (E[u|x]=0), which heteroskedasticity does not violate, so β̂ stays unbiased and consistent — it just loses efficiency. A is the classic misconception (that's omitted-variable bias, a different problem); C and D are nonsense.

Q2. The real problem heteroskedasticity causes is that…
A. the dependent variable becomes non-normal
B. R² is always overstated
C. the usual standard errors are biased, so t-tests, p-values, and confidence intervals are unreliable
D. the regressors become collinear
Show answer

C. The classical SE formula assumes constant variance; when that fails the SEs (and everything built on them) are wrong. A and D describe unrelated issues; B is false — heteroskedasticity does not systematically bias R².

Q3. The Breusch–Pagan test works by…
A. regressing the squared residuals on the explanatory variables and testing whether they explain anything
B. comparing two nested models' R²
C. counting how many residuals are positive
D. dividing the largest residual by the smallest
Show answer

A. If variance depends on x, the squared residuals should be predictable from x; the auxiliary-regression statistic n·R² is compared to a chi-square. B describes the F-test for nested models; C and D are not the BP procedure.

Q4. Compared with the Breusch–Pagan test, the White test…
A. requires the errors to be normally distributed
B. can only be used with one regressor
C. tests for autocorrelation rather than heteroskedasticity
D. is more general because it adds squares and cross-products of the regressors, catching nonlinear forms of heteroskedasticity
Show answer

D. White augments the auxiliary regression with squares and interactions, so it detects more patterns (at the cost of degrees of freedom). A and B are false; C confuses it with a serial-correlation test.

Q5. The main practical appeal of heteroskedasticity-robust (HC) standard errors is that…
A. they change the coefficient estimates to remove the bias
B. they give valid inference whether or not heteroskedasticity is present, with no model for the variance
C. they always produce smaller standard errors than OLS
D. they require you to know each observation's variance in advance
Show answer

B. Robust SEs use the squared residuals in the sandwich formula, so they are valid in large samples either way and need no variance model. A is false (coefficients are untouched); C is false (they're often larger); D describes WLS, not robust SEs.
