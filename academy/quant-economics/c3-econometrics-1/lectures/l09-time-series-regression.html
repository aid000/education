Week 9 · Lecture 9

Time-Series Regression & Autocorrelation

When your data arrive in order through time, today's error often whispers to tomorrow's — and if you don't listen, your standard errors will lie to you.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Adapt the OLS framework to time-series data and model deterministic trends and seasonality.
  • Explain what autocorrelated errors are and exactly how they corrupt inference (not the slope, the standard error).
  • Compute and interpret the Durbin–Watson statistic and connect it to the first-order autocorrelation ρ.
  • Use Newey–West (HAC) standard errors to get valid t-stats when errors are serially correlated.
  • Diagnose spurious regression between trending series and use lagged regressors responsibly.

9.1Regression When Data Arrive in Order

Until now most of our data has been cross-sectional — a snapshot of many households or firms at one moment, where the order of the rows means nothing. Time-series data is different: each observation carries a timestamp, and the rows have a natural sequence. We index observations by t = 1, 2, …, T (months, quarters, years) instead of by individual i. The mechanics of OLS don't change — we still minimize the sum of squared residuals — but the story behind the error term changes completely.

y_t = β0 + β1 x_t + u_t,   t = 1, ..., TA static time-series regression: the t subscript signals the observations are ordered through time.

The classical assumption that did the heavy lifting in cross-sections was that errors are independent across observations. With a snapshot of unrelated households that is plausible. With time, it is usually false: whatever shock pushed unemployment above its trend last month — a credit crunch, a supply shock — tends to still be pushing this month. The errors are serially correlated, also called autocorrelated. That single fact drives most of this lecture.

A second new ingredient is that many economic series simply grow. GDP, the price level, and population all march upward over time. We capture that systematic movement with a deterministic trend and, for sub-annual data, with seasonal terms — leaving the error to describe only the unpredictable part.

9.2Trends & Seasonality

If you regress one trending series on another and forget the trend, the regression will credit a real common cause — the passage of time — to the regressor. The fix is to put time into the model explicitly. A linear time trend adds the index t itself as a regressor:

y_t = β0 + β1 x_t + β2 t + u_tβ2 absorbs the part of y that grows mechanically with time, so β1 measures the relationship net of trend.

Including t is algebraically the same as detrending both y and x first and then regressing the detrended pieces — a time-series echo of the partialling-out logic from multiple regression (Week 3). For series that grow at a roughly constant percentage rate, a trend on the log of y is more natural: there, β2 is the approximate growth rate per period.

For monthly or quarterly data, seasonality is the predictable within-year pattern: retail sales spike every December, construction slows every winter. We model it with seasonal dummy variables — for quarterly data, three dummies (one quarter is the omitted baseline), exactly the dummy-variable machinery from Week 8.

AnalogyThink of a time series as a song. The trend is the slow rise in volume over the whole track; the seasonal pattern is the repeating chorus; the error is the live-recording crowd noise. Detrending and deseasonalizing strip out the volume sweep and the chorus so you can finally hear whether two recordings share the same background hum — and autocorrelation is the fact that the crowd noise this second sounds a lot like the crowd noise last second.

9.3Autocorrelated Errors & What They Break

The most common model of serial correlation is first-order autoregression, AR(1): each error is a fraction ρ of the previous error plus fresh noise.

u_t = ρ · u_(t-1) + e_t,   |ρ| < 1,   e_t white noiseρ is the first-order autocorrelation. ρ > 0 (positive serial correlation) is overwhelmingly the typical case in macro data.

Here is the crucial, often-misremembered point. Autocorrelation does not bias the OLS slope estimates — as long as the regressors are exogenous, β̂ is still unbiased and consistent. It simply loses its "best" badge — it is no longer the most efficient linear estimator (no longer BLUE). What it does wreck is the standard errors. The usual OLS variance formula assumes errors don't talk to each other; when they do, that formula is simply the wrong arithmetic.

With positive autocorrelation, successive observations carry overlapping information, so your T data points contain fewer than T independent pieces of evidence. OLS doesn't know this and reports standard errors that are too small. The downstream damage:

SE too small -> t-stat too big -> reject H0 too oftenYou "find" significant effects that aren't there: confidence intervals are too narrow and false positives balloon.
Common mistakeStudents panic that autocorrelation makes the estimated slope "wrong" and try to throw out the coefficient. It doesn't — β̂ is still unbiased. The disease is in the inference: the standard errors, t-statistics, and p-values are untrustworthy. So the cure is to fix the standard errors (Newey–West, §9.4), not to discard the point estimate.

9.4Detecting It: Durbin–Watson, and Fixing It: Newey–West

Before you trust a time-series regression, test the residuals for serial correlation. The classic detector is the Durbin–Watson (DW) statistic, built from the OLS residuals û_t:

DW = Σ(û_t − û_(t−1))² / Σ û_t²  ≈  2(1 − ρ̂)The approximation links DW to the estimated first-order autocorrelation ρ̂ of the residuals.

Read it through that approximation. If residuals are uncorrelated, ρ̂ ≈ 0 and DW ≈ 2. Strong positive autocorrelation pushes ρ̂ toward 1, so DW → 0. Strong negative autocorrelation pushes ρ̂ toward −1, so DW → 4. As a rule of thumb, DW comfortably below about 1.5 is a red flag for positive serial correlation — the usual macro case.

Once you've detected it, the modern, low-effort fix is Newey–West standard errors — a member of the HAC family (Heteroskedasticity- and Autocorrelation-Consistent). The idea generalizes the robust standard errors from Week 5: instead of assuming the error variance is a tidy diagonal, HAC builds the variance from the residuals while explicitly allowing nearby errors to covary, up to a chosen number of lags L (the bandwidth). You keep the same β̂ — only the reported standard errors change, and now they tell the truth.

t = β̂1 / SE_HAC(β̂1)Same slope as plain OLS; the HAC standard error in the denominator is corrected for serial correlation, so the t-stat is finally valid.
✦ Worked Example — from DW to a corrected t-stat

You regress quarterly consumption growth on income growth over T = 120 quarters and get slope β̂1 = 0.80 with a plain-OLS standard error of 0.10. The Durbin–Watson statistic comes back DW = 0.6. Diagnose the problem and judge what the corrected inference will look like.

  1. Estimate ρ from DW. Using DW ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂): 0.6 = 2(1 − ρ̂) → 1 − ρ̂ = 0.30 → ρ̂ ≈ 0.70. That is strong positive autocorrelation.
  2. Interpret. DW = 0.6 is far below 2 (and below the 1.5 rule of thumb), so the residuals are highly persistent. The independence assumption is badly violated.
  3. Locate the damage. The slope β̂1 = 0.80 is still unbiased. But the plain SE of 0.10 is too small — it ignores the overlapping information in successive quarters.
  4. Plain t-stat (invalid). t = 0.80 / 0.10 = 8.0 — looks wildly significant, but this number can't be trusted.
  5. After Newey–West. The HAC standard error will be larger — say it rises to about 0.20. Then t = 0.80 / 0.20 = 4.0. Still significant here, but only half as large; in borderline cases this correction is exactly what flips a bogus "significant" result back to "not significant."

9.5Spurious Regression & Lagged Regressors

The figure above is the single most dangerous trap in time-series work. Regress any two independently trending series on each other and you will typically get a large R², a huge t-statistic, and a tiny p-value — all of it meaningless. This is spurious regression: the apparent relationship is just two things separately drifting upward through time. The residuals from such a regression are wildly autocorrelated (DW near 0), which is itself the warning siren. The remedy is to model the trend explicitly (§9.2), or to regress changes on changes, or to formally test for a genuine long-run link (cointegration — the topic of later courses).

Finally, time gives us a regressor we never had before: the past. A lagged regressor uses an earlier value of an explanatory variable, recognizing that economic effects take time to act — a rate change today may not move spending until next quarter.

y_t = β0 + β1 x_t + β2 x_(t-1) + u_tA distributed-lag model: the total effect of x on y is β1 + β2, the contemporaneous plus the delayed response.

A lagged dependent variable (y_(t−1) on the right) is even more powerful for capturing persistence, but it interacts dangerously with autocorrelated errors — there DW is biased toward 2 and can hide the problem, so you lean on other tests. We flag that here and develop dynamic models in the sequel course.

🛠️ Hands-On: trend, Durbin–Watson & Newey–West in Python

We use the same free stack from Week 1/2 — Python with pandas, numpy, statsmodels, and pandas_datareader for live FRED data (the one-time install lives in the early lectures). Nothing new to install this week. Below we pull two real macro series, fit a trended regression, read the Durbin–Watson statistic, and re-fit with Newey–West (HAC) standard errors.

# time_series_regression.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from statsmodels.stats.stattools import durbin_watson
from pandas_datareader import data as web

# Real quarterly FRED data: personal consumption (PCEC) & disposable income (DPI)
raw = web.DataReader(["PCEC", "DPI"], "fred", "1990-01-01", "2019-12-31")
df = raw.resample("QS").last().dropna()

# growth rates (regress changes on changes, not levels -> avoids spurious trend)
df["cons_g"] = 100 * np.log(df["PCEC"]).diff()
df["inc_g"]  = 100 * np.log(df["DPI"]).diff()
df = df.dropna()

# 1) Plain OLS
ols = smf.ols("cons_g ~ inc_g", data=df).fit()
print(ols.summary())

# 2) Durbin-Watson on the residuals (~2 = clean; near 0 = positive autocorr)
dw = durbin_watson(ols.resid)
print(f"Durbin-Watson = {dw:.3f}   implied rho-hat ~ {1 - dw/2:.3f}")

# 3) Newey-West HAC: SAME slope, corrected standard errors
hac = smf.ols("cons_g ~ inc_g", data=df).fit(
        cov_type="HAC", cov_kwds={"maxlags": 4})   # L = 4 quarters
print(hac.summary())                               # compare the SE / t on inc_g

Compare the two summary() tables: the coefficient on inc_g is identical, but the HAC standard error is larger and its t-statistic smaller — that is the autocorrelation correction in action. Try refitting on levels instead of growth rates to watch a spurious, ultra-high-R² regression appear, with a Durbin–Watson statistic crashing toward 0.

🔗 Where this is heading

This lecture treated the trend as a fixed line and the error as something to clean up. The deeper truth — central to the Time-Series Econometrics sequel — is that many macro and financial series have a stochastic trend (a unit root), where the right question becomes whether two series are cointegrated (share a genuine long-run path) rather than merely co-trending. From there you reach ARIMA forecasting, vector autoregressions (VARs), and the volatility models (ARCH/GARCH) that price risk in the Quant Finance track. Q1. When the errors in a time-series regression are positively autocorrelated (but the regressors are exogenous), what happens to OLS?
A. The slope estimates become biased and must be discarded
B. The slope estimates stay unbiased, but the usual standard errors are too small
C. Both the slopes and the standard errors are unaffected
D. The regression cannot be estimated at all
Show answer

B. Autocorrelation does not bias β̂ (so A is wrong); it makes the conventional standard errors too small, inflating t-stats. C is wrong because inference is affected, and D is wrong because OLS still runs fine — the issue is interpreting it.

Q2. A Durbin–Watson statistic of approximately 2 indicates…
A. strong positive autocorrelation
B. strong negative autocorrelation
C. little or no first-order autocorrelation in the residuals
D. that the regression has no intercept
Show answer

C. Since DW ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂), a value near 2 means ρ̂ ≈ 0. Positive autocorrelation pushes DW toward 0 (so A is wrong), negative toward 4 (B is wrong), and DW says nothing about the intercept (D).

Q3. What is the purpose of Newey–West (HAC) standard errors?
A. To change the slope estimates so they account for the trend
B. To remove seasonality from the dependent variable
C. To produce standard errors valid under heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation, leaving the slopes unchanged
D. To delete autocorrelated observations from the sample
Show answer

C. HAC corrects the variance/standard-error calculation while keeping β̂ exactly the same. It does not alter the slopes (A), does not deseasonalize (B), and never drops data (D).

Q4. You regress two unrelated, upward-trending series in levels and get R² = 0.95 with a t-stat of 18 and DW = 0.2. This is most likely…
A. a spurious regression driven by the shared time trend
B. strong proof that one series causes the other
C. evidence the errors are perfectly independent
D. impossible, because unrelated series can't be correlated
Show answer

A. A near-perfect fit plus DW near 0 is the textbook fingerprint of spurious regression. B mistakes the artifact for causation; C is backwards (DW ≈ 0 means severe autocorrelation); D is false — co-trending series correlate strongly by accident.

Q5. A Durbin–Watson statistic of 0.5 implies an estimated first-order autocorrelation ρ̂ of approximately…
A. ρ̂ ≈ −0.75
B. ρ̂ ≈ 0
C. ρ̂ ≈ 0.25
D. ρ̂ ≈ 0.75
Show answer

D. From DW ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂): 0.5 = 2(1 − ρ̂) → 1 − ρ̂ = 0.25 → ρ̂ ≈ 0.75, strong positive autocorrelation. A has the sign wrong, B corresponds to DW ≈ 2, and C corresponds to DW ≈ 1.5.

Model response

Ignoring autocorrelation doesn't bias the coefficient — β̃ stays unbiased as long as the regressors are exogenous — but it corrupts the uncertainty you report around it. When errors are positively autocorrelated, your T time-series observations carry fewer than T independent pieces of evidence, because today's shock is largely a rerun of yesterday's. The classical OLS variance formula doesn't know this; it still divides by the full T as if every observation were fresh information, so it reports a standard error that is systematically too small. A too-small SE inflates the t-statistic and shrinks the p-value, so you "find" statistically significant relationships — and narrower confidence intervals — more often than the data actually justify. That is overconfidence in the literal statistical sense: your stated certainty exceeds your genuine certainty.

I'd expect this most wherever economic variables have real momentum — month-to-month or quarter-to-quarter persistence driven by slow-moving underlying conditions. The lecture's own examples are the classic cases: unemployment, inflation, and GDP growth all move in business-cycle swings that last many quarters, so a shock this quarter is still partly present next quarter (ρ > 0, DW well below 2). Asset-return volatility is another prime candidate: calm and turbulent periods cluster in time (the volatility-clustering idea flagged as "where this is heading," toward GARCH-style models), so financial time series are especially prone to autocorrelated errors and the resulting overconfident inference — exactly why Newey–West/HAC standard errors are the default fix in applied macro and finance work rather than an optional extra.
