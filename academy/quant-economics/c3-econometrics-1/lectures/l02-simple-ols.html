Simple Linear Regression (OLS)

Draw the one straight line that sits as close as possible to every data point at once — and you've built the workhorse of all of empirical economics.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write the simple linear regression model and separate the systematic part from the error term.
  • Define ordinary least squares (OLS) as the line that minimizes the sum of squared residuals.
  • Derive the closed-form slope and intercept estimators from the minimization problem.
  • Compute fitted values and residuals, and explain why residuals are not the same as the model's errors.
  • Interpret R-squared as the share of variation explained, and read off the meaning of the slope coefficient.

2.1The Simple Linear Regression Model

In Week 1 we asked what econometrics does: turn data into quantified relationships. The simplest such relationship links one outcome y (the dependent variable) to one explanatory variable x (the regressor). Think of y as a household's monthly consumption and x as its income, or y as a country's inflation and x as its unemployment rate. We do not assume the points fall exactly on a line — real data never do. Instead we split y into a part explained by x and a leftover:

y = β0 + β1 · x + uβ0 = intercept (value of y when x = 0); β1 = slope (how much y moves per one-unit rise in x); u = error capturing everything else.

The error term u is the honest admission that income is not the only thing driving consumption — tastes, family size, and luck all live inside u. The slope β1 is the object we usually care about: it is the marginal effect of x on y. If β1 = 0.8, then a $1 rise in income is associated with an $0.80 rise in consumption on average (the marginal propensity to consume, a number macro forecasters obsess over).

The catch: β0 and β1 are unknown population parameters. We never see them. All we have is a sample of n paired observations (x₁, y₁), …, (xₙ, yₙ), from which we must produce estimates, written β0-hat and β1-hat. Estimating those two numbers is the entire job of this lecture.

2.2Least Squares: Picking the Best Line

Lay your scatter of points on a graph and imagine sliding a ruler around. Any candidate line gives each point a fitted value y-hat = β0-hat + β1-hat·x, and the vertical gap between the actual point and the line is the residual:

eᵢ = yᵢ − y-hatᵢ = yᵢ − (β0-hat + β1-hat · xᵢ)The residual is what the line failed to predict for observation i — measured vertically, in the units of y.

We want a line that is close to all the points. Why not just add up the residuals and minimize that? Because positive and negative gaps cancel: a wildly wrong line can have residuals that sum to zero. The fix is to square each residual before adding — squares are always positive, and they punish a big miss far more than several small ones. The total we minimize is the sum of squared residuals (SSR):

minimize over (β0-hat, β1-hat):   SSR = Σ eᵢ² = Σ ( yᵢ − β0-hat − β1-hat · xᵢ )²Ordinary least squares = the (intercept, slope) pair that makes this sum as small as it can be.

That is the whole idea of ordinary least squares. The figure shows the geometry: each dashed segment is a residual, and OLS is the unique line that minimizes the combined area of all the little squares built on those segments.

AnalogyPicture each data point tethered to the line by a spring that runs straight up or down. A spring's stored energy grows with the square of how far it's stretched. OLS is the line that settles into the lowest total spring energy — pulled equally hard from above and below, it comes to rest exactly through the "balance point" of the cloud of points.

2.3Deriving the Slope and Intercept

SSR is a smooth, bowl-shaped function of the two unknowns β0-hat and β1-hat, so its minimum is where both partial derivatives are zero. Setting them to zero gives the two normal equations, which solve cleanly. Using sample means x-bar and y-bar:

β1-hat = Σ(xᵢ − x-bar)(yᵢ − y-bar) / Σ(xᵢ − x-bar)²= sample covariance of x and y divided by the sample variance of x. The slope is "how x and y move together" scaled by "how much x moves."
β0-hat = y-bar − β1-hat · x-barThe intercept is pinned so the line passes through the point of means (x-bar, y-bar).

Two facts fall straight out of the normal equations and are worth memorizing. First, the residuals always sum to zero (Σeᵢ = 0), so the line never has a systematic over- or under-shoot. Second, the residuals are uncorrelated with x (Σxᵢeᵢ = 0) — there is no leftover linear signal in x that the line ignored. These are not assumptions; they are algebraic guarantees of how OLS is built.

Notice the slope formula's intuition: the numerator is the sample covariance of x and y (do they rise together?), and the denominator is the variance of x (how spread out is x?). Divide them and you get the rise in y per unit of x. This is why OLS fails outright if x never varies — the denominator is zero and the slope is undefined.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the residual eᵢ with the model error uᵢ. The error uᵢ = yᵢ − (β0 + β1 xᵢ) uses the true, unknown parameters and can never be observed. The residual eᵢ uses the estimated hatted parameters and is something you compute from your fitted line. They look alike on paper, but conflating "the thing the world generated" with "the thing my line left over" is the classic beginner's error — and it quietly sabotages every inference argument that comes later.

2.4Goodness of Fit: R-squared & Reading the Slope

We have the best line — but is it any good? We measure that by asking how much of y's variation the line accounts for. Decompose the total variation in y (its total sum of squares, SST) into the part the line explains (SSE) plus the part it leaves as residual (SSR):

SST = SSE + SSR  →   R² = SSE / SST = 1 − SSR / SSTSST = Σ(yᵢ − y-bar)²,  SSE = Σ(y-hatᵢ − y-bar)²,  SSR = Σeᵢ². The decomposition holds exactly for OLS.

R-squared is the fraction of the variation in y explained by x. It runs from 0 (the line explains nothing; you'd do just as well guessing y-bar every time) to 1 (every point sits exactly on the line). In a simple regression with one regressor, R² is literally the square of the correlation between x and y. An R² of 0.64 means x accounts for 64% of the up-and-down in y.

Finally, the payoff — reading the slope. Always state it with units: "a one-[unit-of-x] increase in x is associated with a β1-hat-[unit-of-y] change in y, on average, holding everything in u fixed." Note the careful words associated with, not causes: regression measures correlation in the data, and turning that into a causal claim requires the assumptions we build in the coming weeks.

Common mistakeA high R² does not mean the relationship is causal, important, or correctly specified — and a low R² does not mean the slope is useless. Individual behavior is noisy, so a true and policy-relevant effect can sit inside a regression with R² near 0.1. Chasing R² for its own sake is how analysts get fooled by spurious fits.
✦ Worked Example — fit a line by hand

Five neighborhoods report average household income x (in $1,000s) and average monthly consumption y (in $100s): (1,3), (2,4), (3,4), (4,6), (5,8). Find the OLS line, an R², and interpret the slope.

  1. Means: x-bar = (1+2+3+4+5)/5 = 3;   y-bar = (3+4+4+6+8)/5 = 5.
  2. Deviations & products: (x−x-bar) = (−2,−1,0,1,2); (y−y-bar) = (−2,−1,−1,1,3). Products (x−x-bar)(y−y-bar) = (4, 1, 0, 1, 6), summing to 12.
  3. Variance numerator: Σ(x−x-bar)² = 4+1+0+1+4 = 10.
  4. Slope: β1-hat = 12 / 10 = 1.2.
  5. Intercept: β0-hat = y-bar − β1-hat·x-bar = 5 − 1.2×3 = 1.4. So y-hat = 1.4 + 1.2x.
  6. Fitted & residuals: y-hat = (2.6, 3.8, 5.0, 6.2, 7.4); residuals e = (0.4, 0.2, −1.0, −0.2, 0.6). Check: they sum to 0. ✓
  7. Goodness of fit: SSR = Σe² = 0.16+0.04+1.0+0.04+0.36 = 1.6;  SST = Σ(y−y-bar)² = 4+1+1+1+9 = 16. So R² = 1 − 1.6/16 = 0.90.
  8. Interpretation: each extra $1,000 of income is associated with about $120 more monthly consumption (slope 1.2 in units of $100s), and income explains 90% of the variation in consumption across these neighborhoods.

🛠️ Hands-On: run OLS in Python

This course runs entirely on free, open tools and public data: statsmodels for regression, pandas for data, pandas-datareader to pull live series from FRED (the Federal Reserve's free database), and NumPy. Week 2 setup: install once below, then reuse it the rest of the course.

⬇ Install Python + statsmodels + pandas + pandas-datareader (one-time setup)

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the stack:

python3 -m venv econ-env
source econ-env/bin/activate       # macOS / Linux
econ-env\Scripts\activate          # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install statsmodels pandas pandas-datareader numpy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import statsmodels, pandas, pandas_datareader; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set. No API key is needed for FRED.

First, reproduce the worked example exactly, then fit a real consumption-vs-income relationship from live FRED data. Notice the slope, intercept, and R² that statsmodels prints.

# simple_ols.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm

# --- 1. reproduce the worked example by hand-built data ---
x = np.array([1, 2, 3, 4, 5])
y = np.array([3, 4, 4, 6, 8])

X = sm.add_constant(x)          # add a column of 1s for the intercept
model = sm.OLS(y, X).fit()
print(model.params)             # [1.4, 1.2] -> intercept, slope
print("R-squared:", model.rsquared)   # 0.90

# --- 2. real data: US consumption vs disposable income (FRED) ---
from pandas_datareader import data as web
pce = web.DataReader("PCE",  "fred", "2000-01-01")     # consumption
dpi = web.DataReader("DSPI", "fred", "2000-01-01")     # disposable income
df = pd.concat([pce, dpi], axis=1).dropna()
df.columns = ["consumption", "income"]

Xr = sm.add_constant(df["income"])
fit = sm.OLS(df["consumption"], Xr).fit()
print(fit.summary())            # slope ~ marginal propensity to consume

The first block prints [1.4, 1.2] and R² = 0.90 — exactly your by-hand answer. The second estimates the marginal propensity to consume from decades of real US data; the slope on income is the headline number macro forecasters care about.

🔗 Where this is heading

One regressor is rarely enough — consumption depends on wealth and interest rates too, not just income. Week 3 generalizes OLS to multiple regression, where each slope is read "holding the other variables fixed," finally giving teeth to the ceteris paribus claims you make in every economics course. From there we ask when OLS estimates are trustworthy: the Gauss-Markov assumptions and the standard errors that turn a point estimate into a confidence interval and a hypothesis test (the inference half of the course). The same least-squares engine reappears in the Quant Finance track as the workhorse behind factor models and the CAPM "beta," and in the machine-learning track as linear regression — your first supervised learner.

❓ Lecture 2 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Ordinary least squares chooses the intercept and slope to minimize…
A. the sum of the residuals
B. the sum of the squared residuals
C. the largest single residual
D. the sum of the absolute residuals
Show answer

B. OLS minimizes Σeᵢ². (A) is useless because positive and negative residuals cancel — the OLS residuals already sum to zero for many lines. Minimizing the max residual (C) or the absolute residuals (D) defines different estimators (minimax and LAD), not OLS.

Q2. The OLS slope estimator β1-hat equals…
A. the variance of y divided by the variance of x
B. the correlation between x and y
C. the sample covariance of x and y divided by the sample variance of x
D. y-bar minus x-bar
Show answer

C. β1-hat = Cov(x,y)/Var(x). (B) is unitless and only proportional to the slope, not equal to it. (A) ignores how x and y co-move. (D) is nonsense dimensionally — that expression isn't the slope.

Q3. The residual eᵢ differs from the model error uᵢ because…
A. the residual uses the estimated coefficients, while the error uses the true unknown parameters
B. they are exactly the same thing
C. the error is always larger than the residual
D. the residual is measured horizontally and the error vertically
Show answer

A. eᵢ = yᵢ − (β0-hat + β1-hat xᵢ) is observable; uᵢ uses the true β's and is not. (B) conflates them — the central error of the lecture. (C) has no general ordering. (D) is wrong: both are vertical, in units of y.

Q4. An R² of 0.90 in a simple regression means…
A. the slope coefficient equals 0.90
B. 90% of observations lie exactly on the line
C. x causes 90% of y
D. x explains 90% of the variation in y
Show answer

D. R² is the share of the variation (SST) explained by the line. (A) confuses R² with the slope. (B) misreads it as a count of points. (C) is the causal trap — R² measures fit, never causation.

Q5. A fitted line is y-hat = 2 + 3x, with x measured in years of schooling and y in thousands of dollars of annual earnings. The slope says…
A. each extra year of schooling is associated with $3,000 more earnings per year, on average
B. someone with zero schooling earns $3,000
C. schooling explains 3% of earnings
D. earnings rise by 300% per year of schooling
Show answer

A. The slope 3 is the change in y (in $1,000s) per one-unit rise in x — about $3,000 per extra year, on average. (B) describes the intercept (2 -> $2,000), not the slope. (C) confuses the slope with R². (D) misreads a level change as a percentage change.
