The Classical Assumptions & Gauss-Markov

OLS is just one line through a cloud of points — five quiet assumptions are what turn that line into the best answer money can buy.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the five classical linear model (CLM) assumptions and explain the economic meaning of each.
  • Distinguish "linear in parameters" from "linear in variables," and why only the former matters for OLS.
  • Define unbiasedness precisely as a property of an estimator's sampling distribution.
  • Explain why zero conditional mean of the error is the assumption that does the heavy lifting.
  • State the Gauss-Markov theorem and explain the claim that OLS is BLUE (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator).

4.1From a Fitted Line to a Promise

In Weeks 2 and 3 you computed OLS coefficients: feed in data, turn the crank, get numbers. But a number alone is worthless until you can say what it estimates and how trustworthy it is. The classical linear model (CLM) assumptions are the contract you sign with your data. If the contract holds, OLS comes with a guarantee. If it is broken, your estimates can be confidently, precisely wrong.

We treat the data as generated by a true population model. With k regressors, written for one observation:

y = b0 + b1*x1 + b2*x2 + ... + bk*xk + uThe b's are fixed unknown population parameters; u is the unobserved error capturing everything else that moves y.

OLS produces estimates b-hat of those true b's. Because our sample is random, b-hat is itself a random variable — draw a different sample and you get a different b-hat. The CLM assumptions describe the world precisely enough that we can pin down the sampling distribution of b-hat and prove things about it.

There are five assumptions for unbiasedness and efficiency (a sixth, normality of errors, gets added next week for exact inference). We take them one at a time.

4.2The Five Classical Assumptions

MLR.1 — Linear in parameters. The population model is a linear combination of the parameters. Crucially this is about the b's, not the x's. You may transform variables freely:

log(wage) = b0 + b1*educ + b2*exper + b3*exper^2 + uThis is still "linear in parameters" — exper^2 is just another regressor. OLS handles it directly.

What is not allowed is something like y = b0 + x^(b1) + u, where a parameter sits in an exponent. The takeaway: OLS is far more flexible than "fitting straight lines."

MLR.2 — Random sampling. The n observations are a random sample from the population that obeys MLR.1. This is what lets us treat each row as an independent draw and average our way to the truth. It fails badly with serially correlated time series or selected samples (e.g., only surveying people who already have a job).

MLR.3 — No perfect collinearity. No regressor is constant, and no regressor is an exact linear combination of the others. If x2 = 3*x1 exactly, the data cannot tell their effects apart and the OLS formula literally cannot be computed (you cannot invert the matrix). Note this bans only perfect collinearity; high-but-imperfect correlation is allowed (it just inflates variance — Week 6).

MLR.4 — Zero conditional mean of the error. The big one:

E[u | x1, x2, ..., xk] = 0Given any values of the regressors, the average of all the unobserved stuff is zero — the error is unrelated to the x's.

This says the regressors carry no information about the error. If they do — if an omitted variable that affects y is also correlated with a regressor — then MLR.4 fails and OLS becomes biased. This is the assumption that separates a causal estimate from a mere correlation.

MLR.5 — Homoskedasticity. The error has the same variance regardless of the regressors:

Var(u | x1, ..., xk) = sigma^2 (a constant)The spread of the points around the line does not widen or shrink as x changes.

MLR.5 is not needed for unbiasedness — it is the assumption that makes OLS efficient (minimum variance) and makes the standard textbook standard-error formula correct. When it fails (heteroskedasticity), OLS is still unbiased but its usual standard errors are wrong, which is why robust standard errors exist (Week 5).

AnalogyThink of MLR.4 as an honest scale and MLR.5 as a steady hand. An honest scale (MLR.4) gives the right weight on average — no systematic thumb on the dial — so repeated weighings center on the truth (unbiasedness). A steady hand (MLR.5) means the jitter around that average is the same for a feather as for a brick — equally precise everywhere (efficiency). A scale can be honest but jittery: still unbiased, just noisier than necessary.
Common mistakeStudents panic that "the relationship looks curved, so I can't use OLS." MLR.1 requires linearity in the parameters, not in the variables. A model with x^2, log(x), or interaction terms is perfectly fine for OLS. Conversely, do not assume MLR.4 holds just because your code ran — software never checks it for you. Zero conditional mean is an assumption about the world, not an output.

4.3What Unbiasedness Means

Under MLR.1–MLR.4, OLS is unbiased:

E[b-hat_j] = b_j for every coefficient jAcross all possible random samples, the OLS estimator centers exactly on the true parameter.

Unbiasedness is a statement about the average over many hypothetical samples, not about your one dataset. Your particular b-hat may be too high or too low — unbiasedness only promises that there is no systematic tendency in either direction. The estimator is like a dart thrower whose darts scatter around the bullseye with no consistent lean.

The figure makes this concrete. Each thin line is the OLS fit from a different random sample drawn from the same true line; the fits scatter around the truth, and their average coincides with it.

4.4Gauss-Markov: Why OLS Is BLUE

Unbiasedness alone is cheap — infinitely many estimators are unbiased (you could even use only the first two data points). What we want is the unbiased estimator with the smallest variance, so our one sample lands as close to the truth as possible. The Gauss-Markov theorem delivers exactly that.

MLR.1–MLR.5 => OLS is BLUEBest Linear Unbiased Estimator: among all estimators that are linear in y and unbiased, OLS has the minimum variance.

Decode the acronym one word at a time:

  • Linear — the estimator is a linear function of the outcome values y (OLS is: b-hat is a weighted sum of the y's).
  • Unbiased — it centers on the true parameter (this needs MLR.1–4).
  • Best — smallest variance in the class of linear unbiased estimators (this needs MLR.5, homoskedasticity).

Read the boundaries carefully. "Best" is only within the class of linear, unbiased estimators. A nonlinear estimator, or a slightly biased one, could in principle have lower variance — and when MLR.5 fails, OLS is no longer even "best" among linear unbiased estimators (Generalized Least Squares can beat it). ✦ Worked Example — diagnosing the assumptions in a wage regression

You estimate log(wage) = b0 + b1*educ + b2*exper + u on a cross-section of workers and get b1-hat = 0.083 (an extra year of schooling associates with about 8.3% higher wage). Walk through the assumptions.

  1. MLR.1 (linear in parameters): Yes. The log on the left and the linear right-hand side are fine — a one-unit rise in educ predicts a 0.083 rise in log-wage, i.e. ~8.3%.
  2. MLR.2 (random sampling): Plausible if workers were sampled at random; suspect if the survey only reached currently-employed people (selection).
  3. MLR.3 (no perfect collinearity): Holds — educ and exper are correlated but not perfectly. If you also added "age = educ + exper + 6" exactly, OLS would break.
  4. MLR.4 (zero conditional mean): Likely violated. Unobserved ability sits in u and is positively correlated with educ. So E[u | educ] > 0 for high-educ workers, biasing b1-hat upward — the 8.3% mixes the return to schooling with the return to ability.
  5. MLR.5 (homoskedasticity): Often violated for wages — the spread of log-wage tends to grow with education. OLS stays unbiased, but report heteroskedasticity-robust standard errors.
  6. Verdict: Because MLR.4 is shaky, b1-hat = 0.083 is a biased causal estimate. It is still a valid descriptive association; turning it causal needs an instrument or a natural experiment (later courses).

🛠️ Hands-On: a Monte Carlo demonstration of unbiasedness

We use the same free stack from Week 1/2 — numpy, pandas, statsmodels, and pandas-datareader for FRED. The full one-time install lives in the Week 1 lecture's setup box; nothing new is needed here. We will build a world where the assumptions hold and watch OLS center on the truth.

# gauss_markov_demo.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np
import statsmodels.api as sm

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
TRUE_B0, TRUE_B1 = 2.0, 0.5     # the truth we are trying to recover

def one_sample(n=100):
    x = rng.normal(5, 2, n)
    u = rng.normal(0, 1, n)     # MLR.4 & MLR.5: mean 0, constant variance, indep of x
    y = TRUE_B0 + TRUE_B1 * x + u
    X = sm.add_constant(x)      # adds the intercept column
    return sm.OLS(y, X).fit().params   # [b0_hat, b1_hat]

estimates = np.array([one_sample() for _ in range(5000)])
print("mean b1_hat:", estimates[:, 1].mean())   # ~0.50  -> unbiased
print("std  b1_hat:", estimates[:, 1].std())     # the sampling spread Gauss-Markov minimizes

The mean of 5000 slope estimates lands on 0.50 — that is unbiasedness, not luck. Now break MLR.4: replace the error with u = rng.normal(0,1,n) + 0.7*x (error correlated with x) and rerun. The mean b1_hat jumps well above 0.50 — bias, exactly as the theory warns. Try real data too: pull two FRED series with pandas_datareader (e.g. inflation and unemployment) and regress one on the other to see these assumptions in the wild.

Q1. Which assumption is the one that, when violated, makes OLS biased?
A. Homoskedasticity (constant error variance)
B. Zero conditional mean of the error, E[u | x] = 0
C. No perfect collinearity
D. Linearity in the variables
Show answer

B. Unbiasedness rests on MLR.1–4; the workhorse is zero conditional mean. Heteroskedasticity (A) only ruins efficiency and standard errors, not unbiasedness. Perfect collinearity (C) prevents computing OLS at all rather than biasing it. (D) is a misstatement — OLS needs linearity in parameters, not variables.

Q2. The model y = b0 + b1*x + b2*x^2 + u is…
A. not estimable by OLS because it is nonlinear
B. a violation of the no-perfect-collinearity assumption
C. estimable only after taking logs of both sides
D. perfectly fine for OLS — it is linear in the parameters
Show answer

D. MLR.1 requires linearity in the parameters (the b's), not the variables; x^2 is just another regressor. (A) confuses curve shape with parameter form. (B) is wrong — x and x^2 are not a perfect linear relationship. (C) is unnecessary.

Q3. "OLS is unbiased" most precisely means…
A. your single estimate equals the true parameter
B. the estimate has the smallest possible variance
C. the average of the estimator over many random samples equals the true parameter
D. the residuals always sum to zero
Show answer

C. Unbiasedness is a property of the sampling distribution: E[b-hat] = b. (A) is false — any one sample can be off. (B) describes efficiency/"Best," not unbiasedness. (D) is a mechanical algebra fact of OLS with an intercept, unrelated to bias.

Q4. The "Best" in "Best Linear Unbiased Estimator" specifically requires which assumption?
A. Homoskedasticity (MLR.5)
B. Random sampling (MLR.2)
C. Normality of the errors
D. Linearity in parameters (MLR.1)
Show answer

A. "Best" = minimum variance among linear unbiased estimators, which Gauss-Markov derives using homoskedasticity (MLR.5). Drop it and OLS need not be efficient. (B) and (D) are needed for unbiasedness, not the minimum-variance claim. (C) is not part of Gauss-Markov at all — it is the extra assumption for exact inference.

Q5. If the error variance grows with x (heteroskedasticity) but all other CLM assumptions hold, then OLS is…
A. biased, and its standard errors are correct
B. impossible to compute
C. still unbiased, but no longer the minimum-variance linear estimator and its usual standard errors are wrong
D. identical in every way to the homoskedastic case
Show answer

C. Heteroskedasticity violates only MLR.5, so unbiasedness (MLR.1–4) survives, but the "Best" claim and the textbook standard errors do not — hence robust standard errors. (A) reverses the truth. (B) is false — OLS still computes fine. (D) ignores the broken efficiency and standard errors.

  • Stated the five CLM assumptions: linear in parameters, random sampling, no perfect collinearity, zero conditional mean, and homoskedasticity.
  • Separated linear in parameters from linear in variables — x^2 and logs are fine for OLS.
  • Defined unbiasedness as E[b-hat] = b, a property of the sampling distribution over many samples.
  • Identified zero conditional mean (MLR.4) as the assumption that makes a coefficient causal rather than merely correlational.
