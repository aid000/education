What Econometrics Does & Working with Data

Economic theory tells you what should happen; econometrics is how you measure whether it actually did — and by how much.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what econometrics is and why theory alone can't put numbers on economic relationships.
  • Distinguish correlation from causation and name what can break the link.
  • Tell apart cross-sectional, time-series, and panel data, and give an example of each.
  • Define the population regression function and the conditional expectation it represents.
  • Load real public data with pandas and FRED and describe what "fitting a line" to it means.

1.1What Econometrics Is — and Why You Need It

Economic theory is full of statements like "raising the minimum wage reduces employment" or "more schooling increases earnings." Those are qualitative claims about direction. But a policymaker has to decide between a $1 and a $2 increase, and a worker wants to know whether a fourth year of college is worth the tuition. Direction is not enough — you need a number, and you need to know how much to trust it. Econometrics is the discipline of using statistical methods on economic data to estimate those numbers and quantify the uncertainty around them.

Formally, econometrics turns a vague relationship into a model. If y is earnings and x is years of schooling, we hypothesize a relationship and then ask the data to fill in the slope:

y = b0 + b1 * x + ub1 is the effect we want to measure (extra earnings per year of school); u is everything else affecting y that we did not include.

The term u — the error term — is the heart of the subject. It holds ability, luck, family background, and measurement noise. Almost every hard question in this course is really a question about what is hiding inside u and whether it is tangled up with the x we care about.

AnalogyTheory is the map; data is the territory. A map tells you a road generally heads north. Econometrics is the GPS that measures your actual bearing and tells you the margin of error — "heading 11° east of north, give or take 3°." Without the measurement, you only have the direction, not the distance or the confidence.

1.2Correlation Is Not Causation

The first thing data shows you is correlation: when one variable is high, the other tends to be high (positive) or low (negative). The sample correlation coefficient summarizes the strength of a linear association:

r = cov(x, y) / ( sd(x) * sd(y) )r ranges from -1 to +1. r = 0 means no linear association; it does NOT mean the variables are unrelated.

But correlation answers "do they move together?" — not "does x cause y?" Ice-cream sales and drownings rise together every summer; neither causes the other. A hidden third variable (hot weather) drives both. Such a variable is a confounder, and it is the classic reason a correlation lies about causation. The other two classic traps are reverse causation (y actually drives x) and selection (who ends up in the data is not random).

This is why the error term matters. If the things hiding in u are correlated with x, then the slope we estimate mixes the true effect of x with the effect of the confounder. The entire arc of this course is a sequence of tools — controls, panel data, instruments, experiments — for prying causation out of correlation.

Common mistakeReading a regression slope as a causal effect by default. A slope is just the best-fitting line through the data — a description of correlation. It only becomes a causal effect when you can argue the error term u is unrelated to x. Writing "a 1-year increase in schooling causes a 9% rise in wages" from a raw regression — when ability sits in u and drives both — is the original sin of applied work.

1.3Three Kinds of Data

Before any model, you must know the shape of your data, because it determines which methods are valid. Economic data comes in three structures:

Cross-sectional

Many units (people, firms, countries) observed at a single point in time. Example: the income, age, and education of 5,000 households surveyed this year. Order doesn't matter; the key assumption is that the units are a random sample from a population.

Time-series

One unit observed repeatedly over time. Example: quarterly U.S. GDP from 1960 to today. Order is everything — today depends on yesterday, so observations are not independent, which breaks the simplest assumptions and needs its own toolkit (Course C4).

Panel (longitudinal)

Many units observed over multiple time periods — the best of both. Example: 50 states' unemployment and policy each year for 20 years. Panels let you control for stable, unobserved differences between units (a state's culture, a person's innate ability), which is a powerful weapon against confounding.

1.4The Population Regression Function

Now make "fit a line" precise. Imagine the entire population of workers. For any fixed level of schooling x, there is a whole distribution of earnings — some lucky, some not. The single most natural summary is the average earnings among workers with that much schooling. As x varies, that average traces out a curve called the population regression function (PRF) — the conditional expectation of y given x:

E[ y | x ] = b0 + b1 * xThe PRF reads: the AVERAGE of y, among all population members who share value x, is a straight line in x.

The slope b1 answers: if I compare two groups one year of schooling apart, how much higher is their average earnings? An individual is then their group average plus a personal deviation, which is exactly the error term:

y = E[y | x] + u = b0 + b1 * x + u, with E[u | x] = 0The key assumption E[u|x]=0 says the unobserved part averages to zero at every x — nothing systematic is left out.

We never see the whole population — only a sample. So we cannot compute the PRF directly; we estimate it, producing a fitted line with estimated coefficients b0-hat and b1-hat. The gap between the true PRF and our estimate is what statistical inference is about. Next week's method, ordinary least squares, is the workhorse for producing that estimate.

✦ Worked Example — reading a slope and a correlation

A small survey of 5 workers gives years of schooling x and hourly wage y (dollars): (12, 18), (14, 22), (16, 28), (16, 26), (18, 36). Compute the means, the slope of the best-fit line, and interpret it.

  1. Means: x-bar = (12+14+16+16+18)/5 = 76/5 = 15.2 years. y-bar = (18+22+28+26+36)/5 = 130/5 = 26.0 dollars.
  2. Deviations & products. For each point compute (x - x-bar) and (y - y-bar): (-3.2, -8), (-1.2, -4), (0.8, 2), (0.8, 0), (2.8, 10).
  3. Cross-products sum: (-3.2)(-8) + (-1.2)(-4) + (0.8)(2) + (0.8)(0) + (2.8)(10) = 25.6 + 4.8 + 1.6 + 0 + 28 = 60.0.
  4. Sum of squared x-deviations: 10.24 + 1.44 + 0.64 + 0.64 + 7.84 = 20.8.
  5. Slope: b1-hat = 60.0 / 20.8 ≈ 2.88. Intercept: b0-hat = 26.0 - 2.88 × 15.2 ≈ -17.9.
  6. Interpretation: on average, one more year of schooling is associated with about $2.88 higher hourly wage in this sample. "Associated with," not "causes" — ability and family background still sit in u (Section 1.2). You will reproduce this exact slope automatically with OLS next week.

🛠️ Hands-On: pull real data from FRED with pandas

This course runs entirely on free, open tools and public data. Week 1 setup: install the stack once (below), then reuse it for every lecture and the final project. FRED is the free public economic database we will use all term.

⬇ Install the econometrics stack (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the packages:

python3 -m venv econ-env
source econ-env/bin/activate         # macOS / Linux
econ-env\Scripts\activate            # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install pandas numpy statsmodels pandas-datareader matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import pandas, numpy, statsmodels; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

Now download two real U.S. time series from FRED, line them up, and look at their correlation. No API key is required for these public series.

# intro_data.py - Lecture 1
import pandas as pd
import numpy as np
import pandas_datareader.data as web

# Pull two public FRED series: real GDP and the unemployment rate
start, end = "1990-01-01", "2023-12-31"
gdp = web.DataReader("GDPC1", "fred", start, end)   # quarterly real GDP
unemp = web.DataReader("UNRATE", "fred", start, end) # monthly unemployment %

# Align to quarterly frequency and join (this is TIME-SERIES data)
unemp_q = unemp.resample("QS").mean()
df = pd.concat([gdp, unemp_q], axis=1).dropna()
df.columns = ["gdp", "unemp"]

# Growth in GDP vs change in unemployment - a first look at correlation
df["gdp_growth"] = df["gdp"].pct_change() * 100
print(df.head())
print("correlation:", df[["gdp_growth", "unemp"]].corr().iloc[0, 1].round(3))

You'll see a negative correlation: quarters with stronger GDP growth tend to have lower unemployment — the empirical shadow of Okun's law. But remember Section 1.2: correlation here is suggestive, not proof of a causal mechanism. Try swapping in a cross-sectional dataset to feel the difference in structure.

🔗 Where this is heading

Right now we can only describe associations. Week 2 makes the fitted line rigorous with ordinary least squares (OLS) and its assumptions. ❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The error term u in the model y = b0 + b1·x + u represents…
A. a mistake the researcher made in arithmetic
B. everything affecting y that is not captured by x
C. the slope of the regression line
D. the correlation between x and y
Show answer

B. u collects all other influences on y (ability, luck, omitted factors, noise). It is not an arithmetic error (A), it is not the slope b1 (C), and it is not a correlation coefficient (D).

Q2. Ice-cream sales and drownings are strongly correlated. The best explanation is…
A. eating ice cream causes people to drown
B. drownings cause people to buy ice cream
C. a confounder (hot weather) drives both
D. the correlation must be a calculation error
Show answer

C. Hot weather raises both ice-cream sales and swimming (hence drownings). The correlation is real, so it is not an error (D); the direct causal stories (A, B) are spurious because of the omitted third variable.

Q3. Annual GDP and unemployment for a single country observed from 1960 to 2023 is an example of…
A. time-series data
B. cross-sectional data
C. panel data
D. experimental data
Show answer

A. One unit (the country) observed repeatedly over time is time-series. Cross-section (B) is many units at one time; panel (C) is many units over many periods; nothing here is a controlled experiment (D).

Q4. The population regression function E[y | x] = b0 + b1·x is best described as…
A. the value of y for every individual with schooling x
B. the average of y among all population members who share value x
C. a guaranteed causal effect of x on y
D. the correlation between x and y rescaled
Show answer

B. The PRF is a conditional expectation — the average y at each x, not each individual's value (A). It is causal only under extra assumptions (C), and it is a conditional mean, not a rescaled correlation (D).

Q5. A regression of wages on schooling gives a slope of 2.88. Without further assumptions, the safest interpretation is…
A. each extra year of schooling causes wages to rise by 2.88
B. schooling and wages are unrelated
C. the slope proves there is no confounding
D. in the sample, an extra year of schooling is associated with about 2.88 higher wages
Show answer

D. A raw slope describes association, not causation, so the causal claim (A) is unwarranted while ability sits in u. A nonzero slope means they are related (B is wrong), and the slope itself says nothing about whether confounding is absent (C).

✓ What you learned

  • Econometrics puts numbers and uncertainty on economic relationships that theory states only as directions.
  • The error term u holds everything left out of the model — and is where confounding hides.
  • Correlation is not causation: confounders, reverse causation, and selection can all break the link.
  • Data comes as cross-sectional, time-series, or panel, and the structure dictates the method.
  • The population regression function is the conditional mean E[y|x]; fitting a line estimates it from a sample, which you pulled live from FRED with pandas.
