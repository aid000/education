Week 3 · Lecture 3

Multiple Regression

The real world has many moving parts at once — multiple regression is how you hold all but one of them still and read off the effect of the variable you care about.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Extend simple OLS to several regressors and write the model in scalar and matrix form.
  • Interpret a slope coefficient as a ceteris-paribus partial effect — the effect of one variable holding the others fixed.
  • Diagnose omitted-variable bias and predict its sign from two simple rules.
  • Recognize multicollinearity and explain what it does (and does not) do to your estimates.
  • Read a real multiple-regression output table — coefficients, standard errors, t-stats, and R².

3.1From One Regressor to Many

Last week's simple regression fit wages on education alone. But wages also depend on experience, and people with more education tend to have different amounts of experience — so a one-variable line confounds the two. Multiple regression fixes this by letting the data carry several explanatory variables at once:

y = β0 + β1x1 + β2x2 + ... + βkxk + uOne dependent variable y, k regressors, an intercept β0, and an unobserved error u.

Stacking the n observations, the same model is written compactly in matrix form, which is how the computer actually solves it:

y = Xβ + u    →    β̂ = (X'X)-1X'yX is the n×(k+1) matrix of regressors (a column of 1s plus the x's). OLS still minimizes the sum of squared residuals; the closed-form solution just generalizes the one-variable formula.

The principle is unchanged from Week 2: choose the coefficients that minimize Σ(yi − ŷi)². What changes is the meaning of each slope — and that is the heart of this lecture.

3.2Partial Effects: the Ceteris-Paribus Coefficient

In the simple model, β1 was "the change in y per unit of x." In multiple regression each slope answers a sharper question. The coefficient βj is the partial effect of xj:

βj = ∂E[y | x] / ∂xjThe expected change in y for a one-unit rise in xj, holding all other regressors fixed — the econometric meaning of "ceteris paribus."

So in a wage regression on education and experience, βeducation is the return to one more year of schooling among workers with the same experience. The regression "controls for" experience automatically. There is a beautiful mechanical fact behind this, the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem: βj equals the simple-regression slope of y on the part of xj that is left over after the other regressors have been used to predict xj. In words, the coefficient uses only the variation in xj that is unrelated to the controls.

AnalogyThink of a recipe where you want to know how much an extra egg changes the cake — but cooks who add more eggs also tend to add more sugar. Multiple regression is like running a controlled kitchen: it compares cakes that had the same sugar but differed in eggs. It mathematically "partials out" the sugar so the egg coefficient reflects eggs alone, not the sugar that rode along with them.

3.3Omitted-Variable Bias

What happens if you leave out a relevant variable? Suppose the true model is y = β0 + β1x1 + β2x2 + u but you regress y on x1 only. The estimate β̃1 then absorbs part of x2's effect. The bias has a clean formula:

E[β̃1] = β1 + β2 · δwhere δ is the slope of regressing the omitted x2 on the included x1. Bias = (effect of the omitted variable) × (its correlation with the included one).

Two things must both be true for bias: the omitted variable must affect y (β2 ≠ 0) and be correlated with the included regressor (δ ≠ 0). The sign of the bias is the product of the two signs:

The classic case: regress wages on education while omitting innate ability. Ability raises wages (β2 > 0) and is positively correlated with schooling (δ > 0), so the simple-regression return to education is biased upward — part of the "education effect" is really an ability effect riding along.

Common mistakeThrowing in every variable you can find is not a cure. A control belongs in the model only if it is a genuine confounder — correlated with both y and your regressor of interest. Adding variables that are themselves caused by your regressor (so-called "bad controls," like adding occupation when studying the wage return to education) can create bias rather than remove it. More variables is not automatically more correct.

3.4Multicollinearity & Reading the Output

OLS needs the regressors to carry distinct information. Multicollinearity means two or more regressors are highly correlated, so the data struggle to separate their individual effects. With perfect collinearity (e.g., including both "years of experience" and "age minus years of schooling minus 6," which are identical), X'X is singular and there is no unique solution at all. The realistic case is near-collinearity, summarized by the variance inflation factor:

VIFj = 1 / (1 - Rj²)Rj² is from regressing xj on all the other regressors. A VIF of 10 means xj's standard error is √10 ≈ 3.2× larger than if it were uncorrelated with the others.

The crucial point: multicollinearity inflates standard errors (estimates get imprecise, t-stats fall, coefficients can swing wildly when you add a variable) but it does not bias them — OLS stays unbiased. It is a "not enough independent variation" problem, not a "wrong answer" problem. A real regression output table organizes all of this:

Read every coefficient as "effect of this variable, holding the others fixed," check the t-stat for significance, and read R² as the share of y's variance the model explains together — never as proof any single variable is causal.

✦ Worked Example — predicting omitted-variable bias by hand

A researcher regresses test scores on hours studied and finds a slope of 5.0 points/hour. You suspect family income was wrongly omitted: it raises scores, and richer families' kids also study more. Using the simple two-variable bias rule, predict the direction of the bias and what the true study effect likely is.

  1. True model: score = β0 + β1(hours) + β2(income) + u, but income was omitted.
  2. Sign of β2: income raises scores → β2 > 0.
  3. Sign of δ: regress the omitted income on included hours; richer kids study more → δ > 0.
  4. Bias = β2 · δ = (positive)(positive) = positive. The 5.0 estimate is biased upward.
  5. Numbers: suppose β2 = 0.4 points per $1k of income and δ = 6 ($1k income per study-hour). Bias ≈ 0.4 × 6 = 2.4. So the true study effect is roughly 5.0 − 2.4 = 2.6 points/hour — the naive regression overstated it by nearly double.

🛠️ Hands-On: multiple regression in statsmodels

We use the course's free stack — pandas, numpy, statsmodels, and pandas-datareader for live FRED data. The full one-time install lives in the Week 1 / Week 2 setup (the install <details> in those lectures); reuse that same environment here. Below we pull three macro series from FRED and fit a multiple regression, then watch a coefficient move as we add a control.

# multiple_regression.py — Lecture 3
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
import pandas_datareader.data as web

# Pull three monthly FRED series (free, public): 
# UNRATE = unemployment rate, CPIAUCSL = CPI, FEDFUNDS = policy rate
start = "2000-01-01"
df = pd.concat([
    web.DataReader("UNRATE",   "fred", start),
    web.DataReader("CPIAUCSL", "fred", start),
    web.DataReader("FEDFUNDS", "fred", start),
], axis=1).dropna()
df["infl"] = df["CPIAUCSL"].pct_change(12) * 100   # yr-over-yr inflation %
df = df.dropna()

# Simple regression: policy rate on inflation alone
m1 = smf.ols("FEDFUNDS ~ infl", data=df).fit()

# Multiple regression: ADD unemployment as a control
m2 = smf.ols("FEDFUNDS ~ infl + UNRATE", data=df).fit()

print(m2.summary())                       # full table: coef, std err, t, R^2
print("infl coef alone :", round(m1.params["infl"], 3))
print("infl coef w/ ctrl:", round(m2.params["infl"], 3))   # watch it shift

The infl coefficient is now the partial effect of inflation on the policy rate holding unemployment fixed — a crude empirical Taylor rule. Compare m1 and m2: the change is omitted-variable bias in action. Add a VIF check with statsmodels.stats.outliers_influence.variance_inflation_factor to gauge how correlated infl and UNRATE are.

💬 Discussion

A policy analyst regresses individual health outcomes on the number of doctor visits per year and finds a positive coefficient — more visits, worse health. Taken at face value, it suggests "going to the doctor harms you." Using the language of partial effects and omitted-variable bias, explain what is likely going wrong, name a specific omitted variable, predict the sign of the bias it creates, and propose one concrete way (a control or a different design) to get closer to the true causal effect.

Show a model response

The regression omits underlying sickness / baseline health severity. Sicker people both visit the doctor more (so it is correlated with the regressor) and have worse outcomes for reasons unrelated to the visits (so it affects y). Using the sign rule: the omitted "severity" has a negative effect on good-health outcomes (β2 < 0) and a positive correlation with visits (δ > 0), so the bias is β2·δ < 0 on the health scale — it drags the visits coefficient downward (toward "visits look harmful"), masking or reversing any true benefit. This is classic confounding by indication, a reverse-causality flavored omitted-variable problem.

Fixes, from cheapest to strongest: (1) control for baseline severity — prior diagnoses, a pre-period health index, comorbidities — so the coefficient compares equally-sick patients; (2) use a panel with individual fixed effects to difference out each person's stable health type; (3) best of all, find an instrument or natural experiment that shifts visits for reasons unrelated to health (e.g., a co-pay change), the instrumental-variables idea coming later in the course.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer:

  • Names a plausible omitted confounder (baseline severity), not just "other stuff."
  • Argues it is correlated with both the regressor and the outcome.
  • Applies the sign rule (β2·δ) to predict the bias direction.
  • Distinguishes a usable fix (control / fixed effects / IV) from "just add every variable."

❓ Lecture 3 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a multiple regression of wage on education and experience, the coefficient on education is best interpreted as…
A. the total correlation between education and wage
B. the change in wage per year of education, holding experience fixed
C. the average wage of educated workers
D. the change in wage when both education and experience rise together
Show answer

B. Each slope is a partial (ceteris-paribus) effect: it isolates education's effect among workers with the same experience. (A) is the raw, uncontrolled relationship; (C) is a mean, not a slope; (D) describes changing two variables at once, which a partial effect deliberately does not do.

Q2. For omitting a variable to bias your OLS estimate, the omitted variable must be…
A. measured on the same scale as y
B. statistically significant in some other study
C. both a determinant of y and correlated with an included regressor
D. normally distributed
Show answer

C. Bias = β2·δ requires both β2 ≠ 0 (it affects y) and δ ≠ 0 (it correlates with the included regressor). If either is zero, omitting it causes no bias. Scale (A), other studies (B), and distribution (D) are irrelevant to whether bias arises.

Q3. A variable that raises y (β2 > 0) is omitted, and it is negatively correlated with the included regressor (δ < 0). The bias on the included coefficient is…
A. negative (downward)
B. positive (upward)
C. zero
D. impossible to determine without the data
Show answer

A. Sign of bias = sign(β2)×sign(δ) = (+)×(−) = negative. (B) would need matching signs; (C) needs β2 or δ to be zero; (D) is wrong because the sign rule gives a definite answer once you know both signs.

Q4. High (but not perfect) multicollinearity among regressors primarily causes…
A. biased coefficient estimates
B. a violation of the OLS minimization itself
C. R² to fall to zero
D. large standard errors and imprecise, unstable estimates
Show answer

D. Collinearity inflates variances (high VIF), so estimates are imprecise and can swing when variables are added — but they remain unbiased, ruling out (A). OLS still solves fine unless collinearity is perfect (B), and overall fit/R² can still be high (C).

Q5. In a regression output, the t-statistic reported for a coefficient is computed as…
A. coefficient divided by its standard error
B. coefficient times R²
C. standard error divided by the coefficient
D. the coefficient minus the intercept
Show answer

A. t = coef / std err tests whether the coefficient differs from zero; |t| > about 2 signals significance at 5%. (C) is the reciprocal; (B) and (D) are not how the test statistic is built.
