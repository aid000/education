Inference: Standard Errors & t-tests

A regression coefficient is just one number from one sample — inference tells you how much to trust it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the OLS estimator has a sampling distribution — and what its center and spread mean.
  • Define the standard error of a coefficient and name the three things that shrink it.
  • Build the t-statistic and read it as "estimate measured in standard-error units."
  • Construct and interpret a confidence interval for a coefficient.
  • Read every column of a statsmodels regression coefficient table and say what it means.

5.1The Coefficient Is a Random Variable

In Week 3 you computed an OLS slope β̂ by minimizing squared residuals, and in Week 4 the Gauss-Markov assumptions told you it was the best linear unbiased estimator. But step back: that number came from one particular sample. Draw a different sample of the same population — a different set of households, firms, or years — and the regression would spit out a slightly different number. The estimate β̂ is therefore not a fixed quantity; it is a random variable with a distribution called the sampling distribution.

Under the classical assumptions, that distribution has two properties we care about. It is centered on the truth (unbiasedness: its mean equals the real population parameter β), and it has a spread that measures how much the estimate would bounce around from sample to sample. Inference is entirely about that spread: a coefficient is only useful if we know how precisely it is pinned down.

Var(β̂_j) = σ² / [ SST_j · (1 - R²_j) ]Sampling variance of the j-th OLS slope. σ² is the error variance; SST_j is the total variation in regressor x_j; R²_j is from regressing x_j on the other regressors.

Read that formula as a story about precision. The variance — and therefore the uncertainty — shrinks when the error variance σ² is small (the model fits tightly), when SST_j is large (the regressor varies a lot, so it has lots of "leverage"), and when R²_j is small (the regressor is not nearly a copy of the others — low collinearity). Those are the three levers behind every standard error you will ever read.

AnalogyEstimating β from one sample is like clocking a runner's speed from a single stopwatch read. The true speed is fixed, but your read jitters — reaction time, where you stood. The standard error is the spread you'd see if many people timed the same race: a wide spread means "don't trust the second decimal." More data, a cleaner stopwatch, and a longer track (more variation to measure against) all tighten the spread — exactly the three levers above.

5.2The Standard Error

We never observe the true error variance σ², so we estimate it from the residuals. The unbiased estimate divides the residual sum of squares by the degrees of freedom n − k − 1, where n is the sample size and k is the number of slope coefficients (the +1 is the intercept):

σ̂² = ( Σ û_i² ) / (n - k - 1)Estimated error variance. The denominator n - k - 1 is the residual degrees of freedom — observations minus parameters estimated.

Plug σ̂² into the variance formula and take the square root: that gives the standard error of the coefficient, se(β̂_j). It is, in plain words, our estimate of the typical sample-to-sample distance between β̂_j and the truth — the coefficient's measured precision, in the same units as the coefficient itself.

se(β̂_j) = √[ σ̂² / ( SST_j · (1 - R²_j) ) ]Standard error = estimated standard deviation of the sampling distribution of β̂_j. Reported in the second column of every regression table.

A coefficient of 4.0 with a standard error of 0.5 is sharply estimated; the same 4.0 with a standard error of 6.0 could plausibly be anything from negative to large positive — statistically indistinguishable from zero. The point estimate alone tells you almost nothing without its standard error beside it.

Common mistakeA standard error is not the standard deviation of your data, and it is not a measure of how big the effect is. It measures the precision of the estimate, not the size of the relationship. A huge coefficient with a huge standard error is weak evidence; a tiny coefficient with a tiny standard error can be rock-solid. Never judge importance from the coefficient alone.

5.3The t-statistic

To test the most common claim — "does this variable matter at all?", i.e. the null hypothesis H₀: β_j = 0 — we measure how far the estimate sits from zero in units of its own standard error. That ratio is the t-statistic:

t = ( β̂_j - 0 ) / se(β̂_j)How many standard errors the estimate lies away from the hypothesized value (here 0). The general form replaces 0 with any null value β_j,0.

Under the classical assumptions (and normal errors, or a large sample via the central limit theorem), this t-statistic follows a Student's t distribution with n − k − 1 degrees of freedom. The intuition is clean: if the true effect were zero, the estimate would scatter around zero by a few standard errors at most. A |t| of, say, 5 means the estimate is five standard errors from zero — wildly unlikely if the truth were really zero, so we reject the null. As a rule of thumb, |t| ≳ 2 corresponds to significance at roughly the 5% level for any reasonably large sample.

5.4Confidence Intervals & Reading the Table

A single number plus a standard error naturally produces a range of plausible values. The 95% confidence interval stretches a critical number of standard errors on each side of the estimate:

β̂_j ± t* · se(β̂_j)t* is the critical value from the t distribution (≈ 1.96 for large samples at 95%). The interval is the set of null values that would NOT be rejected.

The correct reading: if we repeated the sampling many times and built this interval each time, about 95% of those intervals would contain the true β. A practical shortcut connects it back to the t-test — if the 95% interval excludes zero, the coefficient is significant at the 5% level, because zero is then a value the data reject. The interval and the t-test are two views of the same evidence.

Now you can decode the heart of a regression printout. The coefficient table from statsmodels has one row per regressor and these columns:

Column by column: coef is β̂_j; std err is se(β̂_j); t is their ratio; P>|t| is the two-sided p-value (the probability of a |t| this large if β were truly zero); and the last two columns are the 95% confidence interval. Reading a regression output fluently is applied econometrics — every empirical paper, central-bank model, and policy memo rests on tables exactly like this one.

✦ Worked Example — from estimate to verdict

You regress monthly consumption growth on income growth using n = 124 monthly observations and one slope (k = 1). The output gives β̂ = 0.62 with se(β̂) = 0.10. Test H₀: β = 0 at the 5% level and build the 95% confidence interval.

  1. Degrees of freedom: n − k − 1 = 124 − 1 − 1 = 122.
  2. t-statistic: t = (0.62 − 0) / 0.10 = 6.2.
  3. Compare to critical value: with 122 df, the two-sided 5% critical value t* ≈ 1.98 (≈ 1.96 for large n). Since |6.2| ≫ 1.98, we reject H₀ — income growth significantly predicts consumption growth.
  4. p-value: a t of 6.2 with 122 df has a two-sided p-value far below 0.001, so statsmodels would print P>|t| = 0.000.
  5. 95% confidence interval: 0.62 ± 1.98 × 0.10 = 0.62 ± 0.198 = [0.42, 0.82]. The interval excludes 0, agreeing with the t-test, and says: a one-point rise in income growth is associated with a 0.42-to-0.82 point rise in consumption growth.

🛠️ Hands-On: standard errors & t-tests in statsmodels

We continue with the free course stack — statsmodels, pandas, pandas-datareader (FRED), and NumPy. If you set up the environment back in the Week 1/2 install step, you already have everything; no new tools this week. Here we pull two real FRED series, run a regression, and read inference straight off the summary — then verify the t-stat and interval by hand.

# inference.py — Lecture 5: standard errors, t-tests, CIs
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
from pandas_datareader import data as web

# Pull two real FRED series: personal consumption & disposable income
start, end = "1990-01-01", "2023-12-01"
pce = web.DataReader("PCEC96", "fred", start, end)   # real consumption
inc = web.DataReader("DPIC96", "fred", start, end)   # real disposable income

df = pd.concat([pce, inc], axis=1).dropna()
df.columns = ["cons", "inc"]
df = df.pct_change().dropna() * 100         # growth rates, in percent

X = sm.add_constant(df["inc"])              # adds the intercept column
model = sm.OLS(df["cons"], X).fit()
print(model.summary())                      # full table: coef, std err, t, P>|t|, CI

# Pull the pieces out programmatically and reconstruct inference by hand
b   = model.params["inc"]                   # coefficient
se  = model.bse["inc"]                      # standard error
t   = b / se                                # t-statistic vs H0: beta = 0
ci  = model.conf_int().loc["inc"]           # 95% confidence interval
print(f"beta={b:.3f}  se={se:.3f}  t={t:.2f}")
print(f"95% CI = [{ci[0]:.3f}, {ci[1]:.3f}]")
print("statsmodels t:", model.tvalues["inc"], " p:", model.pvalues["inc"])

Check that your hand-computed t = b/se matches model.tvalues, and that the printed CI excludes zero. Then experiment: shorten the sample (smaller n raises the standard error) and watch the t-statistic shrink and the interval widen — the precision levers from §5.1 in action.

🔗 Where this is heading

The t-test answers "does one coefficient matter?" Next week we test several at once — joint restrictions like "do all of these variables together do nothing?" — with the F-test (Week 6), which generalizes the t-test exactly as the variance generalizes a single deviation. Later you'll learn that the textbook standard error above assumes homoskedastic, uncorrelated errors; when that fails (it usually does with real economic data), you'll swap in robust and clustered standard errors — the same t-statistics, computed from a sturdier variance estimate. Every causal-inference and time-series method later in this track ultimately reports a coefficient, a standard error, and a t-stat exactly like the ones here.

❓ Lecture 5 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The standard error of a coefficient measures…
A. how large the estimated effect is
B. the spread of the estimator's sampling distribution — its precision
C. the standard deviation of the dependent variable
D. the probability that the coefficient is zero
Show answer

B. The standard error is the estimated standard deviation of β̂ across repeated samples — a precision measure. (A) confuses precision with effect size; (C) is the data's spread, not the estimate's; (D) describes a p-value, not a standard error.

Q2. Which change would decrease the standard error of a slope coefficient?
A. more variation in the regressor (larger SST_j)
B. a smaller sample size
C. higher collinearity (larger R²_j)
D. a larger error variance σ²
Show answer

A. se = √[σ̂²/(SST_j(1−R²_j))], so larger SST_j shrinks it. (B) raises it via fewer observations and df; (C) raises it because (1−R²_j) gets smaller; (D) raises it directly through the numerator.

Q3. A coefficient is 4.0 with a standard error of 0.5. Its t-statistic for H₀: β = 0 is…
A. 0.125
B. 2.0
C. 8.0
D. 4.5
Show answer

C. t = (4.0 − 0)/0.5 = 8.0 — the estimate sits eight standard errors from zero, so we reject the null decisively. (A) inverts the ratio; (B) and (D) are arithmetic mismatches.

Q4. In a statsmodels table, the column P>|t| reports…
A. the t-statistic itself
B. the width of the confidence interval
C. the coefficient divided by the sample size
D. the two-sided p-value — the chance of a |t| this large if β truly equaled zero
Show answer

D. P>|t| is the two-sided p-value for H₀: β = 0. (A) is the separate t column; (B) is read from the [0.025, 0.975] columns; (C) is not a quantity statsmodels reports.

Q5. A 95% confidence interval for a coefficient is [0.42, 0.82]. What can you conclude at the 5% level?
A. The coefficient is significantly different from zero, since the interval excludes 0
B. The coefficient is not significant, because the interval is narrow
C. There is a 95% probability the estimate equals 0.62
D. The true β must lie exactly at the midpoint
Show answer

A. An interval excluding 0 means 0 is a rejected null at the 5% level, so the coefficient is significant. (B) reverses the logic; (C) misstates the frequentist meaning — the interval, not the point, is random; (D) the true β need not be the midpoint at all.

✓ What you learned

  • The OLS estimate is a random variable with a sampling distribution centered on the truth and spread by its variance.
  • The standard error measures that spread; it shrinks with smaller σ², more regressor variation, and lower collinearity.
  • The t-statistic = β̂ / se measures the estimate in standard-error units; |t| ≳ 2 signals significance for large samples.
  • A confidence interval β̂ ± t*·se gives plausible values; excluding zero means significance at that level.
  • You can read every column of a statsmodels coefficient table — coef, std err, t, P>|t|, and the 95% interval.
