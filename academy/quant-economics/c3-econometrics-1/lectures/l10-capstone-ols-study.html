10.1From a Question to a Specification

The capstone is due this week, and it is the payoff for the whole course: a single end-to-end OLS study on data you pull yourself. Every regression starts not with code but with a question. "Do higher interest rates slow real consumption?" "Does a stock's excess return move one-for-one with the market?" The question fixes your dependent variable y and your candidate regressors x.

A specification is a deliberate set of choices: which variables enter, in what units, and in what functional form. Economic theory and units drive those choices. Variables that grow multiplicatively (prices, GDP, wages) usually enter in logs, so coefficients read as elasticities or growth rates:

y_i = β0 + β1·x_i1 + β2·x_i2 + ... + βk·x_ik + u_iThe population model. OLS estimates the β's by minimizing the sum of squared residuals. Each β_j is the effect of x_j holding the other regressors fixed.

For a log-log specification, β1 is an elasticity: a 1% rise in x is associated with a β1% change in y. For a log-level specification (log y on level x), 100·β1 is the approximate percent change in y per one-unit rise in x. Getting the form right before you estimate is half the battle — it is what makes the number you report mean something.

AnalogyThink of the capstone like a medical work-up, not a single thermometer reading. The point estimate is the patient's temperature — useful, but not the diagnosis. The diagnostic battery is the full panel of tests that tells you whether to trust the reading or send it back. A doctor who reports only the temperature and ignores every other test is doing exactly what a sloppy regression write-up does.

10.2The Diagnostic Battery

OLS coefficients are unbiased under the zero-conditional-mean assumption alone, but the standard errors printed by default rely on two extra assumptions: homoskedasticity (constant error variance) and no autocorrelation (uncorrelated errors). If those fail, your β's are still fine but your t-stats, p-values, and confidence intervals are wrong. The battery checks each assumption in turn.

Heteroskedasticity

The error variance should not depend on the regressors. The Breusch–Pagan and White tests regress the squared residuals on the x's and ask whether they explain any variation. The Breusch–Pagan statistic is built from the auxiliary regression's fit:

LM = n · R²_aux ~ χ²(k)Regress squared residuals on the regressors; n·R² of that auxiliary regression is chi-square with k degrees of freedom under the null of homoskedasticity. Big LM (small p) -> heteroskedasticity.

Autocorrelation

With time-series data the errors in adjacent periods are often correlated. The Durbin–Watson statistic screens for first-order autocorrelation, and the Breusch–Godfrey test generalizes to higher orders. Durbin–Watson sits near 2 when errors are clean:

DW = Σ(e_t − e_{t-1})² / Σ e_t² ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂)e_t are the residuals and ρ̂ is the estimated first-order residual correlation. DW near 2 -> no autocorrelation; DW near 0 -> strong positive autocorrelation.

Functional form

Ramsey's RESET test adds powers of the fitted values back into the regression; if they are jointly significant, your linear form is missing curvature or an omitted nonlinear term. It is a cheap guard against fitting a straight line through a clearly bent relationship.

Common mistakeHeteroskedasticity and autocorrelation do not bias your slope estimates — a huge number of students "fix" a heteroskedasticity test by throwing away or transforming the coefficient. They bias only the standard errors. The correct response is to keep the OLS point estimates and switch to robust or HAC standard errors, not to discard the estimate.

10.3Robust and HAC Standard Errors

When a diagnostic flags a problem, the repair is usually a different variance estimator, not a different model. Heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC) standard errors — White / Huber, called HC1–HC3 in software — give valid inference even when the error variance varies, without you having to model how it varies.

Var_hat(β_hat) = (X'X)⁻¹ ( Σ x_i x_i' e_i² ) (X'X)⁻¹The "sandwich" estimator: the meat Σ x_i x_i' e_i² uses each squared residual instead of one common σ². This is what HC robust standard errors compute.

For time-series autocorrelation you need HAC (Newey–West) standard errors, which widen the sandwich's meat to include cross-products of nearby residuals up to a chosen lag. The point estimates never change — only the reported uncertainty does, and it almost always gets wider, which is the honest direction.

10.4Writing It Up Honestly — The Deliverable

The capstone deliverable is a short report, not a wall of output. A defensible write-up has four parts, and graders look for all four:

  • Question & specification: the economic question, the variables, and why the functional form fits.
  • Estimates: the coefficients with their chosen standard errors, the effect size in plain language, and a confidence interval.
  • Diagnostics: the battery results, which standard errors you used and why.
  • Caveats: omitted variables, possible endogeneity, sample limits — the threats to a causal reading.

The single most valuable habit is separating association from causation. A clean regression with robust errors still only delivers a partial correlation unless your identification argument is strong. Saying "a 1% increase in x is associated with a β1% change in y, holding the controls fixed" is honest; "x causes y" usually is not — and the caveats section is where you earn the reader's trust by saying so.

✦ Worked Example — interpreting a real diagnostic run

You regress monthly log real personal consumption on log real disposable income (both from FRED) over n = 240 months and get a slope β̂1 = 0.86 with classical SE = 0.02. Durbin–Watson = 0.7, and Breusch–Pagan gives LM = 11.4 with p = 0.001. Decide what to report.

  1. Read the slope: in a log-log model β̂1 is an elasticity — a 1% rise in disposable income is associated with a 0.86% rise in consumption (consistent with a marginal propensity to consume below one).
  2. Heteroskedasticity: BP p = 0.001 < 0.05 -> reject homoskedasticity. The classical SE of 0.02 is not trustworthy on its own.
  3. Autocorrelation: DW = 0.7 is far below 2, signalling strong positive autocorrelation — unsurprising for monthly macro series.
  4. Choose the error type: both heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation are present, so neither plain classical nor HC-only errors suffice. Use HAC (Newey–West) standard errors, which handle both.
  5. Report honestly: "β̂1 = 0.86 (Newey–West SE ≈ 0.05; 95% CI ≈ [0.76, 0.96]). Estimate unchanged from OLS; the wider interval reflects autocorrelated, heteroskedastic macro errors. Causality is not claimed — income and consumption are jointly determined."

🛠️ Hands-On: the full study in Python

This is the capstone in code: pull FRED data with pandas-datareader, fit OLS with statsmodels, run the diagnostic battery, and re-fit with HAC errors. The Week-1/2 install details already covers statsmodels, pandas, and numpy; the only new piece is pandas-datareader for the FRED pull.

⬇ New tool this week: pandas-datareader (FRED access)

Activate the Week-1 environment, then add the one new package:

source econ-env/bin/activate        # the env from Week 1
pip install pandas-datareader

Verify: python -c "import pandas_datareader as pdr; print('ok')" — FRED is a free public source and needs no API key.

# capstone_ols.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
from statsmodels.stats.diagnostic import het_breuschpagan, acorr_breusch_godfrey, linear_reset
import pandas_datareader.data as web

# 1. pull real monthly FRED data: real consumption (PCEC96) and real disposable income (DSPIC96)
c = web.DataReader("PCEC96",  "fred", "2004-01-01", "2023-12-01")
y = web.DataReader("DSPIC96", "fred", "2004-01-01", "2023-12-01")
df = pd.concat([c, y], axis=1).dropna()
df.columns = ["cons", "inc"]
df["lcons"] = np.log(df["cons"])
df["linc"]  = np.log(df["inc"])

# 2. estimate by OLS (log-log -> slope is an elasticity)
m = smf.ols("lcons ~ linc", data=df).fit()
print(m.summary())                       # slope, R^2, classical SE, Durbin-Watson

# 3. diagnostic battery
bp = het_breuschpagan(m.resid, m.model.exog)   # (LM, LM_p, F, F_p)
bg = acorr_breusch_godfrey(m, nlags=2)          # autocorrelation, higher order
reset = linear_reset(m, power=2, use_f=True)    # functional form
print("Breusch-Pagan p =", round(bp[1], 4))
print("Breusch-Godfrey p =", round(bg[1], 4))
print("RESET p =", round(reset.pvalue, 4))

# 4. flags present -> re-fit with HAC (Newey-West) standard errors; betas are identical
m_hac = smf.ols("lcons ~ linc", data=df).fit(cov_type="HAC", cov_kwds={"maxlags": 4})
print(m_hac.summary())                   # same slope, wider, honest SE / CI

Compare the two summaries: the slope is byte-for-byte identical, but the HAC standard error is larger and the confidence interval wider. That is the entire lesson of the diagnostics chapter, executed on real data — and it is exactly the skeleton your capstone report should follow.

🔗 Where this is heading

You now have the complete OLS toolkit, but the assumptions you tested also point to the next courses. When robust errors are not enough because a regressor is correlated with the error (endogeneity), you need instrumental variables and two-stage least squares — the heart of Econometrics II. 5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Your Breusch–Pagan test rejects homoskedasticity (p = 0.002). What is the correct response?
A. Drop the regressor that caused it
B. Keep the OLS estimates but report robust (HC) standard errors
C. Conclude the slope estimate is biased and unusable
D. Re-run OLS until the test passes
Show answer

B. Heteroskedasticity affects only the standard errors, not the unbiasedness of the slope, so you keep the estimate and use HC errors. A and D are data-dredging; C is wrong because the point estimate stays unbiased.

Q2. In a log-log regression, a slope coefficient of 0.86 means…
A. a one-unit rise in x raises y by 0.86 units
B. a 1% rise in x is associated with a 0.86% change in y
C. y increases by 86 units per unit of x
D. x explains 86% of the variation in y
Show answer

B. In a log-log model the slope is an elasticity — a percent-for-percent relationship. A and C describe level-level models; D confuses the slope with R².

Q3. A Durbin–Watson statistic of 0.6 on monthly macro data most likely indicates…
A. strong positive autocorrelation in the residuals
B. perfect multicollinearity
C. no problem — values near 0 are ideal
D. the model has no intercept
Show answer

A. DW near 2 means clean errors; values near 0 signal strong positive autocorrelation. C inverts the rule, and B and D are unrelated to the DW statistic.

Q4. Your data show both heteroskedasticity and serial correlation. Which standard errors are appropriate?
A. Classical (default) standard errors
B. HC1 robust errors only
C. No standard errors are valid; abandon OLS
D. HAC (Newey–West) standard errors
Show answer

D. HAC errors are robust to both heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation. Classical (A) assume neither; HC-only (B) handle heteroskedasticity but not serial correlation; C is an overreaction — OLS estimates remain usable.

Q5. Ramsey's RESET test comes back significant. This is evidence that…
A. the functional form may be misspecified (missing nonlinearity)
B. the errors are heteroskedastic
C. the sample is too small
D. the regressors are perfectly collinear
Show answer

A. RESET adds powers of the fitted values; their significance points to missing curvature or a nonlinear term. B is the job of Breusch–Pagan/White, and C and D are detected by other means entirely.
