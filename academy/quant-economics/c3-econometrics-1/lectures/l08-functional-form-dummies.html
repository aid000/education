Functional Form, Dummies & Interactions

The straight line was just the warm-up — logs, squares, and switches let one regression bend, branch, and speak in percentages.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Interpret slope coefficients under log-log, log-level, and level-log functional forms (elasticities and percentages).
  • Add quadratic terms to capture diminishing or U-shaped effects, and locate the turning point.
  • Encode categorical variables as dummy (indicator) variables and read off the baseline category.
  • Use interaction terms to let one variable's effect differ across groups or levels.
  • Estimate and interpret all of the above in Python with statsmodels' formula API.

8.1Logs: Reading Coefficients as Percentages

Through Week 7 every regression was linear in levels: a one-unit rise in x shifted y by a fixed number of units. But many economic relationships are naturally multiplicative — wages grow by a percentage per year of schooling, output rises by a percent per percent of capital. The fix is not to abandon OLS; it is to feed it the logarithm of a variable. OLS stays perfectly linear in the coefficients — it just regresses transformed columns.

The key fact is that for small changes, the change in a log is a proportional change: Δln(y) ≈ Δy / y. That single approximation generates three interpretations depending on which side you log.

log-log:  ln(y) = b0 + b1·ln(x)  ->  b1 = elasticity (% in y per 1% in x)A 1% rise in x is associated with a b1 percent change in y. This is the constant-elasticity model — the bread and butter of demand and production estimation.
log-level:  ln(y) = b0 + b1·x  ->  100·b1 ≈ % change in y per 1-unit xA semi-elasticity: each extra unit of x moves y by about 100·b1 percent. The classic "return to a year of schooling" wage equation.
level-log:  y = b0 + b1·ln(x)  ->  b1/100 ≈ change in y per 1% in xEach 1% rise in x moves y by about b1/100 units of y.
AnalogyLogs are a change of currency. Modeling raw dollars is like quoting prices in pennies — technically fine, but a $1 move means something totally different to a $20 item than to a $20,000 one. Switching to logs is like switching to percent off: a "10% increase" is comparable whether the starting wage is small or large. The regression line lives in that universal percentage language.
Common mistakeThe "100·b1 ≈ %" rule is an approximation that drifts for large coefficients. If a log-level slope is 0.30, the exact effect is e^(0.30) − 1 = 0.35, i.e. 35%, not 30%. For coefficients under about 0.10 the approximation is fine; above that, report the exact e^(b) − 1. Also: you can only log strictly positive variables — never a column with zeros or negatives.

8.2Quadratics: When the Effect Bends

A straight line forces the marginal effect of x to be the same everywhere. Reality often disagrees: an extra year of experience boosts wages a lot early on and little later; pollution may rise then fall as income grows. To let the slope change, add the square of the regressor as its own column:

y = b0 + b1·x + b2·x^2 + uStill linear in the coefficients, so OLS applies — we just include x and x-squared as two predictors.

Now the marginal effect is no longer a single number. Taking the derivative, dy/dx = b1 + 2·b2·x: the effect of x itself depends on the level of x. If b2 < 0 the curve is a hill (rises then falls); if b2 > 0 it is a valley. The slope hits zero — the turning point — at:

x* = −b1 / (2·b2)Where the marginal effect switches sign. Check it lies inside your data's range before declaring a "peak"; otherwise the curvature is just gentle bending, not a real reversal.

8.3Dummy Variables: Putting Categories in a Regression

Regression needs numbers, but much real data is categorical: region, sector, union/non-union, treated/control. A dummy (indicator) variable solves this — it equals 1 when an observation is in a category and 0 otherwise. Its coefficient is the shift in the intercept for that category relative to a baseline.

wage = b0 + b1·educ + d·female + ufemale = 1 for women, 0 for men. d is the average wage gap, holding education fixed. Men are the omitted baseline (the b0 group).

With a category that has k levels (say four regions), you include k − 1 dummies and leave one out as the baseline. Each coefficient is then "this category versus the omitted one." Including all k dummies plus an intercept makes the columns sum to the constant — perfect collinearity — and OLS cannot run. This is the famous dummy variable trap.

Common mistakeDo not include a dummy for every category alongside the intercept. With four regions you add three dummies, not four. statsmodels' C(region) does this automatically by dropping one level; if you build dummies by hand with pandas, pass drop_first=True. Forgetting this triggers the dummy variable trap and a singular-matrix error or a silently dropped column.

8.4Interactions: Letting an Effect Vary Across Groups

A plain dummy shifts the intercept — it lets two groups start at different heights but assumes they share the same slope. Often that is wrong: the return to education might be larger for one group than another. To let the slope itself differ, multiply the dummy by the continuous variable and include the product as its own regressor — an interaction term:

wage = b0 + b1·educ + b2·female + b3·(female·educ) + ub2 shifts the intercept for women; b3 shifts their education slope. Together they allow a fully separate line per group.

Reading it off: for men (female = 0) the line is b0 + b1·educ. For women (female = 1) it is (b0 + b2) + (b1 + b3)·educ. So b2 is the gap in intercepts and b3 is the gap in slopes — the extra wage return to each year of schooling for women relative to men. If b3 is statistically zero, the two groups share a slope and a single dummy suffices.

AnalogyA dummy alone is a volume knob — it raises or lowers one group's whole track by a fixed amount. An interaction is a graphic equalizer: it lets the group respond differently to the same input. Same song, different shape.
✦ Worked Example — read every coefficient

A wage regression on monthly earnings gives: ln(wage) = 1.20 + 0.085·educ + 0.022·exper − 0.0004·exper^2 − 0.18·female, with wage in dollars, educ and exper in years. Interpret each piece.

  1. Education slope (log-level): 0.085 means each extra year of schooling raises wages by about 100·0.085 = 8.5%. Exact: e^(0.085) − 1 = 0.0887, so 8.9% — close, since the coefficient is small.
  2. Experience, linear part: the marginal effect of experience is 0.022 − 2·(0.0004)·exper. At exper = 0 the first year adds about 2.2%.
  3. Experience, curvature: the negative squared term means each added year helps less. Returns flatten — a classic concave profile.
  4. Turning point: x* = −0.022 / (2·(−0.0004)) = 0.022 / 0.0008 = 27.5 years. Beyond ~27.5 years of experience the model predicts wages stop rising — and that point sits comfortably inside a normal career range, so it is meaningful.
  5. Gender dummy: −0.18 means women earn, on average, about e^(−0.18) − 1 = −0.165, i.e. roughly 16.5% less than men with the same education and experience (men are the omitted baseline).

🛠️ Hands-On: functional forms in statsmodels

We keep using the free course stack — statsmodels, pandas, numpy, and pandas-datareader for FRED — installed back in the Week 1/2 setup (see the install details in Lectures 1–2). No new packages needed here; the star of this lecture is statsmodels' formula API, whose mini-language encodes every transformation we just covered.

# l08_functional_form.py — logs, quadratics, dummies, interactions
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf

# Simulate a wage panel so the snippet runs anywhere (no download).
rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n = 4000
educ   = rng.integers(8, 20, n)
exper  = rng.integers(0, 40, n)
female = rng.integers(0, 2, n)
# true model: log wage with curvature in exper + a gender gap
ln_wage = (1.20 + 0.085*educ + 0.022*exper - 0.0004*exper**2
           - 0.18*female + rng.normal(0, 0.30, n))
df = pd.DataFrame(dict(ln_wage=ln_wage, educ=educ, exper=exper,
                       female=female))

# Formula API: I() protects math, C() makes dummies, ':' is interaction
m = smf.ols("ln_wage ~ educ + exper + I(exper**2) + C(female)",
            data=df).fit()
print(m.params.round(4))

# Turning point in experience: -b_exper / (2*b_exper^2)
b1, b2 = m.params["exper"], m.params["I(exper ** 2)"]
print("exper turning point:", round(-b1/(2*b2), 1), "years")

# Let the education return differ by group via an interaction
mi = smf.ols("ln_wage ~ educ * C(female)", data=df).fit()
print(mi.params.round(4))   # educ:C(female)[T.1] is the slope gap b3

The educ * C(female) shorthand expands to educ + C(female) + educ:C(female) — main effects plus the interaction, exactly the model in 8.4. Swap the simulated frame for real FRED-linked microdata or a CSV and the same formulas hold. Then report the percentage interpretation (e^(b) − 1), not just the raw coefficient.

Q1. In the model ln(y) = b0 + b1·ln(x), the coefficient b1 is interpreted as…
A. the elasticity: the % change in y per 1% change in x
B. the change in y in units per 1-unit change in x
C. the % change in y per 1-unit change in x
D. the change in y per 1% change in x
Show answer

A. Logging both sides gives a constant-elasticity model, so b1 reads directly as % per %. B is the level-level form, C is log-level (semi-elasticity), and D is level-log — all use only one log.

Q2. A log-level wage regression returns a coefficient of 0.40 on a training dummy. The exact percentage effect is…
A. exactly 40%
B. about 49%, since e^(0.40) − 1 ≈ 0.49
C. about 33%, since ln(1.40) ≈ 0.33
D. undefined because you cannot log a dummy
Show answer

B. The "100·b ≈ %" rule drifts at large coefficients; the exact effect is e^(0.40) − 1 ≈ 0.492, i.e. about 49%. A uses the approximation (too low here). C inverts the formula wrongly. D is false — the dependent variable is logged, not the dummy.

Q3. A model is y = b0 + b1·x + b2·x^2 with b1 = 0.022 and b2 = −0.0004. The marginal effect of x reaches zero at x* equal to…
A. 0.0176
B. 55
C. 27.5
D. there is no turning point
Show answer

C. x* = −b1/(2·b2) = −0.022 / (2·(−0.0004)) = 0.022/0.0008 = 27.5. B forgets the factor of 2 in the denominator; A divides the coefficients without the formula; D is wrong since a nonzero b2 always yields a turning point.

Q4. You have a categorical variable with 4 regions and want to include it in a regression with an intercept. You should add…
A. all 4 region dummies
B. 3 dummies, leaving one region as the baseline
C. 1 dummy coded 1, 2, 3, 4
D. 8 dummies, two per region
Show answer

B. With k categories include k−1 dummies plus the intercept; the omitted level is the baseline. A is the dummy-variable trap (perfect collinearity). C imposes a false numeric ordering and equal spacing. D is meaningless duplication.

Q5. In wage = b0 + b1·educ + b2·female + b3·(female·educ), the coefficient b3 represents…
A. the average wage of women
B. the intercept gap between women and men
C. the overall return to education for everyone
D. the difference in the education slope for women relative to men
Show answer

D. The interaction coefficient shifts the slope: women's return to education is b1 + b3, so b3 is the slope gap. B describes b2 (the intercept shift); C describes b1 (the baseline slope); A is not a single coefficient at all.
