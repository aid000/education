Hypothesis Testing & F-tests

Your regression handed you numbers — hypothesis testing tells you which of them you're actually allowed to believe.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Frame an economic claim as a null and alternative hypothesis about regression coefficients.
  • Run and interpret a t-test on a single coefficient, including one- vs. two-sided forms.
  • Build an F-test for the joint significance of several coefficients and compare nested models.
  • Distinguish R² from adjusted R², and explain why adding regressors never lowers R².
  • Read a p-value correctly — and explain what statistical significance does and does not mean.

6.1Turning a Claim into a Testable Hypothesis

Last week you learned that each OLS coefficient comes with a standard error — a measure of how much it would bounce around if you redrew the sample. A point estimate alone is never the end of the story: 0.04 could be a genuine effect or pure sampling noise. Hypothesis testing is the disciplined way to decide between those two readings.

You start by writing down a null hypothesis H₀ — the boring, "nothing is going on" claim you'll try to overturn — and an alternative H₁ that you'd accept if the data make H₀ implausible. For a single coefficient βj, the workhorse null is "this variable has no effect":

H0: beta_j = 0   vs.   H1: beta_j != 0The two-sided test. One-sided versions (beta_j > 0 or beta_j < 0) are used when theory predicts a direction.

The logic is that of proof by contradiction under uncertainty: we provisionally assume H₀ is true, ask how surprising our estimate would be in that world, and reject H₀ only if the estimate is too surprising to credit to chance. We never "prove" H₀ — we only fail to reject it.

AnalogyHypothesis testing is a courtroom. The null hypothesis is the defendant, presumed innocent ("the effect is zero"). The estimate and its standard error are the evidence. You convict — reject H₀ — only when the evidence is strong beyond a reasonable doubt (your significance level α). Failing to convict is not the same as proving innocence; it just means the evidence wasn't strong enough.

6.2The t-test on a Single Coefficient

To measure "how surprising" an estimate is under H₀, we standardize it. The t-statistic counts how many standard errors the estimate sits away from the null value:

t = ( beta_hat_j - beta_j0 ) / SE( beta_hat_j )For the usual null beta_j0 = 0 this reduces to t = beta_hat_j / SE(beta_hat_j) — the coefficient divided by its standard error.

Under H₀ (and the classical assumptions), this statistic follows a t-distribution with n − k − 1 degrees of freedom, where n is the sample size and k the number of slope coefficients. The t-distribution is bell-shaped like the normal but with fatter tails that shrink toward the normal as the sample grows.

We then compute a p-value: the probability, if H₀ were true, of seeing a t-statistic at least as extreme as the one we got. A small p-value means our data would be a rare fluke in the null world — so we doubt the null. We reject H₀ when p < α, with α = 0.05 the default convention.

A handy rule of thumb: with a reasonably large sample, |t| > 2 roughly corresponds to significance at the 5% level. But always let the reported p-value decide the close calls.

Common mistakeA p-value is not "the probability that H₀ is true," and it is not the probability your result happened by chance. It is the probability of data this extreme assuming H₀ holds. Likewise, "fail to reject" never means "H₀ is proven" — a noisy, small sample can fail to reject almost anything.

6.3The F-test: Testing Several Coefficients at Once

A t-test handles one restriction at a time. But economic questions are often joint: "do any of these three policy dummies matter?" or "are seasonal effects present as a group?" Testing them one by one is wrong — with enough separate tests, some will look significant by luck alone. The F-test tests a bundle of restrictions simultaneously.

The idea is to compare two nested models. The unrestricted model has all the regressors. The restricted model imposes H₀ (e.g., sets q coefficients to zero), so it's a special case of the unrestricted one. If the restrictions are harmless, dropping those variables should barely hurt the fit. We measure the damage with the residual sum of squares (SSR):

F = [ (SSR_r - SSR_ur) / q ] / [ SSR_ur / (n - k - 1) ]SSR_r = restricted model's residual sum of squares; SSR_ur = unrestricted; q = number of restrictions; n - k - 1 = unrestricted df.

The numerator is the per-restriction rise in unexplained variation when you force H₀; the denominator scales it by the noise level. A large F means the restrictions cost a lot of fit, so the dropped variables jointly mattered — reject H₀. Under H₀, the statistic follows an F-distribution with (q, n − k − 1) degrees of freedom, and again you read off a p-value. Notice F can also be written using R²: F = [(R²ur − R²r)/q] / [(1 − R²ur)/(n − k − 1)].

Two special cases you will meet constantly. (1) Overall significance: the F at the bottom of every regression table tests H₀ that all slopes are zero at once — "does the model explain anything?" (2) The t–F link: for a single restriction (q = 1), F = t², so the two tests agree exactly.

6.4R² vs. Adjusted R², and What "Significant" Really Means

The R² is the share of the variation in y explained by the regressors: R² = 1 − SSR/SST. It is tempting to treat a high R² as a "good model" badge — but R² has a trapdoor. Adding any regressor, even a column of random noise, can only raise R² or leave it unchanged, because OLS can always set the new coefficient near zero if the variable is useless, and will nudge it to grab any tiny accidental correlation.

R2_adj = 1 - ( SSR / (n - k - 1) ) / ( SST / (n - 1) )Adjusted R² penalizes each added regressor through the (n - k - 1) degrees-of-freedom term, so it can fall when a variable doesn't earn its keep.

Adjusted R² fixes this by charging a "rent" for every regressor: it rises only if the new variable improves fit by more than chance would predict. That makes it a fairer yardstick when comparing models with different numbers of regressors — though, crucially, neither R² is a hypothesis test, and a high R² never validates the model's assumptions.

Finally, keep two meanings of "significant" apart. Statistical significance means an effect is distinguishable from zero given the noise — it is partly a story about sample size, since huge samples make even trivial effects significant. Economic significance means the effect is large enough to matter in the real world. A coefficient can be wildly statistically significant yet economically negligible, or economically huge yet statistically uncertain.

Common mistakeChasing R² by piling in regressors is a classic blunder — you can drive R² to 1 with enough junk variables while destroying out-of-sample accuracy (overfitting). Judge models on adjusted R², theory, and out-of-sample fit, never on raw R² alone. And never confuse a tiny p-value with a big effect: significance answers "is it zero?", not "is it large?".
✦ Worked Example — a t-test and a joint F-test end to end

You estimate a wage equation on n = 526 workers with k = 4 regressors (education, experience, tenure, female). The unrestricted fit gives R²ur = 0.36 and SSRur = 132.0. The coefficient on education is 0.083 with SE = 0.0078.

  1. t-test on education: t = 0.083 / 0.0078 = 10.6. With df = 526 − 4 − 1 = 521 the critical value is ≈ 1.96, and 10.6 ≫ 1.96 → reject H₀. Education's effect on log wage is strongly significant (p ≈ 0).
  2. A joint question: do experience and tenure jointly matter? Drop both (q = 2) and refit. The restricted model gives SSRr = 141.2.
  3. Numerator: (SSRr − SSRur) / q = (141.2 − 132.0) / 2 = 9.2 / 2 = 4.6.
  4. Denominator: SSRur / (n − k − 1) = 132.0 / 521 = 0.2534.
  5. F-statistic: F = 4.6 / 0.2534 = 18.2 on (2, 521) df. The 5% critical value is ≈ 3.0, so 18.2 ≫ 3.0 → reject H₀: experience and tenure are jointly significant.
  6. Read it: both the single-variable and the joint tests say these wage drivers are real. The economic story (an extra year of schooling raises wages ~8.3%) is also large — statistically and economically significant.

🛠️ Hands-On: t-tests and F-tests in statsmodels

We use the course's free stack — pandas, numpy, statsmodels, and pandas-datareader for FRED data. If you set up the environment in the Week 1–2 lectures (the install <details> there), you already have everything; just activate it.

Below we pull a few macro series from FRED, fit a model, read the t-tests off the summary, then run an explicit joint F-test with f_test and compare a nested model with anova_lm.

# hypothesis_tests.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import statsmodels.formula.api as smf
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.stats.anova import anova_lm
import pandas_datareader.data as web

# --- pull free public data from FRED ---
start, end = "1990-01-01", "2019-12-01"
codes = {"INDPRO": "ip", "UNRATE": "unemp",
         "FEDFUNDS": "ffr", "CPIAUCSL": "cpi"}
raw = web.DataReader(list(codes), "fred", start, end).rename(columns=codes)

# monthly % changes, drop the first NaN row
df = raw.pct_change().dropna() * 100
df.columns = ["g_ip", "g_unemp", "g_ffr", "g_cpi"]

# --- unrestricted model: does IP growth respond to the others? ---
ur = smf.ols("g_ip ~ g_unemp + g_ffr + g_cpi", data=df).fit()
print(ur.summary())          # t-stats & p-values in the coef table

# single-coefficient t-test is right there: P>|t| column
print("t on g_ffr:", ur.tvalues["g_ffr"],
      " p:", ur.pvalues["g_ffr"])

# --- joint F-test: are g_ffr AND g_cpi jointly zero? ---
ftest = ur.f_test("g_ffr = 0, g_cpi = 0")
print(ftest)                 # F, p-value, and df for the joint null

# --- nested-model comparison via SSR (same F) ---
r = smf.ols("g_ip ~ g_unemp", data=df).fit()   # restricted
print(anova_lm(r, ur))       # compares restricted vs unrestricted

print("R2:", ur.rsquared, " adj R2:", ur.rsquared_adj)
print("overall F:", ur.fvalue, " p:", ur.f_pvalue)

The P>|t| column gives each two-sided t-test; f_test and anova_lm give the joint F (they agree). Compare rsquared with rsquared_adj — then add a column of np.random.randn noise to the model and watch R² tick up while adjusted R² falls.

💬 Discussion

A widely-read empirical study reports that a job-training program raises annual earnings by $38, with a t-statistic of 7.5 (p < 0.0001) on a sample of 2.1 million people. A policymaker reads the abstract and concludes the program "works and should be expanded nationwide." What is wrong — and what is right — about that conclusion? In your answer, separate statistical from economic significance, comment on the role of the huge sample, and say what additional evidence you'd want before scaling the program.

Show a model response

The t-statistic of 7.5 tells us the $38 effect is statistically distinguishable from zero — we can be confident the true effect is not exactly $0. But that is almost entirely a story about the enormous sample: with 2.1 million observations, standard errors shrink so far that even a trivially small effect becomes "significant." The right question is economic significance: $38 a year is roughly a rounding error in household income, and almost certainly smaller than the per-person cost of running the program. So the policymaker is right that the effect is real and positive, but wrong to treat a tiny p-value as evidence that the effect is large or that expansion passes a cost-benefit test.

Before scaling, I'd want: (1) a confidence interval on the dollar effect, not just a p-value, to see the plausible range; (2) the program's per-participant cost, to compute net benefit; (3) evidence the design is causal (random assignment or a credible quasi-experiment), since a significant OLS coefficient can still be biased by omitted variables; and (4) heterogeneity — whether the average hides large gains for some subgroups.

✓ Self-check rubric: Did you (a) state that significance ≠ large effect, (b) explain how sample size drives the small p-value, (c) raise cost-benefit / economic magnitude, and (d) note that significance does not establish causality? A strong answer hits all four.

🔗 Where this is heading

Every test today rested on a quiet assumption: that the standard errors are computed correctly. Week 7 breaks that assumption — when error variance isn't constant (heteroskedasticity), the classical SEs are wrong and so are your t- and F-tests, until you switch to robust standard errors. The F-test for nested models is also the gateway to structural-break and Chow tests later in the course, and the same likelihood-ratio logic reappears in the maximum-likelihood models of C4. In the Quant Finance track, joint F-tests are exactly how you test whether a set of risk factors jointly explains asset returns.

Q1. The t-statistic for testing H₀: βj = 0 is computed as…
A. β̂j divided by its standard error
B. β̂j multiplied by its standard error
C. the standard error divided by β̂j
D. β̂j divided by the sample size
Show answer

A. With βj0 = 0, t = β̂j/SE(β̂j). B and C invert or multiply incorrectly; D confuses the SE with n.

Q2. A correct interpretation of a p-value of 0.03 is…
A. there is a 3% chance the null hypothesis is true
B. if H₀ were true, there is a 3% chance of data at least this extreme
C. the effect is 3% as large as it could be
D. we are 3% confident in the alternative hypothesis
Show answer

B. A p-value is a tail probability computed assuming H₀ is true. A wrongly states it's the probability H₀ is true; C and D are not probabilities the p-value reports.

Q3. The F-test for joint significance is preferred over running several separate t-tests because…
A. the F-test always gives a smaller p-value
B. t-tests cannot be used on more than one coefficient ever
C. running many separate tests inflates the chance of a false positive, while the F-test assesses the restrictions jointly
D. the F-test does not require any degrees of freedom
Show answer

C. Multiple separate tests raise the family-wise error rate; the F-test handles all q restrictions in one shot. A is false (no guarantee), B is false, and D is false — F uses (q, n−k−1) df.

Q4. Compared with R², the adjusted R²…
A. can never decrease when a regressor is added
B. equals R² only when the sample size is infinite
C. is a formal hypothesis test of model fit
D. can fall when an added regressor doesn't improve fit enough to justify its lost degree of freedom
Show answer

D. Adjusted R² penalizes extra regressors, so it can decline — that's its whole point. A describes plain R²; B is false; C is wrong because neither R² is a hypothesis test.

Q5. For a single linear restriction (q = 1), the relationship between the F-statistic and the t-statistic is…
A. F = t²
B. F = |t|
C. F = 2t
D. there is no relationship
Show answer

A. With one restriction the F-statistic is exactly the square of the corresponding t-statistic, so the two tests agree. B, C, and D misstate this exact identity.
