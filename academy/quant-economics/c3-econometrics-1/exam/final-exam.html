Foundations, Data & Simple OLS · Weeks 1–2
1. In the model y = β0 + β1·x + u, the error term u represents…
Answer: B. u collects every other influence on y — ability, luck, omitted factors, noise — not captured by the included regressor x.
2. Ordinary least squares chooses the intercept and slope to minimize…
Answer: D. OLS minimizes Σeᵢ². Summing raw residuals (A) is useless since positive and negative gaps cancel; minimizing the max (B) or absolute residuals (C) defines different estimators entirely.
3. The population regression function E[y | x] = β0 + β1·x is best described as…
Answer: A. The PRF is a conditional expectation — the average y at each x, not each individual's value (B). It is causal only under extra assumptions (C), and it is not a rescaled correlation (D).
Multiple Regression & Omitted-Variable Bias · Week 3
4. In a multiple regression of wage on education and experience, the coefficient on education is best interpreted as…
Answer: C. Each slope in multiple regression is a ceteris-paribus partial effect — education's effect among workers who share the same experience.
5. For omitting a variable to bias your OLS estimate of β1, the omitted variable must be…
Answer: B. Bias = β2·δ requires both β2 ≠ 0 (it affects y) and δ ≠ 0 (it correlates with the included regressor). If either is zero, omitting it causes no bias.
6. A variable that raises y (β2 > 0) is omitted, and it is negatively correlated with the included regressor (δ < 0). The bias on the included coefficient is…
Answer: A. Sign of bias = sign(β2)×sign(δ) = (+)×(−) = negative. The sign rule gives a definite answer once both signs are known.
Classical Assumptions & Gauss-Markov · Week 4
7. Which classical assumption, when violated, makes the OLS slope estimator biased?
Answer: D. Unbiasedness rests on MLR.1–4, and zero conditional mean is the assumption that does the heavy lifting. Heteroskedasticity (A) only ruins efficiency and standard errors; perfect collinearity (B) prevents computing OLS rather than biasing it; (C) is a misstatement — OLS needs linearity in parameters, not variables.
8. The model y = β0 + β1·x + β2·x² + u is…
Answer: C. MLR.1 requires linearity in the parameters (the β's), not the variables — x² is just another regressor. (A) confuses curve shape with parameter form; (B) is wrong since x and x² are not a perfect linear relationship; (D) is unnecessary.
9. In "OLS is BLUE" (Best Linear Unbiased Estimator), the word "Best" specifically requires which assumption?
Answer: B. "Best" means minimum variance among linear unbiased estimators, which Gauss-Markov derives using homoskedasticity. Drop MLR.5 and OLS need not be efficient. (A) and (D) are needed for unbiasedness, not the minimum-variance claim; (C) is not part of Gauss-Markov at all — it is the extra assumption added for exact inference.
10. "OLS is unbiased" most precisely means…
Answer: D. Unbiasedness is a property of the sampling distribution: E[β̂] = β. (A) is false — any one sample can be off; (B) describes efficiency ("Best"), not unbiasedness; (C) is a mechanical algebra fact of OLS, unrelated to bias.
Inference: Standard Errors, t-tests & CIs · Week 5
11. The standard error of a regression coefficient measures…
Answer: C. The standard error is the estimated standard deviation of β̂ across repeated samples — a precision measure. (A) confuses precision with effect size; (B) is the data's spread, not the estimate's; (D) describes a p-value.
12. Which change would decrease the standard error of a slope coefficient?
Answer: A. se = √[σ̂²/(SST_j(1−R²_j))], so a larger SST_j shrinks it. (B) raises it via fewer observations and degrees of freedom; (C) raises it because (1−R²_j) shrinks; (D) raises it directly through the numerator.
13. A coefficient is 4.0 with a standard error of 0.5. Its t-statistic for H₀: β = 0 is…
Answer: D. t = (4.0 − 0)/0.5 = 8.0 — the estimate sits eight standard errors from zero, decisively rejecting the null. (A) inverts the ratio; (B) and (C) are arithmetic mismatches.
14. A 95% confidence interval for a coefficient is [0.42, 0.82]. What can you conclude at the 5% level?
Answer: B. An interval excluding 0 means 0 is a rejected null value at the 5% level, so the coefficient is significant. (A) misstates the frequentist meaning — the interval, not the point, is the random object; (C) reverses the logic; (D) is unrelated to interval width.
Hypothesis Testing & F-tests · Week 6
15. The correct interpretation of a p-value of 0.03 is…
Answer: A. A p-value is a tail probability computed assuming H₀ is true. (B) wrongly states it is the probability H₀ is true; (C) and (D) are not quantities a p-value reports.
16. An F-test for the joint significance of several coefficients is preferred over running separate t-tests on each one because…
Answer: C. Multiple separate tests raise the family-wise error rate; the F-test handles all q restrictions in one shot. (A) has no guarantee, (B) is false, and (D) is false — F uses (q, n−k−1) degrees of freedom.
17. A job-training estimate is highly statistically significant (t = 7.5) in a sample of 2.1 million people, yet raises earnings by only $38 a year. The best summary of this result is…
Answer: D. With millions of observations, standard errors shrink so far that even a tiny effect becomes "significant" — that is a story about sample size, not magnitude. (A) and (B) confuse the two kinds of significance entirely; (C) misdescribes what R² measures.
Heteroskedasticity & Robust Standard Errors · Week 7
18. Under heteroskedasticity (but with exogenous regressors), the OLS coefficient estimates are…
Answer: B. Unbiasedness depends only on exogeneity, E[u|x]=0, which heteroskedasticity does not violate, so β̂ stays unbiased and consistent — it just loses efficiency. (A) is the classic misconception (that is omitted-variable bias, a different problem); (C) and (D) are false.
19. The real practical problem heteroskedasticity causes is that…
Answer: C. The classical standard-error formula assumes constant variance; when that fails, the SEs — and everything built on them — are wrong. (A) and (D) describe unrelated issues; (B) is false, since heteroskedasticity does not systematically bias R².
20. The main practical appeal of heteroskedasticity-robust (HC) standard errors is that…
Answer: A. Robust SEs use the squared residuals in the sandwich formula, so they are valid in large samples whether or not heteroskedasticity is present, with no variance model needed. (B) is false since coefficients are untouched; (C) is false — they are often larger; (D) describes WLS, not robust SEs.
Functional Form & Dummy Variables · Week 8
21. A log-level wage regression returns a coefficient of 0.50 on a training-program dummy. The exact percentage effect on wages is…
Answer: B. The "100·b ≈ %" rule drifts for larger coefficients; the exact effect is eb − 1 = e0.50 − 1 ≈ 0.6487, about 65%. (A) is the approximation, too low here; (C) inverts the formula; (D) is false — the dependent variable is logged, not the dummy.
Time-Series, Autocorrelation & the Capstone · Weeks 9–10
22. When the errors in a time-series regression are positively autocorrelated but the regressors are exogenous, what happens to OLS?
Answer: D. Autocorrelation does not bias β̂ (so A is wrong); it makes the conventional standard errors too small, which inflates t-stats and false positives. (C) is wrong because inference is affected, and (B) is wrong because OLS still runs fine — only its interpretation needs care.
23. A Durbin–Watson statistic very close to 0 on a time-series regression most likely indicates…
Answer: A. Since DW ≈ 2(1 − ρ̂), a value near 0 implies ρ̂ near +1 — strong positive autocorrelation. Negative autocorrelation (B) would push DW toward 4; no autocorrelation (C) keeps DW near 2; DW says nothing about the intercept (D).
24. The purpose of Newey–West (HAC) standard errors is to…
Answer: C. HAC corrects the variance/standard-error calculation while keeping β̂ exactly the same. It does not alter the slopes (A), does not deseasonalize (B), and never drops data (D).
25. Your capstone regression's diagnostic battery flags both heteroskedasticity (Breusch–Pagan rejects) and serial correlation (Durbin–Watson ≈ 0.7). Which standard errors should you report?
Answer: D. HAC errors are robust to both heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation at once. Classical errors (A) assume neither problem exists; HC-only errors (B) handle heteroskedasticity but not serial correlation; (C) is an overreaction — the OLS point estimates remain unbiased and usable.
