Week 8 · Lecture 8

Solving Growth Models Numerically

The Euler equation tells you the law of motion — but to actually see the economy converge to its steady state, you have to make a computer walk the capital path forward.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why most growth models have no closed-form solution and must be solved numerically.
  • Discretize a continuous capital path into a finite grid of time steps and approximate the dynamics.
  • Use the shooting method to find the saddle path of the optimal-growth model.
  • Calibrate the structural parameters (α, β, δ, …) so the model's steady state matches observed data.
  • Plot a transition path with QuantEcon/SciPy helpers and sanity-check the numerical solution.

8.1Why We Need a Computer

By Week 7 you derived the Euler equation of the optimal-growth (Ramsey–Cass–Koopmans) model: along the optimal path, the household equates the marginal cost of saving one more unit today against the discounted marginal benefit of consuming it tomorrow. In discrete time, with capital k, consumption c, production f(k), depreciation δ, and discount factor β, the system is two coupled equations:

u'(c_t) = β · u'(c_{t+1}) · [ f'(k_{t+1}) + 1 - δ ]
k_{t+1} = f(k_t) + (1-δ)·k_t - c_tEuler equation (top) + resource constraint (bottom). Two equations, two unknown sequences {c_t}, {k_t}.

For a few special cases — log utility, Cobb–Douglas production, full depreciation (δ = 1) — this collapses to a tidy closed form. But move to a realistic δ ≈ 0.1, or CRRA utility with curvature γ ≠ 1, and the algebra has no pencil-and-paper answer. The path exists and is unique, but you can only compute it. That is the whole job of this lecture: turn a clean theorem into numbers and a plot.

The deeper reason is structure. The model is a two-point boundary-value problem: we know where capital starts (today's k0, given) and where it must end up (the steady state k*, in the long run), but not the consumption level that threads between them. Finding that single missing number is what numerical methods are for.

8.2Discretizing the Capital Path

A "path" is a continuous object — capital at every instant — but a computer holds only finitely many numbers. So we discretize: chop time into steps t = 0, 1, 2, … , T and store the path as arrays k[0], k[1], …, k[T] and c[0], …, c[T]. Given a starting capital and a guess for initial consumption, the two equations above let us march forward one step at a time — this is the discrete law of motion:

k_{t+1} = f(k_t) + (1-δ)·k_t - c_t,   then   c_{t+1} = c_t · [ β·(f'(k_{t+1}) + 1 - δ) ]^(1/γ)Forward simulation: from (k_t, c_t) compute next period's capital, then next period's consumption from the Euler equation (CRRA case).

This is just forward Euler integration applied to an economic system. The smaller the time step, the closer the discrete path tracks the true continuous one — at the cost of more arithmetic. With annual data the natural step is one year, and a horizon of T = 150–200 periods is plenty to reach the steady state.

AnalogyThink of aiming a basketball at a hoop. You know your starting position (k0) and the target (the steady state). The angle and force of your throw are the initial consumption c0. Physics (the law of motion) then determines the entire arc once you've chosen the launch. You can't steer mid-flight — you can only pick the launch and watch where it lands.

8.3The Shooting Method & the Saddle Path

The optimal-growth steady state is a saddle point: of all the paths leaving k0, exactly one converges to k*. Pick initial consumption too high and you over-consume — capital is drawn down and crashes to zero. Pick it too low and you over-save — capital piles up and overshoots past k*. Only one knife-edge value of c0 lands you on the stable arm. Finding it is exactly like artillery: adjust the launch, fire, see where it lands, repeat — hence the shooting method.

find c_0 such that  k_T(c_0) ≈ k*Treat the terminal capital as a function of the initial consumption guess, then root-find / bisect on c_0 until the path lands on the steady state.

Because "too high crashes" and "too low overshoots" sit on opposite sides, the terminal error is monotone in c0 — so plain bisection is bulletproof: bracket one guess that overshoots and one that crashes, then repeatedly halve the interval. Each halving buys one more bit of precision, so ~40 iterations pins c0 to machine accuracy.

Common mistakeDon't confuse the shooting method with simply iterating the law of motion forward from an arbitrary c0. A single forward simulation almost never lands on the steady state — because the path is a saddle, any wrong c0 diverges, often explosively after period 100. The forward march is only the inner loop; the shooting method is the outer root-finding loop that tunes c0. Forgetting the outer loop is the #1 reason a "growth simulation" blows up to NaN.

8.4Calibration & Sanity Checks

A numerical solution is only as good as its parameters. Calibration chooses the structural parameters so the model's steady state reproduces known long-run data — a discipline that turns an abstract model into a quantitative tool. The classic targets:

  • α (capital share) ≈ 0.33 — from the share of national income paid to capital.
  • δ (depreciation) ≈ 0.05–0.10 — from the investment/capital ratio in the data.
  • β (discount factor) ≈ 0.96 — pinned so the steady-state real interest rate ≈ 4% per year.
  • γ (risk aversion / inverse elasticity of substitution) ≈ 1–2.

At the deterministic steady state the Euler equation simplifies because consumption is constant (ct+1 = ct), so the growth factor in brackets must equal 1:

β·(f'(k*) + 1 - δ) = 1  ⇒   f'(k*) = 1/β - 1 + δSteady-state condition. For Cobb–Douglas f(k)=A·k^α, f'(k)=αA·k^(α-1), so k* solves αA·k*^(α-1) = 1/β - 1 + δ.

Once you have a path, sanity-check it before trusting any plot: (1) the path should be monotone and smooth, with no oscillation or NaNs; (2) it should settle at the k* you computed analytically from the formula above; (3) refining the time step or tightening the bisection tolerance should not visibly move the answer (convergence); (4) the Euler-equation residual — the gap between the two sides at every period — should be tiny (≈ 1e-8), not 0.1.

AnalogyCalibration is like tuning a flight simulator so a known route takes the real-world fuel and time. You're not predicting that route — you already know it — you're adjusting the dials (α, β, δ) until the simulator reproduces reality, then you trust it to explore new scenarios (a tax change, a productivity boom) you haven't observed.
✦ Worked Example — compute the steady state, then shoot

Take Cobb–Douglas production f(k) = A·kα with A = 1, α = 0.33, depreciation δ = 0.05, discount β = 0.96, log utility (γ = 1). Find k* analytically, then describe the shoot.

  1. Required marginal product: f'(k*) = 1/β − 1 + δ = 1/0.96 − 1 + 0.05 = 1.04167 − 1 + 0.05 = 0.09167.
  2. Solve for k*: f'(k) = αA·kα−1 = 0.33·k−0.67. Set 0.33·k*−0.67 = 0.09167 → k*−0.67 = 0.2778 → k*0.67 = 3.600.
  3. Raise to 1/0.67: k* = 3.6001/0.67 = 3.6001.4925 ≈ 6.74.
  4. Steady-state consumption: c* = f(k*) − δ·k* = 6.740.33 − 0.05·6.74 = 1.876 − 0.337 ≈ 1.54.
  5. The shoot: start at k0 = 1 (well below k*). Guess c0; march the law of motion T = 200 years. If capital crashes below 0 before T, c0 was too high — lower the upper bracket. If kT > k* and still rising, c0 was too low — raise the lower bracket. Bisect ~40 times: the converged value is c0 ≈ 0.96, and the path rises smoothly from k = 1 to k* ≈ 6.74.

🛠️ Hands-On: shoot the optimal-growth path in Python

We use the free QuantEcon + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the install details block lives in those early lectures — reuse that environment). No new tool is needed here: scipy.optimize.brentq does the bisection root-find, and matplotlib plots the transition path.

# shoot_growth.py — Lecture 8: solve Ramsey growth by shooting
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- calibrated parameters ---
A, alpha, delta, beta, gamma = 1.0, 0.33, 0.05, 0.96, 1.0
T, k0 = 200, 1.0

f      = lambda k: A * k**alpha               # production
f_prime= lambda k: alpha * A * k**(alpha-1)   # marginal product

# analytic steady state: f'(k*) = 1/beta - 1 + delta
k_star = (alpha * A / (1/beta - 1 + delta))**(1/(1-alpha))

def simulate(c0):
    # march the law of motion forward from (k0, c0)
    k, c = k0, c0
    for _ in range(T):
        k_next = f(k) + (1-delta)*k - c
        if k_next <= 0:                        # capital crashed -> c0 too high
            return -1.0
        c = c * (beta*(f_prime(k_next) + 1 - delta))**(1/gamma)
        k = k_next
    return k                                    # terminal capital

# shoot: find c0 so terminal k matches k* (terminal error monotone in c0)
c0 = brentq(lambda c: simulate(c) - k_star, 0.5, 1.4, xtol=1e-10)
print(f"k* = {k_star:.3f},  shot c0 = {c0:.4f}")

# replay the winning path and plot it
k, c, path = k0, c0, [k0]
for _ in range(T):
    k = f(k) + (1-delta)*k - c
    c = c * (beta*(f_prime(k) + 1 - delta))**(1/gamma)
    path.append(k)

plt.plot(path);  plt.axhline(k_star, ls='--', color='orange')
plt.xlabel("year");  plt.ylabel("capital k");  plt.title("Transition to steady state")
plt.show()

Expected output: k* = 6.741, shot c0 ≈ 0.96, and a curve that rises smoothly from 1 to 6.74 and flattens — the green saddle path from §8.3. Sanity check: confirm path[-1] ≈ k_star, then bump T to 400 and verify the answer barely moves.

Q1. Why must the optimal-growth model usually be solved numerically?
A. The Euler equation is wrong and needs correcting by simulation
B. For realistic parameters (δ ≠ 1, γ ≠ 1) the coupled Euler + resource equations have no closed-form solution
C. Computers are faster than algebra in all cases
D. The model has no steady state
Show answer

B. A few knife-edge cases (log utility, δ=1) solve in closed form, but realistic calibrations don't — the path exists and is unique but can only be computed. (A) is false (the Euler equation is correct); (C) overstates — algebra is exact when available; (D) is false, the model has a well-defined steady state.

Q2. In the shooting method, what is the single unknown we search over?
A. The depreciation rate δ
B. The steady-state capital k*
C. The initial consumption c0
D. The discount factor β
Show answer

C. We know k0 (given) and the target k* (computed from the steady-state condition); the missing number that threads between them is the initial consumption c0. (A) and (D) are calibrated parameters, fixed before shooting; (B) is the known target, not the unknown.

Q3. You guess an initial consumption that is too high. What happens to the simulated capital path?
A. Capital is drawn down and eventually crashes toward zero
B. Capital overshoots and grows without bound
C. Capital lands exactly on k* anyway
D. Nothing changes — c0 has no effect on capital
Show answer

A. Over-consuming starves investment, so capital declines and collapses (the red path in §8.3). Over-saving (too-low c0) causes the overshoot in (B). (C) only happens at the exact saddle value; (D) is false — c0 enters the resource constraint every period.

Q4. Calibration of the discount factor β is typically pinned by requiring that…
A. the capital share equals 1/3
B. consumption equals output
C. the steady-state real interest rate matches its observed value (≈ 4%/yr)
D. depreciation equals zero
Show answer

C. At the steady state f'(k*) = 1/β − 1 + δ, so β controls the implied real return; we choose β so that return ≈ 4%/year. (A) calibrates α, not β; (B) is false in steady state (output also covers depreciation/investment); (D) is not a calibration target and is empirically wrong.

Q5. Which is the best sanity check that your numerical growth solution is trustworthy?
A. The code runs without a syntax error
B. The plot looks visually pleasing
C. The first three periods increase
D. The path reaches the analytic k*, is smooth, and barely moves when you refine the time step or tolerance
Show answer

D. Trust comes from convergence to the independently computed steady state, smoothness, a tiny Euler residual, and insensitivity to grid refinement. (A) running ≠ correct; (B) aesthetics prove nothing; (C) a few rising periods can precede a later blow-up to NaN.
