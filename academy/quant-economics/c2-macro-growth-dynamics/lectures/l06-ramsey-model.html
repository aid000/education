Week 6 · Lecture 6

The Ramsey Model of Optimal Growth

Solow handed savings to us as a fixed dial; Ramsey lets a far-sighted household turn that dial itself — and the path it chooses threads a razor-thin saddle to the steady state.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans problem: a representative household choosing consumption over time to maximize discounted utility.
  • Derive and interpret the consumption Euler equation — the rule that governs how consumption grows.
  • Build the two-equation dynamic system and read its phase diagram with the two nullclines.
  • Explain saddle-path stability: why exactly one trajectory converges to the steady state, and why that pins down today's consumption.
  • Contrast Ramsey with Solow: the savings rate here is an optimal choice, not an assumed constant.

6.1From Solow's Dial to Ramsey's Choice

In the Solow model (Weeks 2–3) you assumed households save a fixed fraction s of output every period. That dial was handed to us from outside the model. It makes Solow tractable, but it begs a question: why would anyone save exactly 20%, or 30%, forever? The savings rate is really the outcome of a decision — give up consumption today to have more capital, and therefore more consumption, tomorrow. The Ramsey-Cass-Koopmans model makes that decision explicit.

We imagine a single representative household (a stand-in for the whole economy) that lives forever and chooses an entire path of consumption to maximize its lifetime well-being. Utility from consumption c is summarized by a function u(c) with diminishing marginal utility, and future utility is discounted at rate ρ (rho) — impatience. The household's objective is:

max ∫0∞ e^(-ρ t) · u(c(t)) dtMaximize the present value of utility over all time. ρ > 0 is the rate of time preference: utility tomorrow counts a little less than utility today.

The economy obeys a resource constraint: output produced but not consumed becomes new capital. Writing everything in per-worker terms with production function f(k) and depreciation rate δ (delta), capital accumulates as

k̇ = f(k) - c - (δ + n) kk̇ is the time-derivative of capital per worker. Output f(k) splits into consumption c and investment; investment must also cover depreciation δ and equip new workers arriving at population-growth rate n.

So Ramsey is Solow's accumulation equation plus a household that gets to pick c at every instant instead of mechanically saving a fixed share. The savings rate becomes an output of the model, not an input.

AnalogyThink of a household with a fruit orchard. Solow says: "Every year, replant exactly 25% of your harvest, no matter what." Ramsey says: "You choose how much to eat versus replant each year, balancing your hunger now against a bigger orchard later." When the orchard is small, the extra fruit from one more planted tree is huge, so you replant aggressively; as it grows large, the marginal payoff shrinks and you eat more. The savings rate emerges from that ongoing trade-off.

6.2The Consumption Euler Equation

The heart of the model is the rule for how consumption should change over time. Intuitively, the household is constantly asking: "If I give up one unit of consumption now, save it, and consume the proceeds an instant later, am I better off?" At the optimum the answer must be a tie — otherwise the household would shift consumption around to do better. Formalizing that indifference (via the Hamiltonian, the focus of Week 7) gives the consumption Euler equation:

ċ / c = (1/θ) · ( f'(k) - δ - ρ )The growth rate of consumption equals the gap between the net return on capital f'(k) − δ and impatience ρ, scaled by 1/θ. θ (theta) is the inverse of the elasticity of intertemporal substitution.

Read it slowly. The term f'(k) − δ is the net marginal product of capital — the real interest rate the economy earns on saved output. If that return exceeds impatience ρ, saving pays more than the household's reluctance to wait, so it chooses rising consumption (ċ > 0). If the return falls short of ρ, consumption declines. When return exactly equals impatience, consumption is flat — that is the steady state.

The parameter θ controls how strongly the household responds. A large θ means consumption smoothing is prized: even a big interest-rate gap produces only a gentle tilt in the consumption path. With the common CRRA (constant relative risk aversion) utility u(c) = c^(1−θ)/(1−θ), θ is exactly the coefficient of relative risk aversion, and 1/θ is the elasticity of intertemporal substitution from Week 5.

Common mistakeThe Euler equation governs the growth rate of consumption, not its level. It tells you which direction consumption is moving, but not where to start. Choosing the starting level c(0) is a separate, delicate step handled by the saddle path (Section 6.4). Students who try to solve the model by picking an arbitrary c(0) and "letting the Euler equation run" almost always launch onto a divergent trajectory.

6.3The Steady State & the Golden Rule

The model now lives in two state variables that move together: capital k and consumption c. At a steady state both stop changing (k̇ = 0 and ċ = 0). Setting the Euler equation to zero immediately pins down steady-state capital k*:

f'(k*) = ρ + δSteady-state capital is where the net marginal product of capital equals impatience. This is the modified golden rule.

Setting k̇ = 0 then gives the matching steady-state consumption:

c* = f(k*) - (δ + n) k*In the long run the household consumes all output beyond what is needed to maintain the capital stock for current and new workers.

This is the modified golden rule. The plain "golden rule" of Solow maximized steady-state consumption and required f'(k) = δ + n. Ramsey's k* is smaller: because impatient households discount the future at ρ > 0, they rationally choose to accumulate less capital than the consumption-maximizing amount. Patience (low ρ) pushes k* up toward the golden rule; impatience pushes it down. The model thus delivers a principled answer to "how much should a society save?" — something Solow could never address.

AnalogyThe golden-rule capital stock is like the orchard size that maximizes the fruit you can eat every year forever. But a slightly impatient gardener doesn't want to wait the extra decades it takes to grow that maximal orchard — the foregone fruit during the long wait isn't worth it to someone who values today. So Ramsey's gardener settles at a somewhat smaller, sooner-reached orchard. Impatience ρ literally shrinks the optimal capital stock.

6.4The Phase Diagram & Saddle-Path Stability

To see the full dynamics we plot c against k and draw the two nullclines — the curves where each variable stops moving:

  • The ċ = 0 nullcline is the vertical line at k = k*: consumption is constant only when capital sits at the modified-golden-rule level.
  • The k̇ = 0 nullcline is the hump-shaped curve c = f(k) − (δ + n)k: for each k, the consumption level that holds capital fixed.

These two curves cross at the steady state (k*, c*) and carve the plane into four regions. Following the arrows of motion in each region reveals a saddle: most trajectories fly off — either capital crashes to zero (over-consumption) or consumption is so suppressed that capital piles up uselessly (violating the household's transversality condition). Only one trajectory in each direction actually converges to (k*, c*).

This is saddle-path stability, and it solves the level problem from Section 6.2. Capital k is a state variable: it is inherited from the past and cannot jump. Consumption c is a jump variable: the household chooses it freely at each instant. Given the inherited k(0), there is exactly one value of c(0) that places the economy on the saddle path. Pick anything higher and capital eventually crashes; pick anything lower and capital over-accumulates forever. So the household optimally jumps c onto the saddle path — and that single choice ties down the entire future.

Common mistakeSaddle-path stability does not mean the system is unstable or pathological. It means stability is conditional: the system converges only along one special trajectory. This is a feature, not a bug — it is precisely what makes the optimal consumption level determinate. Confusing "saddle" with "globally unstable" leads students to wrongly conclude the model has no solution.
✦ Worked Example — solve for the steady state

Take Cobb-Douglas production in per-worker form f(k) = k0.3 (so α = 0.3), with parameters: discount rate ρ = 0.04, depreciation δ = 0.05, population growth n = 0.01, and CRRA θ = 2. Find k*, c*, and the implied optimal savings rate — then compare it to Solow's golden rule.

  1. Marginal product: f'(k) = 0.3 · k−0.7. Set it equal to ρ + δ: 0.3 · k−0.7 = 0.04 + 0.05 = 0.09.
  2. Solve for k*: k−0.7 = 0.09 / 0.3 = 0.30, so k0.7 = 1/0.30 = 3.333, giving k* = 3.3331/0.7 = 3.3331.4286 ≈ 5.58.
  3. Output: f(k*) = 5.580.3 ≈ 1.675.
  4. Steady-state consumption: c* = f(k*) − (δ + n)k* = 1.675 − (0.06)(5.58) = 1.675 − 0.335 ≈ 1.340.
  5. Optimal savings rate: investment = (δ + n)k* = 0.335, so s* = 0.335 / 1.675 ≈ 0.200, exactly 20%. Notice this fell out of the optimization — we never assumed it.
  6. Golden-rule comparison: Solow's golden rule sets f'(k) = δ + n = 0.06, giving 0.3·k−0.7 = 0.06 → kgold = 51/0.7 ≈ 9.97 — nearly double Ramsey's k*. Because the household discounts the future (ρ = 0.04 > 0), it rationally accumulates less capital than the consumption-maximizing golden rule. Impatience has a price, and the model quantifies it.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate the saddle path in Python

We use the free QuantEcon stack — numpy, scipy, matplotlib — installed back in the Week 1/2 setup (see the install <details> in Lecture 1). Nothing new to install here; this lecture just integrates the Ramsey ODE system and shoots for the saddle path.

# ramsey_phase.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- parameters (match the worked example) ---
alpha, rho, delta, n, theta = 0.3, 0.04, 0.05, 0.01, 2.0

def f(k):  return k**alpha
def fp(k): return alpha * k**(alpha - 1)        # marginal product f'(k)

# --- steady state: f'(k*) = rho + delta ---
kstar = (alpha / (rho + delta))**(1 / (1 - alpha))
cstar = f(kstar) - (delta + n) * kstar
print(f"k* = {kstar:.3f},  c* = {cstar:.3f}")   # k* = 5.583,  c* = 1.340
print(f"optimal savings rate s* = {(delta+n)*kstar/f(kstar):.3f}")

# --- the two-equation dynamic system (k, c) ---
def ramsey(t, y):
    k, c = y
    kdot = f(k) - c - (delta + n) * k
    cdot = (c / theta) * (fp(k) - delta - rho)
    return [kdot, cdot]

# --- shooting: from k0 below k*, find c0 that lands on the saddle ---
k0 = 2.0
lo, hi = 0.01, f(k0)                  # bracket for c0
for _ in range(60):                   # bisection on the initial jump
    c0 = 0.5 * (lo + hi)
    sol = solve_ivp(ramsey, [0, 80], [k0, c0], max_step=0.05)
    kT, cT = sol.y[0, -1], sol.y[1, -1]
    if kT > kstar:                    # overshot capital -> c0 too low
        lo = c0
    else:                             # capital crashed -> c0 too high
        hi = c0
print(f"saddle-path c0 from k0={k0}: {c0:.4f}")

The bisection is a shooting method: it hunts for the one initial consumption c0 that neither lets capital crash nor overshoot — i.e. the point on the saddle path. Try plotting the converging trajectory against the two nullclines to recreate the phase diagram above, then raise ρ and watch k* shrink.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Euler equation and saddle-path logic you met here are the load-bearing beams of modern macro. Week 7 derives that Euler equation rigorously with the Hamiltonian and the transversality condition (dynamic optimization in continuous time). From there, adding random shocks to productivity turns the deterministic Ramsey model into the stochastic growth / RBC model and ultimately the DSGE frameworks central banks use to set policy. The same saddle-path stability reappears as the cornerstone of rational-expectations solution methods — and the shooting and value-function techniques carry straight into the dynamic-programming and asset-pricing courses of the Quant Finance track.

💬 Discussion

The Ramsey model says a society's optimal savings rate depends on its rate of time preference ρ — how much it discounts the welfare of the future. Should the ρ we use for long-horizon policy questions (like climate investment or infrastructure) be the same ρ that describes how individuals actually behave? Make the case either way, and explain what your choice implies for how much capital the model tells us to accumulate.

Show a model response

There is a genuine tension between a positive ρ (describing observed behavior) and a normative ρ (what an impartial planner ought to use). Individuals plainly discount the future — revealed by real interest rates of a few percent — so a descriptive model of private saving needs ρ around 0.02–0.05. But for policy that trades off our welfare against generations not yet born, many argue the only ethically defensible pure time-preference rate is near zero: a person's welfare shouldn't count for less merely because they are born later. Discounting future generations for impatience is hard to justify the way discounting your own later years is.

The Ramsey arithmetic makes the stakes concrete. From Section 6.3, f'(k*) = ρ + δ, so a lower ρ raises k* toward the golden rule: a near-zero discount rate prescribes far more accumulation (and far more climate or infrastructure investment) than a market-calibrated ρ. The headline disagreements between famous climate-economics studies are, at bottom, disagreements about exactly this parameter. My view: use a market-consistent ρ when the goal is to predict private behavior, but a much smaller pure-time-preference rate (keeping any growth-related discounting via θ) when the goal is to evaluate intergenerational policy — and always report how sensitive the answer is to that choice, because it dominates the result.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer should:

  • Distinguish positive (descriptive) from normative (prescriptive) uses of ρ.
  • Connect ρ explicitly to k* via f'(k*) = ρ + δ — lower ρ ⇒ more capital.
  • Take a defensible position rather than sitting on the fence, and acknowledge the strongest counter-argument.
  • Note the sensitivity of the policy conclusion to the chosen ρ.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The single biggest difference between the Ramsey model and the Solow model is that in Ramsey…
A. the savings rate is chosen optimally by households rather than assumed constant
B. there is no depreciation of capital
C. the production function exhibits increasing returns
D. population growth is ignored
Show answer

A. Ramsey endogenizes saving via an optimizing household; Solow takes s as an exogenous constant. (B), (C), (D) are wrong — Ramsey keeps depreciation, the same diminishing-returns f(k), and population growth n.

Q2. The consumption Euler equation ċ/c = (1/θ)(f'(k) − δ − ρ) implies that consumption is rising over time exactly when…
A. the savings rate exceeds the depreciation rate
B. the net return on capital f'(k) − δ exceeds the discount rate ρ
C. capital is above its steady-state level
D. utility is linear
Show answer

B. ċ > 0 requires f'(k) − δ − ρ > 0, i.e. the net return beats impatience. (C) is backwards — high k means low f'(k), so consumption is falling. (A) and (D) don't determine the sign of the bracket.

Q3. "Saddle-path stability" in the Ramsey model means that…
A. every initial point converges to the steady state
B. the steady state is globally unstable and the model has no solution
C. only one trajectory converges to the steady state, so c(0) must be chosen to land on it
D. the economy oscillates forever around the steady state
Show answer

C. A saddle has a unique convergent path; the household jumps consumption onto it given inherited k(0). (A) describes a stable node, not a saddle; (B) misreads conditional stability as no solution; (D) describes a center/limit cycle, which this system does not have.

Q4. The steady-state condition f'(k*) = ρ + δ tells us that, relative to Solow's golden-rule capital stock (where f'(k) = δ + n), Ramsey's k* is…
A. always larger, because households are forward-looking
B. exactly equal, regardless of ρ
C. undefined, because the Euler equation has no steady state
D. smaller whenever ρ > n, because impatience lowers optimal accumulation
Show answer

D. Ramsey requires f'(k*) = ρ + δ while golden rule needs f'(k) = δ + n; since f' is decreasing, a larger required f' (i.e. ρ > n) means a smaller k*. (A) is backwards, (B) ignores ρ's role, (C) is false — the steady state exists at f'(k*) = ρ + δ.

Q5. In the phase diagram, which variable is free to "jump" at time zero, and why?
A. Capital k, because it is inherited from the past
B. Consumption c, because it is a choice the household makes each instant given the inherited capital
C. Both jump simultaneously to the steady state
D. Neither — both are fixed forever by initial conditions
Show answer

B. c is a control/jump variable the household picks freely; k is a state variable that cannot jump because it is the accumulated stock from the past. (A) reverses the roles, (C) ignores that k cannot jump, (D) ignores that c is a free choice.
