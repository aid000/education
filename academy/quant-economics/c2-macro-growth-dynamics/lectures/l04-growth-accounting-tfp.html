Growth Accounting & Total Factor Productivity

Output grows when you add machines and workers — but the part that no machine or worker explains is the real engine of long-run prosperity.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the aggregate production function and differentiate it into a growth-accounting equation.
  • Decompose output growth into contributions from capital, labor, and the Solow residual (TFP).
  • Interpret the output elasticities (capital/labor shares) as the weights in that decomposition.
  • Explain why diminishing returns mean sustained per-capita growth must come from productivity, not capital deepening.
  • Estimate TFP and its growth rate from real macro data in Python.

4.1From a Production Function to a Growth Equation

In Weeks 1–3 you built the Solow model: output Y is produced from capital K (machines, structures, equipment) and labor L (worker-hours), with a productivity term A that scales how much output a given bundle of inputs yields. The workhorse is the Cobb–Douglas production function:

Y = A · K^(α) · L^(1−α)α = capital's output elasticity (0 < α < 1); 1−α = labor's. A is total factor productivity (TFP).

Growth accounting asks a simple but powerful question: when output rose 3% last year, how much came from more capital, how much from more labor, and how much from getting more out of the same inputs? To answer it we don't need to know the level of A — we differentiate the production function in growth rates. Taking logs and the time derivative (a "log-difference") turns products into sums:

gY = gA + α·gK + (1−α)·gLg(·) denotes a growth rate, e.g. gY = (dY/dt)/Y. The exponents become the weights.

This is the growth-accounting equation. Output growth is a weighted sum of input growth rates — capital weighted by α, labor by 1−α — plus the growth of productivity gA. Everything on the right except gA is directly measurable, which is exactly what makes the equation useful.

AnalogyThink of baking more loaves of bread. You can bake more by buying more ovens (capital) or hiring more bakers (labor) — but you can also bake more from the same ovens and bakers by discovering a better recipe or a faster process. Growth accounting is the kitchen's audit: it separates "we bought more stuff" from "we got smarter about using it." That second part is TFP.

4.2The Solow Residual: Productivity as What's Left Over

We can measure output growth gY, capital growth gK, and labor growth gL from national accounts. We can also measure α — under competitive markets, factors are paid their marginal products, so capital's income share equals its output elasticity α (empirically about 1/3 for most economies). The one thing we cannot observe directly is productivity growth. So we solve for it as a residual:

gA = gY − α·gK − (1−α)·gLThe Solow residual: measured output growth minus the part explained by measured input growth.

This leftover is the Solow residual, the standard empirical estimate of TFP growth. It captures everything that raises output without raising measured inputs: better technology, smarter organization, improved skills, more efficient allocation of resources across firms. It is famously called "a measure of our ignorance" because it absorbs both genuine technical progress and any mismeasurement of the inputs.

It is often cleaner to work in per-worker terms. Define y = Y/L and k = K/L. Cobb–Douglas in intensive form is y = A·k^(α), and its growth-accounting version is:

gy = gA + α·gkGrowth in output per worker = TFP growth + α times growth in capital per worker (capital deepening).
Common mistakeThe Solow residual is not "technology" in any narrow sense, and a positive residual is not proof of an invention. Because gA is whatever is left over, any error in measuring K, L, or α gets dumped into it. Mismeasured capacity utilization in a recession, for example, shows up as a fall in measured TFP that isn't real technological regress. Treat the residual as an accounting identity, not a clean reading of innovation.

4.3Why Long-Run Growth Must Come from Productivity

Here is the deepest lesson of the Solow framework. Suppose productivity is fixed (gA = 0) and you try to grow per-capita output purely by accumulating capital — "capital deepening." Can you sustain growth that way forever? No. Because α < 1, capital has diminishing returns: each new machine per worker adds less output than the last. The marginal product of capital falls as k rises.

MPK = ∂y/∂k = α·A·k^(α−1) → 0 as k → ∞With α < 1 the exponent α−1 is negative, so the marginal product of capital shrinks toward zero.

As economies invest, depreciation eventually eats all the new investment, capital per worker stops rising, and growth in y from capital deepening grinds to a halt — the economy reaches a steady state (Week 3). At that point, from gy = gA + α·gk, if gk = 0 then gy = gA. The only source of sustained growth in output per worker is productivity growth. Capital can give you a one-time level boost and a transition, but not a permanent growth rate.

This is why economists obsess over productivity: differences in TFP, far more than differences in capital stocks, explain why some economies are ten times richer per person than others. A country can import machines, but it cannot import the institutions, know-how, and efficient allocation that make those machines productive.

4.4Estimating TFP from Data

To put numbers on the residual you need four ingredients, all available from national accounts and labor statistics: real output growth gY, capital-stock growth gK, labor (hours) growth gL, and the capital share α. The recipe:

  1. Compute each input's growth rate, usually as a log-difference: gX ≈ ln(Xt) − ln(Xt−1).
  2. Set α to the average capital income share (capital income / GDP); often α ≈ 0.33.
  3. Form the residual gA = gY − α·gK − (1−α)·gL.

The level of TFP can then be backed out as At = Yt / (Ktα · Lt1−α), which is useful for cross-country comparisons. A subtle but important refinement, the Törnqvist/Divisia index, uses the average of the income shares in adjacent years as the weight rather than a single fixed α — this is what official statistical agencies use to allow the shares to drift over time.

✦ Worked Example — decompose a year of growth

An economy reports: real output up 3.0%, capital stock up 2.0%, hours worked up 1.0%. The capital income share is α = 0.30. Decompose the 3.0% and find TFP growth.

  1. Capital's contribution: α·gK = 0.30 × 2.0% = 0.6 pp.
  2. Labor's contribution: (1−α)·gL = 0.70 × 1.0% = 0.7 pp.
  3. Explained by inputs: 0.6 + 0.7 = 1.3 pp of the 3.0%.
  4. Solow residual (TFP growth): gA = 3.0 − 1.3 = 1.7 pp.
  5. Interpretation: only 1.3 of the 3.0 percentage points came from accumulating more inputs; the majority — 1.7 pp, over half — came from rising productivity. This economy is growing mainly by getting more out of what it has, the hallmark of healthy, sustainable growth.

🛠️ Hands-On: growth accounting in Python

We use the same free QuantEcon stack from Week 1 — Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib. (If you skipped it, the one-time install details lives in the Week 1/2 lectures.) Below we build a tiny growth-accounting routine: feed it output, capital, and labor series plus α, and it returns the Solow residual year by year.

# growth_accounting.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np

# Annual index series (illustrative): output Y, capital K, labor-hours L
Y = np.array([100.0, 103.0, 106.1, 109.3, 112.6])
K = np.array([300.0, 306.0, 312.1, 318.4, 324.7])
L = np.array([ 50.0,  50.5,  51.0,  51.5,  52.0])
alpha = 0.33                       # capital income share

# Log-difference growth rates (approx. percentage growth)
gY = np.diff(np.log(Y))
gK = np.diff(np.log(K))
gL = np.diff(np.log(L))

# Solow residual = TFP growth
gA = gY - alpha * gK - (1 - alpha) * gL

for t, (y, k, l, a) in enumerate(zip(gY, gK, gL, gA), start=1):
    print(f"yr {t}:  gY={y*100:5.2f}%  K-contrib={alpha*k*100:5.2f}  "
          f"L-contrib={(1-alpha)*l*100:5.2f}  TFP={a*100:5.2f}%")

# Average TFP growth and TFP's share of total growth
print(f"\nMean TFP growth: {gA.mean()*100:.2f}%")
print(f"TFP share of growth: {gA.sum()/gY.sum()*100:.1f}%")

# Back out the TFP LEVEL series: A = Y / (K**alpha * L**(1-alpha))
A_level = Y / (K**alpha * L**(1 - alpha))
print("TFP level index:", np.round(A_level / A_level[0] * 100, 2))

Swap in real data — output, a perpetual-inventory capital stock, and total hours — and you have replicated the core of what statistical agencies publish as "multifactor productivity." Try re-running with α = 0.30 vs 0.40 to see how sensitive the residual is to the share you assume; that sensitivity check is itself a standard part of the analysis.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Solow residual is powerful but unsatisfying — it leaves growth's main driver unexplained, falling like manna from heaven. Endogenous growth theory (later this course) makes A the result of deliberate choices: R&D, human-capital investment, and learning-by-doing, where ideas are nonrival and can generate increasing returns. Next week (Week 5) we turn to the household side — consumption, savings & intertemporal choice — which determines how much output gets reinvested as capital in the first place. Q1. In the growth-accounting equation gY = gA + α·gK + (1−α)·gL, the term gA represents…
A. growth in total factor productivity (the Solow residual)
B. growth in the capital stock
C. the depreciation rate
D. the savings rate
Show answer

A. gA is productivity growth — the part of output growth not explained by measured input growth, i.e. the Solow residual. Capital growth (B) is gK; depreciation (C) and savings (D) don't appear in this identity at all.

Q2. Under competitive markets, the exponent α in Y = A·K^α·L^(1−α) equals…
A. the depreciation rate
B. capital's share of total income
C. the population growth rate
D. always exactly 0.5
Show answer

B. If factors are paid their marginal products, capital's output elasticity α equals capital's income share (empirically about 1/3). It is not the depreciation rate (A), population growth (C), and is generally near 0.33, not 0.5 (D).

Q3. Why can capital deepening alone not sustain growth in output per worker forever?
A. because labor always grows faster than capital
B. because the capital share α exceeds 1
C. because capital has diminishing returns (α < 1), so its marginal product falls toward zero
D. because more capital reduces total output
Show answer

C. With α < 1, each extra unit of capital per worker adds less than the last, so MPK falls and growth from deepening peters out at the steady state. Labor growth (A) isn't the reason; α is between 0 and 1, not >1 (B); and more capital raises, not lowers, output (D).

Q4. An economy has gY = 4%, gK = 3%, gL = 2%, and α = 0.5. What is the Solow residual (TFP growth)?
A. 4.0%
B. 2.5%
C. 1.0%
D. 1.5%
Show answer

D. gA = gY − α·gK − (1−α)·gL = 4 − 0.5×3 − 0.5×2 = 4 − 1.5 − 1.0 = 1.5%. Answer C forgets the labor term; B and A misapply the weights.

Q5. The Solow residual is sometimes called "a measure of our ignorance" because…
A. it is always negative
B. it is computed as a leftover and absorbs both true productivity and any input mismeasurement
C. it can never be estimated from data
D. it only applies to agricultural economies
Show answer

B. Because gA is whatever is left after subtracting measured input contributions, anything we mismeasure — utilization, capital, the share α — lands in it alongside genuine technical change. It is routinely positive (A), is estimated all the time (C), and applies to any economy (D).
