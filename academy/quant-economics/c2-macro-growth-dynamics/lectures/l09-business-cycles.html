Business Cycles & Stochastic Dynamics

Growth tells you where the economy is heading; shocks decide how bumpy the ride will be — and the same model can capture both.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish short-run fluctuations (the cycle) from the long-run trend (growth), and define detrending.
  • Add a stochastic productivity shock modeled as an AR(1) process to the deterministic growth model.
  • State the real-business-cycle (RBC) idea: cycles as the optimal response to technology shocks.
  • Read an impulse-response function and explain persistence and propagation.
  • Define the stochastic steady state, then simulate the model and interpret the output series.

9.1Trend vs. Cycle: Two Questions in One Series

Every macro time series you have studied so far — output, capital, consumption — has trended upward for decades. That smooth upward drift is long-run growth, the subject of the Solow and Ramsey models from earlier weeks. But if you zoom in, the series wobbles: it speeds up in booms and contracts in recessions. Those wobbles around the trend are the business cycle.

The key insight of this week is that these are not two separate phenomena requiring two separate models. We can take the very same dynamic growth model and ask a second question of it: what happens when the economy is repeatedly knocked off its path by random disturbances? To answer it, we first need to separate trend from cycle — a step called detrending.

ŷ_t = log(Y_t) - trend_tThe cyclical component ŷ_t is the log of output minus a smooth estimated trend (e.g., from an HP filter). It oscillates around zero.

Working in logs matters: a log deviation is approximately a percentage deviation from trend, so a value of ŷ_t = -0.02 means output is about 2% below its long-run path — a mild recession. Detrending turns a steadily climbing series into a stationary one that hovers around zero, which is exactly the object a cycle model tries to explain.

AnalogyThink of a child walking up a long staircase while bouncing a ball. The staircase is the growth trend — it only ever goes up. The ball's bobbing up and down relative to the child's hand is the business cycle. Detrending is just measuring the ball's height relative to the hand instead of relative to the ground floor: it strips away the climb so you can study the bounce.

9.2A Stochastic Shock: The AR(1) Process

What knocks the economy around? In the real-business-cycle view, the dominant force is total factor productivity (TFP) — the same A from your growth-accounting week (Lecture 4). In the deterministic model A grew at a fixed rate. Now we let it be random. The standard choice is a first-order autoregressive process, the AR(1), applied to log-productivity z_t = log(A_t):

z_{t+1} = ρ z_t + ε_{t+1},   ε_{t+1} ~ N(0, σ^2)z_t is log-TFP as a deviation from its mean; ρ (rho) is persistence in [0,1); ε is an i.i.d. mean-zero "innovation" with standard deviation σ (sigma).

This tiny equation does an enormous amount of work. Each period a fresh random shock ε arrives — a good harvest, a new process improvement, a supply disruption. The coefficient ρ controls persistence: how much of today's productivity carries into tomorrow. If ρ is near 1, a single good shock lingers for many periods; if ρ = 0, productivity is white noise and yesterday tells you nothing about today.

Because |ρ| < 1, the process is stationary — it always drifts back toward its mean of zero. Its unconditional variance has a clean closed form, which you will use when calibrating σ to match data:

Var(z) = σ^2 / (1 - ρ^2)Higher persistence ρ or larger innovations σ both make productivity more volatile. This is the link between a structural shock and an observed cycle's amplitude.
Common mistakeDo not confuse ρ (persistence) with σ (innovation size). σ controls how big each new surprise is; ρ controls how long surprises last. Two economies can have identical σ yet wildly different cycles: the one with higher ρ turns small shocks into long, drawn-out booms and slumps because the disturbances accumulate before fading.

9.3The RBC Idea & Impulse Responses

Bolting the AR(1) onto the optimizing growth model gives the real-business-cycle (RBC) model. Its radical claim: cycles are not "failures" of the economy to be smoothed away — they are the optimal response of forward-looking households and firms to real technology shocks. When a positive productivity shock hits, capital is temporarily more productive, so it is optimal to work more, invest more, and consume more; as the shock decays, all of these glide back. The model's law of motion combines the capital accumulation equation with the productivity process:

k_{t+1} = g(k_t, z_t),   z_{t+1} = ρ z_t + ε_{t+1}g is the policy function for next-period capital (solved numerically in Lecture 8). The economy's state is the pair (k_t, z_t): how much capital you have and how productive it is.

To see how a single shock propagates, we trace an impulse-response function (IRF): start at the steady state, hit the system with one unit shock at t = 0 (and no shocks after), and plot the path each variable follows back to rest. The IRF is the model's fingerprint — it shows both the size of the immediate response and the speed of the return.

Notice the economics in the picture: output (green) moves less than productivity (blue) on impact because capital cannot jump, but it lingers longer because the extra investment slowly builds and then slowly depreciates. This internal propagation — converting a sharp, short shock into a smooth, drawn-out cycle — is the part of the RBC mechanism that economists scrutinize most.

9.4The Stochastic Steady State & Simulation

In a deterministic model the economy converges to a single point: the steady state where k stops changing. Once shocks arrive every period, the economy never sits still — so what replaces the steady state? The answer is the stochastic steady state: not a point but a probability distribution. After enough periods the economy forgets its starting point and settles into a stable distribution over states (k, z) — sometimes called the stationary or ergodic distribution.

(k_t, z_t) -> stationary distribution π(k, z) as t -> infinityLong-run averages of simulated paths converge to the means of this distribution. "Steady state" becomes a cloud, not a dot.

Two practical consequences. First, the mean of the stochastic steady state is generally not the deterministic steady state: because the policy function is curved (concave production), good and bad shocks do not cancel exactly — a subtle effect related to precautionary behavior. Second, we study the model by simulation: draw a long sequence of shocks ε_t, push them through the law of motion, and read summary statistics — standard deviations (volatility), autocorrelations (persistence), and cross-correlations (co-movement) — straight off the simulated series.

These simulated moments are exactly what you compare against detrended data. If the model's simulated output volatility and the correlation of consumption with output roughly match what you measured in the real series, the model is doing its job. That comparison — matching second moments — is the empirical heart of quantitative business-cycle research.

AnalogyA deterministic steady state is a ball resting at the bottom of a bowl. The stochastic steady state is that same ball with a tiny motor constantly flicking it in random directions: it never stops moving, but it spends predictable fractions of its time at each height. You stop asking "where does it end up?" and start asking "what's the distribution of where it tends to be?"
✦ Worked Example — persistence, volatility, and a one-step forecast

Log-TFP follows z_{t+1} = 0.9 z_t + ε_{t+1} with ε ~ N(0, σ^2) and σ = 0.01. Suppose today z_t = 0.02 (productivity 2% above trend). Compute (a) the unconditional standard deviation of z, (b) the expected value of z next period, and (c) the half-life of a shock.

  1. Unconditional variance: Var(z) = σ² / (1 - ρ²) = 0.0001 / (1 - 0.81) = 0.0001 / 0.19 ≈ 0.000526.
  2. Unconditional std dev: √0.000526 ≈ 0.0229, i.e. log-TFP typically swings about 2.3% around trend.
  3. One-step forecast: E[z_{t+1} | z_t] = ρ z_t = 0.9 × 0.02 = 0.018. The 2% surplus is expected to shrink to 1.8% next period — mean reversion in action.
  4. Half-life: a shock decays like ρ^h. Set 0.9^h = 0.5 → h = ln(0.5)/ln(0.9) = (-0.693)/(-0.105) ≈ 6.6 periods for half the shock to fade.
  5. Interpretation: with ρ = 0.9 the economy is quite persistent — a single productivity surprise still has half its force after roughly 6 to 7 quarters, which is why one shock can produce a multi-year cycle.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate an AR(1) and read its moments

We use the free QuantEcon + NumPy + Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the install <details> in the early lectures). QuantEcon ships a ready-made AR(1) / linear-state-space helper, but here we simulate the process directly so every step is visible, then check the moments against the closed-form formulas from this lecture.

# ar1_business_cycle.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

rho, sigma, T = 0.9, 0.01, 2000
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# simulate z_{t+1} = rho*z_t + eps_{t+1}
z = np.zeros(T)
eps = rng.normal(0.0, sigma, size=T)
for t in range(T - 1):
    z[t + 1] = rho * z[t] + eps[t + 1]

# compare simulated vs theoretical unconditional std dev
theo_sd = sigma / np.sqrt(1 - rho**2)
print(f"theoretical sd = {theo_sd:.4f}")     # 0.0229
print(f"simulated   sd = {z.std():.4f}")      # ~0.0229

# first-order autocorrelation should be ~ rho
print(f"autocorr(1)   = {np.corrcoef(z[:-1], z[1:])[0,1]:.3f}")  # ~0.9

# impulse response: one unit shock, then let it decay
H = 20
irf = rho ** np.arange(H)                      # 1, rho, rho^2, ...
plt.plot(irf, marker="o"); plt.axhline(0, lw=.8)
plt.title("IRF of log-TFP to a unit shock"); plt.xlabel("periods"); plt.show()

The simulated standard deviation and autocorrelation should land right on the theoretical values — your check that the code matches the math. Next, raise rho to 0.97 and watch the IRF stretch out and the volatility climb. In Lecture 10 you will feed this z series into the solved policy function to generate output, consumption, and investment paths for a full RBC simulation.

💬 Discussion

The RBC model treats recessions as the economy's optimal response to bad productivity shocks — implying, provocatively, that downturns are efficient and policy intervention could only make things worse. Do you find this convincing? Identify one feature of real recessions that the pure RBC story explains well, and one that it struggles with. What would you add to the model to address the gap — and what evidence would convince you the addition is needed?

Show a model response

Strong sample answer. The RBC model explains co-movement remarkably well: in the data, output, consumption, investment, and hours worked all rise and fall together, and a single productivity shock that makes capital and labor jointly more valuable produces exactly that pattern — investment more volatile than output, consumption smoother. That is a genuine success and a high bar for any alternative.

Where it struggles: the model needs negative aggregate technology shocks to generate recessions, yet it is hard to point to episodes where society literally "forgot" how to produce. It also has no role for involuntary unemployment, demand shortfalls, or money — and the data show strong correlations between monetary/credit conditions and cycles that a purely real model cannot speak to.

To address the gap I would add nominal rigidities (sticky prices/wages) and a monetary policy rule, turning the RBC core into a New Keynesian DSGE model in which demand shocks and policy genuinely affect output. The evidence that would convince me this is needed: monetary-policy surprises that move real output, and recessions where measured TFP barely falls while hours collapse — both pointing to a mechanism beyond technology.

Q1. In the process z_{t+1} = ρ z_t + ε_{t+1}, the parameter ρ controls…
A. how persistent a shock is — how much of today's deviation carries into tomorrow
B. the size of each new random innovation
C. the long-run growth rate of the trend
D. the depreciation rate of capital
Show answer

A. ρ is the persistence coefficient: a higher ρ makes shocks linger. The innovation size is σ (B); ρ is not a growth rate (C) — the process is stationary with mean zero — nor depreciation (D).

Q2. Detrending a macro series in logs is done so that…
A. the trend grows faster
B. the cyclical component becomes stationary and reads as a percentage deviation from trend
C. shocks are removed entirely from the data
D. consumption equals investment
Show answer

B. Subtracting a smooth trend from log output yields a stationary series that hovers around zero, where each value is approximately a percent deviation from trend. It does not change the trend's speed (A) or erase the cycle (C) — the cycle is exactly what's left.

Q3. For an AR(1) with ρ = 0.8 and σ = 0.02, the unconditional standard deviation of z is closest to…
A. 0.020
B. 0.016
C. 0.033
D. 0.400
Show answer

C. Var(z) = σ²/(1-ρ²) = 0.0004/(1-0.64) = 0.0004/0.36 ≈ 0.00111; the std dev is √0.00111 ≈ 0.033. Choice A ignores persistence (just σ); B is below σ, impossible here; D confuses variance scaling.

Q4. The core claim of the real-business-cycle (RBC) model is that fluctuations are…
A. caused mainly by money-supply mistakes
B. proof that markets fail and must be smoothed by policy
C. purely random noise with no economic structure
D. the optimal response of forward-looking agents to real productivity shocks
Show answer

D. RBC explains cycles as the efficient, optimal reaction to real (technology) shocks. It deliberately excludes money (A) and argues cycles are not failures to be smoothed (B); the dynamics are highly structured, not pure noise (C).

Q5. Once stochastic shocks are added every period, the "stochastic steady state" of the model is best described as…
A. a single fixed point the economy converges to
B. a stationary probability distribution over states the economy settles into
C. the moment all shocks become zero
D. the highest level of output ever reached
Show answer

B. With shocks every period the economy never rests at a point; it settles into a stable (ergodic) distribution over states. A fixed point (A) is the deterministic case; shocks never permanently vanish (C); and the steady state is about the long-run distribution, not a peak (D).
