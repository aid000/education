Convergence & the Golden Rule

The same diminishing returns that pin down a steady state also tell poor economies to grow faster — and warn rich ones that you really can save too much.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace the transition path by which capital per worker moves toward the Solow steady state.
  • Distinguish absolute from conditional convergence and explain what each predicts about poor vs rich countries.
  • Derive the golden-rule savings rate that maximizes steady-state consumption per worker.
  • Diagnose dynamic inefficiency — when an economy over-saves and could consume more forever by saving less.
  • Simulate transition dynamics and locate the golden-rule capital stock numerically with QuantEcon and SciPy.

3.1How an Economy Moves Toward Its Steady State

Last week we found the Solow steady state — the capital stock per worker k* where investment exactly replaces what depreciation and a growing workforce erode. But almost no real economy starts at its steady state. The interesting question is dynamic: starting from some k₀ below or above k*, how does the economy travel toward it?

The engine is the law of motion for capital per worker. With output per worker f(k)=k^α, a constant savings rate s, depreciation rate δ, and labor-force growth n, the change in capital per worker each period is saving minus "break-even" investment:

Δk = s·f(k) − (n + δ)·kCapital deepens when actual investment s·f(k) exceeds the (n+δ)·k needed just to equip new workers and replace worn-out machines.

Because f has diminishing returns (α < 1), the saving curve s·f(k) is steep at low k and flattens out, while break-even investment (n+δ)·k is a straight line. Where the curve sits above the line, Δk > 0 and capital grows; where it sits below, Δk < 0 and capital shrinks. They cross exactly once at k* — and that crossing is stable: the gap shrinks from either side.

The crucial consequence is the speed of adjustment. Far below k*, the vertical gap between the saving curve and the line is large, so capital — and therefore output — grows fast. As k nears k*, the gap closes and growth decelerates toward zero. Growth is not constant along the path; it is fastest when you are furthest behind.

3.2Absolute vs Conditional Convergence

That "grows faster when poorer" property is the model's headline empirical claim — convergence. But it comes in two flavors, and the difference is the single most-tested idea in growth economics.

Absolute (unconditional) convergence says: poor economies grow faster than rich ones, full stop, so income levels eventually catch up. This holds only if all economies share the same steady state — same s, n, δ, and technology — so that the only thing separating them is where they currently sit on the same diagram.

Conditional convergence drops that assumption. Each economy converges to its own steady state, set by its own parameters. The prediction becomes: an economy grows faster the further it is below its own k* — not below everyone else's. A country can be poor yet near its own (low) steady state, in which case it grows slowly, not fast.

growth rate of y ≈ β · ( log y* − log y )Convergence regression: a country closes a fraction β of its own gap to its own steady-state income y* each period. β > 0 is the signature of convergence.

Empirically, absolute convergence fails across the whole world — poor countries have not uniformly caught up — but conditional convergence holds well once you control for savings rates, population growth, and human capital. That single distinction reconciles the Solow model with the data and is why it is still the workhorse of cross-country growth analysis.

AnalogyThink of cars on a highway all heading to different exits. Absolute convergence would say every car ends at the same exit, so whoever is farthest back drives fastest and they all bunch up. Conditional convergence says each car has its own exit: the one farthest from its exit accelerates hardest, but a car already near its (early) exit cruises slowly. Distance-to-your-own-destination, not distance-to-the-leader, sets your speed.
Common mistakeDo not read "poor countries grow faster" as a law of nature. The Solow model predicts faster growth only relative to a country's own steady state. Two equally poor countries with different savings rates head for different destinations — and the one nearer its own k* will grow slower, not faster. Mixing up absolute and conditional convergence is the classic exam trap.

3.3The Golden-Rule Savings Rate

Higher saving builds a bigger capital stock and more output per worker in steady state. So should a society save as much as possible? No — because people consume what they don't save. The honest objective is to maximize steady-state consumption per worker, not output. That trade-off has an interior optimum: the golden rule.

In steady state, investment must cover break-even, so consumption per worker is output minus break-even investment:

c*(k) = f(k) − (n + δ)·kSteady-state consumption = what's produced minus what must be reinvested to hold k constant. We choose the k* (via s) that makes this biggest.

Maximize over the steady-state capital stock by setting the derivative to zero. The marginal product of capital must equal the break-even slope:

f'(k_gold) = n + δGolden rule: at the consumption-maximizing capital stock, the extra output from one more unit of capital exactly equals the cost of equipping/replacing it. (With f=k^α: k_gold = (α/(n+δ))^(1/(1−α)).)

Geometrically, this is the point on the Solow diagram where the production function f(k) has the same slope as the break-even line (n+δ)k — the place where the vertical gap between output and break-even investment (which is consumption) is widest. Save the rate s_gold that lands the steady state exactly there, and the economy enjoys the highest sustainable consumption forever.

3.4Over-Saving and Dynamic Inefficiency

The golden rule has a startling corollary. Because consumption rises and then falls as the steady-state capital stock grows past k_gold, it is entirely possible to save too much. An economy whose savings rate pushes its steady state above k_gold is said to be dynamically inefficient.

What makes this special is the word "forever." If k* > k_gold, then cutting the savings rate permanently raises consumption per worker — not just eventually, but in every single period, including the transition. You consume more now (saving less) and more later (the lower steady state still has higher consumption). There is no trade-off at all; it is a free lunch. This is unambiguously wasteful: capital so abundant that its marginal product f'(k) has fallen below n+δ, so each extra machine fails even to pay for its own upkeep.

k* > k_gold ⟺ f'(k*) < n + δ ⟺ dynamically inefficientOver-accumulation: capital is so deep its marginal product no longer covers depreciation plus workforce growth. Saving less raises consumption in every period.

Contrast this with the more common case k* < k_gold (under-saving). There, raising saving does increase long-run consumption, but only by sacrificing consumption today — a genuine intertemporal trade-off. That is efficient, just a matter of how patient the society is. Empirically most economies sit on the under-saving side, so dynamic inefficiency is rare in practice — but it is the model's sharpest warning that "more capital" is not an unconditional good, and it reappears in the overlapping-generations models and pension-system debates of later weeks.

Common mistakeDynamic inefficiency is not just "saving more than the golden rule lowers steady-state consumption." The deeper, defining point is that fixing it requires no sacrifice — consumption rises in every period. By contrast, under-saving (k* < k_gold) can only be fixed by giving up consumption today. Confusing the two cases hides the entire reason dynamic inefficiency is considered pure waste.
✦ Worked Example — find the golden rule and check for over-saving

An economy has f(k)=k^0.5 (so α = 0.5), depreciation δ = 0.05, labor growth n = 0.01, and currently saves s = 0.6. Find the golden-rule capital stock and savings rate, then determine whether this economy is dynamically inefficient.

  1. Golden-rule capital: set f'(k) = n + δ. Here f'(k) = 0.5·k^(−0.5) = 0.06, so k^(−0.5) = 0.12, giving k_gold = (1/0.12)² ≈ 69.4.
  2. Golden-rule savings rate: at any steady state s·f(k*) = (n+δ)·k*, so s = (n+δ)·k* / f(k*). For the Cobb–Douglas case there is a shortcut: s_gold = α = 0.5.
  3. Current steady state: with s = 0.6, solve 0.6·k^0.5 = 0.06·k → k^0.5 = 10 → k* = 100.
  4. Compare: k* = 100 > k_gold ≈ 69.4, so the economy is saving past the golden rule. Equivalently s = 0.6 > s_gold = 0.5.
  5. Diagnosis — dynamically inefficient. Cutting s from 0.6 to 0.5 raises consumption per worker in every period: c at k=100 is 10 − 0.06·100 = 4.0, while c at k_gold≈69.4 is √69.4 − 0.06·69.4 ≈ 8.33 − 4.17 = 4.16 — higher, with no sacrifice along the way.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate the transition & locate the golden rule

We reuse the free QuantEcon + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the one-time install details lives in those lectures — no new packages are needed here). Below we iterate the law of motion to watch convergence, then use scipy.optimize to pin down the golden-rule capital stock.

# golden_rule.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

alpha, delta, n = 0.5, 0.05, 0.01   # f(k)=k^alpha; depreciation; labor growth
f      = lambda k: k**alpha
f_prime = lambda k: alpha * k**(alpha - 1)

# --- 1. transition dynamics for a given savings rate ---
def simulate(s, k0, T=200):
    k = np.empty(T)
    k[0] = k0
    for t in range(T - 1):
        k[t+1] = k[t] + s*f(k[t]) - (n + delta)*k[t]   # Delta k = s f(k) - (n+d)k
    return k

path = simulate(s=0.6, k0=5.0)        # start poor, watch it climb
print("k* reached:", round(path[-1], 2))   # -> 100.0

# --- 2. golden rule: solve f'(k) = n + delta ---
k_gold = brentq(lambda k: f_prime(k) - (n + delta), 1e-6, 1e6)
s_gold = (n + delta) * k_gold / f(k_gold)   # = alpha for Cobb-Douglas
print("k_gold:", round(k_gold, 2), " s_gold:", round(s_gold, 3))  # -> 69.44  0.5

# --- 3. is s=0.6 dynamically inefficient? ---
c = lambda k: f(k) - (n + delta)*k
print("c at s=0.6 steady state:", round(c(path[-1]), 3))   # 4.0
print("c at golden rule       :", round(c(k_gold), 3))     # 4.17  -> higher!

The simulation shows fast early growth that decelerates near k* — the convergence property in action. Then the optimizer confirms s = 0.6 over-saves: consumption is strictly higher at the golden rule. Try sweeping s from 0.1 to 0.9, plotting steady-state c against s with Matplotlib, and watch the hump peak exactly at s = α.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Solow model treats the savings rate as a dial we turn from outside. Next week's growth accounting (Week 4) measures how much of real growth comes from capital deepening versus the residual we call total factor productivity — and reveals that, in the long run, it's technology, not saving, that drives sustained growth. Later, endogenous growth and overlapping-generations models let households choose saving optimally over time, turning the golden rule into a full intertemporal optimization (the modified golden rule with discounting) — the same dynamic-programming machinery that powers the consumption-and-portfolio problems in the Quant Finance track.

💬 Discussion

Across the world, absolute convergence fails: many poor countries have not caught up to rich ones, and some have fallen further behind. Yet the Solow model insists that a country below its steady state should grow fast. Reconcile these. Using the absolute-vs-conditional distinction, explain why a poor country might grow slowly without contradicting the model — and what a policymaker who wants faster growth should actually change. Is "just save more" always good advice?

Show a model response

The contradiction dissolves once we separate level from destination. The Solow model predicts faster growth only relative to a country's own steady state, which is set by its savings rate, population growth, depreciation, human capital, and the efficiency with which it uses technology. A poor country with low s, high n, and weak institutions has a low k* — so even though its income is low, it may already be near its own steady state and therefore grow slowly. That is conditional convergence: the data confirm a positive convergence coefficient β once we control for these determinants, even though raw, uncontrolled (absolute) convergence is absent.

For a policymaker, the lever is therefore the steady state itself, not just the gap to it. Raising the savings/investment rate, slowing population growth, and — above all — improving total factor productivity (institutions, education, openness to technology) shifts k* upward and lifts the whole transition path. But "just save more" is not unconditionally good: a country already saving beyond the golden rule (k* > k_gold) is dynamically inefficient, and more saving would lower consumption in every period. And even below the golden rule, higher saving only raises long-run consumption by costing consumption today — an intertemporal trade-off, not a free lunch. The honest target is the golden rule, where f'(k) = n + δ, after which TFP — next week's topic — does the heavy lifting for sustained growth.

Q1. In the Solow model, why does an economy starting below k* grow fastest at first and then slow down?
A. The savings rate rises automatically as capital accumulates
B. Diminishing returns make the gap between s·f(k) and (n+δ)k largest when k is small, and the gap shrinks as k nears k*
C. Depreciation falls as the capital stock grows
D. Population growth speeds up over time
Show answer

B. Δk = s·f(k) − (n+δ)k; the vertical gap (hence growth) is biggest far below k* and closes as k rises. A is wrong — s is constant. C and D invert reality: δ and n are constant parameters here.

Q2. "Poor countries grow faster than rich ones only after controlling for savings rates, population growth, and human capital." This describes…
A. absolute (unconditional) convergence
B. divergence
C. conditional convergence
D. the golden rule
Show answer

C. Conditional convergence: each country approaches its own steady state, so the catch-up appears only after controlling for the determinants of that steady state. Absolute convergence (A) would need no controls and fails in the data. D is a savings-rate concept, not a convergence one.

Q3. The golden-rule capital stock is defined by which condition?
A. f'(k) = n + δ
B. s·f(k) = 0
C. f(k) = (n + δ)k
D. the savings rate equals 1
Show answer

A. Maximizing c* = f(k) − (n+δ)k gives f'(k) = n + δ. C would mean zero consumption (all output reinvested); B describes no saving at all; D (save everything) leaves nothing to consume.

Q4. An economy has a steady state with k* > k_gold. Which statement is TRUE?
A. It is under-saving and should save more
B. It maximizes steady-state output, which is the right goal
C. Raising consumption requires sacrificing consumption today
D. It is dynamically inefficient: cutting the savings rate raises consumption per worker in every period, with no sacrifice
Show answer

D. k* > k_gold means f'(k*) < n+δ — capital over-accumulated. Lowering s raises consumption now and in the new lower steady state: a free lunch. A is backwards; B targets output not consumption; C describes the under-saving case (k* < k_gold).

Q5. For f(k)=k^α with α = 0.4, what is the golden-rule savings rate?
A. 0.4
B. 0.6
C. 1.0
D. it depends on n and δ separately
Show answer

A. For Cobb–Douglas, s_gold = α. At the golden rule (n+δ)k = f'(k)·k = α·f(k), so saving s·f(k) = (n+δ)k implies s = α = 0.4. The n and δ values cancel out, so D is wrong; B is the capital share's complement (labor share); C would consume nothing.
