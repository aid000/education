Week 10 · Lecture 10

Capstone: Calibrate & Solve a Growth Model

Ten weeks of theory converge into one task: take a real economy, pin a growth model to its numbers, solve it on a laptop, and let it forecast what a policy change would do.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Calibrate a growth model's deep parameters (α, δ, n, ρ, θ) to real macro data using long-run moments.
  • Solve the calibrated model numerically for its steady state and full transition path.
  • Run a transition experiment: shock the economy off steady state and trace its convergence back.
  • Run a policy experiment: change a parameter, recompute, and quantify the effect on output, consumption, and welfare.
  • Frame and structure the capstone deliverable — a calibrated, solved, and validated growth-model report.

10.1Putting It Together: What a Capstone Actually Is

For nine weeks you built the pieces one at a time: Solow's accumulation equation (Weeks 2–3), growth accounting and the Solow residual (Week 4), the consumption–savings trade-off (Week 5), the Ramsey household's optimal path (Week 6), and the stochastic business-cycle layer (Week 9). The capstone is where those pieces stop being separate exercises and become a single working artifact: a model that has been made to agree with a real economy and then used to answer a counterfactual question.

Every quantitative macro study follows the same three-step arc, and your capstone is a small instance of it:

Calibrate  ->  Solve  ->  ExperimentPin parameters to data, compute the model's equilibrium path, then perturb something and re-solve to read off the counterfactual.

The deliverable is not "a clever derivation." It is a short, honest report that says: here is my model, here are the numbers I fed it and why, here is what it predicts, and here is how I checked that the prediction is not nonsense. That last clause — validation — is what separates a model that informs a decision from one that merely decorates a slide.

AnalogyThink of the model as a flight simulator for an economy. Calibration is tuning the simulator so that, flown level, it matches the real aircraft's cruise speed and fuel burn — its long-run behavior. Solving is letting the simulator actually fly. An experiment is asking "what if we cut the engine thrust by 10%?" — you change one dial and watch the trajectory bend. You'd never bet a real flight plan on a simulator you never checked against the real plane; calibration is that check.

10.2Calibration: Tying the Model to Real Data

Calibration means choosing the model's deep parameters so that, at its steady state, the model reproduces a handful of well-measured long-run features of the economy — the great ratios and average growth rates that macroeconomists treat as stable targets. We do not fit every wiggle; we match a few robust moments and trust the theory for the rest.

For the calibrated Ramsey-Solow model from Weeks 2–6, the workhorse mapping is:

α = capital share of income  ≈  0.33In Cobb-Douglas Y = K^α (AL)^(1-α), the exponent α equals capital's share of national income, read straight off the national accounts.
f'(k*) = ρ + δ  and  α / (capital-output ratio) = ρ + δThe steady-state Euler condition. With K/Y observed (~3 in annual data) and δ pinned from depreciation data, this equation closes for ρ.

The standard recipe, in order:

  • α from the labor/capital income split (~1/3 capital, 2/3 labor).
  • n from the long-run labor-force growth rate; g (technology growth) from trend per-capita output growth (~2%/yr).
  • δ from the ratio of depreciation to the capital stock (~5–10%/yr).
  • ρ backed out from the steady-state interest condition f'(k*) = ρ + δ once K/Y is known.
  • θ from micro evidence on the elasticity of intertemporal substitution (Week 5), typically θ ≈ 1–2.

The point of calibration is discipline: every number has a real-world source you can defend, so the model's later predictions inherit that credibility instead of being free-floating assumptions.

Common mistakeDo not confuse calibration with estimation. Calibration sets parameters to match a few long-run targets by construction and then judges the model by how well it fits moments you did not target. Estimation (econometrics) chooses parameters to minimize fit error over the whole sample. Reporting an R-squared on the data you calibrated to is double-counting — the model was built to hit those moments, so a good fit there proves nothing.

10.3Solving Numerically & Running the Transition

With parameters fixed, "solving" the model means two things: finding the steady state (an algebra/root-finding problem) and computing the transition path from some starting capital k(0) to that steady state (a dynamic problem). For Solow the transition is a simple forward iteration of k̇ = s·f(k) − (δ + n + g)k. For Ramsey it is a two-point boundary-value problem — you must find the unique initial consumption c(0) that lands the economy on the saddle path (Week 6), which we solve by shooting.

Once the model can be solved, a transition experiment is straightforward: start the economy below steady state (say at half its steady-state capital, as after a war or a catch-up takeoff) and watch how fast it converges. The speed is governed by the convergence rate β from Week 3:

k(t) - k*  ≈  ( k(0) - k* ) · e^(-β t),  with  β ≈ (1-α)(δ + n + g)Near the steady state the gap shrinks exponentially. With α=1/3 and (δ+n+g)≈0.08, β≈0.053 — about a 5%/yr closing of the gap, the empirical "iron law of convergence."

The same machinery powers a policy experiment: change one parameter — a higher savings rate, a lower tax wedge on capital (which raises the effective ρ households face), or a one-time jump in TFP — recompute the new steady state, and trace the new transition. The difference between the baseline path and the policy path is the policy's effect, period by period. This is the core technique behind every quantitative answer to "what would this reform do to growth?"

AnalogyA policy experiment is a controlled lab swap. You run the model once as the baseline (the control), freeze that trajectory, then change a single ingredient and run it again (the treatment). Subtracting the two paths isolates the effect of that one change — exactly like a chemist running two identical reactions and varying only the temperature. If you change three dials at once, you can no longer attribute the result to any one of them.

10.4Interpreting Results & Framing the Report

A solved model spits out numbers; your job is to turn them into a claim a reader can trust. Three habits make the difference. First, report levels and rates separately: a savings-rate hike raises the level of long-run output but, in Solow, leaves the long-run growth rate unchanged (only g shifts the rate) — confusing the two is the classic error. Second, report the transition timing: a reform that doubles output "eventually" but takes 60 years to do half of it is a very different policy from one that pays off in a decade. Third, translate output gains into welfare, using the household utility you calibrated, since more capital bought by crushing today's consumption is not obviously a gain.

Welfare is naturally expressed as a consumption-equivalent variation (CEV): the constant percentage by which baseline consumption would have to rise, in every period, to make the household as well off as the policy does.

CEV:  V_policy = V_baseline( c · (1 + λ) )  => solve for λλ is the permanent % consumption boost equivalent to the reform. Positive λ means the policy raises welfare; a level gain financed by painful early saving can still give λ < 0.

The report itself should be short and reproducible. A clean structure: (1) the question, (2) the model and its equations, (3) a calibration table — every parameter, its value, and its data source, (4) results: steady state, baseline transition, and the policy/transition experiment with figures, (5) validation against untargeted moments, and (6) a candid limitations paragraph. Anyone should be able to re-run your code and land on your numbers.

Common mistakeDo not declare victory because output rises. A higher savings rate always raises steady-state capital and output in this class of models — that is mechanical, not insight. The real question is whether welfare rises, accounting for the consumption you sacrifice during the transition. A capstone that reports only the output gain and ignores the welfare cost of the transition has answered the easy half of the question and skipped the hard half.
✦ Worked Example — calibrate, solve, and run a savings policy

Calibrate a Solow model in per-effective-worker terms with Cobb-Douglas f(k) = kα. Targets: capital share α = 1/3, depreciation δ = 0.05, population growth n = 0.01, technology growth g = 0.02. The economy currently saves s = 0.20. A reform proposes raising the savings rate to s′ = 0.25. Find the old and new steady states and the long-run output gain.

  1. Steady-state condition: set k̇ = 0 in k̇ = s·kα − (δ+n+g)k, giving s·kα = (δ+n+g)k, so k* = [ s / (δ+n+g) ]1/(1−α).
  2. Effective depreciation: δ + n + g = 0.05 + 0.01 + 0.02 = 0.08.
  3. Baseline k* (s = 0.20): k* = (0.20/0.08)1/(1−1/3) = (2.5)1.5 ≈ 3.953. Output y* = k*1/3 = 3.9530.333 ≈ 1.581.
  4. Policy k* (s′ = 0.25): k*′ = (0.25/0.08)1.5 = (3.125)1.5 ≈ 5.524. Output y*′ = 5.5240.333 ≈ 1.768.
  5. Long-run output gain: y*′ / y* = 1.768 / 1.581 ≈ 1.118 — about an 11.8% higher long-run output level. Shortcut check: y*′/y* = (s′/s)α/(1−α) = (1.25)0.5 = 1.118. ✓
  6. Convergence speed: β ≈ (1−α)(δ+n+g) = (2/3)(0.08) ≈ 0.053, so the economy closes ~5.3% of the gap to the new k* per year — roughly a 13-year half-life. The 11.8% gain is real but slow; consumption actually dips in the early years while the extra 5% of output is diverted into capital. That early dip is exactly the welfare cost a CEV calculation would price in (Section 10.4).

🛠️ Hands-On: calibrate, solve, and run the policy experiment

We use the free QuantEcon stack — numpy, scipy, matplotlib — installed back in the Week 1/2 setup (see the install <details> in Lecture 1). Nothing new to install here; this capstone script wires together calibration, a steady-state solve, the transition, and the savings policy experiment from the worked example.

# capstone_growth.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

# --- 1. CALIBRATION (parameters tied to data targets) ---
alpha = 1/3        # capital share of income
delta = 0.05       # depreciation rate
n     = 0.01       # population growth
g     = 0.02       # technology growth
deff  = delta + n + g            # effective depreciation = 0.08

f      = lambda k: k**alpha      # per-effective-worker output

# --- 2. SOLVE for steady state, given a savings rate ---
def steady_state(s):
    # root of s*f(k) - deff*k = 0  (closed form, but solve numerically)
    return brentq(lambda k: s*f(k) - deff*k, 1e-6, 100)

k_base = steady_state(0.20)
k_pol  = steady_state(0.25)
print(f"baseline k*={k_base:.3f}  y*={f(k_base):.3f}")
print(f"policy   k*={k_pol:.3f}  y*={f(k_pol):.3f}")
print(f"long-run output gain: {f(k_pol)/f(k_base)-1:+.1%}")

# --- 3. TRANSITION + POLICY EXPERIMENT (forward-iterate the ODE) ---
def transition(s, k0, T=120, dt=0.25):
    k = k0; path = [k]
    for _ in range(int(T/dt)):
        k += dt*(s*f(k) - deff*k)   # Euler step of k-dot
        path.append(k)
    return np.array(path)

# start AT the old steady state, then flip on the reform at t=0
base = transition(0.20, k_base)        # control: stays flat
pol  = transition(0.25, k_base)        # treatment: climbs to k_pol
print(f"half the gain reached at ~year {0.25*np.argmax(f(pol) >= (f(k_base)+f(k_pol))/2):.0f}")

Subtracting f(base) from f(pol) period by period is the policy's output effect over time — the heart of the capstone. Next, compute the discounted utility of each consumption path c = (1−s)·f(k) and solve for the CEV λ to price the early-transition consumption dip.

Q1. The three-step arc of a quantitative growth study is best described as…
A. calibrate, solve, experiment
B. regress, forecast, publish
C. assume, prove, generalize
D. observe, randomize, control
Show answer

A. Pin parameters to data (calibrate), compute the equilibrium path (solve), then perturb and re-solve (experiment). B describes a pure econometrics workflow; C is abstract theorem-proving; D is the structure of a field RCT, not a model study.

Q2. In a Cobb-Douglas calibration, the capital share parameter α is most directly read from…
A. the unemployment rate
B. capital's share of national income
C. the inflation rate
D. the savings rate
Show answer

B. With Y = Kα(AL)1−α, the exponent α equals capital's income share — about 1/3 in the data. The savings rate (D) is a separate parameter; unemployment (A) and inflation (C) play no role in this real growth model.

Q3. A reform permanently raises the Solow savings rate. In the long run this changes output's…
A. growth rate but not its level
B. level but not its long-run growth rate
C. both level and long-run growth rate permanently
D. neither — savings is irrelevant in Solow
Show answer

B. A higher s lifts steady-state capital and the level of output per effective worker, but the long-run growth rate is set by g (technology) alone. A and C wrongly grant savings a permanent growth effect; D ignores the clear level effect.

Q4. Why is calibration not the same as econometric estimation?
A. Calibration uses more data than estimation
B. Calibration requires a supercomputer; estimation does not
C. Calibration matches a few long-run targets by construction and is judged on untargeted moments, rather than minimizing fit error over the whole sample
D. Calibration can only be used for financial data
Show answer

C. Calibration sets parameters to hit chosen long-run moments and validates against moments it did not target; estimation minimizes overall fit error. A is backwards (calibration typically uses fewer moments); B and D are simply false.

Q5. A capstone reports that a savings-rate hike raises long-run output by 12%. The most important thing still missing is…
A. a proof that the steady state exists
B. a higher value for α
C. a faster computer to run the model
D. a welfare measure accounting for the consumption sacrificed during the transition
Show answer

D. A higher s mechanically raises output, so the level gain is no surprise; the open question is whether welfare rises once you price the early consumption dip (e.g., via a consumption-equivalent variation). A is already implied by the model; B would change the answer, not complete it; C is irrelevant to a model this small.
