Dynamic Optimization & the Euler Equation

When today's choice changes every tomorrow, you don't optimize a number — you optimize an entire path, and one equation links each step to the next.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the general structure of a dynamic optimization problem: objective, state, control, and law of motion.
  • Derive the consumption Euler equation and read it as an intertemporal no-arbitrage condition.
  • Write down the Bellman equation and explain what the value function represents.
  • State the principle of optimality and see why it lets a lifetime problem collapse into a two-period trade-off.
  • Explain at an intuitive level why a transversality condition is needed to pin down the right path.

7.1The Shape of a Dynamic Problem

In Weeks 4–6 you met growth models — Solow, then Ramsey — where a household chooses how much to consume and how much to save over time. That "over time" is the whole difficulty. A static optimizer picks one number to maximize one payoff. A dynamic optimizer picks a whole sequence, and every choice today reshapes the menu of choices available tomorrow.

Strip away the economics and every dynamic problem has the same four parts:

max Σt=0∞ βt u(ct) subject to kt+1 = g(kt, ct)Objective (discounted lifetime utility) maximized over controls ct, subject to a law of motion linking today's state kt to tomorrow's kt+1.

The state kt is what you carry into period t — here, capital (or wealth). The control ct is what you freely choose this period — consumption. The law of motion g says how the control today moves the state to tomorrow: whatever you don't consume out of output becomes next period's capital. The discount factor β ∈ (0,1) says a unit of utility tomorrow is worth β units today — impatience.

The prize is not a single number but the entire optimal path {c0, c1, c2, …}. Two completely different mathematical languages describe the same optimum: a sequence view (optimize the whole path at once, giving the Euler equation) and a recursive view (optimize one step at a time, giving the Bellman equation). The rest of this lecture builds both — and shows they agree.

AnalogyThink of hiking a ridgeline to the highest point reachable by sunset. The state is where you're standing and how much daylight remains; the control is the direction you step now. You can't just sprint toward the nearest peak — a greedy step uphill might strand you on a dead-end spur. Dynamic optimization is choosing each step so the whole route is best, not just the next footfall.

7.2The Euler Equation as Intertemporal No-Arbitrage

Here is the central trick. Suppose you have an optimal consumption path and you consider a tiny, reversible experiment: consume one less unit today, invest it, then consume the proceeds tomorrow. If the path is truly optimal, this swap can't make you better off — otherwise you'd have done it already. The first-order gain must exactly cancel the first-order loss.

Spell out the two sides. Giving up one unit of consumption today costs you marginal utility u′(ct) right now. That unit earns a gross return Rt+1 = 1 + rt+1 (in the Ramsey model R = 1 + f′(k) − δ). Tomorrow it buys Rt+1 extra units, each worth u′(ct+1) of utility — but tomorrow's utility is discounted by β. Setting gain equal to loss:

u′(ct) = β Rt+1 u′(ct+1)The consumption Euler equation: the marginal utility cost of saving one more unit today equals the discounted marginal utility benefit of the return tomorrow.

This is the Euler equation, and it is the workhorse of all of modern macro and dynamic finance. Read it as intertemporal no-arbitrage: along an optimal path there is no free lunch from shifting a marginal unit of consumption between adjacent periods. It is the dynamic cousin of the static rule "set marginal benefit equal to marginal cost" — only now the benefit and cost live in different periods, bridged by β and R.

With the common CRRA utility u(c) = c1−σ/(1−σ), we have u′(c) = c−σ, and the equation rearranges into a clean growth rule for consumption:

ct+1 / ct = ( β Rt+1 )1/σConsumption grows when the return R beats impatience (βR > 1) and falls when impatience wins. σ controls how strongly you respond.

The parameter σ is the inverse of the intertemporal elasticity of substitution: a high σ means you dislike a bumpy consumption path and won't tilt it much even when returns are attractive. This single relation is what asset-pricing economists fit to market data, and what central-bank models use to forecast how interest-rate changes ripple into spending.

Common mistakeThe Euler equation is a necessary condition for optimality, not the whole solution. It only relates consecutive periods — it tells you the shape of the path but not its level. Infinitely many paths satisfy the Euler equation; most of them are absurd (e.g. consumption that explodes or capital that piles up forever and is never enjoyed). You still need a starting condition (today's capital k0) and an endpoint condition (the transversality condition in 7.4) to select the one true optimum.

7.3The Bellman Equation & the Value Function

The sequence view optimizes the whole path in one shot. The recursive view, due to Richard Bellman, does something cleverer: it defines a function that summarizes the entire future from any state, so the infinite problem becomes a single repeating two-period decision.

Define the value function V(k) as the best lifetime utility you can achieve starting with capital k and behaving optimally forever after:

V(k) = best total discounted utility achievable from state k onwardV is "the most you can ever get from here." It packs the whole optimized future into one number for each starting state.

The magic is that V satisfies a self-referential equation. The best you can do from k equals the best single-period choice today plus the discounted best-you-can-do from wherever that choice leaves you tomorrow:

V(k) = maxc [ u(c) + β V(k′) ], where k′ = g(k, c)The Bellman equation. Today's payoff u(c) plus the discounted value V(k′) of the state your choice produces — and V appears on both sides.

The function inside the max, splitting "now" from "the discounted future," is the heart of dynamic programming. The maximizer at each k is the policy function c = π(k): a rule that tells you the optimal control for every state, not just along one path. That is enormously useful — once you know π(k), you can simulate the economy from any starting point or after any shock without re-solving from scratch.

7.4Principle of Optimality & the Transversality Condition

Why is it legitimate to chop an infinite-horizon problem into a single "now versus the rest" decision? Because of Bellman's principle of optimality:

Principle of optimalityAn optimal plan has the property that, whatever the first choice and the resulting state, the remaining choices form an optimal plan for the problem that starts at that new state. In plain terms: tails of optimal plans are themselves optimal. A best route from A to C through B must contain a best route from B to C — otherwise you could improve the tail and beat the supposed best route.

This is exactly what lets V(k′) stand in for the entire optimized future inside the Bellman equation: we never have to worry that "behaving optimally from tomorrow on" conflicts with "having behaved optimally today." It also reconciles the two views — differentiating the Bellman equation and applying the envelope theorem regenerates the very same Euler equation we derived from the sequence problem in 7.2.

Pinning down the level: transversality

The Euler equation fixes the path's shape; the initial capital k0 fixes one end. But with an infinite horizon there is no "last period" to nail down the other end — and that loose end admits bad solutions. Imagine a household that keeps deferring consumption forever, accumulating capital it never enjoys. Every such path can satisfy the Euler equation, yet it is obviously not optimal: you left utility on the table.

The transversality condition (TVC) rules these out. Intuitively it says: the discounted value of the capital you're still holding must vanish as time runs to infinity — don't end up hoarding valuable resources you never consume.

limt→∞ βt u′(ct) kt+1 = 0The leftover capital, valued in today's discounted marginal-utility terms, must shrink to nothing. It forbids "saving forever and never spending."

Together, the trio is complete: the Euler equation (consecutive-period optimality) + the initial condition k0 (where you start) + the transversality condition (no wasteful hoarding at the end) select one unique optimal path — the saddle path you saw converging to the steady state in the Ramsey phase diagram of Week 6.

Common mistakeStudents often think the transversality condition means capital must go to zero. It does not. In the Ramsey model capital converges to a positive steady state k*. What must go to zero is the discounted shadow value βtu′(ct)kt+1 of the terminal stock — and because βt decays geometrically, that product vanishes even though kt itself stays bounded above zero.
✦ Worked Example — a two-period Euler equation with numbers

A household lives two periods with CRRA utility u(c) = c1−σ/(1−σ), σ = 2. It has wealth W = 100, earns gross return R = 1.10 on savings, and discounts the future with β = 0.95. How much should it consume today (c0) and tomorrow (c1)?

  1. Budget: what isn't consumed today grows by R, so c1 = R(W − c0) = 1.10(100 − c0).
  2. Euler equation: u′(c0) = βR·u′(c1) becomes c0−2 = βR·c1−2.
  3. Growth ratio: c1/c0 = (βR)1/σ = (0.95×1.10)1/2 = (1.045)0.5 ≈ 1.0222. So consumption tilts slightly upward — the 10% return just beats the 5% impatience.
  4. Solve the level: substitute c1 = 1.0222·c0 into the budget: 1.0222·c0 = 1.10(100 − c0) = 110 − 1.10·c0. Then (1.0222 + 1.10)·c0 = 110, so c0 = 110 / 2.1222 ≈ 51.83.
  5. Finish: c1 = 1.0222 × 51.83 ≈ 52.98. Check: savings = 100 − 51.83 = 48.17; grown by 1.10 gives 52.99 ≈ c1. The path satisfies both the budget and the Euler equation — it is the unique optimum.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the Bellman equation by value-function iteration

We keep using the free QuantEcon stack from Week 1 — Python + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib. (If you skipped it, the one-time install details block lives in the Week 1/2 lectures.) Here we let the computer find the value function V(k) and the policy π(k) by iterating the Bellman operator until it stops changing — the numerical embodiment of the principle of optimality. This is a preview; the full machinery (interpolation, continuous controls) arrives in Week 8.

# vfi_growth.py -- value-function iteration for a simple growth model
import numpy as np

# model: log utility, Cobb-Douglas output, full depreciation (clean closed form to check)
beta, alpha = 0.95, 0.34
k_grid = np.linspace(0.05, 4.0, 400)      # discretized state space

def u(c):
    return np.where(c > 1e-10, np.log(c), -1e10)   # penalize c <= 0

V = np.zeros_like(k_grid)                  # initial guess: V = 0 everywhere
for it in range(1000):
    # for each k today, value of each feasible k' tomorrow
    C = k_grid[:, None] ** alpha - k_grid[None, :]   # c = f(k) - k'
    payoff = u(C) + beta * V[None, :]                # u(c) + beta * V(k')
    V_new = payoff.max(axis=1)                       # Bellman: maximize over k'
    if np.max(np.abs(V_new - V)) < 1e-6:
        break
    V = V_new

policy = k_grid[payoff.argmax(axis=1)]     # optimal k'(k) = the saving rule
print(f"converged in {it} iterations")
print(f"savings rate near k=1: {policy[np.argmin(abs(k_grid-1))]/(1.0**alpha):.3f}")
# theory says optimal savings rate = alpha*beta = 0.34*0.95 = 0.323 -- compare!

For this log-utility / full-depreciation case the optimal policy is known exactly: save a constant fraction αβ of output. Your iterated policy should reproduce ≈ 0.323 — a satisfying check that value-function iteration really does solve the Bellman equation. Try changing β and watch the saving rate move.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Euler equation and the Bellman equation are the two engines under the hood of nearly all modern dynamic macro and finance. Week 8 turns the toy iteration above into a real numerical solver — interpolation over a grid, continuous controls, and convergence diagnostics — so you can crack models with no closed form. The same Euler logic, applied to risky returns, becomes the stochastic discount factor at the core of consumption-based asset pricing in the Quant Finance track, and the recursive Bellman approach scales straight up to the dynamic-programming and reinforcement-learning methods used to control inventories, trading strategies, and entire macro policy models.

Q1. In a dynamic optimization problem, the variable you carry from one period into the next (e.g. capital) is the…
A. control variable
B. state variable
C. discount factor
D. objective function
Show answer

B. The state summarizes everything you bring into a period. The control (A) is what you freely choose now (consumption); the discount factor (C) weights future utility; the objective (D) is the thing being maximized.

Q2. The consumption Euler equation u′(ct) = βRt+1u′(ct+1) is best interpreted as…
A. the statement that consumption is constant over time
B. the law of motion for capital
C. an intertemporal no-arbitrage condition: no gain from shifting a marginal unit of consumption between adjacent periods
D. a guarantee that the steady state is reached in one period
Show answer

C. It equates the marginal-utility cost of saving today with the discounted marginal-utility benefit tomorrow — no free lunch from re-timing consumption. Consumption is constant (A) only in the knife-edge case βR = 1; the law of motion (B) is a separate equation; nothing forces one-period convergence (D).

Q3. With CRRA utility, consumption grows over time (ct+1 > ct) precisely when…
A. βR > 1 (the return beats impatience)
B. βR < 1
C. σ = 0
D. the discount factor β equals 1
Show answer

A. Since ct+1/ct = (βR)1/σ, the ratio exceeds 1 exactly when βR > 1. B gives falling consumption; σ=0 (C) isn't valid CRRA and doesn't determine the sign; β=1 (D) alone doesn't pin it down — it depends on R too.

Q4. In the Bellman equation V(k) = maxc[ u(c) + βV(k′) ], the value function V(k) represents…
A. the utility of consuming in the current period only
B. the amount of capital available next period
C. the market interest rate
D. the best total discounted lifetime utility achievable starting from state k
Show answer

D. V(k) packs the whole optimized future into one number per state. Current-period utility (A) is just the u(c) term; next-period capital (B) is k′; the interest rate (C) lives in the return R, not in V.

Q5. Why isn't the Euler equation alone enough to identify the optimal path, requiring a transversality condition?
A. The Euler equation is usually wrong and must be corrected
B. The transversality condition replaces the need for an initial capital stock
C. Many paths satisfy the Euler equation, including ones that wastefully accumulate capital forever; the TVC rules those out
D. The Euler equation only applies to static problems
Show answer

C. The Euler equation fixes only the shape (consecutive-period optimality); infinitely many paths obey it. The TVC, by forcing the discounted terminal capital value to zero, eliminates wasteful hoarding paths. The Euler equation is correct (A) and is precisely a dynamic condition (D); the initial stock k0 is still needed and is not replaced by the TVC (B) — the two are complementary endpoint conditions.

✓ What you learned

  • Every dynamic problem has the same parts: an objective, a state, a control, and a law of motion.
  • The Euler equation u′(ct) = βRu′(ct+1) is intertemporal no-arbitrage — and with CRRA gives ct+1/ct = (βR)1/σ.
  • The Bellman equation recasts the infinite horizon recursively; the value function V(k) is the best achievable future from each state.
  • The principle of optimality ("tails of optimal plans are optimal") justifies the recursion and reconciles it with the Euler equation.
  • Euler + initial condition + transversality condition together select the single optimal saddle path.
