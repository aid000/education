Week 2 · Lecture 2

The Solow Growth Model

One equation explains why poor countries can grow fast, why saving more raises living standards only temporarily, and why capital alone cannot make us rich forever.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write down an aggregate production function Y = F(K, L) and explain constant returns to scale.
  • Convert output and capital to per-worker (per-capita) terms and read the production function y = f(k).
  • Derive the capital-accumulation equation dK = sY − δK and its per-capita form dk = s·f(k) − δk.
  • Explain diminishing returns to capital and why they force the economy toward a single steady state k*.
  • Solve for the steady-state capital k* and show how the saving rate s and depreciation δ pin it down.

2.1Output From Capital and Labor

Last week we asked what drives long-run growth. The Solow model gives the first rigorous answer. It starts from a single idea: an economy turns two inputs — its stock of machines, buildings, and tools (capital, K) and its number of workers (labor, L) — into output Y. The recipe is the aggregate production function:

Y = F(K, L)Total output as a function of the capital stock K and the labor force L.

The workhorse choice is the Cobb–Douglas form, which fits a striking empirical fact: capital's share of national income has stayed near one-third for over a century.

Y = A · K^α · L^(1−α),   0 < α < 1A is total factor productivity (technology); α is capital's share of income (empirically ≈ 1/3).

Two properties matter. First, constant returns to scale: double both K and L and output exactly doubles — because the exponents α and 1−α sum to 1. Second, each input alone has diminishing returns: add machines while holding workers fixed and each extra machine adds less output than the last. That second property is the engine of everything that follows.

2.2Thinking Per Worker

We care about living standards — output per person — not the raw size of the economy. A country of a billion poor people produces more total output than a small rich one, yet we'd rather live in the rich one. So we divide everything by L. Because the production function has constant returns to scale, dividing by L gives a clean per-worker version:

y = Y/L,   k = K/L  ⟹   y = A · k^α = f(k)y is output per worker; k is capital per worker. The whole model now lives in one variable, k.

This is the move that makes the model solvable: a two-input economy collapses to a single curve, y = f(k). Diminishing returns survive the rescaling — the curve rises but bends over, getting flatter as k grows. The slope, f′(k) = αAk^(α−1), is the marginal product of capital, and it falls toward zero as a worker is handed ever more machines.

AnalogyPicture one cook in a kitchen. The first oven is transformative; the second still helps; by the tenth oven the cook is just running between idle appliances. Output per cook keeps rising with each oven — but by smaller and smaller amounts. That bending-over curve is f(k), and the flattening is diminishing returns to capital.

2.3How Capital Accumulates

Where does capital come from? Households save a constant fraction s of output, and that saving is invested in new capital. Meanwhile a constant fraction δ (delta) of the existing capital stock wears out each period — machines rust, buildings age. The change in the capital stock is investment minus depreciation:

dK/dt = s·Y − δ·KNew capital from saving (s·Y) minus capital lost to wear (δ·K). This is the law of motion for K.

Dividing by L (and, for now, assuming the labor force is constant) turns this into the model's central equation in per-worker terms — the fundamental Solow equation:

dk/dt = s·f(k) − δ·kPer-worker capital rises when saving per worker s·f(k) exceeds the depreciation per worker δ·k.

Read it as a race between two terms. s·f(k) is the actual investment curve — it inherits the bent-over shape of f(k). δ·k is a straight line through the origin: the more capital you have, the more wears out, in exact proportion. When the curve is above the line, k grows; when the line is above the curve, k shrinks.

2.4The Steady State k*

The economy is at rest when capital per worker stops changing — when the saving curve exactly meets the depreciation line and dk/dt = 0. That point is the steady state, k*:

s·f(k*) = δ·k*At the steady state, saving exactly replaces worn-out capital, so k holds constant.

Diminishing returns guarantee this works. Near k = 0 the curve s·f(k) is steep and rises above the line δk, so capital grows. As k climbs, the curve flattens while the line keeps rising, until they cross — exactly once — at k*. Below k*, the economy accumulates capital and grows; above k*, capital decays toward k*. The steady state is stable: wherever you start, you converge to the same k*.

With Cobb–Douglas f(k) = A·k^α, we can solve for k* explicitly. Set s·A·k^α = δ·k and rearrange:

k* = ( s·A / δ )^( 1 / (1 − α) )Steady-state capital per worker. It rises with the saving rate s and technology A, and falls with depreciation δ.

This single formula carries the model's headline lessons. A higher saving rate s lifts the investment curve and pushes k* (and hence steady-state output y* = A·(k*)^α) up. Faster depreciation δ steepens the line and drags k* down. But notice what s does not do: because the exponent 1/(1−α) is finite, doubling s does not double k* — diminishing returns mean a higher saving rate buys a permanently richer economy, not a permanently faster-growing one.

Common mistakeA higher saving rate raises the level of the steady state, not its long-run growth rate. Once an economy reaches k*, per-worker capital and output stop growing — saving more shifts you to a higher plateau but does not keep you climbing forever. Confusing a one-time level effect with a permanent growth effect is the classic Solow error, and it's why the model says capital accumulation alone cannot sustain growth (technology A must grow — next week).
✦ Worked Example — find the steady state

An economy has Cobb–Douglas output per worker y = A·k^α with A = 1, α = 0.5, a saving rate s = 0.30, and depreciation δ = 0.10. Find the steady-state capital per worker k*, steady-state output y*, and confirm it is a rest point.

  1. Write the steady-state condition: s·A·k^α = δ·k, i.e. 0.30·k^0.5 = 0.10·k.
  2. Use the formula: k* = (s·A/δ)^(1/(1−α)) = (0.30/0.10)^(1/0.5) = 3^2 = 9.
  3. Steady-state output: y* = A·(k*)^α = 1·9^0.5 = 3 units per worker.
  4. Check the balance: saving = s·y* = 0.30·3 = 0.9; depreciation = δ·k* = 0.10·9 = 0.9. They match, so dk/dt = 0 — capital is exactly replaced. ✓
  5. Policy thought: raise s to 0.40. Then k* = (0.40/0.10)^2 = 16 and y* = 4. Saving 33% more capital per period (0.30→0.40) raised output per worker by only 33% (3→4), and the economy still ends at a flat plateau — a level gain, not faster growth.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate Solow convergence in Python

This course uses the free QuantEcon scientific stack — Python, NumPy, SciPy, Matplotlib, and the quantecon library. Week 1–2 setup: install it once (below) and reuse it for every lecture and the course project.

⬇ Install Python + QuantEcon + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib (one-time setup)

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the stack:

python3 -m venv econ-env
source econ-env/bin/activate       # macOS / Linux
econ-env\Scripts\activate          # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install quantecon numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import quantecon, numpy, scipy; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

Now iterate the fundamental Solow equation forward in time and watch k climb to its steady state. SciPy's root finder confirms the closed-form k*.

# solow.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq

A, alpha, s, delta = 1.0, 0.5, 0.30, 0.10

def f(k):                 # per-worker production
    return A * k**alpha

# closed-form steady state
k_star = (s * A / delta)**(1 / (1 - alpha))
print("closed-form k* =", round(k_star, 4))   # 9.0

# confirm numerically: find k where s*f(k) - delta*k = 0
g = lambda k: s * f(k) - delta * k
print("numeric  k* =", round(brentq(g, 0.1, 100), 4))   # 9.0

# simulate the law of motion dk = s*f(k) - delta*k
k = 1.0                    # start far below k*
for t in range(60):
    k = k + s * f(k) - delta * k
print("k after 60 periods =", round(k, 4))   # -> 9.0

Both methods return k* = 9, and the simulation converges there from below — visual proof that the steady state is stable. Try lowering the start to k = 20 (above k*) and watch it fall back to 9. Then plot s·f(k) and δk together to redraw the Solow diagram yourself.

🔗 Where this is heading

The bare Solow model predicts that without technological progress, growth must eventually stop — yet rich economies keep growing. Next week we add a growing labor force and a growing technology level A, which restores sustained growth and yields the famous convergence prediction (poorer economies grow faster) and the Golden Rule saving rate that maximizes consumption. Later in C2 we let households choose s optimally (the Ramsey model), and in the Quant Finance track the same diminishing-returns and steady-state logic reappears in capital-budgeting and long-horizon return models.

❓ Lecture 2 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a Cobb–Douglas production function Y = A·K^α·L^(1−α), the exponents summing to 1 means the function has…
A. increasing returns to scale
B. constant returns to scale
C. decreasing returns to scale
D. no marginal product of capital
Show answer

B. When the exponents sum to 1, scaling both inputs by the same factor scales output by that factor — constant returns to scale. Increasing (A) and decreasing (C) returns would require the exponents to sum to more or less than 1. (D) is false: the marginal product of capital is αAk^(α−1) > 0, it just diminishes.

Q2. Why does the per-worker production function y = f(k) get flatter as k rises?
A. capital depreciates faster at high k
B. the saving rate falls as the economy grows
C. diminishing returns to capital — each extra unit of k adds less output
D. the labor force is shrinking
Show answer

C. The slope f′(k) = αAk^(α−1) falls as k rises — diminishing marginal product of capital. (A) δk is linear, not curved, and doesn't bend f(k); (B) s is constant in the basic model; (D) L is assumed fixed here.

Q3. The fundamental Solow equation dk/dt = s·f(k) − δk says capital per worker grows whenever…
A. output exceeds consumption
B. the saving rate exceeds the depreciation rate
C. actual investment s·f(k) exceeds depreciation δk
D. the marginal product of capital is zero
Show answer

C. k rises exactly when the investment curve sits above the depreciation line. (B) compares two rates directly, which is wrong — s multiplies f(k) and δ multiplies k, so it's the products that must be compared. (A) is always true under positive saving and is not the condition. (D) describes the limit, not growth.

Q4. For Cobb–Douglas with A = 1, the steady state satisfies s·k^α = δ·k. With s = 0.2, α = 0.5, δ = 0.05, what is k*?
A. k* = 4
B. k* = 8
C. k* = 12
D. k* = 16
Show answer

D. k* = (s/δ)^(1/(1−α)) = (0.2/0.05)^(1/0.5) = 4² = 16. Check: s·k^0.5 = 0.2·4 = 0.8 and δ·k = 0.05·16 = 0.8 — they balance. The distractors come from forgetting to square (A = s/δ = 4) or arithmetic slips.

Q5. In the basic Solow model, permanently raising the saving rate s will…
A. raise the steady-state level of output per worker, but not its long-run growth rate
B. raise the long-run growth rate of output per worker forever
C. have no effect on the steady state at all
D. drive capital per worker to infinity
Show answer

A. A higher s lifts the investment curve and raises k* and y* — a one-time level effect. It cannot raise the long-run growth rate (B): once at k*, per-worker growth is zero without technological progress. (C) is wrong because k* clearly depends on s; (D) is wrong because diminishing returns cap k at the finite k*.
