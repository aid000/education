Week 5 · Lecture 5

Consumption, Savings & Intertemporal Choice

Every dollar you spend today is a dollar you can't spend tomorrow — and the math of that trade-off is the engine room of all modern macroeconomics.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up the household's consumption-savings problem as a choice across time.
  • Derive the two-period intertemporal budget constraint and interpret its slope.
  • Represent time preference with a discount factor and explain what it measures.
  • Explain consumption smoothing from the curvature of the utility function.
  • Derive the consumption Euler equation and read it as a no-arbitrage condition.

5.1The Household's Problem Across Time

Weeks 3 and 4 treated saving as a fixed fraction of output — a mechanical rule. That was a useful shortcut, but it dodged the central question of modern macro: where does the saving rate come from? The answer is that it is chosen. A household decides how much of today's income to consume now and how much to set aside so it can consume later. That single decision — repeated by millions of households — is the foundation of investment, interest rates, and the entire growth dynamics we study this course.

Strip the problem to its core. A household lives for two periods, period 1 ("today") and period 2 ("tomorrow"). It receives income y1 today and y2 tomorrow, and must pick consumption c1 and c2. It cares about both, but not equally: a unit of consumption today usually feels more valuable than the same unit promised for next year. We capture that with a lifetime utility function:

U = u(c1) + β · u(c2)Total well-being is today's utility plus discounted future utility. β (beta) is the discount factor, 0 < β ≤ 1.

The function u(·) is the period utility (or "felicity") — the satisfaction from one period's consumption. We assume it is increasing (more is better) and concave (each extra unit adds less than the one before — diminishing marginal utility). That concavity is not a technicality; it is the whole reason households dislike a feast-then-famine income path, as we'll see in 5.3.

5.2The Intertemporal Budget Constraint

The household can move resources between periods through a bond (a savings account) paying a real interest rate r. Let s be saving in period 1: whatever income is not consumed, s = y1 - c1. That saving grows to (1 + r)·s by period 2 and is spent then alongside second-period income:

c2 = y2 + (1 + r)(y1 - c1)Period-2 spending equals period-2 income plus the principal-and-interest from period-1 saving. If c1 > y1 the household borrows and s is negative.

Rearranging to put all the terms on comparable footing gives the present-value form — the single most useful identity in this lecture:

c1 + c2 / (1 + r) = y1 + y2 / (1 + r)The present value of lifetime consumption equals the present value of lifetime income. Call the right side W, lifetime wealth.

Read it carefully. The household is not constrained period-by-period — it is constrained only by total wealth W, the present value of its income stream. It can consume more than y1 today by borrowing against y2, or less by saving. The relative price of consuming today rather than tomorrow is (1 + r): giving up one unit of c2 frees up only 1/(1+r) units of c1, because future dollars are discounted.

5.3Time Preference & Consumption Smoothing

The discount factor β measures impatience. A common way to write it is β = 1/(1 + ρ), where ρ (rho) is the rate of time preference — the premium you place on getting satisfaction sooner rather than later. If ρ = 0.05, you value a util tomorrow at about 95% of a util today. Lower β (higher ρ) means a more impatient household that tilts consumption toward the present.

Now bring back concavity. Because marginal utility falls as consumption rises, a household with a lumpy income path — a big y1 and a small y2 — would be giving up high-marginal-utility consumption tomorrow to fund low-marginal-utility consumption today. It can do better by shifting some resources forward, flattening the path. This is consumption smoothing: households prefer a steady stream of consumption to a volatile one with the same present value.

AnalogyThink of consumption like water and your two periods like two connected tanks. Income pours unevenly into them, but you'd rather both tanks sit at a comfortable level than have one overflowing and one nearly dry. Saving and borrowing are the pipe between the tanks: it lets you move water from the flush period to the lean one. The interest rate is a small toll charged on water flowing through the pipe, and impatience is a slight preference for keeping water in the "today" tank.

This is exactly why the permanent income hypothesis and the life-cycle hypothesis — the workhorses behind every empirical consumption study — predict that households respond mildly to temporary income changes (a one-time bonus is mostly saved and spread out) but strongly to permanent ones (a raise that lasts changes lifetime wealth W and is largely consumed).

Common mistakeSmoothing does not mean households always set c1 = c2. Perfectly flat consumption only emerges in the knife-edge case where the household is exactly as impatient as the market rewards waiting, i.e. β(1 + r) = 1. If the interest rate is high enough to overcome impatience, the optimal path slopes upward (save now, consume more later); if impatience dominates, it slopes downward. "Smoothing" means the path is flatter than the income path — not perfectly flat.

5.4The Consumption Euler Equation

Now we optimize, and in doing so take our first real step into dynamic optimization — the toolkit that drives the rest of this course. Substitute the budget constraint c2 = y2 + (1 + r)(y1 - c1) into lifetime utility so everything is a function of the single choice c1:

U(c1) = u(c1) + β · u( y2 + (1 + r)(y1 - c1) )One unknown, c1. Maximize by setting the derivative to zero.

Differentiate with respect to c1 and apply the chain rule (the inner derivative of c2 with respect to c1 is -(1 + r)):

u'(c1) - β(1 + r) · u'(c2) = 0The marginal utility lost today must equal the discounted marginal utility gained tomorrow.

Cleaning up gives the celebrated consumption Euler equation:

u'(c1) = β(1 + r) · u'(c2)At the optimum, the household is indifferent between consuming one more unit now and saving it for (1+r) units later.

This is a no-arbitrage condition across time, and it is the single most important equation in modern macro. Read the logic: take one dollar from today. You lose u'(c1) of marginal utility now. Invested, that dollar becomes (1 + r) dollars tomorrow, each worth u'(c2) of marginal utility, the whole bundle discounted by β because it arrives later. At the optimum these exactly balance — otherwise the household could reshuffle consumption and gain. Notice it pins down the growth rate of consumption, not its level: rearranged, u'(c1)/u'(c2) = β(1 + r) tells you whether the path slopes up, down, or flat, directly confirming the warning in 5.3.

✦ Worked Example — solve a two-period problem end to end

A household has log utility, u(c) = ln(c), so u'(c) = 1/c. It earns y1 = 100 today and y2 = 0 tomorrow (a worker saving for retirement). The interest rate is r = 0.10 and the discount factor is β = 0.95. Find c1, c2, and saving.

  1. Euler equation with log utility: 1/c1 = β(1 + r)·(1/c2), so c2 = β(1 + r)·c1.
  2. Plug in numbers: c2 = 0.95 × 1.10 × c1 = 1.045·c1. Because β(1+r) = 1.045 > 1, consumption rises over time — the interest reward beats the impatience.
  3. Budget constraint (present value): c1 + c2/1.10 = 100 + 0/1.10 = 100.
  4. Substitute c2: c1 + (1.045·c1)/1.10 = 100 → c1 + 0.95·c1 = 100 → 1.95·c1 = 100.
  5. Solve: c1 = 51.28, then c2 = 1.045 × 51.28 = 53.59. Saving today is s = y1 - c1 = 100 - 51.28 = 48.72.
  6. Sanity check: savings grow to 48.72 × 1.10 = 53.59 = c2. ✓ The household spreads 100 of today's income into a smooth, slightly rising path of roughly 51 then 54.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the household problem in Python

We use the free QuantEcon stack — Python, NumPy, SciPy, and Matplotlib — exactly as set up in the Week 1/2 install (see the install details in Lectures 1–2). Here we let scipy.optimize maximize lifetime utility directly, then confirm it reproduces the Euler-equation answer from the worked example. No new packages needed.

# consumption_savings.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar

# parameters
y1, y2 = 100.0, 0.0
r, beta = 0.10, 0.95

# given c1, the budget constraint fixes c2
def c2_of(c1):
    return y2 + (1 + r) * (y1 - c1)

# lifetime utility with log felicity (minimize the negative)
def neg_U(c1):
    c2 = c2_of(c1)
    if c1 <= 0 or c2 <= 0:
        return 1e9                 # forbid non-positive consumption
    return -(np.log(c1) + beta * np.log(c2))

res = minimize_scalar(neg_U, bounds=(1e-6, y1), method='bounded')
c1 = res.x
c2 = c2_of(c1)
print(f"c1 = {c1:.2f},  c2 = {c2:.2f},  saving = {y1 - c1:.2f}")

# verify the Euler equation: u'(c1) == beta*(1+r)*u'(c2)
lhs, rhs = 1/c1, beta * (1 + r) * (1/c2)
print(f"Euler check: {lhs:.4f} vs {rhs:.4f}")   # should match

Running it prints c1 = 51.28, c2 = 53.59, saving = 48.72 and a matching Euler check — the numerical optimizer rediscovers the analytic answer. Try sweeping r from 0 to 0.3 and plotting c1 with Matplotlib: you'll see saving rise as the reward for waiting grows. In Week 6 this same solver scales up to the infinite-horizon Ramsey problem.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Euler equation you just derived is not a two-period curiosity — it is the beating heart of dynamic macro. Stretch the horizon to infinity and add a production sector and you get the Ramsey model (Week 6), where the same condition determines the entire optimal path of capital and growth. Add uncertainty about future income and it becomes the stochastic Euler equation behind the consumption-based asset pricing models (Weeks 9–10) that link macro to the Quant Finance track — the very equation used to explain why risky assets must offer a premium. The intuition is always the one from this lecture: at the optimum, you cannot gain by moving a marginal unit of consumption across time.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the present-value budget constraint c1 + c2/(1+r) = y1 + y2/(1+r), the right-hand side represents…
A. the present value of lifetime income (lifetime wealth)
B. period-1 consumption only
C. the household's total saving
D. the interest rate times income
Show answer

A. The right side discounts both periods' income back to today, giving lifetime wealth W. B is just one term; C (saving) is y1 - c1, a different quantity; D misreads the discounting as multiplication by r.

Q2. The discount factor β in U = u(c1) + β·u(c2) captures…
A. the real interest rate paid on bonds
B. how impatient the household is — how much it values future vs. present utility
C. the rate of inflation between the two periods
D. the curvature of the utility function
Show answer

B. β (often 1/(1+ρ)) is pure time preference. The interest rate r (A) is a market price, not a preference; inflation (C) is handled by using real variables; curvature (D) is a property of u(·), not β.

Q3. Consumption smoothing arises primarily because the period utility function u(·) is…
A. linear in consumption
B. decreasing in consumption
C. concave (diminishing marginal utility)
D. independent of consumption
Show answer

C. Concavity means each extra unit is worth less, so households dislike volatile paths and spread consumption out. Linear utility (A) would make them indifferent to the path; B and D contradict "more is better."

Q4. The consumption Euler equation u'(c1) = β(1+r)·u'(c2) is best interpreted as…
A. a statement that consumption must be equal in both periods
B. a no-arbitrage condition: the marginal utility lost today equals the discounted marginal utility gained tomorrow
C. the requirement that all income be saved
D. a guarantee that consumption always falls over time
Show answer

B. It balances the marginal cost (lost utility now) against the marginal benefit (discounted future utility) of shifting one unit across time. It does not force c1 = c2 (A) — that only holds if β(1+r)=1 — and says nothing about saving everything (C) or always falling (D).

Q5. With log utility, β = 0.96 and r = 0.04, the Euler equation gives c2 = β(1+r)·c1. The optimal consumption path is therefore…
A. rising over time, since β(1+r) > 1
B. falling over time, since β(1+r) < 1
C. falling over time, since β < 1
D. flat, since β(1+r) = 0.96 × 1.04 = 0.9984 ≈ 1, so c2 ≈ c1
Show answer

D. Compute β(1+r) = 0.96 × 1.04 = 0.9984, essentially 1, so c2 ≈ c1 — a nearly flat path. A and B misjudge the product; C looks only at β and ignores that r nearly offsets it. The sign of (β(1+r) - 1), not β alone, sets the slope.
