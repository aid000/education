Growth & Macro Dynamics: The Big Picture

A worker in a rich country can be twenty times more productive than one in a poor country — explaining that gap is the first job of macro dynamics.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the central questions of macro dynamics: why nations differ in income and what drives long-run growth.
  • Distinguish the three core macro quantities — capital, labor, and output — and the production function linking them.
  • Tell stocks apart from flows and explain why the distinction governs every dynamic model.
  • Define a dynamical system, a steady state, and transition dynamics in plain language.
  • Explain why we model time explicitly, and simulate a one-variable capital path in Python.

1.1The Central Questions

In micro you studied a single market at a single moment. Macro dynamics zooms out to the whole economy and lets the clock run. The two questions that organize the entire course are deceptively simple:

1. Why are some countries rich and others poor? Output per worker differs across countries by a factor of more than twenty. That is not a small wrinkle — it is the difference between abundance and subsistence for billions of people.

2. What makes an economy grow over the long run? A country growing at 2% per year doubles its income in about 35 years; one growing at 1% takes 70. Small, persistent differences in the growth rate compound into enormous gaps in living standards.

doubling time ≈ 70 / (growth rate in %)The "rule of 70": at g% per year, income doubles roughly every 70/g years. The math is ln(2)/ln(1+g/100) ≈ 70/g for small g.

Answering these questions requires a model of how an economy's quantities evolve over time — which is exactly what a dynamical system gives us. Before we can model change, though, we need to name the pieces that change.

1.2Capital, Labor & Output: The Production Function

Strip an economy down and three quantities remain. Output (Y) is everything produced in a period — the goods and services we call GDP. It is made using two inputs: capital (K), the stock of tools, machines, buildings, and infrastructure; and labor (L), the hours of human work. A production function is the recipe that maps inputs to output:

Y = F(K, L)Output is a function of the capital and labor used to produce it. More inputs, more output — but not in equal proportion.

The workhorse choice is the Cobb–Douglas form, because it captures the key empirical fact that capital's share of income is roughly constant:

Y = A · K^(α) · L^(1 - α)A is total factor productivity (the efficiency of the recipe); α ≈ 1/3 is capital's share of output. Exponents summing to 1 mean constant returns to scale.

Two properties matter for everything that follows. First, constant returns to scale: double both K and L and output exactly doubles. Second, diminishing returns to each input: hold L fixed and keep adding capital, and each extra machine adds less output than the last. That second property is the engine that pulls economies toward a steady state, as we will see.

AnalogyThink of a kitchen. Labor is the cooks; capital is the ovens. Adding a second oven to one overworked cook helps a lot. Adding a tenth oven to that same cook barely helps — the cook can't tend them all. That is diminishing returns to capital, and it is why simply piling up machines cannot make a country rich forever.

1.3Stocks vs Flows — and Why Time Matters

The single most important distinction in dynamic modeling is between a stock and a flow. A stock is a quantity measured at a point in time — it has no time unit attached. A flow is a quantity measured per unit of time — it adds to or drains from a stock.

Capital K is a stock (the machines that exist today). Investment I and depreciation are flows (the machines built this year, the machines that wore out this year). The link between them is the law that makes capital a dynamic variable:

K(t+1) = K(t) + I(t) - δ · K(t)Next period's capital = current capital + new investment - depreciation. δ is the fraction of capital that wears out each period (e.g. 0.10).

This is why we model time explicitly: the stock today is the inheritance of every flow that came before. You cannot understand where the economy is without tracking how it got there. The bathtub captures it perfectly — the water level is a stock, the faucet and drain are flows.

Common mistakeDo not add a stock to a flow as if they share units. "Capital is 5 trillion" (a stock) and "investment is 1 trillion per year" (a flow) are different kinds of quantities — like confusing the water level in a tub with the rate the faucet runs. The accumulation equation is the only legal bridge between them, and it works only because the flow is multiplied by a time step.

1.4Dynamical Systems, Steady States & Transition

A dynamical system is just a rule that says: given the state today, here is the state tomorrow. Write the state as x and the rule as a function g:

x(t+1) = g( x(t) )A law of motion. Feed in today's state, get tomorrow's. Iterate it and you trace out the entire future path.

Our capital accumulation equation is exactly this kind of rule. Two ideas describe its behavior. A steady state x* is a state that reproduces itself — once you reach it, you stay:

x* = g( x* )A fixed point of the law of motion. At the steady state, inflows exactly balance outflows and the stock stops changing.

Transition dynamics is the path the system follows on its way to the steady state. Because capital has diminishing returns, an economy starting with little capital grows fast at first, then slows as it approaches x* — investment is eventually entirely eaten by depreciation, and growth in capital stops. This single mechanism explains the headline prediction we will derive next week: poor countries with the same technology should grow faster and converge toward rich ones.

✦ Worked Example — iterate the capital stock to its steady state

Suppose investment each period is a fixed amount I = 30, the depreciation rate is δ = 0.10, and capital starts at K(0) = 100. Trace the path and find the steady state.

  1. Law of motion: K(t+1) = K(t) + 30 - 0.10·K(t) = 0.90·K(t) + 30.
  2. Period 0 → 1: K(1) = 0.90(100) + 30 = 90 + 30 = 120. Capital rises (investment beat depreciation of 10).
  3. Period 1 → 2: K(2) = 0.90(120) + 30 = 108 + 30 = 138. Still rising, but the jump (18) is smaller than before (20).
  4. Period 2 → 3: K(3) = 0.90(138) + 30 = 124.2 + 30 = 154.2. The increments keep shrinking — transition dynamics in action.
  5. Steady state: set K* = 0.90·K* + 30 → 0.10·K* = 30 → K* = 300. There, investment (30) exactly replaces depreciation (0.10×300 = 30) and capital stops changing.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate the capital path in Python

All course we use the free scientific Python stack — NumPy and SciPy for the math, Matplotlib to plot, and the QuantEcon library for ready-made economic tools. Week 1 setup: install once below, then reuse it every lecture and for the project.

⬇ Install Python + QuantEcon + NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the stack:

python3 -m venv macro-env
source macro-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux
macro-env\Scripts\activate         # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install quantecon numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import quantecon, numpy, scipy; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

Now code the law of motion as a simple loop and watch capital climb to its steady state of 300.

# capital_path.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

I, delta, K0, T = 30.0, 0.10, 100.0, 40   # investment, depreciation, start, periods

K = np.empty(T + 1)
K[0] = K0
for t in range(T):
    K[t + 1] = K[t] + I - delta * K[t]    # the dynamical-system rule

K_star = I / delta                        # steady state: 30 / 0.10 = 300
print(f"K(1)={K[1]:.1f}, K(2)={K[2]:.1f}, steady state K*={K_star:.0f}")

plt.plot(K, label="capital K(t)")
plt.axhline(K_star, ls="--", color="orange", label="steady state K*")
plt.xlabel("time"); plt.ylabel("capital"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

The printout confirms K(1)=120, K(2)=138, K*=300, and the plot shows the path flattening as it converges. Try lowering δ to 0.05: the steady state jumps to 600 — slower depreciation lets capital accumulate to a higher level.

🔗 Where this is heading

This week's pieces — a production function, a stock that accumulates from flows, and a law of motion with a steady state — are the entire skeleton of next week's Solow growth model (Week 2), where we let investment itself depend on output and derive the convergence prediction formally. From there we add deliberate saving and consumption choices (the Ramsey model), endogenous technology growth, and finally stochastic shocks that make output fluctuate — the bridge to business-cycle and asset-pricing models that connect this course to the Quant Finance track.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Which of the following is a stock rather than a flow?
A. the capital that exists in the economy today
B. investment per year
C. depreciation per year
D. output produced this quarter
Show answer

A. A stock is measured at a point in time with no time unit. Capital-on-hand is a stock; investment (B), depreciation (C), and output-per-quarter (D) are all measured per unit of time, so they are flows.

Q2. In the Cobb–Douglas function Y = A·K^α·L^(1−α), the property that doubling both K and L exactly doubles Y is called…
A. diminishing returns
B. total factor productivity
C. constant returns to scale
D. depreciation
Show answer

C. Because the exponents sum to 1, scaling all inputs by a factor scales output by the same factor — constant returns to scale. Diminishing returns (A) refers to adding one input alone; TFP (B) is the multiplier A; depreciation (D) is unrelated.

Q3. A steady state x* of a dynamical system x(t+1) = g(x(t)) satisfies…
A. x* = 0
B. x* = g(x*)
C. g(x*) grows without bound
D. g(x*) is always negative
Show answer

B. A steady state is a fixed point: applying the rule returns the same state, so the system stops changing. It need not be zero (A), and a steady state is by definition not exploding (C) or sign-restricted (D).

Q4. With K(t+1) = 0.90·K(t) + 30 and K(0) = 100, what is K(1)?
A. 100
B. 110
C. 130
D. 120
Show answer

D. K(1) = 0.90(100) + 30 = 90 + 30 = 120. Answer B (110) forgets the investment scaling; C (130) forgets depreciation entirely; A (100) assumes no change.

Q5. Why does an economy with diminishing returns to capital eventually stop growing through capital accumulation alone?
A. because labor disappears over time
B. because each extra unit of capital adds less output, until investment only replaces depreciation
C. because output has constant returns to scale
D. because total factor productivity falls to zero
Show answer

B. Diminishing returns mean each new machine yields less, so eventually new investment just offsets depreciation and capital reaches its steady state. Labor does not vanish (A); constant returns to scale (C) is about scaling all inputs together, not the limit on capital alone; TFP staying fixed (D) is an assumption, not the mechanism.
