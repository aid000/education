Week 4 · Lecture 4

Capacitors & Inductors

The two elements that store energy — and bring time into the circuit. Everything so far was instantaneous; now history matters.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the i–v laws for a capacitor (i = C dv/dt) and an inductor (v = L di/dt).
  • Compute energy stored in a capacitor (½CV²) and an inductor (½LI²).
  • Explain the continuity rules: capacitor voltage and inductor current can't jump instantly.
  • Give the DC steady-state behavior: capacitor → open, inductor → short.
  • Find RC and RL time constants and sketch the resulting exponential transient.

4.1The Capacitor

A capacitor is two conductive plates separated by an insulator. Apply a voltage and charge piles up on the plates — it stores energy in an electric field. The stored charge is proportional to voltage, with the constant being the capacitance C (in farads):

q = C · v

Differentiate (current is the flow of charge, i = dq/dt) to get the capacitor's defining law — current depends on how fast the voltage changes, not its value:

i = C · dv/dt   [A]No change in voltage ⟹ no current. A fast-changing voltage ⟹ large current.

The energy held in that field is:

E = ½ · C · v²  [J]
AnalogyA capacitor is like a stretchy water balloon on a pipe. Pushing water in stretches it (stores energy); the more you push, the harder it pushes back. Water only flows while the balloon is filling or emptying — once it's stretched to match the pressure, flow stops. That's why a steady (DC) voltage eventually gives zero capacitor current.

4.2The Inductor

An inductor is a coil of wire. Current through it creates a magnetic field; changing that current induces a voltage that opposes the change (Lenz's law, from your AP Physics C E&M). It stores energy in a magnetic field. Its defining law is the dual of the capacitor's — voltage depends on how fast the current changes:

v = L · di/dt   [V]L is the inductance, in henries (H). Constant current ⟹ zero inductor voltage.

Its stored energy mirrors the capacitor's:

E = ½ · L · i²  [J]
AnalogyAn inductor is like the inertia of water already moving in a pipe (a heavy flywheel on the flow). You can't start or stop it instantly — it resists changes in current. Push harder and it speeds up gradually; try to cut the flow and it "wants" to keep going.

4.3Continuity & DC Steady State

Because energy can't teleport, two quantities are continuous — they can't jump instantaneously:

capacitor voltage can't jump  ·  inductor current can't jump

(A capacitor's voltage would need infinite current to change instantly; an inductor's current would need infinite voltage. Neither is physical.)

Long after a switch settles (DC steady state)

If nothing is changing, dv/dt = 0 and di/dt = 0, so the defining laws collapse:

capacitor → i = 0 ⟹ open circuit  ·  inductor → v = 0 ⟹ short circuit
Common mistakeStudents routinely swap these. Keep them straight: in DC steady state a capacitor is an OPEN (blocks DC) and an inductor is a SHORT (a plain wire). And for continuity it's the capacitor's voltage and the inductor's current that can't jump — not the other way around.

4.4Time Constants & Transients

Put a resistor with a capacitor (or inductor) and the circuit responds over time with an exponential curve. The speed is set by the time constant τ:

RC circuit: τ = R · C   ·   RL circuit: τ = L / R  [seconds]

A charging capacitor approaches its final voltage as v(t) = V(1 − e−t/τ). The useful milestones:

at t = 1τ → 63% of final · at 3τ → 95% · at 5τ → ~99% ("settled")

Discharge is the mirror image — an exponential decay v(t) = V₀ e−t/τ, falling to 37% after one τ. Bigger R or bigger C ⟹ longer τ ⟹ slower circuit.

✦ Worked Example

A 5 V source charges an initially-empty 10 µF capacitor through a 20 kΩ resistor. Find (a) the time constant, (b) the capacitor voltage after one time constant, (c) the time to be "fully" charged, and (d) the final stored energy.

  1. Time constant: τ = RC = (20×10³)(10×10⁻⁶) = 0.2 s.
  2. Voltage at t = τ: v = 5(1 − e⁻¹) = 5 × 0.632 ≈ 3.16 V (63% of 5 V).
  3. "Fully" charged: ~5τ = 5 × 0.2 = 1.0 s (reaches ~99% of 5 V).
  4. Final energy: E = ½CV² = ½ × 10×10⁻⁶ × 5² = 125 µJ.

❓ Lecture 4 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The current through a capacitor is largest when…
A. its voltage is at its maximum
B. its voltage is changing fastest
C. its voltage is constant
D. its stored charge is zero
Show answer

B. i = C·dv/dt — current depends on the rate of change of voltage, not its value. Constant voltage ⟹ zero current.

Q2. The energy stored in a 4 µF capacitor charged to 50 V is…
A. 5 mJ
B. 100 µJ
C. 10 mJ
D. 0.2 J
Show answer

A. E = ½CV² = ½ × 4×10⁻⁶ × 50² = ½ × 4×10⁻⁶ × 2500 = 5×10⁻³ J = 5 mJ.

Q3. In DC steady state, an ideal capacitor behaves like a…
A. short circuit
B. open circuit
C. fixed resistor
D. voltage source
Show answer

B. With constant voltage, dv/dt = 0 so i = 0 — no current flows, i.e. an open circuit. (An inductor, by contrast, becomes a short.)

Q4. An RL circuit has L = 2 H and R = 1 kΩ. Its time constant is…
A. 2 s
B. 2 ms
C. 500 ms
D. 0.5 ms
Show answer

B. For RL, τ = L/R = 2 / 1000 = 0.002 s = 2 ms. (Note it's L/R, not L·R.)

Q5. Which quantity cannot change instantaneously?
A. the voltage across a capacitor
B. the voltage across a resistor
C. the current through a capacitor
D. the voltage of an ideal source
Show answer

A. Capacitor voltage is continuous (an instant jump would need infinite current). Likewise inductor current can't jump. Resistor voltage and capacitor current can change instantly.

✓ What you learned

  • The capacitor law i = C·dv/dt (energy ½CV²) and the inductor law v = L·di/dt (energy ½LI²) — duals of each other.
  • Continuity: a capacitor's voltage and an inductor's current cannot change instantaneously.
  • DC steady state: a capacitor → open, an inductor → short.
  • Time constants τ = RC and τ = L/R, and the exponential milestones (63% at 1τ, ~99% at 5τ).
  • Ran a transient (.tran) simulation in Ngspice and watched a capacitor charge.
