Week 10 · Lecture 10

Intro to Transistors

The device that amplifies and switches — and the reason chips exist. The capstone of this course and the gateway to fabrication and architecture.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the core transistor idea: a small input controls a large current.
  • Describe the BJT: terminals, current gain β, and its three operating regions.
  • Describe the MOSFET: gate control, threshold voltage, and why it dominates chips.
  • Contrast the transistor's two jobs — amplifier and switch.
  • Connect this to the next courses: fabrication (C2) and architecture (C3).

10.1The Big Idea: Controlled Current

A transistor is a three-terminal device where a small signal on the control terminal governs a much larger current between the other two. That single capability — a separate, independent control — is what the diode lacked, and it unlocks both amplification (small signal → big signal) and switching controlled by another circuit. Stack millions of these switches and you have a processor.

AnalogyA transistor is a faucet (or a relay): a light touch on the handle controls a powerful flow you aren't supplying by hand. A tiny effort at the control terminal commands a large current elsewhere — that's gain. A diode, by contrast, is a check valve with no handle: it can only respond to the flow itself.

10.2The BJT (Bipolar Junction Transistor)

The BJT has three terminals — base (B), collector (C), emitter (E). In an NPN, a small base current controls a much larger collector current, set by the current gain β (typically 50–300):

IC = β · IB  (in the active region)

Its three regions: cutoff (off — no base drive, no IC), active (amplifying — IC = βIB), and saturation (fully on — a closed switch). Amplifiers live in active; digital switches swing between cutoff and saturation.

10.3The MOSFET — the Chip's Workhorse

The MOSFET has terminals gate (G), drain (D), source (S). Unlike the current-controlled BJT, it is voltage-controlled: a voltage on the (insulated) gate forms a conducting channel between drain and source. An n-channel MOSFET turns on once the gate-source voltage exceeds the threshold voltage Vth.

Because the gate is insulated, it draws almost no steady current — so MOSFETs are tiny, low-power, and pack by the billions. That's why they, not BJTs, are the building block of digital chips (and the subject of Courses 2 and 3).

Common mistakeKeep the control mechanisms straight: the BJT is current-controlled (IC = βIB, base draws current); the MOSFET is voltage-controlled (gate voltage vs Vth, gate draws ~no current). Saying "base voltage times β" or "gate current" mixes the two models.

10.4Two Jobs, and the Road Ahead

Every transistor does one of two jobs. As an amplifier (e.g. the common-emitter stage), a small input swing produces a larger, inverted output swing — the analog heart of sensors and audio. As a switch (cutoff ↔ saturation/on), it represents a logic 0 or 1 — the digital heart of every gate and memory cell.

That switch is where this whole course has been heading. In Course 2 (Fabrication) you'll build a MOSFET physically, layer by layer; in Course 3 (Architecture) you'll wire millions of them into logic gates, an ALU, and a CPU. The circuits intuition from these ten weeks is the foundation for both.

✦ Worked Example

(a) An NPN BJT with β = 100 has a base current of 20 µA in the active region. Find the collector current. (b) As a switch, what VGS condition turns on an n-MOSFET with Vth = 1 V?

  1. Collector current: IC = β·IB = 100 × 20 µA = 2 mA.
  2. Current gain: a 20 µA input controls a 2 mA output — 100× larger. That ratio is the amplification.
  3. MOSFET on-condition: the channel forms when VGS > Vth = 1 V. Below 1 V it's off (cutoff); well above 1 V it's a closed switch.

Q1. For a BJT in the active region, the collector current is…
A. IC = β·IB
B. IC = IB/β
C. IC = β·VBE
D. IC = 0
Show answer

A. A small base current is amplified by β: IC = β·IB. With β = 100 and IB = 20 µA, IC = 2 mA.

Q2. An n-channel MOSFET turns on when its gate-source voltage…
A. exceeds the threshold voltage Vth
B. exceeds the breakdown voltage
C. equals zero
D. equals the drain voltage
Show answer

A. The channel forms only when VGS > Vth — the basis of the MOSFET switch.

Q3. A common-emitter amplifier's output, relative to its input, is…
A. in phase
B. inverted (180°)
C. identical
D. at a different frequency
Show answer

B. Rising input increases collector current, pulling the collector voltage down — an inverted output.

Q4. Which statement is correct about control type?
A. Both BJT and MOSFET are current-controlled
B. The BJT is current-controlled; the MOSFET is voltage-controlled
C. The BJT is voltage-controlled; the MOSFET is current-controlled
D. Both are voltage-controlled
Show answer

B. BJT: base current controls collector current. MOSFET: gate voltage controls the channel (gate draws ~no current).

Q5. Used as a digital switch, a transistor swings between which two regions?
A. active and breakdown
B. cutoff (off) and saturation/on
C. forward and reverse
D. resonance and damping
Show answer

B. A switch is fully off (cutoff) or fully on (saturation) — representing logic 0 and 1. Amplifiers instead stay in the active region.

✓ What you learned

  • A transistor's third terminal independently controls a larger current — enabling amplification and switching.
  • The BJT is current-controlled (IC = βIB) with cutoff / active / saturation regions.
  • The MOSFET is voltage-controlled (on when VGS > Vth), insulated gate, low power — the chip workhorse.
  • Two jobs: amplifier (common-emitter, inverting) and switch (cutoff ↔ saturation = logic 0/1).
  • This is the bridge to Course 2 (fabrication) and Course 3 (architecture).
