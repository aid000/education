Week 2 · Lecture 2

DC Resistive Circuits

Combine resistors, split voltage and current on purpose, and solve any resistive network with two systematic methods.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Reduce series and parallel resistor combinations to a single equivalent.
  • Apply the voltage-divider and current-divider rules.
  • Solve a circuit systematically with node-voltage analysis (KCL).
  • Solve a circuit systematically with mesh-current analysis (KVL).
  • Confirm a hand-solved network in Ngspice.

2.1Series Resistors & the Voltage Divider

Resistors are in series when the same current flows through each (they share one node with nothing else attached). Series resistances simply add:

Req = R₁ + R₂ + … + Rn

Because the current is common, each resistor takes a share of the source voltage proportional to its resistance — the voltage divider:

Vout = Vin · R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)

2.2Parallel Resistors & the Current Divider

Resistors are in parallel when they share both nodes, so the same voltage appears across each. Conductances (1/R) add, which means the equivalent resistance is always less than the smallest resistor:

1 / Req = 1/R₁ + 1/R₂ + …  ⟹  (two) Req = R₁R₂ / (R₁ + R₂)

The current splits in inverse proportion to resistance — the current divider. For two resistors, the current through R₁ is I·R₂/(R₁+R₂): the opposite resistor appears on top.

Common mistakeDon't add parallel resistors like series ones. The parallel equivalent is always smaller than the smallest resistor — if you ever compute a "parallel" Req larger than one of its parts, you've slipped a step. And in the current divider the opposite resistor goes on top (use R₂ to find the current in R₁), which catches almost everyone the first time.

2.3Node-Voltage Analysis

Series/parallel tricks fail once a circuit has bridging connections. The general method is node-voltage analysis, built on KCL:

① Pick a reference node (ground, 0 V). ② Label each remaining node's unknown voltage. ③ Write KCL at each unknown node, expressing every branch current via Ohm's law as (Vnode − Vother)/R. ④ Solve the resulting linear system.

Why it scalesOne equation per unknown node, no matter how tangled the wiring. This is exactly how SPICE solves circuits internally — you're learning the simulator's own method.

2.4Mesh-Current Analysis

The dual approach, built on KVL: define a circulating mesh current in each loop, write KVL around each loop (summing i·R drops), and solve. Node analysis is usually fewer equations when there are many loops but few nodes; mesh analysis wins when the reverse is true. Both give the same answer — pick whichever yields fewer unknowns.

✦ Worked Example

A 12 V source drives R₁ = 2 kΩ in series with the parallel combination of R₂ = 6 kΩ and R₃ = 3 kΩ. Find the total current and the voltage across the parallel pair.

  1. Collapse the parallel pair: R₂ ∥ R₃ = (6·3)/(6+3) = 2 kΩ.
  2. Total resistance: Req = R₁ + 2 kΩ = 2k + 2k = 4 kΩ.
  3. Total current: I = 12 V / 4 kΩ = 3 mA.
  4. Voltage across the parallel pair (node X): V = I · 2 kΩ = 3 mA · 2 kΩ = 6 V. (The other 6 V drops across R₁.)

Q1. Three resistors of 1 kΩ, 2 kΩ, 3 kΩ in series have an equivalent resistance of…
A. 0.55 kΩ
B. 6 kΩ
C. 2 kΩ
D. 1 kΩ
Show answer

B. Series resistances add: 1 + 2 + 3 = 6 kΩ.

Q2. Two equal resistors R in parallel give an equivalent of…
A. 2R
B. R
C. R/2
D. R²
Show answer

C. R∥R = R·R/(R+R) = R/2 — always less than the smallest resistor.

Q3. In a divider, Vin = 10 V across R₁ = 1 kΩ and R₂ = 4 kΩ in series. The voltage across R₂ is…
A. 2 V
B. 5 V
C. 8 V
D. 10 V
Show answer

C. Vout = 10·R₂/(R₁+R₂) = 10·4/5 = 8 V.

Q4. When current reaches a parallel pair, more of it flows through the…
A. larger resistor
B. smaller resistor
C. resistor closer to the source
D. they always split equally
Show answer

B. Current divides in inverse proportion to resistance — the path of least resistance carries more.

Q5. Node-voltage analysis writes one equation per unknown node using…
A. KVL (voltage law)
B. KCL (current law)
C. the power balance
D. Thévenin's theorem
Show answer

B. Node analysis sums currents (KCL) at each node; mesh analysis uses KVL around loops.
