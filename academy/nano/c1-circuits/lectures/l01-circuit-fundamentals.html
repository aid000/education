Week 1 · Lecture 1

Circuit Fundamentals

The four quantities every circuit is built from — and the two laws that govern all of them.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define charge, current, voltage, and power, and state their units.
  • Apply Ohm's law to relate voltage, current, and resistance.
  • Use Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL) and Voltage Law (KVL) to write circuit equations.
  • Track power: identify which elements deliver energy and which absorb it.
  • Simulate a simple resistive circuit in Ngspice and read off node voltages.

1.1Charge & Current

Everything in a circuit comes back to electric charge (q, in coulombs). You already met charge in AP Physics C; here we care about charge in motion. Current is the rate at which charge flows past a point:

i = dq / dt   [amperes, A = C/s]One amp is one coulomb of charge passing a point each second.

By convention, current direction is the direction positive charge would move — even though in a metal wire it's actually electrons drifting the other way. This "conventional current" is universal in circuit analysis, so we adopt it from day one.

AnalogyThink of a pipe of water. Charge is the amount of water; current is the flow rate (liters per second) past a marker. The pipe doesn't store or lose water — what flows in flows out. That conservation idea becomes Kirchhoff's Current Law in §1.4.

1.2Voltage & Power

Voltage (v, in volts) is the energy per unit charge between two points — the "push" that drives current. It is always measured across two points (a difference), never at a single point in isolation.

v = dw / dq   [volts, V = J/C]One volt means one joule of energy is transferred per coulomb of charge moved.

When charge moves through a voltage difference, energy is transferred. The rate of that transfer is power:

p = v · i   [watts, W = J/s]

Sign matters. Using the passive sign convention — current entering the + terminal of an element — a positive p means the element absorbs power (a resistor heating up); a negative p means it delivers power (a battery sourcing energy).

AnalogyBack to the pipe: voltage is the pressure difference (or a height drop). Water falling through a bigger drop releases more energy per liter — just as charge falling through a bigger voltage releases more energy per coulomb.

1.3Ohm's Law & Resistance

A resistor opposes current flow. For an ideal (linear) resistor, voltage and current are directly proportional — that proportionality constant is the resistance R (in ohms, Ω):

v = i · R   ⟺   i = v / R   ⟺   R = v / i

Combining Ohm's law with p = v·i gives the power dissipated in a resistor three equivalent ways:

p = v · i = i² · R = v² / R

A resistor always absorbs power and turns it into heat — its p is never negative. This is why power resistors get warm and why power dissipation is a first-order design concern in every chip.

Common mistakeOhm's law applies to resistors, not to every element. Capacitors, inductors, diodes, and transistors do not obey v = iR. We'll handle each on its own terms later in the course.

1.4Kirchhoff's Laws

Two conservation laws let you analyze any circuit, no matter how complex.

Kirchhoff's Current Law (KCL)

Charge can't pile up at a junction (a node), so the current in must equal the current out:

Σ iin = Σ iout   (at any node)

This is just conservation of charge — the "pipe junction" idea made precise.

Kirchhoff's Voltage Law (KVL)

Energy is conserved around any closed loop, so the voltage rises and drops around a loop sum to zero:

Σ v = 0   (around any closed loop)

Walk around a loop adding each element's voltage (with sign); you must return to the same potential you started at. KCL + KVL + Ohm's law are enough to solve every circuit in the first half of this course.

✦ Worked Example

A 9 V battery drives two resistors in series: R₁ = 1 kΩ and R₂ = 2 kΩ. Find the current and the power delivered by the battery.

  1. KVL around the loop: 9 V = i·R₁ + i·R₂ = i·(1k + 2k) = i·(3000 Ω).
  2. Solve for current: i = 9 / 3000 = 3 mA (same current through both — they're in series, KCL).
  3. Power from battery: p = v·i = 9 V × 3 mA = 27 mW.
  4. Check: R₁ absorbs i²R₁ = (3m)²·1000 = 9 mW; R₂ absorbs 18 mW; total 27 mW ✓ — matches what the battery delivers (energy conserved).

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate it in Ngspice

Ngspice is the free, open-source circuit simulator we use all course. Week 1 setup: install it once (below), then you'll reuse it every lecture and for the course project.

⬇ Install Ngspice (one-time, Week 1 setup)

macOS (with Homebrew):

brew install ngspice

Linux (Debian / Ubuntu):

sudo apt install ngspice

Windows: download the installer from ngspice.sourceforge.io and add it to your PATH — or install LTspice (free GUI) and paste the same netlist into a SPICE directive. Either works for this course.

Verify: run ngspice -v in a terminal — you should see a version string. If so, you're set.

Now create a file series.cir, run ngspice series.cir, and confirm the simulator agrees with the worked example above.

* Series resistor divider — Lecture 1
V1 1 0 9          ; 9V source from node 1 to ground
R1 1 2 1k         ; 1k between node 1 and node 2
R2 2 0 2k         ; 2k between node 2 and ground
.op               ; solve the DC operating point
.end

Expected: node 2 sits at 6 V (the divider), and the branch current reads 3 mA. Change R2 to 2k → 5k and predict the new node-2 voltage before re-running.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Current is best defined as…
A. energy per unit charge
B. the rate of flow of charge past a point
C. the total charge stored in an element
D. resistance times voltage
Show answer

B. Current i = dq/dt — charge per unit time (amperes). Energy per unit charge (A) is voltage.

Q2. A 2 kΩ resistor carries 5 mA. The voltage across it is…
A. 10 V
B. 2.5 V
C. 0.4 V
D. 100 V
Show answer

A. Ohm's law: v = iR = (5×10⁻³)(2×10³) = 10 V.

Q3. Kirchhoff's Current Law is a statement of the conservation of…
A. energy
B. momentum
C. charge
D. power
Show answer

C. KCL says charge can't accumulate at a node — current in equals current out. KVL is the energy-conservation law.

Q4. Using the passive sign convention, an element with p = −4 W is…
A. absorbing 4 W
B. short-circuited
C. dissipating 4 W as heat
D. delivering 4 W to the rest of the circuit
Show answer

D. Negative absorbed power means the element is a source — it delivers 4 W (e.g. a battery).

Q5. Two resistors, 1 kΩ and 3 kΩ, are in series across 8 V. The current is…
A. 8 mA
B. 2 mA
C. 6 mA
D. 0.5 mA
Show answer

B. Series resistances add: 1k + 3k = 4k. i = v/R = 8/4000 = 2 mA.
