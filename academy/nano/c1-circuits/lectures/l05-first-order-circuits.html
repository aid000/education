Week 5 · Lecture 5

First-Order Circuits

One formula solves every circuit with a single capacitor or inductor — no differential equations required, just three numbers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recognize a first-order circuit (one independent energy-storage element).
  • Apply the universal first-order formula using initial value, final value, and τ.
  • Find x(0⁺) with the continuity rule and x(∞) with DC steady-state rules.
  • Solve a switched RC step response and evaluate it at any time.
  • Read a phasor as a rotating vector — the bridge into AC analysis (Week 6).

5.1What Makes a Circuit "First-Order"

A circuit with exactly one independent energy-storage element — a single capacitor or a single inductor (plus any resistors and sources) — is a first-order circuit. The name comes from the math: applying KVL/KCL with i = C dv/dt (or v = L di/dt) yields a first-order differential equation.

The good news: you don't have to solve that differential equation each time. Every first-order response has the same exponential shape you saw in Lecture 4, so we can write the answer down directly from three quantities.

5.2The Universal First-Order Formula

Any voltage or current in a first-order circuit follows:

x(t) = x(∞) + [ x(0⁺) − x(∞) ] · e−t/τstart at x(0⁺), head exponentially toward x(∞), at a rate set by τ

So you only need three numbers:

① x(0⁺) — the value just after switching (the initial condition).
② x(∞) — the final, settled value (DC steady state).
③ τ — the time constant (RC or L/R), using the resistance the C or L "sees."

AnalogyThink of a thermostat. The temperature always heads from wherever it is now toward the set point, fast at first and slowing as it closes in. You don't need the furnace's differential equation — just the current temperature, the target, and how quickly the room responds (τ). First-order circuits behave exactly the same way.

5.3Step Response: a Switched RC

The classic first-order problem: a source connected to an RC by a switch that flips at t = 0. Below, closing the switch steps the input, and the capacitor charges along the universal curve.

Common mistakeGet the two endpoint values from the right models. For x(0⁺), use continuity — the capacitor voltage equals what it was just before switching (often 0 for an uncharged cap), not the source value. For x(∞), redraw the circuit at DC steady state with the capacitor as an open (inductor as a short) and solve that resistive circuit. Mixing these up is the #1 first-order error.

5.4A Glimpse Ahead: Phasors

First-order transients describe how a circuit settles. But what if the input never settles — a sinusoid that oscillates forever? Solving that with differential equations every time is painful, so engineers use a shortcut: represent each sinusoid as a phasor — a rotating vector in the complex plane whose length is the amplitude and whose angle is the phase.

That's the preview — next week we turn phasors into impedance and analyze AC circuits with the same Ohm's-law/KVL tools you already know.

✦ Worked Example

In the switched RC above, Vs = 12 V, R = 5 kΩ, C = 4 µF, and the capacitor is initially uncharged. The switch closes at t = 0. Find vC(t) and its value at t = 10 ms.

  1. Initial value x(0⁺): capacitor voltage is continuous and it was uncharged, so vC(0⁺) = 0 V.
  2. Final value x(∞): at DC steady state the capacitor is an open → no current → no drop across R → vC(∞) = Vs = 12 V.
  3. Time constant: τ = RC = (5×10³)(4×10⁻⁶) = 20 ms.
  4. Assemble: vC(t) = 12 + (0 − 12)e−t/20ms = 12(1 − e−t/20ms) V.
  5. At t = 10 ms: t/τ = 0.5, e−0.5 = 0.607, so vC = 12(1 − 0.607) ≈ 4.72 V.

🛠️ Hands-On: step response in Ngspice

Use an initial condition (IC=0) and the UIC flag so Ngspice starts the capacitor uncharged, then watch it charge.

❓ Lecture 5 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A circuit is "first-order" when it contains…
A. no energy-storage elements
B. exactly one independent capacitor or inductor
C. two or more capacitors and inductors
D. only resistors and sources
Show answer

B. One independent storage element gives a first-order differential equation — hence the exponential response.

Q2. In x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)]e−t/τ, the term x(∞) represents…
A. the value right after switching
B. the time constant
C. the final, steady-state value
D. the moment the switch flips
Show answer

C. x(∞) is the settled value the response heads toward; x(0⁺) is the starting value.

Q3. Just after a switch flips (t = 0⁺), a capacitor's voltage equals…
A. zero, always
B. its value just before switching (voltage is continuous)
C. the source voltage
D. whatever makes the current zero
Show answer

B. Capacitor voltage can't jump, so v(0⁺) = v(0⁻). It's only 0 if the cap happened to be uncharged beforehand.

Q4. A 10 V source charges an uncharged cap through R = 2 kΩ, C = 5 µF (switch closes at t = 0). The capacitor voltage at one time constant is…
A. 6.32 V
B. 3.68 V
C. 10 V
D. 5 V
Show answer

A. τ = RC = 10 ms. v(τ) = 10(1 − e⁻¹) = 10 × 0.632 = 6.32 V (63% of the final 10 V).

Q5. To find the final value x(∞) in a DC circuit, treat a capacitor as ___ and an inductor as ___.
A. short / open
B. short / short
C. open / open
D. open / short
Show answer

D. At DC steady state the capacitor is an open (no current) and the inductor is a short (no voltage). Solve that resistive circuit for x(∞).

✓ What you learned

  • A first-order circuit has one independent C or L and an exponential response.
  • The universal formula x(t) = x(∞) + [x(0⁺) − x(∞)]e−t/τ solves it from three numbers.
  • Find x(0⁺) by continuity (cap voltage / inductor current can't jump) and x(∞) from DC steady state (cap open, inductor short).
  • Worked a switched-RC step response and evaluated it at a given time.
  • Met the phasor — a rotating vector representing a sinusoid — the gateway to AC analysis.
