Diodes & Applications

The first nonlinear element — a one-way valve for current — and the rectifiers and switches it makes possible.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the diode's one-way behavior and read its I–V characteristic.
  • Use the ideal and the 0.7 V (silicon) diode models.
  • Explain how a rectifier converts AC to DC (half-wave and full-wave).
  • Recognize clippers, clamps, and the Zener voltage regulator.
  • See the diode as a switch — the conceptual bridge to transistors.

9.1The Diode: a One-Way Valve

A diode is a pn junction (its semiconductor physics is covered in the Fabrication course) that conducts in only one direction. Forward-biased (anode positive), it conducts freely; reverse-biased, it blocks. Its current–voltage curve is steeply nonlinear — so Ohm's law and superposition no longer apply directly.

Two handy models: the ideal diode — a perfect switch (short when forward, open when reverse) — and the 0.7 V model, which adds a constant ~0.7 V forward drop for silicon. Use ideal for quick reasoning, 0.7 V when the drop matters.

AnalogyA diode is a one-way valve (a check valve) in a water pipe: water flows freely one way, but slam the valve shut if it tries to flow back. The 0.7 V is the small "cracking pressure" needed before the valve opens at all.

9.2Rectifiers: AC → DC

Because a diode passes only one polarity, it turns alternating current into one-directional current — a rectifier, the heart of every power supply. The simplest is the half-wave rectifier (just one diode), shown below: it passes the positive halves and blocks the negative ones, which flatten to zero. A four-diode bridge instead makes a full-wave rectifier (it flips the negative halves up rather than discarding them). Either way, a smoothing capacitor then fills the gaps to approximate steady DC.

9.3Clippers, Clamps & the Zener

Point diodes at thresholds and you can clip a waveform (chop off peaks above a level — protection and signal shaping) or clamp it (shift its DC level). Run a special Zener diode backwards and it holds a steady breakdown voltage — a simple voltage regulator that pins an output at, say, 5.1 V despite a varying input.

Common mistakeA normal diode is used forward; a Zener regulator is used reverse-biased, operating in its breakdown region on purpose. Reverse breakdown destroys an ordinary diode but is the Zener's whole job. Don't analyze a Zener regulator as if the Zener were forward-conducting.

9.4The Diode as a Switch → Toward Transistors

Strip away the details and a diode is a voltage-controlled switch: above its threshold it's ON (a short), below it's OFF (an open). That's powerful — but a diode's switch is controlled by the same terminals that carry the current, so it can't amplify or be steered by a separate signal. The transistor fixes exactly that: a third terminal that controls the switch independently. That's next week — and the foundation of every chip.

✦ Worked Example

(a) A half-wave rectifier is driven by a 10 V-peak sine. Using the 0.7 V silicon model, what is the peak output voltage? (b) A 5.1 V Zener regulator is fed from 12 V through a 1 kΩ series resistor, with the Zener holding 5.1 V. What current flows through the resistor?

  1. Rectifier peak: on the positive half the diode drops 0.7 V, so Vout,peak = 10 − 0.7 = 9.3 V. The negative half is blocked (≈ 0 V out).
  2. Zener resistor current: the resistor sees the input minus the Zener voltage: 12 − 5.1 = 6.9 V across 1 kΩ.
  3. Compute: I = 6.9 V / 1 kΩ = 6.9 mA (this splits between the load and the Zener).

Model response

A diode is a two-terminal switch: whether it conducts is decided by the very voltage across the terminals that carry the current. So it can steer or block current, but it can't be controlled independently of the current it's switching — and it can't make a small input control a larger output. A transistor adds a third (control) terminal: a small voltage or current at the gate/base decides how much current flows between the other two terminals. That gives two things a diode can't: amplification (small signal → larger signal) and a controlled switch driven by a separate input.

That separate control is exactly what logic needs: the output of one gate must drive the input of the next without the two being the same node. Cascading, fan-out, inversion, and gain all require an independent control terminal — so CMOS logic and every chip are built from transistors, while diodes stay in supporting roles (rectifying, clamping, protection).
