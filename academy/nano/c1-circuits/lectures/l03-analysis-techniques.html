Week 3 · Lecture 3

Techniques of Analysis

Three power tools that turn an intimidating circuit into a one-line answer: superposition, Thévenin/Norton equivalents, and maximum power transfer.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply superposition to find a response from multiple independent sources.
  • Reduce any linear two-terminal network to its Thévenin equivalent (Vth, Rth).
  • Convert between Thévenin and Norton equivalents via source transformation.
  • Find the load resistance that extracts maximum power, and compute that power.
  • Verify a Thévenin equivalent in Ngspice.

3.1Superposition

In Lecture 2 you solved circuits with node and mesh analysis — powerful, but it can mean a lot of simultaneous equations. Superposition is a shortcut that works in any linear circuit (resistors, plus the capacitors/inductors we meet later):

total response = Σ (response from each source acting alone)

To "turn off" the other sources while you analyze one: replace each voltage source with a short (0 V = wire) and each current source with an open (0 A = gap). Solve the simple one-source circuit, repeat for every source, then add the partial answers.

Why "linear"?Superposition relies on responses scaling and adding proportionally. A diode or transistor is non-linear, so you can't superpose sources across one — a key reason we treat those elements separately later.

3.2Thévenin's Theorem

Here's the big idea: any linear two-terminal network — no matter how many sources and resistors — looks, from the outside, exactly like a single voltage source in series with a single resistor.

Vth in series with RthThe Thévenin equivalent — indistinguishable from the original at the terminals.

Finding Vth and Rth

Vth = the open-circuit voltage across the terminals (disconnect the load and measure/compute the voltage).

Rth = the resistance seen looking into the terminals with all independent sources turned off (voltage→short, current→open).

Why it's usefulIf you're going to test many different loads on the same circuit, you find Vth/Rth once, then every load is a trivial two-resistor divider. That's the practical payoff — and the subject of today's discussion below.

3.3Norton's Theorem & Source Transformation

Norton is Thévenin's dual: the same network can instead be modeled as a current source in parallel with a resistor.

IN in parallel with RN,   RN = Rth

IN = the short-circuit current (current that flows if you short the terminals). The two equivalents are linked by Ohm's law:

Vth = IN · Rth

Swapping between the two forms — source transformation — often collapses a messy circuit a step at a time until the answer falls out.

3.4Maximum Power Transfer

Given a source with Thévenin resistance Rth, what load RL draws the most power? Too small a load and the voltage collapses; too large and little current flows. The optimum is the match:

RL = Rth  ⟹   Pmax = Vth² / (4 · Rth)

This "impedance matching" idea reappears everywhere — audio amplifiers, RF antennas, and the signal-integrity work you'll meet in the VLSI courses. (Note: matching maximizes power transfer, not efficiency — at the match, half the power is lost in Rth.)

✦ Worked Example

A 12 V source connects through R₁ = 6 kΩ to node A; R₂ = 3 kΩ runs from A to ground. Find the Thévenin equivalent seen at A–ground, then the load for maximum power and that power.

  1. Vth (open-circuit at A): with the load removed it's a divider — Vth = 12 · R₂/(R₁+R₂) = 12 · 3/9 = 4 V.
  2. Rth (sources off): short the 12 V source; from A you see R₁ ∥ R₂ = (6·3)/(6+3) = 2 kΩ.
  3. Equivalent: 4 V in series with 2 kΩ.
  4. Max power transfer: RL = Rth = 2 kΩ; Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth) = 16/(4·2000) = 2 mW.

B. a short circuit
C. a 1 Ω resistor

B. Voltage sources → short (0 V is a wire); current sources → open (0 A is a gap).

Q3. The Thévenin voltage Vth equals the network's…
A. short-circuit current
B. source voltage, always
C. total power
D. open-circuit terminal voltage
Show answer

D. Vth is the voltage measured across the terminals with the load removed (open circuit).

Q4. A Norton equivalent has IN = 3 mA and RN = 2 kΩ. Its Thévenin voltage is…
A. 1.5 V
B. 6 V
C. 0.67 V
D. 600 V
Show answer

B. Vth = IN·Rth = (3×10⁻³)(2×10³) = 6 V.

Q5. A source with Vth = 10 V, Rth = 5 Ω delivers maximum power to a load when RL = 5 Ω. That power is…
A. 20 W
B. 10 W
C. 5 W
D. 2.5 W

C. Pmax = Vth²/(4Rth) = 100/(20) = 5 W.

Model response

Thévenin thinking shines whenever you test many different loads on the same source network. Example: a sensor's output stage drives different display, logging, and alarm circuits. Instead of re-solving the whole sensor network for each load, you find Vth/Rth once, then every load is a simple two-resistor divider — and Rth immediately tells you how much the output "sags" under load (output resistance). It also makes maximum power transfer and impedance-matching decisions one-liners.

Where it does not help: if you only ever have one fixed circuit with one load and need internal node voltages/currents, the reduction throws away exactly the internal detail you want — full node/mesh analysis is more direct. It also doesn't apply across non-linear elements (a diode/transistor inside the box breaks the linear-equivalent assumption).
