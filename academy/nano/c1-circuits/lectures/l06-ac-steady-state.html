Week 6 · Lecture 6

AC Steady-State

Turn the phasor idea into impedance — then analyze sinusoidal circuits with the same Ohm's-law and KVL tools you already know.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe a sinusoid by amplitude, angular frequency ω, and phase, and write it as a phasor.
  • State the impedances of R, L, and C and how they depend on frequency.
  • Combine impedances in series/parallel and apply Ohm's law in the phasor domain.
  • Compute the magnitude and phase of a current or voltage in an AC circuit.
  • Distinguish real, reactive, and apparent power, and define power factor.

6.1Sinusoids & Phasors

An AC signal is a sinusoid: v(t) = Vm cos(ωt + φ), set by its amplitude Vm, its angular frequency ω = 2πf (rad/s), and its phase φ. Since every signal in a linear circuit driven at one frequency shares that same ω, we can drop the cos(ωt) bookkeeping and track just the amplitude and phase — the phasor V = Vm∠φ (Lecture 5's rotating vector).

6.2Impedance — Ohm's Law for AC

The breakthrough: in the phasor domain each element obeys a generalized Ohm's law V = I·Z, where impedance Z is a complex number (in ohms):

ZR = R  ·  ZL = jωL  ·  ZC = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC)

The real part is resistance; the imaginary part is reactance X. An inductor's reactance (+jωL) grows with frequency; a capacitor's (−j/ωC) shrinks with frequency. The "j" encodes the 90° phase shift — inductor voltage leads current, capacitor current leads voltage.

AnalogyImpedance is "resistance with a sense of timing." A resistor opposes current bluntly. An inductor and capacitor also oppose current, but they do it out of step with the voltage (90° off) — like a person who pushes a swing slightly early or late. The "j" is just bookkeeping for that timing.

6.3Analyzing AC Circuits

Here's the payoff: once everything is an impedance, all your DC tools work unchanged — series impedances add, parallel impedances combine by reciprocals, voltage dividers and KVL/KCL all apply — you just do the arithmetic with complex numbers. Solve for the phasor, then read off magnitude (amplitude) and angle (phase).

|Z| = √(R² + X²)  ·  ∠Z = arctan(X / R)
Common mistakeDon't add impedance magnitudes like resistances. |Z₁| + |Z₂| ≠ |Z₁ + Z₂| in general — you must add the complex impedances (real + imaginary parts separately), then take the magnitude. For a 30 Ω resistor in series with a 40 Ω reactance, |Z| = √(30² + 40²) = 50 Ω, not 70 Ω.

6.4Power in AC Circuits

Because voltage and current can be out of phase by angle θ, AC power splits into three pieces:

P = Vrms Irms cos θ (real, W) · Q = Vrms Irms sin θ (reactive, VAR) · S = Vrms Irms (apparent, VA)

Only real power P does useful work; reactive power Q sloshes back and forth into the L/C each cycle. Their ratio is the power factor, PF = cos θ — and these three form a right triangle (S² = P² + Q²).

✦ Worked Example

A 100 V (rms) source at ω drives a 30 Ω resistor in series with an inductor whose reactance is XL = 40 Ω. Find the impedance magnitude, the rms current, the phase angle, and the real power.

  1. Impedance: Z = 30 + j40 Ω, so |Z| = √(30² + 40²) = √2500 = 50 Ω.
  2. Current: Irms = V/|Z| = 100 / 50 = 2 A.
  3. Phase angle: θ = arctan(40/30) = 53.1° (current lags voltage — inductive).
  4. Real power: P = VrmsIrmscos θ = 100 × 2 × cos 53.1° = 200 × 0.6 = 120 W. (Equivalently I²R = 2²×30 = 120 W.)

🛠️ Hands-On: an AC sweep in Ngspice

Ngspice's .ac analysis sweeps frequency and reports magnitude and phase. Build a series RC low-pass and watch the output roll off as frequency rises.

* RC low-pass AC sweep — Lecture 6
V1 1 0 AC 1
R1 1 2 1k
C1 2 0 159n        ; cutoff f = 1/(2*pi*R*C) ~ 1 kHz
.ac dec 20 10 1meg ; 10 Hz to 1 MHz, 20 pts/decade
.end

Run plot vdb(2) (magnitude in dB) and plot 180/pi*vp(2) (phase). Expected: flat at low frequency, then −20 dB/decade roll-off above ~1 kHz, phase heading toward −90°.

Q1. As frequency increases, the impedance magnitude of an inductor…
A. increases
B. decreases
C. stays constant
D. goes to zero
Show answer

A. ZL = jωL, so |ZL| = ωL rises with frequency. (A capacitor is the opposite.)

Q2. The impedance of an ideal capacitor is…
A. jωC
B. −j/(ωC)
C. ωC
D. R
Show answer

B. ZC = 1/(jωC) = −j/(ωC): purely reactive, negative imaginary, shrinking with frequency.

Q3. A 30 Ω resistor is in series with a reactance of 40 Ω. The impedance magnitude is…
A. 70 Ω
B. 10 Ω
C. 50 Ω
D. 1200 Ω
Show answer

C. |Z| = √(30² + 40²) = √2500 = 50 Ω — add as a vector, not 30 + 40.

Q4. If the voltage and current in a load are 60° out of phase, the power factor is…
A. 0.87
B. 0
C. 1.0
D. 0.5
Show answer

D. PF = cos θ = cos 60° = 0.5.

Q5. In a purely resistive AC circuit, the current and voltage are…
A. 90° out of phase
B. in phase
C. 180° out of phase
D. at different frequencies
Show answer

B. A resistor has real impedance (no j), so θ = 0 — voltage and current peak together, PF = 1.

Model response

Phasors/impedance convert calculus into algebra: instead of writing and solving a differential equation for every circuit, you replace each element with a complex impedance and reuse Ohm's law, series/parallel rules, and dividers — the same toolkit from Lectures 1–3. You get magnitude and phase directly, and frequency dependence falls out naturally (great for filters and Bode plots). It buys huge speed and intuition.

The quiet assumptions: the circuit must be linear (no diodes/transistors in their nonlinear regions), it's driven by a single sinusoidal frequency (or a sum you can superpose frequency-by-frequency), and you're looking at the steady state — all the first-order/second-order transients from earlier weeks have died out. Inside those conditions, impedance is exact; outside them, you're back to differential equations or simulation.
