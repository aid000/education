Week 10 · Lecture 10

Clocking, Power & Scaling

What sets a CPU's clock speed — and why power, not transistors, became the limit.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how the clock synchronizes every sequential element, and why its period is bounded by the critical path plus setup time.
  • Describe clock distribution (the clock tree / H-tree) and how skew and jitter erode the timing budget.
  • Split total power into dynamic (∝ C·V²·f) and static (leakage) components.
  • Explain the "power wall" — why power density, not transistor count, capped frequency and forced multicore.
  • Compute the maximum clock frequency from a given critical-path delay and setup time.

10.1The Clock

A synchronous chip runs on a clock — a global square-wave signal that ticks at a fixed rate and tells every sequential element (every flip-flop) when to capture its next value. On each rising edge, data launched by one flip-flop must have travelled through the combinational logic and arrived — and settled — at the next flip-flop before that edge.

For this to work, the clock period Tclk must be long enough to cover the longest combinational path between any two flip-flops — the critical path — plus the setup time the capturing flip-flop needs to latch a stable value:

Tclk ≥ tpd + tsetuptpd = longest combinational (critical-path) delay; tsetup = flip-flop setup time. The clock frequency is f = 1 / Tclk.

Shorten the critical path and you can shorten the period — i.e. raise the clock frequency. That is why so much of architecture is a hunt for the slowest path and ways to break it up.

AnalogyThe clock is the drummer keeping the whole band in time. Set the tempo too fast and the slowest player — the critical path — can't keep up: a note lands late and the music falls apart. That late note is a timing violation. The band can only play as fast as its slowest member can reliably keep up.

10.2Clock Distribution & Skew

The clock must reach every one of millions of flip-flops at very nearly the same instant. But wires have delay, so the edge arrives at different places at slightly different times. The difference in arrival time between two points is clock skew; cycle-to-cycle wobble in the edge's timing is jitter.

To keep skew small, designers route the clock through a carefully balanced clock tree — often an H-tree, where each branch from the source to a leaf travels the same total distance, so every endpoint sees the edge at the same time.

Skew and jitter are not free — they eat directly into the timing budget. Worst-case skew effectively subtracts from the time available for logic, so a sloppy clock tree lowers the maximum safe frequency. A balanced tree is what lets a real chip run near its critical-path limit.

10.3Power

A chip's total power splits into two parts:

Ptotal = Pdynamic + Pstatic

Dynamic power is burned every time a node switches — charging and discharging its capacitance. It scales with how much capacitance you switch (C), the square of the supply voltage (V²), and how often you switch (the frequency f):

Pdynamic ∝ C · V² · fBecause of the V² term, lowering the supply voltage is the single most powerful knob for cutting power.

Static power is leakage — current that trickles through transistors even when they aren't switching. As transistors shrank, leakage grew from negligible to a major fraction of the budget.

Here is the twist that reshaped the whole industry. As frequency and transistor counts climbed, the power crammed into each square millimetre — the power density — rose faster than chips could be cooled. It was power, not the supply of transistors, that became the binding limit. This is the power wall.

10.4Techniques & the Road Ahead

Designers fight the power wall with several techniques. Clock gating shuts off the clock to blocks that aren't doing work, killing their dynamic power (no switching ⟹ no C·V²·f). Pipelining chops a long critical path into shorter stages, so each stage's tpd is smaller and the clock can run faster without raising voltage. And when single-core frequency stalled at the wall, the industry went multicore — many slower, cooler cores instead of one ever-faster one.

Scaling brings one more headache: as wires get thinner and longer relative to gates, interconnect delay grows and can dominate gate delay — so where signals go matters as much as the logic itself.

That is exactly the boundary of this course. In Course 4 (VLSI Design) you'll size and lay out the transistors behind these gates; in Course 5 (Physical Design) you'll place cells and route the interconnect — including the clock tree — to actually meet timing and power on silicon.

Common mistakeThree things to keep straight. The clock period is set by the slowest (critical) path, not the average path. Dynamic power scales as C·V²·f — so lowering voltage (the squared term) buys the most. And it was the power wall — power density, not running out of transistors — that capped clock frequencies around ~3–4 GHz and drove the shift to multicore.
✦ Worked Example

A pipeline stage has a longest combinational path delay of 2 ns, and the capturing flip-flop needs a setup time of 0.2 ns. Find the maximum clock frequency, and state how you'd go faster.

  1. Minimum period: Tclk ≥ tpd + tsetup = 2 ns + 0.2 ns = 2.2 ns.
  2. Maximum frequency: fmax = 1 / Tclk = 1 / 2.2 ns ≈ 455 MHz.
  3. To go faster: you must shorten the critical path — e.g. by pipelining (splitting the 2 ns logic into shorter stages), since raising voltage to push frequency only worsens the C·V²·f power.

🛠️ Hands-On: fmax from the critical path (paper)

No simulator needed — this is the calculation every timing report boils down to. On paper, take a critical-path delay and a setup time and compute the maximum clock frequency.

* Given a critical-path delay t_pd and setup time t_setup:
T_clk(min) = t_pd + t_setup
f_max      = 1 / T_clk(min)

; Example: t_pd = 3.3 ns, t_setup = 0.2 ns
T_clk(min) = 3.3 ns + 0.2 ns = 3.5 ns
f_max      = 1 / 3.5 ns ≈ 286 MHz

Now try your own: if tpd = 1.8 ns and tsetup = 0.2 ns, what is fmax? (Answer: 1 / 2.0 ns = 500 MHz.) Then ask: what would pipelining the path in two do to fmax?
