Thin-Film Deposition

A chip is built one thin layer at a time. Learn the three ways to lay down those films — grow them, react gases onto them, or physically sputter them — and why the order and quality matter.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a chip is built as a stack of alternating conductor, insulator, and semiconductor films.
  • Describe thermal oxidation and why it consumes silicon as the oxide grows.
  • Distinguish CVD (a chemical reaction of gases) from PVD (physical transport of atoms).
  • Match common films — gate oxide, polysilicon, nitrides, metals — to the right deposition method.
  • Compute deposited film thickness and the silicon consumed during oxidation.

3.1Building a Chip Layer by Layer

A modern chip is not carved from a single block — it is built up as a stack of dozens of thin films. The films alternate in role: conductors (metals, doped polysilicon) carry signals, insulators (SiO₂, silicon nitride) keep layers apart, and semiconductors (the silicon itself, plus polysilicon) form the active devices. Each film is first deposited (or grown) across the whole wafer, then patterned — the unwanted regions are etched away so only the desired shapes remain before the next layer goes down.

This deposit-then-pattern cycle repeats again and again. Because every layer rests on the one below it, the flatness, cleanliness, and exact thickness of the early films set the stage for everything that follows.

AnalogyBuilding a chip is like finishing furniture with coat after coat of paint and varnish — each coat is applied and then carefully carved or sanded into shape before the next goes on. One twist: thermal oxidation is more like rust forming from the metal itself (the surface converts into oxide), whereas the other methods are paint added on top of what's already there.

3.2Thermal Oxidation

Silicon's secret weapon is that it forms an excellent native oxide. In thermal oxidation, the wafer is heated to ~900–1200 °C in a furnace with oxygen (dry oxidation) or water vapor (wet oxidation), and the silicon reacts to grow a high-quality, defect-free film of SiO₂.

Si + O₂ → SiO₂  ·  Si + 2H₂O → SiO₂ + 2H₂

The crucial difference from every other method: oxidation consumes the silicon. The oxygen has to reach the silicon underneath, so the oxide grows partly into the wafer — roughly 44% of the final oxide thickness comes from silicon that was eaten away. The silicon/oxide interface therefore moves downward into the wafer. This produces the cleanest, most reliable dielectric available, which is why it makes the critical gate oxide that sits under a transistor's gate.

silicon consumed ≈ 0.44 × (oxide thickness grown)

3.3CVD — Chemical Vapor Deposition

Most films can't be grown from the wafer — they have to be added. In chemical vapor deposition (CVD), reactive gases (precursors) flow over the heated wafer and react or decompose at its surface, leaving behind a solid film while the byproduct gases are pumped away. Because it relies on a chemical reaction, CVD can deposit a wide range of materials: dielectrics (SiO₂), polysilicon, and silicon nitrides.

Two common variants tune the conditions:

  • LPCVD (low-pressure CVD) — runs at low pressure for excellent uniformity and conformal step coverage, but usually at high temperature.
  • PECVD (plasma-enhanced CVD) — a plasma supplies the energy to drive the reaction, so the film can be deposited at a much lower temperature — essential once temperature-sensitive metals are already on the wafer.

3.4PVD — Physical Vapor Deposition

When you need to lay down metal, there's often no convenient gas-phase reaction. Physical vapor deposition (PVD) instead moves atoms physically from a solid source (the target) to the wafer, with no chemical reaction taking place. Two methods dominate:

  • Sputtering — energetic ions bombard the target, knocking atoms loose that then land on the wafer. This is the workhorse for depositing metal interconnect layers (aluminum, copper, titanium, etc.).
  • Evaporation — the target is heated until its atoms boil off and condense onto the cooler wafer.

Both happen in vacuum so the freed atoms travel in straight lines to the wafer. PVD is prized for clean, pure metal films.

Common mistakeOxidation and deposition are not the same kind of step. Thermal oxidation grows from and consumes the silicon — the Si/oxide interface moves down into the wafer — while deposition (CVD/PVD) adds new material on top of the existing surface. And don't confuse the two deposition families: CVD = a chemical reaction of gases at the surface, whereas PVD = physical transport of atoms from a target with no reaction.
✦ Worked Example

(a) An LPCVD process deposits polysilicon at a rate of 8 nm/min. How thick is the film after 10 minutes? (b) Separately, you grow 100 nm of thermal SiO₂. How much silicon does it consume?

  1. (a) Deposition thickness: thickness = rate × time = 8 nm/min × 10 min = 80 nm.
  2. (b) Silicon consumed: oxidation eats ≈ 0.44 × oxide thickness = 0.44 × 100 nm = 44 nm of silicon. So the Si/oxide interface moves 44 nm down into the wafer while 100 nm of oxide appears.

A chip is built from dozens of stacked thin films. Why does the quality and order of these early layers matter so much — what can go wrong downstream if just one layer (say the gate oxide) is rough, contaminated, or the wrong thickness?

Model response

Every layer is the foundation for the next, so any roughness, contamination, or defect propagates upward through the stack — a single stray particle can cause a short between conductors or an open in a line, and that flaw is locked in once later layers cover it. The gate oxide is especially critical: its thickness and quality directly set the transistor's threshold voltage and leakage current, so an error there shifts or ruins device behavior across the whole chip.

Because of this, bad early layers don't fail loudly at the moment they're made — they show up much later as parametric failures and yield loss during test, long after that layer was deposited. That's why fabs obsess over the cleanliness, uniformity, and exact thickness of the first films: a tiny defect early on becomes a dead die at the end.
