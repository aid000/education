Week 9 · Lecture 9

Testing, Dicing & Packaging

The wafer is finished — now find the good dies, cut them apart, wrap them in a package, and discover why yield, not silicon, sets the price.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why dies are electrically tested on the wafer (sort) before it is cut.
  • Describe how a wafer is diced into individual chips by saw or laser.
  • List what a package does: mount the die, connect it to leads, protect it, and dissipate heat.
  • Define yield and explain why it — not raw material cost — dominates chip economics.
  • Estimate yield with the simple Poisson model Y = e−D·A.

9.1Wafer-Level Test (Sort)

A finished wafer holds hundreds of identical dies — but not all of them work. Before spending money to cut and package each one, the fab runs electrical die sort (also called wafer sort or wafer probe). A probe card lowers fine needles onto the bond pads of one die at a time, applies test patterns, and records pass/fail.

Failing dies are marked or tracked in a digital wafer map so the assembly line knows to skip them. The point is simple: testing on the wafer is cheap, but every step after dicing — handling, bonding, packaging — costs real money. You never want to package a die you already know is dead.

9.2Dicing

Once the wafer is mapped, it is diced — cut into individual chips along the empty scribe lines between dies. The classic tool is a diamond-coated dicing saw spinning at high speed while the wafer is held on adhesive tape; newer fabs use a laser (stealth dicing) to crack the silicon cleanly with far less mechanical stress and a narrower cut.

After dicing, the wafer is no longer a wafer — it is a tray of separate dies, each one a tiny rectangle of silicon ready to be picked up and assembled.

9.3Packaging

A bare die is fragile, hard to handle, and has pads too small to solder to a board. Packaging solves all of this. The die is mounted (die-attach) onto a substrate or lead frame, then electrically connected to the package leads in one of two ways:

Wire bonding draws thin gold or copper wires from the die's pads up to the leads. Flip-chip instead flips the die over onto an array of solder bumps that connect directly to the substrate — shorter paths, more I/O, better for high-performance parts.

Finally the assembly is encapsulated (usually in molded plastic) to protect it from moisture and damage and to help dissipate heat. The package is what gives the chip its electrical I/O — the pins or balls the rest of the system actually talks to.

AnalogyThink of the die as a delicate watch movement and the package as the case: it protects the movement, gives you a crown and buttons to interact with it (the I/O), and conducts away heat. You never wear the bare movement on your wrist — and you never solder a bare die onto a board.

9.4Final Test, Binning & Yield

After packaging, every part is tested again — this is final test, run at full speed and across temperature, because handling and bonding can introduce new failures. Parts that pass are then binned: sorted by how fast or how clean they ran. The same design can ship as a premium high-clock part or a cheaper down-binned one depending on which bin it falls into.

The number that rules everything is yield:

Yield = good dies / total diesthe fraction of dies that actually work

Yield dominates cost because processing a wafer costs roughly the same whether 90% or 30% of its dies work. The wafer cost is essentially fixed; divide it among fewer good dies and the cost per good chip shoots up. A single stray particle landing on a die during processing can kill it.

AnalogyYield is like a bakery. The flour and the oven time cost the same whether a loaf comes out golden or burnt — but you can only sell the good ones. Your burn rate (the defect density) sets the real cost per loaf you actually sell, not the price of flour.
Common mistakeTesting is not a one-time event at the end. It happens at multiple stages: wafer sort before dicing, and final test after packaging. And it is yield, not raw material cost, that dominates chip economics — silicon is cheap, but a single particle can kill a die and a low yield multiplies the cost of every good chip you ship.
✦ Worked Example

A process has a defect density D = 0.5 defects/cm² and the die area is A = 1 cm². Using the simple Poisson yield model, estimate the yield and how many of a wafer's ~700 gross dies (from Lecture 2) come out good.

  1. Poisson model: Y = e−D·A = e−(0.5)(1) = e−0.5.
  2. Evaluate: e−0.5 ≈ 0.61, i.e. about a 61% yield.
  3. Good dies: 0.61 × 700 ≈ 430 good dies out of ~700 — roughly 270 are scrap.
  4. Scaling insight: double the die area to A = 2 cm² and Y = e−1 ≈ 0.37. Bigger dies catch more defects, so yield falls fast — which is why packing more smaller dies on a wafer is so valuable.

🛠️ Hands-On: a Poisson yield calculation on paper

No software needed — just a calculator with an e^x key. Work the Poisson model Y = e^(−D·A) for a couple of cases and watch how die size and defect density trade off.

* Poisson yield: Y = e^(-D*A)
Case 1:  D = 0.5 /cm^2,  A = 1 cm^2  ->  Y = e^(-0.5) = ____
Case 2:  D = 0.5 /cm^2,  A = 2 cm^2  ->  Y = e^(-1.0) = ____
Case 3:  D = 1.0 /cm^2,  A = 1 cm^2  ->  Y = e^(-1.0) = ____

good dies = Y * (gross dies per wafer)
cost per good die = wafer cost / (gross dies * Y)

Notice that doubling the die area (Case 2) and doubling the defect density (Case 3) hurt yield by the same amount — both double the exponent D·A. That is the whole story of fab economics in one equation.

Where does yield loss come from, and why does it dominate the economics of a fab?

Model response

Yield loss comes from random particle/defect contamination, process variation across the litho/etch/implant/deposition steps, edge effects near the rim of the wafer, and insufficient design margin — any one of which can kill a die. Economically, the cost to process a wafer is essentially fixed regardless of how many of its dies end up working, so the cost per good die ≈ (wafer cost) ÷ (dies per wafer × yield). A low yield therefore multiplies the effective cost per shipped chip directly.

This is also why die size matters so much: larger dies expose more area to defects, so by the Poisson model Y = e−D·A they yield worse as area grows. That is why defect control (cleaner processes, lower D) and higher density (more, smaller dies per wafer) are central to fab economics — they are the two levers that push yield, and therefore cost per chip, in the right direction.

✓ What you learned

  • Wafer sort probes each die on the wafer and maps the failures before dicing.
  • Dicing cuts the wafer along the scribe lines into individual dies, by saw or laser.
  • Packaging mounts the die, connects it (wire bond or flip-chip bumps), and encapsulates it for protection, heat, and electrical I/O.
  • Final test & binning re-test the packaged parts and sort them by speed/quality.
  • Yield = good ÷ total dies dominates cost; estimate it with Y = e−D·A, and bigger dies yield worse.
