Week 7 · Lecture 7

Basic Transistor Fabrication

Six weeks of single steps — deposit, pattern, etch, dope — now combine into one device. We build a working MOSFET, then pair two of them into the CMOS that runs all digital logic.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Identify the parts of a MOSFET: substrate, source/drain, gate oxide, gate, and channel.
  • Describe the gate stack: thermal gate oxide → deposited gate → pattern & etch.
  • Explain self-aligned source/drain and why self-alignment was essential to scaling.
  • Order the full step sequence to form a gate and self-aligned source/drain.
  • State what CMOS is, why it needs an n-well and a p-well, and why it sips power.

7.1Putting It Together

For six weeks you learned single fabrication steps in isolation. A MOSFET (metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor) is what you get when you orchestrate them into one device. Strip it down and a MOSFET is just four things stacked on silicon:

doped silicon + thin gate oxide + gate electrode + source/drain regions

There is no new magic here — the transistor is built by repeating the same deposit → pattern → etch → dope steps from earlier weeks, in the right order, on the right layers. Mastering the sequence is mastering transistor fabrication.

7.2The Gate Stack

The heart of the transistor is the gate stack — the gate electrode sitting on a very thin insulator. It is built in three moves:

grow gate oxide → deposit gate → pattern & etch gate

1. Grow a thin gate oxide

Using thermal oxidation (Week 3), the silicon surface is grown into a thin, high-quality layer of SiO₂. This oxide insulates the gate from the channel — the gate controls the channel by an electric field through the oxide, never by touching it.

2. Deposit the gate electrode

A conductive gate is deposited on top of the oxide — historically polysilicon ("poly"), in modern processes a metal. This is the terminal you'll bias to switch the transistor on and off.

3. Pattern and etch the gate

Lithography (Week 4) prints the gate shape; etching (Week 5) removes the unwanted gate material. What remains is a narrow gate stripe — and its width sets the transistor's channel length.

7.3Self-Aligned Source/Drain

Now the clever part. The source and drain must sit right beside the gate, with essentially no gap. Rather than print a separate mask and hope it lines up, we use the patterned gate itself as the implant mask:

implant dopants → gate blocks the channel → source/drain land on either side

Dopant ions (Week 6) rain down on the wafer. Wherever the gate stands, it shields the silicon beneath it — that protected strip becomes the channel. Everywhere else, the silicon is doped, forming the source and drain. Because the same gate that defines the channel also defines where the implant can't reach, the source and drain are self-aligned to the gate — automatically, with zero alignment error.

This was the breakthrough that made scaling practical. As gates shrank to nanometers, a separate alignment step could never have been precise enough to place source and drain that close without overlap or gaps. Letting the gate align them to itself removes the error entirely.

AnalogySelf-alignment is like spray-painting a wall with an object leaning against it, then taking the object away: the object masks its own silhouette, so the unpainted patch lands exactly where the object stood — no measuring, no separate stencil. The gate masks its own channel, so source and drain land precisely beside it.

7.4CMOS

A single MOSFET is a switch. Digital logic is built from CMOS — complementary MOS — which pairs an n-MOS and a p-MOS transistor. The two are complements: in any logic state one is on while the other is off.

Because the two device types need oppositely-doped bodies, CMOS requires both an n-well (to host the p-MOS) and a p-well (to host the n-MOS) built into the wafer. That extra well-doping is the price of putting both flavors on one chip.

CMOS = n-MOS + p-MOS  ·  needs n-well + p-well

The payoff is enormous: in a steady logic state, one transistor is always off, so the path from supply to ground is broken and almost no static (standby) current flows. CMOS draws power mainly only while it switches. That near-zero static power is exactly why CMOS became the basis of all modern digital logic — the topic you'll build on in Course 3, Architecture.

Common mistakeKeep the ordering and structure straight: the gate is patterned BEFORE the source/drain implant (that is precisely what lets it self-align them). The channel sits UNDER the gate and is NOT doped like the source and drain — it's the strip the gate shielded. And CMOS needs BOTH an n-MOS and a p-MOS (hence both an n-well and a p-well) — neither type alone is CMOS.
✦ Worked Example

List, in order, the major process steps to form a MOSFET gate and its self-aligned source/drain.

  1. Grow the gate oxide — thermal oxidation forms a thin SiO₂ layer on the silicon surface.
  2. Deposit the gate — lay down polysilicon (or metal) over the oxide.
  3. Pattern & etch the gate — lithography prints the gate shape; etch removes the rest, leaving the gate stripe.
  4. Implant source/drain — dope the wafer using the patterned gate as the mask, so source/drain self-align to it.
  5. Anneal — heat the wafer to activate the dopants and repair implant damage.

Note: the channel length is set by the gate length — which is why lithography (Week 4) is what ultimately gates transistor density. Smaller printable gates ⟹ shorter channels ⟹ more transistors per wafer.

Q1. In a MOSFET, the thin insulator under the gate is the…
A. channel
B. gate oxide
C. source
D. substrate
Show answer

B. The gate oxide is the thin SiO₂ layer that insulates the gate from the channel. The gate controls the channel by a field through this oxide, never by touching the silicon.

Q2. "Self-aligned" source/drain means…
A. the gate masks the channel, so the source/drain align to it automatically
B. a robot aligns each wafer
C. the mask is reused
D. the wafer self-rotates
Show answer

A. The patterned gate itself blocks the implant over the channel, so the source/drain dope in exactly beside it — aligned automatically, with no separate alignment step.

Q3. The transistor's channel is located…
A. above the gate
B. under the gate, between the source and drain
C. inside the source
D. in the metal layer
Show answer

B. The channel is the strip of substrate directly under the gate, between source and drain — the region the gate shielded from the implant, so it is not doped like S/D.

Q4. CMOS logic requires…
A. only n-MOS
B. only p-MOS
C. both n-MOS and p-MOS devices (n-well and p-well)
D. only diodes
Show answer

C. CMOS is complementary: it pairs an n-MOS and a p-MOS, which need oppositely-doped bodies — hence both an n-well and a p-well. That pairing is what gives near-zero static power.

Q5. In the process flow, the gate is patterned…
A. before the source/drain implant (to enable self-alignment)
B. after the implant
C. after packaging
D. during dicing
Show answer

A. The gate must already be patterned so it can act as the implant mask — that is exactly what makes the source/drain self-align to it.

✓ What you learned

  • A MOSFET = doped silicon + a thin gate oxide + a gate + source/drain regions, built by repeating deposit → pattern → etch → dope.
  • The gate stack: grow a thermal gate oxide, deposit the gate (poly/metal), then pattern & etch it.
  • Self-aligned source/drain: the patterned gate masks the channel, so the implant dopes S/D right beside it — the trick that made scaling practical.
  • The channel sits under the gate and its length = gate length, so lithography gates transistor density.
  • CMOS pairs an n-MOS and a p-MOS (n-well + p-well), draws almost no static power, and is the basis of all digital logic.
