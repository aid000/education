Photoresist & Lithography

How a pattern gets printed onto a wafer. Lithography is the step that decides how small a transistor can be — and why a chip costs what it costs.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Walk through the full photoresist cycle: spin-coat → soft-bake → expose → develop → etch/implant → strip.
  • Distinguish positive from negative resist (which regions wash away after exposure).
  • Explain what a photomask is and how steppers/scanners project and repeat it across the wafer.
  • Use the Rayleigh criterion CD = k₁·λ/NA to find the minimum printable feature.
  • Explain why lithography is the scaling bottleneck for transistor density and cost.

4.1The Photoresist Cycle

Lithography transfers a pattern onto the wafer using a light-sensitive polymer called photoresist. The sequence is the heartbeat of every fabrication run:

Spin-coat — drip liquid resist onto the wafer and spin it fast so it spreads into a thin, uniform film. Soft-bake — gently heat to drive off solvent and firm up the film. Expose — shine UV light through a patterned mask; only the open areas of the mask let light reach the resist, chemically changing it. Develop — a chemical bath removes the resist that should go, leaving a stencil. Etch or implant — the remaining resist protects the film beneath it while the exposed film is etched away (or dopants are implanted). Strip — finally, dissolve off the leftover resist, leaving the patterned film behind.

Positive vs. negative resist

The two resist types respond to light in opposite ways:

positive resist → exposed regions dissolve away  ·  negative resist → exposed regions stayPositive resist copies the clear parts of the mask; negative resist copies the dark parts.

In positive resist, light makes the polymer more soluble, so the regions hit by UV wash out in the developer. In negative resist, light cross-links the polymer and hardens it, so the exposed regions remain and the unexposed ones wash away. Positive resist gives sharper edges and dominates advanced nodes.

AnalogyLithography is a stencil and a spray. The mask is the stencil that decides where light lands; the resist is the layer that the light hardens or softens. After developing, the resist becomes the stencil that protects the wafer during the next step. And just like a spray can, the finest detail you can stencil is limited by how fine your "nozzle" is — here, the nozzle is the wavelength of the light.

4.2The Mask & Exposure Tools

A photomask (or reticle) is a flat quartz plate carrying the pattern for one layer, drawn in an opaque material such as chrome. It is a reusable template — not the finished chip. The exposure tool projects its image onto the resist-coated wafer.

Modern tools are steppers and scanners. They project the mask through a lens that usually reduces it (commonly 4×), so a large, easy-to-make mask prints a tiny field on the wafer. The tool then steps to the next position and repeats, tiling the same pattern across the whole wafer hundreds of times — one exposure per die.

4.3Resolution & Wavelength

How small a feature can lithography print? The limit comes from diffraction and is captured by the Rayleigh criterion. The minimum feature size — the critical dimension (CD) — is:

CD = k₁ · λ / NA  [nm]λ = exposure wavelength · NA = numerical aperture of the lens · k₁ = process factor (~0.25–0.4)

Two knobs shrink the feature: a shorter wavelength λ and a higher numerical aperture NA. The industry has driven λ down for decades — from deep-UV (DUV) at 193 nm to extreme-UV (EUV) at 13.5 nm — and pushed NA above 1 using immersion lenses. The process factor k₁ captures everything else (resist chemistry, illumination tricks); it can be lowered but not below a hard physical floor.

Common mistakeThree slips to avoid. (1) Positive vs. negative resist: in positive resist the light-exposed regions wash away — not the dark ones. (2) The mask is a reusable template projected many times across the wafer; it is not the finished chip. (3) Resolution is set by λ/NA (and k₁), not by the resist alone — a better resist helps k₁ a little, but wavelength and lens dominate.

4.4Why Lithography Limits Everything

Lithography sets the smallest printable feature on the chip. Every transistor, wire, and contact has to fit inside what litho can resolve — so litho directly gates transistor density. Smaller CD ⟹ more transistors per square millimeter ⟹ faster, cheaper-per-function chips. This is the engine behind decades of Moore's-law scaling.

It also gates cost. EUV scanners are the most expensive tools in the fab — well over US$100M each — and when even EUV can't resolve a layer in one shot, fabs resort to multi-patterning: splitting one layer across two or more mask-and-etch passes. That multiplies mask count, process steps, and cycle time, which is exactly why advanced nodes are so costly. Litho is the bottleneck that everything else is designed around.

AnalogyThink of the whole fab as a print shop. You can buy faster presses (deposition, etch) all you like, but the resolution of your printer caps how fine the final poster can be. Lithography is that printer — upgrade it (shorter λ, higher NA) and everything downstream can be made smaller and denser.
✦ Worked Example

Use the Rayleigh criterion CD = k₁·λ/NA to find the minimum feature for two tools, then compare. Take k₁ = 0.3 for both.

  1. (a) DUV (193 nm immersion): λ = 193 nm, NA = 1.35.  CD = 0.3 × 193 / 1.35 = 57.9 / 1.35 ≈ 43 nm.
  2. (b) EUV (13.5 nm): λ = 13.5 nm, NA = 0.33.  CD = 0.3 × 13.5 / 0.33 = 4.05 / 0.33 ≈ 12 nm.
  3. Compare: even with a lower NA, EUV prints features about 3.5× smaller than DUV — the short 13.5 nm wavelength does the heavy lifting. That is the resolution gain that justifies EUV's enormous cost.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In positive photoresist, regions exposed to light…
A. become soluble and wash away during develop
B. harden permanently
C. turn into metal
D. are unaffected
Show answer

A. In positive resist, UV makes the polymer more soluble, so the exposed regions dissolve in the developer. (Negative resist is the opposite — exposed regions cross-link and stay.)

Q2. Minimum feature size scales with…
A. resist thickness only
B. the exposure wavelength (smaller λ → smaller features)
C. wafer diameter
D. temperature
Show answer

B. CD = k₁·λ/NA, so the critical dimension is proportional to the wavelength — shorter λ prints smaller features.

Q3. EUV lithography uses a wavelength of about…
A. 193 nm
B. 365 nm
C. 13.5 nm
D. 1 µm
Show answer

C. Extreme-UV operates at 13.5 nm — over an order of magnitude shorter than 193 nm DUV, which is why it resolves much finer features.

Q4. A photomask is…
A. a reusable template projected onto the wafer (not the final chip)
B. the finished chip
C. a layer of metal
D. a kind of resist
Show answer

A. A mask (reticle) carries one layer's pattern in chrome on quartz. A stepper/scanner projects it — usually reduced — and repeats it across the whole wafer. It is reused, not the product itself.

Q5. Why is lithography called the scaling bottleneck?
A. it is the slowest step
B. it uses the most chemicals
C. it sets the smallest printable feature, gating density and cost
D. it is done last
Show answer

C. Litho fixes the minimum feature size, so it caps transistor density and drives cost (EUV tools, multi-patterning). Everything else is designed around what litho can resolve.

✓ What you learned

  • The photoresist cycle: spin-coat → soft-bake → expose → develop → etch/implant → strip.
  • Positive resist: exposed regions wash away. Negative resist: exposed regions stay.
  • A photomask is a reusable template; steppers/scanners project it (usually reduced) and tile it across the wafer.
  • The Rayleigh criterion CD = k₁·λ/NA — shorter λ (193 nm → 13.5 nm) and higher NA print smaller features.
  • Lithography is the scaling bottleneck: it gates transistor density and cost (EUV, multi-patterning).
