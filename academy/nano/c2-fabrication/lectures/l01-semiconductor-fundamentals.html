Week 1 · Lecture 1

Semiconductor Fundamentals

Why silicon — neither conductor nor insulator — is the material the entire industry is built on.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain band theory: valence band, conduction band, and the band gap.
  • Distinguish conductors, insulators, and semiconductors by their band gaps.
  • Describe intrinsic silicon and how thermal energy creates electron–hole pairs.
  • Explain doping: how donors (n-type) and acceptors (p-type) change carrier concentration.
  • Identify majority and minority carriers in n-type and p-type material.

1.1From Atoms to Energy Bands

In a single isolated atom, electrons occupy discrete energy levels. Bring ~10²³ atoms together into a crystal and those levels split and smear into near-continuous energy bands. Two bands matter for us:

The valence band holds electrons bound to atoms (tied up in bonds). The conduction band is the range of energies where an electron is free to move through the crystal and carry current. Between them sits a forbidden range — the band gap (Eg) — where no electron states exist.

An electron must gain at least Eg to jump from the valence band to the conduction band.

An electron must gain at least Eg of energy to jump from valence to conduction band. How big that gap is decides whether a material conducts.

1.2Conductor, Insulator, Semiconductor

The band gap cleanly separates the three classes of material:

MaterialBand gap EgBehavior
Conductor (metal)≈ 0 (bands overlap)Electrons always free — conducts readily
Semiconductor (Si)≈ 1.1 eVInsulator when cold; conducts when energized or doped
Insulator (SiO₂)≈ 9 eVGap too large — essentially never conducts

Silicon's gap (~1.1 eV) is the sweet spot: small enough that we can controllably turn conduction on and off, large enough that it stays off when we want it to. That controllability is the whole game.

AnalogyThe band gap is a wall between a "parking lot" (valence, electrons stuck) and a "highway" (conduction, electrons moving). Metals have no wall. Insulators have an enormous wall. Semiconductors have a wall low enough that we can build ramps over it on demand — that ramp is doping.

1.3Intrinsic Silicon & Electron–Hole Pairs

Pure ("intrinsic") silicon has each atom sharing four covalent bonds with neighbors. At absolute zero every electron is bound and silicon is a perfect insulator. Add thermal energy (even room temperature) and a few electrons gain enough energy to cross the gap into the conduction band.

When an electron leaves a bond, it leaves behind a vacancy — a hole — that behaves like a positive mobile charge as neighboring electrons hop to fill it. So thermal excitation always creates carriers in pairs:

n = p = ni (intrinsic carrier concentration)In intrinsic Si, free electrons (n) and holes (p) are equal — every freed electron leaves one hole.

At room temperature ni for silicon is only about 10¹⁰ carriers/cm³ — tiny compared to ~10²² atoms/cm³. Intrinsic silicon barely conducts. To make it useful we add carriers deliberately: doping.

1.4Doping: n-type and p-type

Doping means replacing a few silicon atoms with impurity atoms that have a different number of valence electrons. Two cases:

n-type (donors)

Add a group-V atom (phosphorus, arsenic) — it has 5 valence electrons. Four form bonds; the fifth is loosely held and easily donated to the conduction band. The dopant donates a free electron. Electrons are now the majority carrier; holes are the minority.

p-type (acceptors)

Add a group-III atom (boron) — it has only 3 valence electrons, leaving one bond short. That missing bond is a hole that accepts an electron from a neighbor. Holes are now the majority carrier; electrons are the minority.

TypeDopantMajority carrierMinority carrier
n-typeGroup V (P, As)Electrons (−)Holes (+)
p-typeGroup III (B)Holes (+)Electrons (−)

Even tiny doping (1 atom in 10⁶–10⁸) raises carrier concentration by orders of magnitude — enough to set the conductivity precisely. Putting n-type and p-type silicon next to each other forms a pn junction: the basis of every diode and transistor in the rest of this course.

NoteThe doped material as a whole stays electrically neutral — a donor atom contributes a free electron and a fixed positive ion core. Doping changes which carriers are mobile, not the net charge.
✦ Worked Example

A silicon sample is doped with 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ phosphorus atoms. Is it n-type or p-type, and roughly what is the majority-carrier concentration? (ni ≈ 10¹⁰ cm⁻³.)

  1. Identify the dopant: phosphorus is group V → a donor → n-type.
  2. Majority carriers: each donor gives one electron, so n ≈ ND = 10¹⁷ cm⁻³ electrons.
  3. Minority carriers: using p ≈ ni²/n = (10¹⁰)² / 10¹⁷ = 10³ cm⁻³ holes.
  4. Takeaway: doping raised electron concentration from 10¹⁰ to 10¹⁷ — seven orders of magnitude — while holes became vanishingly rare.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The band gap of a material is…
A. the energy released when atoms bond
B. the forbidden energy range between the valence and conduction bands
C. the speed of electrons in the conduction band
D. the number of free electrons per cm³
Show answer

B. The band gap is the range of energies with no allowed electron states, separating the valence and conduction bands.

Q2. Compared to a metal and an insulator, a semiconductor has a band gap that is…
A. larger than both
B. smaller than both
C. intermediate — small enough to control conduction
D. exactly zero
Show answer

C. ~1.1 eV for Si: bigger than a metal's (≈0) but far smaller than an insulator's (≈9 eV), so conduction can be switched on and off.

Q3. In intrinsic silicon at room temperature, the concentration of free electrons compared to holes is…
A. much greater
B. much smaller
C. equal
D. exactly zero
Show answer

C. Thermal excitation creates carriers in pairs, so n = p = ni in intrinsic material.

Q4. Doping silicon with boron (group III) produces…
A. n-type material with electrons as majority carriers
B. p-type material with holes as majority carriers
C. an insulator
D. a metal
Show answer

B. Boron has one fewer valence electron, creating holes that accept electrons → p-type, holes are majority.

Q5. A doped semiconductor is electrically neutral overall because…
A. doping adds equal electrons and holes
B. each mobile carrier is balanced by a fixed ionized dopant core
C. the band gap disappears
D. minority carriers cancel majority carriers
Show answer

B. A donor releases a mobile electron but becomes a fixed positive ion; the net charge stays zero.

✓ What you learned

  • Band theory: a full valence band, an empty conduction band, and a forbidden band gap Eg between them.
  • The gap size sets the class: conductor (overlap), semiconductor (~1.1 eV, switchable), insulator (~9 eV).
  • Thermal energy frees electrons in pairs (electron + hole); intrinsic silicon barely conducts (n = p = ni ≈ 10¹⁰/cm³).
  • Doping sets conductivity: group-V donors → n-type (majority electrons); group-III acceptors → p-type (majority holes); the material stays neutral.
  • Adjacent n- and p-type silicon forms a pn junction — the basis of every diode and transistor.
