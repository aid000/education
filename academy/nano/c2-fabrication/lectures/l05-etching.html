Week 5 · Lecture 5

Etching

Lithography drew the pattern in resist. Etching cuts it into the film — selectively removing whatever the resist left exposed.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State what etching does: selectively remove the material left exposed by the developed resist, transferring the pattern into the film.
  • Describe wet (chemical) etching and explain why it is isotropic — and why that causes undercut.
  • Describe dry / plasma etching and why it can be anisotropic (vertical sidewalls).
  • Explain RIE: directional ion bombardment added to a chemical etch for vertical, directional removal.
  • Define selectivity and explain why you must not etch through the layer you mean to keep.

5.1Etching = Selective Removal

After lithography, the wafer is covered by a patterned resist mask: resist protects some regions, while elsewhere the film underneath is bare. Etching is the step that removes material wherever the resist is open — strip away the exposed film and the resist pattern is transferred into the film beneath it.

resist mask + etch → pattern cut into the film belowEtching subtracts material; it never adds any. Where resist covers, the film survives.

There are two broad families. Wet etching uses liquid chemicals; dry (plasma) etching uses a reactive gas. The crucial difference between them is directionality — whether the etch eats only downward or also sideways. That single property decides how small a feature you can make.

Isotropic vs. anisotropic

An isotropic etch removes material at the same rate in every direction (down and sideways). An anisotropic etch removes material mostly straight down, leaving vertical walls. Fine, dense patterns demand anisotropy.

5.2Wet Etching

Wet etching immerses the wafer in a liquid chemical bath that dissolves the exposed film (for example, hydrofluoric acid attacks silicon dioxide). It is cheap, high-throughput, and can be extremely selective — the right chemistry dissolves one material while barely touching its neighbors.

But the chemistry attacks in every direction equally: the etch is isotropic. As it eats downward through the film, it also eats sideways, creeping in underneath the edge of the resist. That sideways cut is called undercut, and it widens every opening.

Why it fails for fine featuresBecause undercut scales with etch depth, a wet etch deep enough to clear a film also undercuts the resist by roughly that same amount on each side. For micron-scale features the pattern blurs and lines merge — so wet etching is fine for coarse work but unusable for small geometries.

5.3Dry / Plasma Etching

Dry etching replaces the liquid bath with a plasma — a glowing, partially-ionized reactive gas. The plasma generates chemically reactive species (and ions) that react with the exposed film and carry the products away as volatile gases. No wet bath, no soaking.

Crucially, a plasma etch can be made anisotropic: by giving the reactive species a strong vertical preference, material is removed mostly straight down with little sideways attack. The result is near-vertical sidewalls and almost no undercut — exactly what dense, small-geometry patterning needs.

AnalogyWet etching is like soaking a part in acid: the liquid eats in all directions at once and burrows in under the edges, so you get a rounded, undercut pit. Dry / RIE etching is like sandblasting straight down: the attack comes from one direction, so it bores a clean shaft with vertical walls. Anisotropy is simply the difference between a straight-walled trench and a rounded pit.

5.4RIE & Selectivity

Reactive ion etching (RIE) is the workhorse of modern dry etching. It combines two mechanisms: a chemical etch from the reactive plasma plus directional ion bombardment. An electric field accelerates ions straight down onto the wafer, so the etch proceeds preferentially at the bottom of a feature — giving vertical, directional removal that pure chemistry can't.

RIE = reactive plasma chemistry + directional ion bombardment → anisotropic etch

Selectivity

Selectivity is the ratio of how fast the etch removes the target film versus how fast it removes something you want to keep — the resist mask above, or the underlying layer below. High selectivity means the etch chews through the target far faster than the mask or the stop layer, so the mask survives and you don't punch through into the layer beneath.

selectivity = (etch rate of target) ÷ (etch rate of mask or underlayer)Higher is better. Low selectivity erodes the mask or breaks through the layer you meant to keep.
⚠ Common mistakesKeep these straight: wet etching is isotropic — it etches sideways too, undercutting the resist — while dry / RIE etching is anisotropic, giving vertical walls. Etching REMOVES material; it never adds a film (that's deposition). And selectivity matters: if the etch isn't selective enough you will erode the mask or etch right through the layer you intended to keep.
✦ Worked Example — why wet etching fails at small dimensions

You need to open a 1 µm-wide line in a film 1 µm thick using an isotropic wet etch. How wide does the opening end up, and why does this rule out sub-micron features? (Bonus: confirm the etch depth from rate × time.)

  1. Etch depth needed: the film is 1 µm thick, so we must etch 1 µm deep to clear it.
  2. Isotropic ⟹ equal sideways etch: an isotropic etch removes material equally in all directions, so etching 1 µm down also undercuts ≈ 1 µm on each side.
  3. Resulting width: the original 1 µm opening grows by ≈ 1 µm on the left + 1 µm on the right ⟹ widened by ≈ 2 µm, ending up ≈ 3 µm wide.
  4. Conclusion: for sub-micron features the undercut is comparable to or larger than the feature itself — the pattern is destroyed. This is why dry / anisotropic etching is required at scale.
  5. Rate check: if the etch rate is 50 nm/min, then depth = rate × time = 50 nm/min × 4 min = 200 nm — and an isotropic etch would also undercut ≈ 200 nm per side.

🛠️ Hands-On: undercut & etch-depth on paper

No equipment needed — just work the geometry of an isotropic etch with a pencil.

* Given: isotropic wet etch, rate R = 80 nm/min
1. Etch for t = 5 min.  Depth d = R x t = ?
2. Isotropic, so undercut per side ~ d. New width of a
   0.5 um opening = 0.5 um + 2 x undercut = ?
3. Does this opening survive?  Compare undercut to feature.
4. Repeat for an anisotropic (RIE) etch: undercut ~ 0.

Expected: (1) d = 80 × 5 = 400 nm. (2) undercut ≈ 400 nm/side ⟹ width ≈ 0.5 µm + 0.8 µm = 1.3 µm. (3) No — the undercut (0.4 µm) is comparable to the 0.5 µm feature, so it blurs out. (4) RIE: width stays ≈ 0.5 µm, which is why it scales.

Q1. Wet (liquid) etching is generally…
A. isotropic — it etches sideways too, causing undercut
B. perfectly vertical
C. additive (it grows a film)
D. frequency-dependent
Show answer

A. Liquid chemistry attacks in all directions equally, so it eats sideways under the resist (undercut) as well as downward — that's what makes it unusable for fine features.

Q2. To get vertical sidewalls on small features you need…
A. wet etching
B. dry / plasma (anisotropic) etching
C. oxidation
D. CMP (polishing)
Show answer

B. An anisotropic dry / plasma etch removes material mostly straight down, leaving near-vertical walls with little undercut — essential for small geometries.

Q3. RIE achieves its directionality by…
A. heating the wafer
B. spinning the wafer faster
C. adding directional ion bombardment to the chemical etch
D. using a stronger acid
Show answer

C. Reactive ion etching combines the plasma's chemical etch with ions accelerated straight down by an electric field, so removal is preferential at the bottom — vertical and directional.

Q4. "Selectivity" in etching means…
A. etching very quickly
B. removing the target material much faster than the mask / underlying layer
C. etching in a vacuum
D. etching only metals
Show answer

B. Selectivity is the ratio of etch rates — target vs. mask or stop layer. High selectivity means the mask survives and you don't punch through the layer you meant to keep.

Q5. Etching's role in the process is to…
A. add a new film
B. dope the silicon
C. remove material where the resist is open, transferring the pattern
D. polish the surface
Show answer

C. Etching subtracts material wherever the resist is open, cutting the lithographic pattern into the film below. Adding film is deposition; doping and polishing are separate steps.

✓ What you learned

  • Etching is selective removal: strip the exposed film so the resist pattern transfers into the layer below — it subtracts, never adds.
  • Wet etching is cheap and selective but isotropic, so it undercuts the resist and fails for fine features.
  • Dry / plasma etching uses a reactive-gas plasma and can be anisotropic — vertical sidewalls for small geometries.
  • RIE adds directional ion bombardment to the chemical etch for vertical, directional removal.
  • Selectivity = etch the target much faster than the mask or underlayer, so you don't erode the mask or punch through the layer you keep.
