Week 6 · Lecture 6

Doping & Ion Implantation

Put dopants exactly where the mask allows — and learn why industry traded slow diffusion for the precision of an ion beam.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why doping happens after patterning, so n-type/p-type regions land only where the mask opens.
  • Describe thermal diffusion and its limits — poor control of depth and dose, plus lateral spread.
  • Describe ion implantation and how energy sets depth while dose sets the number of ions/cm².
  • Explain why an implant damages the lattice and must be annealed afterward.
  • Connect doping to the pn junctions that make transistors work.

6.1Why Dope After Patterning

Recall from Lecture 1: adding donors (e.g. phosphorus, arsenic) makes silicon n-type, while adding acceptors (e.g. boron) makes it p-type. A working device isn't doped uniformly — it needs n-type and p-type regions sitting side by side in precise locations: the source and drain of a transistor, the wells that hold them, and so on.

That's why doping comes after lithography and etch. The patterned mask — a layer of photoresist or oxide — covers the wafer everywhere except the windows we opened. Dopants reach the silicon only through those windows, so the doped regions land exactly where the layout says they should. Doping is how a flat, lightly-doped wafer becomes a map of n and p territories.

6.2Diffusion

The original doping method is thermal diffusion. The wafer is heated in a furnace (~900–1100 °C) with a gas or solid dopant source at the surface. The high temperature lets dopant atoms hop randomly through the silicon crystal, drifting in from the surface toward higher concentration depths over time.

Diffusion is simple and cheap, but it has real weaknesses. The depth and dose are hard to control independently — both are tangled together through temperature and time. Worse, atoms diffuse in all directions, so dopants spread laterally under the edges of the mask, blurring the region's boundaries. As features shrank, that lateral spread became unacceptable.

6.3Ion Implantation

The modern method is ion implantation. Dopant atoms are ionized, accelerated to high energy, and fired as a beam straight into the wafer. Because the beam is aimed and counted, two things become precisely controllable:

DOSE = ions/cm² (sets concentration)  ·  DEPTH ← ion ENERGY (acceleration voltage)higher energy ⇒ ions penetrate deeper before stopping

The dose is just the total number of ions per cm² delivered — measured directly as beam current over time. The depth is set by the ion energy: faster ions punch deeper before they stop. The mask blocks the beam, so dopants land only in the open windows, with little lateral spread — and it all happens near room temperature. The catch: those energetic ions smash into silicon atoms and damage the crystal lattice on the way in.

6.4Annealing & Junction Formation

An implant on its own is not done. The ions sit in the wrong places — wedged between lattice sites — and the crystal is full of collision damage. Right after the implant, the region barely conducts.

A high-temperature anneal fixes both problems at once. Heating the wafer lets the silicon atoms re-form their crystal lattice (repairing the damage) and lets the dopant atoms settle onto proper lattice sites where they can give up or accept an electron (electrically activating them). Only after the anneal are the dopants active — and the boundary between an n-type region and the adjacent p-type silicon becomes a working pn junction, the building block of every diode and transistor.

AnalogyDiffusion is like a drop of dye spreading slowly through a glass of warm water — it goes everywhere, and you can't really control how far or how concentrated it ends up. Ion implantation is like a precise paintball gun: you set exactly how many pellets (the dose) and exactly how hard you fire them (the energy, which sets depth) — and then you "heal" the dented surface afterward with an anneal.
Common mistakeTwo things to keep straight. (1) Ion implantation gives much better control of dose and depth than diffusion — that precision, plus minimal lateral spread, is exactly why industry switched as features shrank. (2) An implant must be annealed afterward to repair the lattice damage and activate the dopants. An un-annealed implant is not electrically active — the region won't conduct until it's healed.
✦ Worked Example

Energy sets depth; dose sets concentration. Reason through what changes when you adjust each knob — and why the region still won't conduct until you anneal.

  1. Energy → depth: The Gaussian peak sits at a depth set by the acceleration energy. Doubling the implant energy pushes the peak deeper into the wafer (the ions travel farther before stopping), while the total number of ions stays the same.
  2. Dose → how many ions: The dose is the integrated number of ions/cm². Implanting 1×10¹⁵ ions/cm² of boron delivers acceptors and creates a p-type region.
  3. Dose → peak concentration: That fixed dose spreads over the implant's depth profile, so the peak concentration depends on how thinly or thickly the ions are spread. A shallow, tight profile concentrates the same dose into a high peak; a deeper, broader profile spreads it into a lower peak.
  4. Then anneal: Right after implant the boron isn't on lattice sites and the crystal is damaged — the region barely conducts. A high-temperature anneal repairs the lattice and activates the boron; only then does the p-type region behave as designed and form its pn junction with the surrounding n-type silicon.

Model response

Implantation wins on precision and repeatability — you set the dose (ions/cm²) and the depth (via energy) independently and tightly, with little lateral spread, and the resist/oxide mask defines exactly where dopants land at near-room-temperature. Those properties are essential for the small, shallow source/drain regions of scaled transistors, where diffusion's tangled depth/dose and sideways bleed would blur the device.

The costs are complex, expensive equipment and crystal damage that forces a follow-up anneal to repair the lattice and activate the dopants. Diffusion can still make sense where precision matters less and depth/uniformity or cost dominate — e.g., deep wells or simple, cheap, high-throughput doping — because it's just a furnace process with no beamline.
