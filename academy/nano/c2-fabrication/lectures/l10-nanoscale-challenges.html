Week 10 · Lecture 10

Nanoscale Challenges & Advanced Tools

The final lecture: why making transistors smaller stopped being easy, the 3D device structures that saved scaling, and the lithography that prints them. The bridge from fabrication to chip design.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain Moore's Law and the reasons scaling slowed: leakage, variability, and cost.
  • Describe short-channel effects and how they break the planar MOSFET.
  • Contrast planar, FinFET, and gate-all-around (GAA) structures by how the gate wraps the channel.
  • State why EUV (13.5 nm) lithography and multi-patterning are needed at advanced nodes.
  • Connect fabrication to Course 4 (VLSI Design), where you design chips with these devices.

10.1Moore's Law & Why It Slows

Moore's Law is the observation that the number of transistors on a chip roughly doubles every couple of years — driven by shrinking each transistor so more fit in the same area. For decades, smaller meant faster, cheaper, and lower-power all at once. That free lunch ended.

As dimensions dropped below tens of nanometers, three problems grew at once:

rising leakage  ·  growing variability  ·  climbing cost

Leakage: a tiny transistor can't fully turn off — current trickles through even when the gate says "off," wasting power. Variability: when a feature is only a handful of atoms wide, small manufacturing differences (a few stray dopant atoms, edge roughness) change the device's behavior a lot. Cost: the tools and masks needed to print these features got dramatically more expensive, so shrinking no longer automatically lowers cost-per-transistor.

10.2Short-Channel Effects

In a planar MOSFET, the gate sits on top of the channel and controls it from one face. As the gate length shrinks, the drain and source crowd close together and start to influence the channel themselves — the gate loses its grip. This loss of electrostatic control is the family of short-channel effects:

short channel ⟹ gate loses control ⟹ leakage + threshold roll-off

Leakage: current sneaks from source to drain underneath the gate even when the device should be off. Threshold roll-off: the voltage needed to switch the device drops and drifts as the channel shortens, so transistors stop behaving predictably. Push the planar design far enough and the gate simply can't shut the channel off — the structure hits a wall.

10.3New Device Structures: FinFET & GAA

The fix wasn't to shrink the planar transistor further — it was to change its shape so the gate could surround more of the channel and reclaim control. Two structures define modern logic:

The FinFET stands the channel up as a thin silicon fin and wraps the gate over it on three sides. The gate-all-around (GAA) nanosheet goes further: the channel becomes stacked horizontal sheets that the gate completely surrounds on all sides. More wrap means more electrostatic control at the same tiny gate length.

planar (1 side)  →  FinFET (3 sides)  →  GAA (all around)
AnalogyA too-short channel is like trying to dam a very short, wide river with a single gate dropped from above — water just sneaks around the sides. A FinFET or GAA device instead wraps the gate around the channel on multiple sides, like clamping a hose from all directions at once. With the flow pinched from every side, you can truly shut it off — even when the channel is extremely short.

10.4EUV & the Road Ahead

You can only build a feature you can print. For years lithography used 193 nm deep-UV light, stretched far below its wavelength with tricks like multi-patterning (printing one layer with several carefully aligned exposures). To go further, the industry switched to extreme-ultraviolet (EUV) light at a wavelength of:

λEUV = 13.5 nmEUV is far shorter than visible light (~400–700 nm) — it is not visible light at all.

Shorter wavelength prints smaller, sharper features in fewer exposures. Alongside EUV, advanced nodes lean on new materials (high-κ gate dielectrics, metal gates, alternative channel materials) and 3D stacking — building devices and even whole chips vertically rather than only spreading them out. Each of these is a way to keep packing in more function when simple shrinking no longer pays off.

That is where this course ends and the next begins. You now understand how a transistor is physically built — the substrate, films, lithography, etch, doping, interconnect, and the cutting-edge structures above. In Course 4 (VLSI Design) you'll take these devices and design chips with them: turning logic into layouts that real fabs like these can manufacture.

Common mistakeScaling is not just "make it smaller." Short-channel leakage forced the move to 3D structures — the planar MOSFET gave way to the FinFET, and then to GAA. EUV light at 13.5 nm is not visible light (visible is ~400–700 nm). And smaller is no longer automatically cheaper: EUV tools and photomask sets have grown so costly that cost-per-transistor can rise at the newest nodes.
✦ Worked Example

Qualitatively, why does a FinFET let designers keep shrinking the gate length where a planar MOSFET fails? (No heavy math — reason about gate control.)

  1. The problem: in a planar device the gate controls the channel from one face. Shorten the channel and the source/drain start dominating it, so the gate can't shut off the leakage.
  2. The fix — more contact: a FinFET stands the channel up as a thin fin and wraps the gate over three faces. For the same chip footprint, the gate now grips far more of the channel's surface.
  3. The result: with control from three sides, the gate's field reaches all the way through the thin fin and pinches the channel off — even at a very short length. Leakage drops sharply and the device behaves predictably again.
  4. Going further (GAA): a gate-all-around nanosheet surrounds all four sides of each channel, giving the gate the maximum possible control — the best leakage suppression at the smallest dimensions.

Q1. "Short-channel effects" cause…
A. loss of gate control over the channel (leakage)
B. faster switching with no downside
C. higher gate-oxide quality
D. more metal layers
Show answer

A. As the gate shrinks it loses electrostatic control of the channel — current leaks even when the device should be off, and the threshold drifts (roll-off).

Q2. A FinFET improves on the planar MOSFET by…
A. using copper gates
B. removing the gate
C. wrapping the gate around the channel on multiple sides
D. adding more dopant only
Show answer

C. The fin lets the gate wrap the channel on three sides, restoring electrostatic control that the single-sided planar gate loses at short lengths.

Q3. Gate-all-around (GAA)…
A. removes the channel
B. surrounds the channel completely for the best electrostatic control
C. is a packaging method
D. is a type of metal
Show answer

B. GAA nanosheets are surrounded by the gate on all sides — the most gate control of any structure, ideal at the smallest dimensions.

Q4. EUV lithography uses a wavelength of…
A. 1 µm
B. 193 nm
C. 500 nm
D. 13.5 nm
Show answer

D. Extreme-ultraviolet light is 13.5 nm — far shorter than the older 193 nm deep-UV and well outside the visible range.

Q5. Why isn't "just shrink it" enough anymore?
A. wafers got smaller
B. silicon ran out
C. short-channel leakage and rising cost force new structures (FinFET/GAA) and tools (EUV)

Show answer

C. Below tens of nm, leakage and variability break the planar device and tool/mask costs soar — so the industry needed new 3D structures and EUV, not just smaller copies.

✓ What you learned

  • Moore's Law slowed because shrinking brought rising leakage, variability, and cost.
  • Short-channel effects rob the planar gate of electrostatic control — leakage and threshold roll-off.
  • FinFET wraps the gate on 3 sides and GAA surrounds the channel completely, restoring control at tiny sizes.
  • EUV (13.5 nm), multi-patterning, new materials, and 3D stacking carry scaling forward.
