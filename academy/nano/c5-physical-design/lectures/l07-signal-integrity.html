Week 7 · Lecture 7

Signal Integrity

When neighboring wires talk to each other — crosstalk delay and noise, and how to stop it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how coupling capacitance between adjacent wires causes crosstalk.
  • Distinguish crosstalk delay (delta delay on a switching victim) from crosstalk noise (a glitch on a quiet victim).
  • List the main mitigation techniques: spacing, shielding, buffering, and net ordering.
  • State why signoff timing must be SI-aware and fold crosstalk delay into the analysis.
  • Identify the layout patterns — long, closely-spaced parallel runs — that make crosstalk worst.

7.1Crosstalk

Two wires running near each other are not electrically isolated. The metal forms a tiny capacitor across the gap — a coupling capacitance that links the two nets. Nothing in the netlist tells you it exists; it comes purely from the physical layout.

Cc ∝ (parallel run length) / (spacing)Coupling grows with how long the wires run side by side, and shrinks as you push them apart.

When one wire — the aggressor — switches, its changing voltage drives current through that coupling capacitance (i = Cc·dv/dt) and injects charge onto its neighbor, the victim. The faster and bigger the aggressor's transition, the more charge it dumps onto the victim.

AnalogyCrosstalk is two people in adjacent phone booths. A loud neighbor (an aggressor switching) bleeds through the wall into your call (the victim) — either garbling what you hear (a noise glitch) or making you stumble over your words (delay). Shielding is putting a soundproof, grounded wall between the booths.

7.2Crosstalk Delay & Noise

What the injected charge does depends on what the victim is doing at that instant.

Crosstalk delay (victim is also switching)

If the victim is mid-transition, the aggressor's pushiness either helps or fights it — a delta delay:

aggressor switches same direction → victim speeds up
aggressor switches opposite direction → victim slows down

An aggressor moving the same way as the victim effectively adds drive (less delay); one moving the opposite way fights the victim (more delay). The slow-down case is what threatens setup timing.

Crosstalk noise (victim is quiet)

If the victim is supposed to hold a steady value, the injected charge can't change a real logic transition — instead it produces a transient bump: a glitch. If that glitch is big enough and lands at the wrong moment, downstream logic can sample it and falsely flip, corrupting the data.

7.3Mitigation

Every fix attacks the same equation — reduce Cc or weaken its effect:

Increase spacing

Push the victim away from aggressors. Coupling falls with distance, so even one extra track of spacing helps a sensitive net.

Shield critical nets

Route grounded (or power) wires on both sides of the critical net. The shields are quiet, so the victim now couples to steady ground instead of to a switching aggressor — the injected charge has nowhere to come from.

Insert buffers

Break a long coupled run into shorter segments with buffers. Each segment has less parallel length, and the buffer restores a strong, fast edge that is harder to disturb.

Order and space nets

During routing, place the most sensitive nets away from the busiest aggressors, and order/space the tracks so no single victim sees a long, tightly-coupled neighbor.

Common mistakeCrosstalk is not only noise. A switching aggressor can both delay a signal and create a glitch. It is worst for long, closely-spaced parallel nets. The fixes are spacing, shielding, and buffering. And crosstalk delay must be folded INTO signoff timing — never ignored or assumed away.

7.4SI-Aware Timing

Static timing analysis from the previous lecture assumed each net's delay stood on its own. Real nets don't: a victim's delay shifts depending on whether its aggressors switch with it or against it. SI-aware STA recomputes path delays with the worst-case crosstalk contribution included.

net delay = base delay ± crosstalk delta delaySignoff must use the slowed-down (or sped-up) value, not the standalone base delay.

The danger pattern is exactly the one that builds the most coupling: long, closely-spaced parallel runs. Ignoring their crosstalk delta can let a chip pass timing on paper and then fail on silicon.

✦ Worked Example

A clock-like critical net runs 400 µm parallel to two busy data buses, one on each side, at minimum spacing. SI analysis flags a large crosstalk delta delay and a glitch risk. How do you fix it, and what does it cost?

  1. Diagnose: the long parallel run and tight spacing give a large Cc to two active aggressors — the recipe for both delay and noise.
  2. Pick the fix: spacing alone isn't enough for a critical net, so route grounded shield wires on both sides.
  3. Effect: the switching neighbors are replaced by quiet ground. Cc now ties the victim to a steady node, so the crosstalk delta delay and the glitch essentially disappear.
  4. Cost: two extra routing tracks (the two shields) consume area next to the net — you trade routing resources for a clean signal.
  5. Note: Cc grows with run length and shrinks with spacing, so shorter runs and wider gaps reduce how much shielding you need in the first place.

✓ What you learned

  • Adjacent wires share a coupling capacitance; a switching aggressor injects charge onto a victim.
  • Crosstalk causes both a delta delay (speed-up or slow-down on a switching victim) and a noise glitch (on a quiet victim).
  • It is worst for long, closely-spaced parallel nets — Cc grows with run length, shrinks with spacing.
  • Mitigations: spacing, grounded shielding, buffering, and net ordering.
  • Signoff must be SI-aware — crosstalk delay is folded INTO STA, never ignored.
