Placement

Deciding where every standard cell sits — the step that quietly determines routability and timing.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what placement does after the floorplan: assigning exact locations to standard cells in the rows.
  • Distinguish global placement (approximate spreading) from legalization (snap to legal rows/sites).
  • Describe how congestion-driven placement keeps a design routable.
  • Describe how timing-driven placement shortens critical-path nets.
  • Articulate why a wirelength-optimal placement can still miss timing or congestion goals.

3.1What Placement Does

After the floorplan fixes the core area, the rows, and the locations of the big blocks and pins, placement takes over and assigns an exact location to every standard cell. Each cell must land in one of the predefined rows, sitting on the placement-site grid. The job isn't arbitrary: placement chooses positions to minimize total wirelength and congestion while meeting timing.

Those three goals are coupled. Where you put a cell sets how long its nets become, how many nets cross any given region (congestion), and how much delay the critical paths accumulate. Placement is where the abstract netlist first acquires real geometry — and that geometry decides almost everything that follows.

3.2Global Placement, Then Legalization

Placement runs in two stages. Global placement treats cell positions as nearly continuous and spreads the cells across the core to minimize wirelength approximately — at this stage cells may overlap and may not sit exactly on legal sites. It's solving the big-picture "who goes roughly where" problem.

Legalization then cleans up: it snaps every cell into a legal row and onto a valid placement site, removing all overlaps while perturbing the global solution as little as possible. After legalization, the placement is physically valid — every cell has a real, non-overlapping home, ready for the steps that follow.

AnalogyPlacement is seating guests at a wedding. Put people who talk a lot — heavily connected cells — near each other so they don't have to shout across the room (that shouting is wirelength). But don't cram one table so full that nobody can move; an overstuffed table is a congested region the waiters (the router) can't get through.

3.3Congestion-Driven Placement

A placement that minimizes wirelength alone can pull too many cells into the same neighborhood, creating regions where more nets want to pass through than there are routing tracks. Congestion-driven placement estimates routing demand across the core and deliberately spreads cells out of the congested hot spots, trading a little wirelength for routability.

The point is that a placement is only useful if the router can actually finish. By predicting demand before routing and relieving the worst regions, the tool keeps the design routable instead of discovering an unroutable mess later.

3.4Timing-Driven Placement

Not every net matters equally. Nets on timing-critical paths — the ones that limit the achievable clock frequency — deserve special treatment. Timing-driven placement keeps the cells along those critical paths close together, shortening their nets so the path's delay drops.

The tool weights critical nets more heavily during placement, so the optimizer pays a larger "penalty" for stretching them. The result: shorter, faster critical paths, often at the cost of slightly longer non-critical nets that had slack to spare.

Common mistakePlacement is not just packing cells tightly. Raw density is the least of your worries — congestion and timing matter far more. Three things to remember: (1) legalization must remove every overlap and align cells to rows/sites; (2) a placement that's wirelength-optimal can still miss timing if critical-path cells end up far apart; and (3) cramming cells together to shrink area can create congestion the router can't escape.
✦ Worked Example

Why adjacency matters. Two connected cells are placed 500 µm apart. What happens to that net's delay if timing-driven placement moves them to 50 µm apart?

  1. Original net length: the two cells are 500 µm apart, so the connecting wire is roughly 500 µm long.
  2. After moving: bring them to 50 µm apart and the wire shrinks to about 50 µm — roughly 10× shorter.
  3. Delay scales with length: wire RC delay grows with wire length (both R and C rise with length), so a ~10× shorter wire is a dramatically faster net.
  4. Takeaway: this is exactly what timing-driven placement does for critical paths — pull the connected cells together so the critical net is short and fast, buying back timing slack.

🛠️ Hands-On: global + detailed placement in OpenROAD

OpenROAD runs global placement, then legalization (detailed placement), and can report a congestion estimate. Run placement on a block and inspect both the placed cells and the congestion map.

# OpenROAD placement flow — Lecture 3
read_lef tech.lef
read_lef cells.lef
read_def floorplan.def        # floorplan + rows already defined
global_placement -density 0.6 # spread cells, minimize wirelength
detailed_placement            # legalize: snap to rows/sites, no overlaps
check_placement               # verify legality
write_def placed.def

Open the result in the GUI and turn on the Placement Density / Congestion heat-map overlay. Expected: after global_placement cells are spread across the core; after detailed_placement they sit cleanly in rows, and the congestion map highlights any remaining hot regions to watch before routing.

Model response

Placement fixes where every cell sits, and that single choice sets the length of every net — and therefore its RC delay — and the local routing demand (congestion) in each region. Routing can only connect what placement laid out: if connected cells are scattered, nets are long and slow; if too many cells cluster, there aren't enough tracks to route them legally. So a placement that is congested or timing-poor leaves the router no fast or even no legal solution, and you can't rescue it downstream without re-placing.

Placement is the lever that determines the achievable timing and routability; routing is largely the consequence of it. That's why congestion- and timing-driven placement matter so much.
