Week 5 · Lecture 5

Routing Fundamentals

Connecting millions of nets across the metal stack — DRC-clean, and without damaging the gates.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the three-stage routing flow: global routing → track assignment → detailed routing.
  • Explain layer & track planning — preferred directions and track pitch on each metal layer.
  • Reserve upper, thicker metal layers for power and long global nets.
  • Explain antenna effects and fix them with antenna diodes or layer jumps.
  • Apply non-default rules (wider, shielded wires) to critical and clock nets.

5.1The Routing Problem at Scale

After floorplanning and placement, every cell sits at a fixed location — but the cells are still electrically disconnected. Routing draws the physical wires that connect millions of nets across a stack of many metal layers. Trying to solve that all at once is intractable, so a router breaks the job into stages, each refining the one before:

global routing → track assignment → detailed routing

Global routing plans the corridors: it divides the chip into coarse tiles and decides, roughly, which regions and layers each net should pass through — without drawing exact geometry. Track assignment then commits each net to specific routing tracks within those corridors. Finally, detailed routing draws the exact, legal wires and vias — the output must be fully connected and DRC-clean (passing every Design Rule Check: spacing, width, via rules).

This staging keeps the problem tractable: global routing balances congestion across the whole die, while detailed routing only ever has to worry about a small, pre-zoned region at a time.

5.2Layer & Track Planning

The metal stack is not a free-for-all. Each layer has a preferred direction, and the directions alternate from layer to layer — one layer runs horizontal, the next vertical, the next horizontal, and so on. A wire that needs to turn simply jumps to the neighboring layer through a via. This Manhattan, alternating-direction scheme keeps parallel wires from colliding and makes the routing grid predictable.

M1 horizontal · M2 vertical · M3 horizontal · M4 vertical …Preferred directions alternate; a via connects a horizontal segment to a vertical one.

Within a layer, wires snap to evenly spaced tracks; the spacing is the track pitch, set by the minimum wire width plus minimum spacing for that layer. Lower layers are thin and dense (lots of tightly pitched tracks for short local connections). The upper, thicker layers have a much wider pitch and lower resistance, so they are reserved for power distribution and long global nets that must cross large parts of the die with little delay.

5.3Antenna Effects & Fixes

Routing geometry can create a manufacturing hazard. During fabrication, metal layers are built up and etched one at a time using charged plasma. A long stretch of exposed metal acts like an antenna: it collects charge from the plasma. If that metal is connected to a transistor's gate, the accumulated charge has nowhere to go and discharges through the ultra-thin gate oxide — which can permanently damage or destroy the gate.

This is an antenna effect, and it is purely a fabrication-time problem (the finished, powered chip is fine). There are two standard fixes:

add an antenna diode  ·  or jump the net up to a higher layerThe diode bleeds collected charge to the substrate; the layer jump breaks the long single-layer run.

An antenna diode is a small reverse-biased diode tied to the net; during fabrication it provides a safe leakage path that bleeds the collected charge to the substrate before it can damage the gate. Alternatively, the router can jump the net up to a higher metal layer partway along, so no single layer carries a dangerously long run of metal tied to the gate.

AnalogyRouting at scale is a multi-level highway network. Global routing zones the corridors (which regions and decks the traffic uses), track assignment picks the actual lanes, and detailed routing paves them legally so nothing crosses a double line. An antenna diode is like a grounding rod on a tall structure — it bleeds off dangerous charge that builds up during construction before it can do harm.

5.4Special Routing

Not every net is treated identically. The most timing- and noise-sensitive nets — the clock and other critical nets — are routed with non-default rules (NDRs) instead of the standard width and spacing:

  • Wider wires lower the wire's resistance, reducing delay and making the net less sensitive to manufacturing variation.
  • Shielding places grounded (or supply-tied) neighbor wires on each side of the net. The quiet shields absorb capacitive coupling so a switching aggressor next door can't inject noise — protecting the net from crosstalk.

These rules cost extra routing area, so they are applied selectively — exactly to the nets where delay and crosstalk matter most.

Common mistakeRouting is multi-stage — global routing → track assignment → detailed routing — and the detailed result must finish DRC-clean; it is not a single one-shot step or an approximate plan. Antenna violations are a fabrication/manufacturing issue (charge collected during etch damaging the gate oxide), not a logic bug — fix them with antenna diodes or layer jumps. And don't assume every net uses the default rules: critical and clock nets often need non-default (wider/shielded) routing.
✦ Worked Example

A long net routed entirely on M1 connects to a small gate. The antenna ratio is roughly the connected metal area divided by the gate area. The process limit is, say, 400. The net's M1 segment has an area of 6.0 µm² and the gate it drives has an area of 0.01 µm². Is there a violation, and how do we fix it?

  1. Antenna ratio: ratio ≈ (connected metal area) / (gate area) = 6.0 / 0.01 = 600.
  2. Check the limit: 600 > 400 → the ratio exceeds the process limit, so this net would risk damaging the gate during fabrication — an antenna violation.
  3. Fix A — antenna diode: add a diode to the net so collected charge bleeds to the substrate. The diode caps the effective charge regardless of metal area, clearing the violation.
  4. Fix B — layer jump: route part of the run on a higher layer. If only ~2.0 µm² of metal stays on M1 connected to the gate before the jump, that layer's ratio is 2.0 / 0.01 = 200 < 400 — clean. No single layer's metal area is too large for the gate it connects to.

🛠️ Hands-On: route the design and check the reports

This is the Week-5 project checkpoint — your floorplan and placement should already be done. Now run detailed routing in OpenROAD, then inspect the DRC and antenna reports before viewing the routed layout in KLayout.

# OpenROAD: global + detailed routing, then sign-off checks
global_route
detailed_route
# write out the geometry and run the checks
check_antennas -report_file ant.rpt   ; flag any antenna violations
report_design_area
write_def routed.def

Open ant.rpt — expect zero antenna violations (OpenROAD inserts diodes / jumps as needed). Confirm detailed routing reports 0 DRC violations and is fully connected. Then load the design in KLayout (klayout routed.def) and zoom in: you should see alternating-direction metal layers joined by vias, with no opens or shorts.

Q1. Detailed routing must produce…
A. a DRC-clean, fully-connected layout
B. an approximate plan only
C. the floorplan

A. Detailed routing draws the exact, legal wires and vias — its output must be fully connected and pass every Design Rule Check (DRC-clean). The coarse plan comes earlier, in global routing.

Q2. Each metal layer has…
A. a fixed voltage
B. a preferred routing direction (alternating horizontal/vertical)
C. no direction
D. only diagonal wires
Show answer

B. Layers alternate preferred directions — one horizontal, the next vertical — and a via connects a segment on one layer to the perpendicular segment on the next. This keeps the routing grid predictable.

Q3. Antenna violations are caused by…
A. crosstalk between wires
B. IR drop
C. charge accumulating on long metal during fabrication, damaging gates
D. clock skew
Show answer

C. During fabrication, a long exposed metal run collects plasma charge; if it's tied to a gate, that charge discharges through the thin gate oxide and can damage it. It's a manufacturing issue, not a logic or timing bug.

Q4. Antenna violations are fixed by…
A. widening the clock
B. lowering the voltage
C. adding decaps
D. adding antenna diodes or jumping the net to a higher layer
Show answer

D. An antenna diode bleeds the collected charge to the substrate, or the router jumps part of the net to a higher layer so no single layer's metal run is too large for the gate it drives.

Q5. Critical and clock nets often use…
A. the thinnest possible wires
B. no vias
C. non-default rules (wider, shielded) routing
D. random tracks
Show answer

C. Timing- and noise-sensitive nets use non-default rules: wider wires to lower resistance and delay, and grounded shield neighbors to block crosstalk.

✓ What you learned

  • Routing is multi-stage: global routing plans corridors, track assignment picks tracks, and detailed routing draws exact, DRC-clean wires.
  • Each metal layer has a preferred direction that alternates horizontal/vertical, with a fixed track pitch; upper thicker layers carry power and long global nets.
  • Antenna effects are a fabrication hazard — charge on long metal damages the gate oxide.
  • Fix antennas with antenna diodes (bleed charge) or by jumping to a higher layer.
  • Critical and clock nets use non-default rules — wider wires and shielding to control delay and crosstalk.
