Week 2 · Lecture 2

Power & Clock Planning

Delivering clean power to every cell and planning where the clock and voltage domains go.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the power delivery network (PDN): rings, straps, and rails carrying VDD/VSS from the pads to every cell.
  • Explain the IR-drop budget and why the PDN must supply peak current with low voltage loss.
  • Reason about the trade-off between PDN density and the signal-routing tracks it consumes.
  • Budget clock distribution early and identify clock-domain crossings that need synchronizers.
  • Use voltage islands, level shifters, and power switches to save energy across a multi-voltage design.

2.1The Power Delivery Network (PDN)

Once the floorplan fixes where blocks sit, the chip needs a way to get power to all of them. The power delivery network (PDN) is the metal mesh that carries VDD (supply) and VSS (ground) from the package pads down to every standard cell. It is built in layers:

Wide rings encircle the core on the upper, thick metal layers. Straps cross the core at intervals, tapping off the rings. Finally, thin rails run along each standard-cell row, delivering VDD/VSS directly to the cell pins.

The whole network has one hard job: deliver the chip's peak switching current while losing as little voltage as possible along the way. That voltage loss is called IR drop — current (I) times the grid's resistance (R):

Vdrop = I · RLower grid resistance ⟹ less voltage lost ⟹ cells see closer to the full supply.
AnalogyThe power delivery network is a building's electrical system. The main feeders are the rings around the perimeter; the risers climbing through the floors are the straps; and the branch circuits in each room are the rails feeding the cells. Everything is sized so that when every appliance switches on at once, the lights don't dim — that dimming is IR drop.

2.2Power Planning Trade-offs

It is tempting to just make the PDN as big and dense as possible — wider straps, more of them, closer together. That would crush IR drop. But there is a catch: power metal and signal metal compete for the same routing layers.

Every strap you add occupies tracks that signal nets would otherwise use. A denser PDN means fewer signal-routing tracks, which can make the design harder (or impossible) to route. So power planning is a balance:

denser PDN ⟹ lower IR drop  ·  sparser PDN ⟹ more signal tracksThe goal is the lightest grid that still meets the IR-drop budget.

The art is to spend exactly enough metal to keep IR drop within spec — typically a few percent of the supply — and not a track more, leaving the rest for signals.

2.3Clock Planning & Multiple Domains

The clock is the single highest-activity net on the chip — it toggles everywhere, every cycle. So its distribution must be budgeted early, during planning, reserving routing and buffering resources before placement and routing fill them up.

Real designs rarely run on one clock. They have multiple clock domains — separate regions each timed by their own clock (different frequencies or unrelated phases). A CPU core, a memory interface, and an I/O block might each have their own.

Crossing between domains

When a signal travels from one clock domain into another, it is a clock-domain crossing (CDC). The receiving flip-flop may sample the signal right as it changes, leaving its output stuck halfway between 0 and 1 — a hazardous state called metastability. To avoid it, every crossing needs a synchronizer (typically two back-to-back flip-flops) to let the signal settle before the rest of the logic uses it.

signal crosses clock domains ⟹ insert a synchronizer ⟹ avoid metastability

2.4Multi-Voltage & Power Gating

Not every block needs to run at the same voltage. Lowering a block's supply saves energy (dynamic power scales with V²), so designers create voltage islands — regions powered at different VDD levels, each tuned to what its logic actually needs.

That flexibility comes with rules. A signal leaving one island at one voltage and entering another at a different voltage must pass through a level shifter, a cell that translates the logic levels so the receiving gates read it correctly.

Energy can be saved more aggressively still: a block that is idle can be switched off entirely with power switches (power gating). The switches disconnect the block's VDD when it isn't needed, eliminating its leakage, and reconnect it when work arrives.

Common mistakeKeep three planning rules straight. (1) PDN density trades against signal routing — more power metal means fewer signal tracks, not a free lunch. (2) A clock-domain crossing must be synchronized — skip the synchronizer and you risk metastability, not just a timing miss. (3) A signal moving between two voltage islands needs a level shifter — a plain wire (or a via) will not translate the levels.
✦ Worked Example

A block draws 1 A at peak. The power grid from the package pad to that block has a total resistance of 0.05 Ω. On a 1.0 V supply, find the static IR drop and decide whether it meets the usual ~5% budget.

  1. IR drop: V = I·R = 1 A × 0.05 Ω = 0.05 V = 50 mV.
  2. As a fraction of supply: 50 mV / 1.0 V = 5%.
  3. Verdict: right at the usual budget limit — acceptable, but with no margin.
  4. What pushes it over: more current (a hotter block) or a thinner grid (higher R) raises the drop past spec, so the cells see a lower voltage and switch more slowly.

🛠️ Hands-On: generate a PDN and read the IR-drop report

In OpenROAD, the PDN is built from a script and then analyzed for IR drop. Define the grid, generate it, and run the static IR analysis on a block.

# OpenROAD — define and build the power grid, then check IR drop
pdngen                         # generate the PDN from the pdn.cfg (rings/straps/rails)
set_pdnsim_net_voltage -net VDD -voltage 1.0
analyze_power_grid -net VDD    # static IR-drop analysis on VDD
report_power_grid -net VDD     # worst-case IR drop + node voltages

Read the report's worst-case IR drop and compare it to your budget (e.g. 5% of 1.0 V = 50 mV). If it's over, add straps or widen the grid and re-run — then watch the signal-track count fall as you do.

B. More power metal lowers grid resistance (lower IR drop) but occupies routing layers, leaving fewer tracks for signals — a balance.

Q3. A clock-domain crossing requires…
A. a level shifter
B. synchronization to avoid metastability
C. a wider wire
D. nothing special

B. A signal sampled across unrelated clocks can go metastable; a synchronizer (two back-to-back flip-flops) lets it settle first.

Q4. A signal crossing between two voltage islands needs…
A. a decap
B. a synchronizer
C. a level shifter
D. a via only
Show answer

C. Different islands run at different VDD, so the signal's logic levels must be translated by a level shifter — a plain wire or via won't do it.

Q5. IR drop is…
A. crosstalk noise
B. clock skew
C. the voltage lost across the power-grid resistance under load
D. leakage power
Show answer

C. IR drop = I·R, the supply voltage lost across the PDN's resistance when current flows; too much of it starves and slows the cells.

✓ What you learned

  • The PDN — rings, straps, and rails — carries VDD/VSS from the pads to every cell with low IR drop (V = I·R).
  • Power planning is a trade-off: a denser PDN lowers IR drop but leaves fewer signal-routing tracks.
  • Budget the clock early; designs have multiple clock domains, and crossings need a synchronizer to avoid metastability.
  • Voltage islands save energy; signals between them need a level shifter, and idle blocks can be power-gated with power switches.
