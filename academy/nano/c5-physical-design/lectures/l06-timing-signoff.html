Week 6 · Lecture 6

Timing Signoff (STA)

Certifying the chip meets timing across every corner and mode — not just on a good day.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how signoff STA differs from earlier design-stage STA (extracted parasitics, signoff libraries).
  • Define PVT corners and know which corner is critical for setup vs hold.
  • Describe why signoff is multi-corner and multi-mode (MCMM).
  • Declare timing exceptions — false paths and multicycle paths — and explain why they matter.
  • Pick the right corner to sign off a given setup or hold check.

6.1Signoff STA vs Design STA

Throughout placement and routing you ran design-stage STA on estimated wire delays to steer optimization. Signoff STA is different: it runs on the extracted post-route parasitics — the real wire R and C pulled from the finished layout — together with signoff-quality libraries (fully characterized, accurate cell models). It is the final, authoritative timing verdict on the chip, building directly on the STA fundamentals from C4.

Because the parasitics are now real instead of estimated, signoff is the moment of truth: if a path passes here, across all the conditions below, you can tape out. If it fails, no amount of wishful thinking helps — you fix it or you ship a broken part.

6.2Corners (PVT)

Silicon is not one fixed object. A corner is a combination of process (slow / typical / fast — how the fab lot came out), voltage (the supply can sag or rise), and temperature (cold to hot). Gate and wire delays change at every one of these.

The key fact: setup and hold are worst at opposite corners. Setup (the data must arrive before the clock edge) is usually worst at the slow corner, where delays are largest — the path is most likely too slow. Hold (the data must stay stable after the edge) is usually worst at the fast corner, where delays are smallest — data races through too early.

6.3Modes

A chip doesn't run just one way. It has several operating modes — functional (normal use), test/scan (shifting patterns through scan chains during manufacturing test), and low-power (reduced voltage/frequency states). Each mode applies its own clock definitions and timing constraints.

So signoff is multi-mode and multi-corner — MCMM. You must certify timing for every relevant mode at every relevant corner. A path that's fine in functional mode at the slow corner may still violate in scan mode, or at a different corner; MCMM analysis catches all of those combinations at once.

6.4Timing Exceptions

STA assumes every path is a single-cycle, always-active path unless you tell it otherwise. Two timing exceptions correct that:

A false path is a path that is logically never active — for example, a path through a mux input that can never be selected together with the launching condition. You declare it so the tool excludes it from timing. A multicycle path is a real path that is allowed more than one clock cycle to settle (e.g. a slow datapath the design samples every other cycle). You tell the tool how many cycles it gets.

If you forget to declare them, the tool reports non-real violations on false paths or over-tightens multicycle paths — and worse, a wrongly declared false path can hide a real violation. Exceptions must be set correctly, not generously.

AnalogySignoff STA is stress-testing a bridge under every condition — the hottest day, the heaviest load, the weakest concrete batch (the slow corner) — not just a pleasant afternoon. You certify it holds in all corners and modes, not the typical case, because the chip will face all of them in the field.
Common mistakeSignoff checks many corners AND modes (MCMM), not one nominal point. Remember the extremes are opposite: setup is critical at the SLOW corner, hold at the FAST corner. And false/multicycle path exceptions must be set correctly — a wrong or missing exception makes the tool flag non-real violations, or worse, hides a real one.
✦ Worked Example

You have one register-to-register path. Which corner do you use to sign off its setup check, and which for its hold check — and why?

  1. Setup check asks: does the data arrive before the capturing clock edge? It is most likely to fail when delays are largest.
  2. Delays are largest at the slow corner (slow process, low voltage, hot). So sign off setup at the slow corner — if it passes there, it passes everywhere faster.
  3. Hold check asks: does the data stay stable after the edge? It is most likely to fail when delays are smallest and data races through too early.
  4. Delays are smallest at the fast corner. So sign off hold at the fast corner.
  5. You must pass both, each at its own worst corner. Passing setup at slow does not excuse a hold failure at fast — and unlike setup, hold can't be fixed by slowing the clock.

🛠️ Hands-On: multi-corner STA with OpenSTA

OpenLane ships OpenSTA. After a routed design, run STA at more than one corner and compare the worst setup slack (WNS) against the worst hold slack — they'll be critical at different corners.

# OpenSTA — read design at a slow (max) corner for setup
read_liberty sky130_fd_sc_hd__ss_n40C_1v60.lib   ; slow corner
read_verilog design.v
link_design top
read_spef design.spef                            ; extracted parasitics
read_sdc design.sdc
report_checks -path_delay max -group_count 5     ; setup paths

# Now reload at a fast (min) corner and check hold
read_liberty sky130_fd_sc_hd__ff_100C_1v95.lib   ; fast corner
report_checks -path_delay min -group_count 5     ; hold paths

Compare the two: the -path_delay max (setup) WNS should be tightest at the slow corner, while -path_delay min (hold) slack is tightest at the fast corner. Sign off only when both are non-negative across every corner you run.

Model response

Real chips don't all behave like the nominal model: manufacturing spreads the process (some die are slow, some fast), the supply voltage sags under load, temperature swings, and the same chip runs in several modes — and gate/wire delays shift with all of these. Crucially, the worst cases are at opposite extremes: setup violations (paths too slow) appear at the slow/hot/low-voltage corner, while hold violations (paths too fast) appear at the fast corner.

If you sign off only at nominal, you ship parts that pass on the bench but fail in the field — slow-process die that miss setup at temperature, or hold violations that no clock-speed change can fix because hold is independent of period. So every relevant corner × mode must be certified. Skipping them trades a little tool time for field failures and silicon re-spins.
