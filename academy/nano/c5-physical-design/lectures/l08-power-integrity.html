Week 8 · Lecture 8

Power Integrity

Keeping the supply clean and the metal alive — IR drop, electromigration, and decoupling.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish static IR drop (average) from dynamic IR drop (transient sag) across the power-delivery network.
  • Explain electromigration as a long-term reliability limit set by current density J.
  • Describe how decoupling capacitors supply transient current and smooth dynamic droop.
  • Relate simultaneous switching and power-gating inrush to current spikes on the PDN.
  • Apply the standard EM fix: widen a wire to lower its current density.

8.1IR Drop (Static & Dynamic)

The power-delivery network (PDN) — the package bumps, power rings, and metal straps that carry VDD and VSS to every cell — has resistance. Push current through resistance and you lose voltage: IR drop. By the time the supply reaches a cell deep in the die, it is a little below the ideal rail.

Vdrop = I · RPDN   [V]Current drawn by the logic, times the resistance of the metal path back to the source.

IR drop has two flavors. Static IR drop is the steady, average drop assuming an average current draw. Dynamic IR drop is the transient sag that appears the instant many cells switch together and yank a current spike out of the grid faster than the package can supply it.

static IR drop → average draw  ·  dynamic IR drop → transient switching sag

Why it matters: a cell's speed depends on its supply voltage. Too much droop means the cell sees a lower VDD, so it switches slower — eroding timing margin — and in the worst case the voltage sags low enough to corrupt logic outright.

AnalogyThe PDN is a city's water system. Static IR drop is the baseline pressure loss along the pipes from the reservoir to your block. Dynamic IR drop is the sudden pressure crash when everyone on the street turns on their shower at the same instant — the mains simply can't refill the pipes fast enough.

8.2Electromigration (EM)

Electromigration is a wear-out mechanism. When a wire carries a high current density for a long time, the moving electrons literally transfer momentum to the metal atoms and nudge them along — a slow "electron wind." Over months and years, atoms pile up in some places (hillocks) and vacate others, opening up voids that eventually become opens or shorts.

EM risk ∝ current density J = I / AI is the current through the wire; A is its cross-sectional area. High J ⟹ faster wear-out.

The key idea: EM is a long-term reliability limit, not an instantaneous failure. A wire that violates its EM budget does not blow the moment power is applied — it degrades gradually and may fail after the part has shipped. So design rules cap the maximum current density a given metal layer may carry.

8.3Decoupling Capacitors

The package can't deliver a current spike instantly — the inductance and resistance of the path get in the way. The fix is to keep a local charge reservoir right next to the hungry logic: a decoupling capacitor (decap). When a block switches and demands a burst of current, the nearby decap dumps its stored charge immediately, before the slower PDN can respond.

decap supplies transient I ⟹ less dynamic IR dropThe local capacitor sources the switching spike, so the rail near the logic doesn't sag.

Decaps are sprinkled into the empty whitespace of the floorplan — especially in regions of dense, high-activity logic. The closer the decap is to the switching cells, the more effectively it smooths the dynamic droop.

Common mistakeKeep four things straight. (1) IR drop has both a static and a dynamic component. (2) Electromigration is a long-term reliability limit driven by current density — not an instant failure. (3) Decaps fix dynamic droop; they do not help static IR drop much. (4) Excessive IR drop slows timing — a lower supply voltage makes cells slower, not faster.

8.4Dynamic Power & Rush Current

Dynamic power is burned whenever nodes toggle. The trouble for the PDN is not the average — it's the spikiness. When a large bank of flops or a wide datapath switches simultaneously on a clock edge, the instantaneous current can dwarf the average draw, and that spike is exactly what creates dynamic IR drop.

A second source is rush (inrush) current: when a power-gated block is switched back on, its starved internal rail recharges all at once, pulling a large gulp of current the moment the power switches close.

simultaneous switching + power-gating inrush ⟹ current spikesA robust PDN (low resistance) plus local decaps keep the supply stable through them.

The defense is two-fold: a robust PDN with low resistance and plenty of metal, and decaps placed to absorb the spikes locally. Power-gating switches are also turned on in staged sequences (daisy-chained) to spread inrush over time instead of all at once.

✦ Worked Example

EM checking flags a power wire: it carries a current I, but its current density J = I / A exceeds the design-rule limit Jmax. How do you fix it without changing the current it must deliver?

  1. What EM depends on: current density J = I / A, not current alone. A is the wire's cross-sectional area (width × thickness).
  2. Constraint: the wire still has to carry the same current I — so we can't simply lower I.
  3. The lever: increase A. On a fixed metal layer the thickness is fixed, so we widen the wire. Double the width ⟹ double A.
  4. Result: J = I / (2A) = ½ of before — the current density is halved, bringing it back under Jmax.
  5. Takeaway: widening is the standard fix for EM-critical power and signal wires (often combined with adding parallel straps for big rails).

🛠️ Hands-On: PDN / IR-drop analysis in OpenROAD

OpenROAD's analyze_power_grid (PDNSim) reports the voltage drop across the power grid for a placed design. Run it for both the static and the dynamic (vectorless) cases, then read the worst-drop and worst-current numbers.

# inside the OpenROAD shell, after placement + PDN insertion
read_db design.odb
set_pdnsim_net_voltage -net VDD -voltage 0.80

# static IR drop — average current
analyze_power_grid -net VDD -outfile static_ir.rpt

# dynamic IR drop — transient switching
analyze_power_grid -net VDD -dynamic -outfile dynamic_ir.rpt

Open the reports and find Worstcase voltage and Average IR drop. Compare static vs. dynamic — the dynamic worst-drop should be larger. Where the drop is worst (usually the die center), the fix is more straps, wider metal, or more decaps nearby.

A. IR drop is the V = I·R loss across the power-delivery network. Too much droop lowers a cell's supply, slowing it (less timing margin) or breaking it.

Q2. Electromigration is…

B. metal erosion from high current density over time — a reliability limit
C. a crosstalk glitch
D. a routing-congestion issue

B. Sustained high current density slowly moves metal atoms, forming voids/opens over the product lifetime. It's a long-term reliability limit, not an instant failure.

Q3. Decoupling capacitors…
A. fix static IR drop only
B. supply transient current locally, reducing dynamic IR drop
C. increase electromigration

B. A decap is a local charge reservoir that sources the switching current spike instantly, smoothing dynamic droop. It does little for static drop.

Q4. To reduce electromigration in a wire you…
A. widen it (lowering the current density)
B. narrow it
C. raise its current
D. add a decap to it
Show answer

A. EM depends on current density J = I/A. Widening increases A, so for the same current J drops — the standard fix for EM-critical wires.

Q5. Dynamic IR drop is worst during…
A. steady idle
B. power-off
C. a single gate toggling

D. When many cells switch on the same edge, the instantaneous current spike outpaces the package, sagging the rail. Idle and single-gate activity barely move it.

✓ What you learned

  • IR drop is the V = I·R loss across the PDN, with a static (average) and a dynamic (transient) component; excess droop slows cells.
  • Electromigration is a long-term reliability limit set by current density J = I/A — high J slowly opens voids.
  • Decoupling caps are local charge reservoirs that source transient current and smooth dynamic droop (not static).
  • Simultaneous switching and power-gating inrush cause the current spikes; a robust PDN plus decaps absorb them.
  • The standard EM fix is to widen the wire (more area ⟹ lower J), and you can read it all in an OpenROAD IR-drop report.
