RTL Synthesis

Turning RTL into a gate-level netlist — and why your constraints, not just your code, decide the chip.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what logic synthesis does: RTL in, a gate-level netlist of standard cells out.
  • Walk through the synthesis steps: elaborate → generic logic → technology-map → optimize.
  • Describe how timing/area/power constraints steer the trade-offs the tool makes.
  • Define a standard-cell library (.lib) and the timing/area/power data it holds.
  • Predict how the same RTL produces different netlists under different constraints.

6.1What Synthesis Does

Logic synthesis translates your RTL (Verilog/VHDL describing behavior at the register-transfer level) into a gate-level netlist — a concrete list of interconnected logic gates and flip-flops. Those gates aren't abstract: they're drawn from a standard-cell library, a .lib file that holds each cell's timing, area, and power characteristics for a specific manufacturing process.

So synthesis is the bridge from "what the circuit should do" (RTL) to "which real cells implement it" (netlist). Everything downstream — placement, routing, timing sign-off — operates on that netlist of actual library cells.

6.2The Synthesis Steps

Under the hood, synthesis is a pipeline of distinct stages:

1. Elaborate the RTL. Parse the HDL, resolve parameters and generate-blocks, and build an internal representation of the design's logic and registers.

2. Build generic logic. Convert that behavior into technology-independent logic — generic gates, adders, muxes, and registers — without yet committing to any real cell.

3. Technology-map. Map the generic logic onto the actual cells in the .lib library: each chunk of logic is matched to NANDs, NORs, AOIs, flip-flops, etc. that exist in the target process.

4. Optimize. Restructure, resize, and re-map cells to meet the constraints — collapsing redundant logic, buffering long paths, and swapping cell sizes to hit timing, area, or power targets.

6.3Optimizing for Area, Power & Timing

The optimizer doesn't have a single "right" answer — it has a goal you hand it. The constraints you provide (most importantly the clock period, plus area and power budgets) tell the tool what to trade against what. A tight clock forces it to spend area and power buying speed; a loose clock lets it shrink the design.

The consequence is fundamental: the same RTL produces different netlists under different constraints. The HDL describes the function; the constraints describe the goal; only together do they determine the gates you actually get.

AnalogySynthesis is a translator plus optimizer. It takes your intent (the RTL) and renders it in the best available "words" — the standard cells — for the goal you set. Ask the same translator for the fastest phrasing, the shortest, or the quietest, and you get three different sentences from one identical intent. The constraints are the brief you give the translator.

6.4The Standard-Cell Library

A standard-cell library is a set of pre-designed, pre-characterized logic cells — inverters, NANDs, NORs, AOI/OAI gates, multiplexers, flip-flops, and more — each available in several drive strengths (sizes). For every cell the .lib file records its delay (as a function of load and input slew), its area, and its power.

Synthesis uses that data to choose which cell and what size at each point in the netlist. A bigger drive-strength cell switches faster but costs more area and leakage; a smaller one saves area and power but is slower. Picking and sizing cells against the library's numbers is exactly how the optimizer hits your targets.

Common mistakeSynthesis output is decided by the constraints, not the RTL alone. The same RTL with a tight clock gives a bigger, faster netlist; with a loose clock, a smaller one. The tool also needs a target standard-cell library and a clock constraint to do anything meaningful — without them it has no cells to map to and no goal to optimize for. And synthesis is not simulation: it builds hardware, it doesn't run your testbench.
✦ Worked Example

Take one fixed piece of RTL — say, a 32-bit adder, assign sum = a + b; — and synthesize it twice against the same cell library but with different clock constraints. Watch the netlist change.

  1. Loose clock (5 ns). The path has plenty of time, so the tool picks the cheapest implementation that still meets timing — a small, slow ripple-carry-style adder using minimum-size cells. Result: low area, low power.
  2. Tight clock (1 ns). A ripple-carry chain can't propagate the carry in time, so the optimizer restructures the carry logic into a faster, more parallel carry-lookahead-style structure and upsizes cells on the critical path. Result: more cells, bigger cells — more area and power.
  3. Takeaway. Identical RTL, identical library, two different chips. The timing constraint alone drove the change — which is why you can't judge the RTL without knowing the constraints it'll be synthesized under.

🛠️ Hands-On: synthesize with Yosys, then tighten the clock

Yosys is the open-source synthesis tool. Synthesize a small module against a cell library, note the reported cell count and area, then re-run with a tighter clock target and compare.

# synth.ys — synthesize an adder with Yosys (Lecture 6)
read_verilog adder.v
hierarchy -top adder
synth -top adder
dfflibmap -liberty sky130.lib   ; map flip-flops to the library
abc -liberty sky130.lib -D 5000 ; -D = target delay in ps (5 ns, loose)
stat -liberty sky130.lib        ; report cell count + area

Now re-run with -D 1000 (a 1 ns target) and compare the stat output. The tighter target should report more cells and larger area — same RTL, different netlist, driven entirely by the constraint.

C. The optimizer is goal-driven, so the constraints — not the filename or tooling around it — decide the resulting gates.

A. remove flip-flops
B. lower the clock
C. use larger/faster (and more) cells, at the cost of area and power

C. Hitting a tight clock means upsizing cells and adding parallel logic — buying speed with area and power.

Model response

Synthesis elaborates the RTL into generic logic, technology-maps that logic onto a specific process's standard cells, and optimizes the result against your constraints using the library's timing/area/power data. Because the optimization is goal-driven, the "best" netlist is entirely goal-dependent: a tight clock pushes the tool toward bigger, faster cells and more parallel structures (more area and power), while a loose clock yields a smaller, lower-power result from identical RTL.

So the RTL alone does not determine the chip — the constraints and the target library do. Judging RTL "quality" without knowing the clock target, the area/power budget, and the cell library is meaningless, because the same code can be excellent for one set of constraints and poor for another.
