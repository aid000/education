Verification & Signoff

The pre-flight checklist before tape-out — proving the layout is correct and manufacturable.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why signoff must happen before tape-out — fixing a fabbed chip is enormously expensive.
  • Distinguish DRC (geometry rules) from LVS (layout matches the netlist) — both required.
  • Describe functional and formal verification, including RTL-vs-gate equivalence checking.
  • List the final signoff checks: multi-corner STA, power/IR-drop/EM, and parasitic extraction (PEX).
  • Define "tape-out" — releasing the verified GDSII to the fab.

10.1Why Signoff

Before a chip is sent to the fab, you must prove two things: that the layout is correct (it does what the design intended) and that it is manufacturable (the foundry can actually build it). This proof is called signoff — a structured set of checks that every design must pass.

Why so rigorous? Because once the masks are cut and the fab runs, there is no quick fix. A single mistake means re-spinning the design — new masks, weeks of fab time, and costs measured in millions. Unlike software, you cannot patch a chip after it ships. So the discipline is simple: catch everything before tape-out.

10.2DRC & LVS

Two physical-verification checks anchor signoff, and they check fundamentally different things.

DRC (design-rule check) confirms that the layout geometry obeys the foundry's rules — minimum wire widths, minimum spacings, enclosure and overlap requirements. It looks only at shapes: are any two polygons too close, too thin, or improperly aligned for this process?

LVS (layout-versus-schematic) confirms that the layout's connectivity matches the intended netlist. It extracts the devices and wires actually drawn and compares that graph to the schematic/netlist: same devices, same nets, same connections — no missing wires, no accidental shorts.

DRC → geometry obeys the rules  ·  LVS → connectivity matches the netlistDifferent questions. A layout can be DRC-clean yet wired wrong (LVS fail), or correctly wired yet illegal geometry (DRC fail).
AnalogySignoff is the pre-flight checklist before a plane — your chip — takes off, where takeoff is tape-out. DRC checks the build is legal (every part meets spec). LVS checks the plane matches its blueprint (everything is wired where it should be). And timing/power signoff checks it will actually fly under real conditions. You don't skip items on the list, because a fix after takeoff — after the chip has been fabricated — is catastrophically expensive.
Common mistakeDRC and LVS are different checks — DRC verifies geometry rules, LVS verifies the layout matches the schematic/netlist; both are required, and passing one tells you nothing about the other. Signoff timing is run across multiple corners (process/voltage/temperature), not at a single nominal condition. And "tape-out" means releasing the verified GDSII to the fab after all checks pass — not the first simulation or the floorplan.

10.3Functional & Formal Verification

Physical checks (DRC/LVS) prove the layout is built correctly, but you also need to prove it does the right thing. That comes from two complementary methods.

Functional verification runs the design against a testbench in simulation: stimulus is applied, outputs are observed, and they are compared to expected results. Good testbenches drive corner cases and measure coverage so you know how much of the design was actually exercised.

Formal verification proves properties mathematically instead of by simulation. The workhorse here is formal equivalence checking: it proves that the synthesized gate-level netlist is logically equivalent to the original RTL — guaranteeing synthesis (and later optimization) didn't change the function, across all possible inputs, without needing to simulate them one by one.

testbench simulation → does it behave?  ·  formal equivalence → is gate netlist ≡ RTL?

10.4Final Signoff & Tape-out

With function and connectivity confirmed, the last gate is making sure the physical chip will meet spec in the real world. Three families of checks:

Multi-corner timing signoff (STA). Static timing analysis is re-run across many corners — combinations of process (slow/typical/fast silicon), voltage, and temperature (PVT). A path that meets timing at nominal can fail when the silicon is slow, the supply droops, and the die is hot, so you sign off only when every corner is clean.

Power, IR-drop, and electromigration. You verify power is within budget, that IR drop in the power grid doesn't starve cells of voltage, and that current densities won't cause electromigration (metal slowly eroding) over the chip's lifetime.

Parasitic extraction (PEX). The real routed wires add resistance and capacitance. PEX extracts these parasitics from the finished layout so that timing and power signoff use the actual physical values, not estimates.

When — and only when — every one of these passes, the design is frozen and the GDSII layout file is released to the foundry. That release is tape-out.

✦ Worked Example — what each check catches

Two different bugs sit in a finished layout. Which signoff check flags each, and why does that mean both must run?

  1. DRC error: two metal wires are drawn closer than the foundry's minimum spacing. This is a pure geometry violation — flagged by DRC. The connectivity could be perfectly correct; the shapes are simply illegal for the process.
  2. LVS error: the layout is missing a connection that the netlist requires (or has an extra, unintended short). This is a connectivity mismatch — flagged by LVS. The spacing could be perfectly legal; the wiring just doesn't match the schematic.
  3. Why both, in this order: they catch entirely different classes of problem, so a clean DRC says nothing about LVS and vice-versa. Both run before tape-out — DRC first (legal geometry), then LVS (correct connectivity) — and only a layout that passes both moves on to timing and power signoff.

🛠️ Hands-On: run DRC & LVS signoff on your design

OpenLane runs physical signoff for you. Take your placed-and-routed design through its signoff steps and read the reports — DRC and LVS are the gatekeepers to tape-out.

# Run the OpenLane flow (includes DRC + LVS signoff)
make mount
./flow.tcl -design my_core

# Then read the signoff reports:
#   runs/.../reports/signoff/*-drc.rpt   → DRC violations (geometry)
#   runs/.../reports/signoff/*-lvs.rpt   → LVS result    (connectivity)
cat runs/*/reports/signoff/*-drc.rpt
cat runs/*/reports/signoff/*-lvs.rpt

Expected: a 0 violations / clean DRC report and an LVS report showing the layout matches the netlist. If DRC reports spacing/width errors, that's geometry; if LVS reports mismatched nets, that's connectivity — fix the right one before re-running.

✓ What you learned

  • Why signoff happens before tape-out: a fabbed chip can't be patched, so everything must be proven correct and manufacturable first.
  • DRC checks layout geometry against the fab's rules; LVS checks layout connectivity against the netlist — different checks, both required.
  • Functional verification (testbench simulation) and formal equivalence (gate netlist ≡ RTL) prove the design behaves correctly.
  • Final signoff: multi-corner STA, power/IR-drop/EM, and parasitic extraction (PEX).
  • Tape-out = releasing the verified GDSII to the fab once every check passes.
