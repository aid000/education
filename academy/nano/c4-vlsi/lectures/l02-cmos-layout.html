Week 2 · Lecture 2

CMOS Layout Basics

Turning a schematic into manufacturable geometry — layers, design rules, and standard cells.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe a layout as physical polygons drawn on fabrication layers (n-well, diffusion, poly, metal, contacts).
  • Explain why design rules (the DRC) set minimum widths and spacings for manufacturability.
  • Read a stick diagram and explain what a standard cell is and why it has a fixed height.
  • Relate a transistor's W/L ratio to its drive strength, area, and power.
  • Size a CMOS inverter's pMOS and nMOS for balanced rise/fall switching.

2.1From Schematic to Layout

A schematic tells you what transistors connect to what. A layout tells the fab where to put every piece of silicon — it is the physical geometry of the chip: a set of polygons drawn on different layers. Each layer becomes a real step in fabrication (C2): doping a region, growing oxide, depositing polysilicon or metal, etching a contact hole.

The layers you draw for a basic CMOS gate are:

n-well · diffusion (active) · polysilicon · metal1/metal2… · contacts & viasPolysilicon over diffusion forms a transistor gate; contacts tie diffusion/poly up to metal; vias stack metal layers.

Where polysilicon crosses diffusion, a transistor channel forms. n-type diffusion sitting in the substrate makes an nMOS; p-type diffusion inside an n-well makes a pMOS. Contacts are little squares that connect diffusion or poly up to metal1, and the metal layers carry the wires that route signals and power across the chip.

AnalogyA layout is like a stack of transparent map overlays. Each layer — poly, metal1, metal2, … — is one transparency, and the fab lines them all up to build the device. The design rules are the legal spacings printed on every overlay, so the "roads" (wires) on each layer never illegally touch each other or the roads above and below.

2.2Design Rules (DRC)

You can't draw geometry arbitrarily. The fab's process can only resolve features down to a certain size, and lines placed too close together will short or fail to print. Design rules are the contract between the designer and the foundry: a list of minimum widths and minimum spacings (plus enclosures and overlaps) that guarantee the design can actually be manufactured.

DRC: minimum width  ·  minimum spacing  ·  minimum enclosure/overlapChecked automatically by a Design Rule Check (DRC) tool before the layout is sent ("taped out") to the fab.

Every layer has its own rules — metal1 might require a 0.14 µm minimum width and 0.14 µm minimum spacing, for example. A layout that violates even one rule is rejected; it physically cannot be built reliably.

2.3Stick Diagrams & Standard Cells

Before drawing exact polygons, designers sketch a stick diagram: a quick drawing where each layer is a colored line (poly, diffusion, metal) and the topology — what crosses what, what connects where — is captured without committing to precise dimensions. It's the napkin sketch of layout, used to plan a cell's floorplan fast.

The polished, reusable result is a standard cell: a pre-designed, DRC-clean, fully verified layout of a single logic gate (an inverter, NAND2, flip-flop, …). The key constraint is that every cell in a library shares a fixed height.

standard cells: fixed height  ⟹  they tile into rowsSame height means cells abut shoulder-to-shoulder; the VDD rail at the top and GND rail at the bottom line up automatically across a whole row.

Because the height is fixed and the power rails are at fixed positions, a place-and-route tool can drop cells side-by-side into long rows and the power grid connects itself. The cell width varies with complexity, but the height never does.

Common mistakeThree things students get wrong: (1) layout polygons must obey the design rules (DRC) — a single violation and the chip cannot be manufactured; (2) standard cells share a fixed height precisely so they tile into rows (they do not all have the same width or logic); and (3) transistor sizing (W/L) trades speed against area and power — making a transistor bigger is not free.

2.4Transistor Sizing

A transistor's drive strength — how much current it can push, and therefore how fast it switches its load — is set by the ratio of channel width to channel length, the W/L ratio:

drive strength ∝ W / LWider (bigger W) ⟹ more current ⟹ faster switching. Length L is usually held at the process minimum.

Bigger transistors switch faster, but they aren't free: they consume more area, present a larger gate capacitance (so they load whatever drives them), and burn more power. Sizing is the engineer's lever for balancing speed against area and power.

In a CMOS gate, the pull-up network (pMOS) charges the output high and the pull-down network (nMOS) pulls it low. For the output to rise and fall at similar speeds, the two networks must have balanced drive strength — which, as the worked example shows, means the pMOS has to be made wider.

✦ Worked Example

Size a CMOS inverter for balanced switching. The nMOS conducts with electrons; the pMOS conducts with holes, which are roughly 2–3× less mobile. How should the two transistor widths compare so the output rises and falls at the same speed?

  1. Identify the imbalance: drive strength scales with carrier mobility × (W/L). Holes are ~2–3× slower than electrons, so for equal W/L the pMOS pulls up weaker than the nMOS pulls down.
  2. Compensate with width: to recover equal strength, make the pMOS wider by the mobility ratio. With both at minimum length, set Wp ≈ 2–3 × Wn.
  3. Rule of thumb: use Wp ≈ 2 × Wn for a balanced inverter (some processes use up to ~3×). This is exactly why the p-diffusion in the §2.1 layout is drawn wider than the n-diffusion.
  4. Result: the pull-up and pull-down currents match, so the rise time ≈ fall time and the switching threshold sits near VDD/2.

🛠️ Hands-On: explore a real standard cell in KLayout

Open a Sky130 standard cell in KLayout and find the physical layers you just learned. Sky130 is an open-source 130 nm PDK, so the GDS files are freely available.

# grab the open Sky130 standard-cell library, then open a cell
klayout sky130_fd_sc_hd__inv_2.gds   # a size-2 inverter cell

In the layers panel, toggle each layer on and off and locate: diff (diffusion / active), poly (the gate stripe crossing the diffusion), met1 (metal1 — note the VDD rail on top and GND on the bottom), and the licon/mcon contacts tying diffusion up to metal1. Confirm the cell's height matches the other cells in the library, and notice the pMOS diffusion is drawn wider than the nMOS — the 2× sizing from the worked example.

B. minimum widths and spacings for manufacturability

B. Design rules are minimum widths, spacings, and enclosures that guarantee the geometry can actually be manufactured. Violate one and the chip can't be built.

Q3. Standard cells share…

B. one metal layer

C. A fixed height lets cells abut into rows with their VDD/GND rails aligned. Width varies with the cell's complexity, but height does not.

Q4. To balance switching speed, the pMOS is usually made…
A. narrower than the nMOS
B. removed
C. identical to the nMOS
D. wider than the nMOS (holes are slower than electrons)
Show answer

D. Holes are ~2–3× less mobile than electrons, so the pMOS must be widened (rule of thumb Wp ≈ 2 × Wn) to match the nMOS drive strength.

Q5. Increasing a transistor's W/L ratio…
A. lowers power for free
B. increases drive strength and speed but costs area and power

B. A larger W/L pushes more current (faster switching) but consumes more area, adds gate capacitance, and burns more power. Bigger is not free.

✓ What you learned

  • A layout is physical polygon geometry on layers — n-well, diffusion, poly, metal, contacts — that the fab builds (back to C2).
  • Design rules (DRC) set minimum widths and spacings; obey them or the chip can't be manufactured.
  • Stick diagrams sketch layout topology fast; standard cells are verified gate layouts of a fixed height that tile into rows.
  • W/L ratio sets drive strength — bigger is faster but costs area and power.
  • A balanced inverter makes the pMOS ≈ 2× wider than the nMOS because holes are slower than electrons.
