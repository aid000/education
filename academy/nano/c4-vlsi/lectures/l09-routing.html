Routing & Optimization

Wiring up the placed cells through the metal layers — and why it's one of the hardest steps.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how routing connects placed cells, and the split between global and detailed routing.
  • Describe routing congestion and how adding metal layers (at a cost) relieves it.
  • Identify signal-integrity issues fixed at the route stage: antenna effects, crosstalk, and IR drop.
  • Explain why wire RC delay matters and how timing-driven routing prioritizes critical nets.
  • Justify why modern stacks alternate preferred routing directions per metal layer.

9.1Connecting the Cells

After placement fixes where each standard cell sits, routing draws the actual wires that connect them through the chip's stack of metal layers. It happens in two stages. Global routing plans coarse regions and corridors — it decides roughly which channels each net should travel through, balancing the demand across the die. Then detailed routing commits each net to exact tracks and layers, dropping vias where a net needs to change layers, all while obeying the foundry's design rules.

The split is a divide-and-conquer move: planning corridors first keeps the enormous problem tractable, and only afterward does the router sweat the exact geometry. A net that looks fine in the coarse plan can still fail detailed routing if its corridor is too crowded — which is why the two stages feed back on each other.

9.2Congestion & Layers

Each routing layer offers only a finite number of tracks, and many nets want to pass through the same region. When demand exceeds capacity you get congestion — the router runs out of legal places to put wires, and some nets cannot be completed. Congestion is local: a single hot spot can stall an otherwise easy design.

The classic relief valve is to add more metal layers. Modern processes stack a dozen or more layers, each adding routing capacity. But every layer means extra masks and process steps — it costs money. The cheaper lever is a better placement: routability depends heavily on where the cells sit, because placement sets how much wire demand lands in each region. A bad placement crowds connected cells together and makes routing hard or impossible no matter how clever the router is.

9.3Integrity at the Route Stage

Real wires misbehave, and several physical effects are resolved precisely when the routing is drawn:

  • Antenna effect — long metal segments connected to a gate during fabrication can collect charge and damage the thin gate oxide. The router fixes this by adding jumpers to higher layers or inserting antenna diodes.
  • Crosstalk — a switching wire couples capacitively into its neighbors, injecting noise or delay. The router adds spacing or shielding between sensitive, parallel wires.
  • IR drop — current flowing through the resistive power grid causes the local supply voltage to sag (V = I·R). A weak grid slows or corrupts the cells it feeds.

On top of these, long wires are not left bare: the tool inserts buffers / repeaters along them to restore signal strength and keep delay in check.

9.4Timing-Driven Optimization

Routing is not just connectivity — it sets delay. Each wire has resistance and capacitance, and its RC delay adds directly to a path's timing. So the router is timing-driven: it identifies the critical nets (those on the tightest paths) and gives them the shortest, widest, least-congested routes, even at the expense of non-critical nets.

delay ≈ Rwire · Cwireand both R and C grow with wire length → long nets are slow

To hit timing, the tool inserts buffers on long or heavily loaded nets and may resize cells. Whatever violations remain after the main route are cleaned up with targeted engineering change orders (ECOs) — small, localized edits to the netlist and routing that fix the last failing paths without re-running the whole flow.

AnalogyRouting is city traffic planning. Global routing lays out the highway corridors — the broad routes traffic should take across town — while detailed routing assigns each car to an exact lane. Congestion is gridlock: it happens when too many cars (wires) all want the same road (track) at once, and no amount of clever driving gets them through a road that's simply too small.
Common mistakeRouting is not a clean-up afterthought. Wire RC delay can dominate a path's timing at advanced nodes, and a bad placement can make routing outright impossible through congestion. Adding metal layers helps but costs real money. And antenna effects, crosstalk, and IR drop are genuine physical problems that are diagnosed and fixed right here at the route stage — not waved away.
✦ Worked Example

Why do modern metal stacks alternate the preferred routing direction on each layer? Work through what happens if they didn't.

  1. Same direction, one layer: if every wire on a layer ran horizontally, two horizontal nets that need to occupy the same row would collide — there's no way to pass them without one blocking the other.
  2. Alternate the next layer: make metal-1 run mostly horizontal and metal-2 mostly vertical. Now a horizontal net on metal-1 and a vertical net on metal-2 cross at a right angle — they share a point in the 2-D picture but live on different layers, so they don't touch.
  3. Connect only on purpose: the two layers join only where the router drops a via. Crossings without a via are harmless; connections happen only where intended.
  4. Why it scales: alternating directions lets each layer carry a dense set of parallel wires while the next layer carries the orthogonal traffic, multiplying usable capacity — which is exactly why real stacks alternate preferred directions layer by layer.
  5. Recall (C3): wire delay grows roughly with length², so long nets are slow. That's why the tool buffers long nets to break them into shorter, faster RC segments.

🛠️ Hands-On: route a design and inspect congestion

Run the routing step of an OpenLane flow on a placed design, then read the congestion / DRC report it produces and view the final routed layout in KLayout.

# inside an OpenLane flow, run the routing stage
flow.tcl -design spm -from routing -to routing -tag myrun

# inspect the global-route congestion + DRC reports
less designs/spm/runs/myrun/reports/routing/*congestion*
less designs/spm/runs/myrun/reports/routing/*drc*

# open the routed layout (GDS/DEF) to see the metal layers + vias
klayout designs/spm/runs/myrun/results/routing/spm.def

Look for: congestion hot spots (regions over 100% track demand), any remaining DRC violations, and — in KLayout — the alternating horizontal/vertical metal layers stitched together by vias.

Q2. Routing congestion happens when…
A. the clock is too slow
B. too many wires compete for the limited routing tracks
C. there are too few transistors
D. the die is too large
Show answer

B. Each layer has a finite number of tracks; when nets demand more capacity than a region has, the router can't place them all — that's congestion.

Q3. Adding more metal layers…
A. slows the clock
B. removes the need for vias
C. relieves congestion (but adds cost)
D. increases congestion
Show answer

C. Extra layers add routing capacity and relieve congestion, but each layer means more masks and process steps — it costs money.

Q4. At advanced nodes, interconnect (wire) delay…
A. is always negligible
B. only affects power
C. can dominate gate delay
D. disappears with scaling
Show answer

C. Wire RC delay can dominate a path's timing at advanced nodes, which is why routing is timing-driven and critical nets get the best routes.

Place-and-route is highly automated, yet routing remains one of the hardest steps and can make or break a chip's timing — and even its feasibility. Why is routing so hard, and why can a logically correct netlist still fail because of it?

Model response

Routing must connect millions of nets through a finite number of metal tracks without overlaps, while obeying design rules, meeting timing (wire RC delay), and avoiding congestion — an enormous combinatorial optimization with many competing constraints. There's no clean closed-form answer; the router juggles all of these at once, which is why it's one of the slowest, most skill-intensive steps in the flow.

Even a perfectly correct netlist can fail physically: a placement that clusters connected cells creates local congestion where no legal routes exist; long nets can blow the timing budget because their RC delay grows faster than linearly with length; and the power grid or signal-integrity limits (IR drop, crosstalk, antenna rules) may be unsatisfiable as drawn. So logical correctness does not guarantee physical feasibility — placement and routing determine whether the design actually "closes," which is why they dominate turnaround time and skill.

Self-check rubric

  • Congestion and/or wire RC delay can make a correct netlist fail
  • Logical correctness ≠ physical feasibility — placement/routing decide whether it closes

✓ What you learned

  • Routing connects placed cells through the metal layers in two stages: global (coarse corridors) then detailed (exact tracks + vias).
  • Congestion arises when nets demand more than the finite routing tracks; more metal layers relieve it but cost money, and routability depends heavily on placement.
  • The route stage fixes real physical issues: antenna effects, crosstalk, and IR drop, and inserts buffers/repeaters on long wires.
