Week 8 · Lecture 8

Physical Design Basics

Placing the synthesized cells on the die — floorplanning, placement, and the clock tree.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe how physical design turns a synthesized gate netlist into a placed layout.
  • Explain floorplanning: setting die size, placing I/O pads and macros, and building the power grid.
  • State the goal of placement — minimize wirelength and congestion while meeting timing.
  • Explain clock tree synthesis (CTS) and why low skew matters.
  • Compute core utilization and pick a sensible target (~60–70%) that leaves room for routing.

8.1From Netlist to Layout

Synthesis (last few lectures) handed us a gate-level netlist: a list of standard cells (AND, OR, flip-flops, buffers…) and the wires connecting them. But a netlist is just connectivity — it says what connects to what, not where anything sits on the silicon. Physical design is the stage that turns that abstract netlist into real geometry.

It happens in stages: first floorplanning (set the canvas), then placement (drop every cell into a spot), then clock tree synthesis (build the clock distribution), and finally routing — connecting all the signal wires, which is the next lecture.

netlist (what connects) → floorplan → placement → CTS → routing → layout (where it lives)

This lecture covers everything up to and including the clock tree. Once cells are placed and the clock is built, the tool has the geometry it needs to route the signal nets.

8.2Floorplanning

Floorplanning sets up the canvas before any standard cell is placed. The key early decisions are:

• Set the overall die size and the core area where standard cells will live.
• Place the I/O pads around the edge — the connection points to the outside world.
• Position large macros (e.g. SRAM blocks, analog IP) that don't fit the standard-cell flow.
• Define the power grid — the rings and stripes that deliver VDD/GND everywhere.

These decisions have the biggest impact on the final result and are hard to change later. A poor floorplan strands logic far from the macros it talks to, and no amount of clever placement or routing fully recovers from it.

AnalogyPhysical design is arranging furniture in a house. Floorplanning fixes the room layout and the wiring/power closet — the big structural choices you commit to before moving anything in. Placement puts each piece of furniture where it's used most. And clock tree synthesis lays the "clock plumbing" so every room gets the beat at the same instant.

8.3Placement

With the canvas fixed, placement positions every standard cell into the rows of the core. The cells snap to the row grid (sharing power rails), and the tool chooses their x-positions to optimize several goals at once:

• Minimize total wirelength — cells that talk to each other should sit close together, since short wires mean less delay and less power.
• Avoid congestion — don't crowd so many cells into one region that there's no room left to route the wires.
• Meet timing — keep critical-path cells close so their nets are fast enough to satisfy the clock period.

Placement is not just packing cells as tightly as possible. A dense blob may have short wires but be impossible to route. Good placement balances density against routability and timing.

8.4Clock Tree Synthesis (CTS)

Every flip-flop in the design needs the clock. If we just wired the clock source straight to all of them, the edge would arrive at different times — near flops first, far flops later. That difference in arrival time is clock skew, and skew eats into your timing budget and can even cause hold violations.

Clock tree synthesis builds a balanced distribution network — typically an H-tree or a buffered tree — so the clock edge reaches every flip-flop at nearly the same instant. The goal is low skew, not merely connecting the clock net.

skew = (latest clock arrival) − (earliest clock arrival)CTS inserts and balances buffers so this difference is as small as possible.
Common mistakeFloorplanning decisions dominate the final result and are hard to fix later — don't rush them. Placement minimizes wirelength and congestion AND meets timing — it is not just packing cells as tightly as possible (over-packing kills routability). And CTS targets low SKEW — the clock arriving simultaneously at every flop — not merely connecting the clock net.
✦ Worked Example

A block contains a memory macro and the logic that reads/writes it. The core area is 400 µm × 400 µm and the standard cells total 96,000 µm². Reason about the floorplan and check the utilization.

  1. Place the macro near its logic: putting the memory macro adjacent to the access logic shortens those nets — shorter wires mean less delay (and less power), helping the read/write paths meet timing.
  2. Core area: 400 × 400 = 160,000 µm².
  3. Core utilization: util = total cell area / core area = 96,000 / 160,000 = 0.60 = 60%.
  4. Is that healthy? Yes — ~60–70% leaves space for routing. If we shrank the core to push utilization to ~95%, the wires would have nowhere to go: routing becomes congested or even impossible.

🛠️ Hands-On: floorplan & place in OpenLane

OpenLane runs the open-source physical-design flow on a small design. Run just through placement, then open the result in KLayout to see the floorplan and placed cells.

# inside the OpenLane container, on a small design (e.g. spm)
./flow.tcl -design spm -to placement   ; stop after placement

# view the floorplan + placed cells
klayout -e runs/*/results/placement/spm.def

Look for the FLOORPLAN_CORE_UTIL value in the config (the target utilization) and inspect how the macros and standard-cell rows landed. Try lowering the utilization and re-running — the cells should spread out, leaving more routing room.

Q1. Floorplanning sets…
A. the die size, I/O and macro placement, and the power grid
B. the RTL
C. the clock frequency
D. the gate-level logic

A. Floorplanning fixes the canvas: die/core size, I/O pad and macro placement, and the power grid. The RTL and logic come from earlier stages; the clock frequency is a constraint, not set here.

Q2. Placement positions standard cells to…
A. maximize the die size
B. minimize wirelength/congestion and meet timing
C. randomize locations
D. set the clock period
Show answer

B. Placement chooses cell positions to minimize wirelength and congestion while satisfying timing — not just to pack cells tightly.

Q3. Clock tree synthesis (CTS)…
A. generates the clock frequency
B. routes the signal nets
C. distributes the clock with minimal skew to all flip-flops
D. runs DRC
Show answer

C. CTS builds a balanced, buffered network so the clock edge reaches every flip-flop at nearly the same time — minimizing skew. It does not create the clock or route signal nets.

Q4. Early floorplan decisions…
A. are trivial to change anytime
B. have the biggest impact and are hard to fix later
C. don't affect timing
D. only set colors
Show answer

B. Floorplanning commits the structural layout; a bad floorplan strands logic far from its macros, and later stages can't fully recover. So these decisions dominate the result and are costly to revisit.

Q5. Core utilization that is too high (e.g. 95%)…
A. always improves timing
B. lowers power
C. causes routing congestion (or makes routing impossible)
D. has no downside
Show answer

C. Packing cells too densely leaves no room for the wires. Aim for ~60–70% so routing has space; ~95% causes congestion or makes routing infeasible.

✓ What you learned

  • Physical design turns the synthesized gate netlist into placed geometry, ahead of routing.
  • Floorplanning sets die size, I/O and macro placement, and the power grid — the highest-impact, hardest-to-change decisions.
  • Placement positions standard cells to minimize wirelength/congestion while meeting timing — not just packing them tight.
  • Clock tree synthesis builds a balanced network for low skew, so the clock reaches every flip-flop together.
  • Core utilization = cell area / core area; aim for ~60–70% to leave routing room (≈95% congests).
