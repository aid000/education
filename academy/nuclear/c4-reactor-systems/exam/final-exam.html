Reactor types, plant overview & core/fuel · Weeks 1–2
1. A sodium-cooled fast reactor differs fundamentally from a PWR or BWR in that it has no…
Answer: B. A sodium-cooled fast reactor deliberately runs with no moderator, keeping neutrons fast; liquid sodium is still the coolant, control rods are still required, and the low-pressure sodium loop simply does not need a thick PWR-style forged vessel. (A) it has liquid sodium as coolant. (C) control rods are still needed to manage reactivity. (D) the vessel is thinner, not absent, because sodium runs near atmospheric pressure.
2. In a PWR or BWR, light water serves as both coolant and moderator. What is the key safety implication of that coupling?
Answer: A. Because the same water both cools and moderates, losing it removes moderation too — neutrons stay fast and fission drops, a built-in negative coolant-void/temperature feedback. (B) is false; moderation and coolant are the same substance, not independent. (C) no backup moderator exists or is needed. (D) this coupling is itself a negative feedback mechanism.
3. In the primary/secondary/tertiary loop architecture of a PWR plant, which loop is the only one that becomes radioactive?
Answer: C. Only the primary loop flows through the core, so only it becomes radioactive; heat crosses to the secondary and tertiary loops through walls, never by mixing, so those stay clean. (A) and (B) are the clean steam and cooling-water loops. (D) contradicts the entire point of the loop-barrier architecture.
4. Put the reactor core hierarchy in order from smallest to largest.
Answer: A. A ceramic UO₂ pellet stacks into a clad fuel rod; rods pack into a square-lattice assembly (e.g. 17×17); assemblies tile into the full core. (B) and (C) scramble the order. (D) reverses it — that is largest-to-smallest, not smallest-to-largest.
5. Why are commercial LWR lattices deliberately designed to be slightly under-moderated rather than at the moderator-to-fuel ratio that maximizes reactivity?
Answer: B. Sitting on the under-moderated side of the reactivity-vs-moderation curve means a hotter, less dense coolant reduces both moderation and reactivity — a passive negative feedback. (A) is backwards; peak reactivity is at the optimum ratio, not under-moderated. (C) enrichment cost is unrelated to lattice pitch. (D) control rods are still required for startup, shutdown, and power shaping.
Primary coolant system & pressure vessel · Week 3
6. Starting at the cold-leg inlet nozzle, trace the correct coolant flow path inside a PWR reactor vessel.
Answer: C. Both nozzles penetrate the vessel's upper shell. Cold water enters high, is carried down the downcomer (the annular gap between core barrel and vessel wall) to the lower plenum, then turns and flows up through the core before leaving through the hot leg — also an upper-shell nozzle. (A) skips the downcomer/lower-plenum detour entirely. (B) wrongly puts the cold-leg nozzle at the bottom of the vessel. (D) reverses the up/down direction and misplaces the hot-leg nozzle.
7. What maintains the ~155 bar system pressure in a PWR primary loop?
Answer: B. The pressurizer's steam space sets the system pressure, trimmed by immersion heaters (raise) and spray nozzles (lower). (A) The pumps only overcome a few bar of loop friction, not the absolute system pressure — confusing the two is off by more than a factor of thirty. (C) The steam generator transfers heat; it does not set primary pressure. (D) The wall withstands pressure but does not create it.
8. Why must a PWR primary loop run near 155 bar rather than at atmospheric pressure?
Answer: B. High pressure lifts water's saturation temperature (~345 °C at 155 bar) above the ~325 °C hot-leg temperature, so the primary stays subcooled with a comfortable margin to departure from nucleate boiling (DNB). (A) pressure does not meaningfully compress solid ceramic fuel. (C) flow is set by the pumps overcoming friction, not by the static system pressure. (D) PWR chemistry deliberately removes dissolved oxygen, not air.
Steam generators & the secondary loop · Week 4
9. Why does a PWR use a separate secondary loop instead of sending primary coolant straight to the turbine?
Answer: A. The steam generator isolates the radioactive primary loop so the turbine island stays accessible and clean — the defining trade-off of the PWR design. (B) reactor coolant pumps handle high-pressure pumping routinely. (C) PWR primary water is deliberately kept from boiling; that is the BWR's approach. (D) turbines run on steam regardless of its source; the issue is radioactivity, not steam itself.
10. What is the key advantage of a once-through steam generator (OTSG) over a U-tube (recirculating) design?
Answer: B. An OTSG runs straight tubes with no recirculation, fully evaporating and then superheating the secondary water in one pass — hotter, drier steam than a U-tube SG's ~25% quality at the tube bundle. (A) is backwards: an OTSG holds far less secondary water and less thermal buffer. (C) a feedwater pump is still required. (D) the tube wall remains the only barrier in either design.
11. During a steam generator tube rupture (SGTR), which way does water flow across the breach, and why?
Answer: D. The primary side runs at substantially higher pressure, so a rupture drives radioactive primary water into the clean secondary system — the radiological hazard of an SGTR. (A) and (C) reverse the direction and invoke mechanisms (temperature, core suction) that do not drive the flow. (B) the two loops operate at very different pressures, not equal ones.
Control systems: rods, reactivity & poisons · Week 5
12. A reactor's multiplication factor is keff = 1.0010. Approximately what is its reactivity in pcm?
Answer: A. ρ = (k−1)/k = 0.0010/1.0010 ≈ 0.000999 ≈ 99.9 pcm ≈ 100 pcm (1 pcm = 10⁻⁵). (B) and (D) are off by one and three factors of ten respectively. (C) overstates it by a factor of ten — a common slip when forgetting pcm is 10⁻⁵, not 10⁻⁴.
13. Several hours after a reactor steps down from full power to a lower power, what does the xenon-135 concentration do, and why?
Answer: D. Lower flux nearly shuts off the neutron-burnup removal path for xenon, while the iodine-135 inventory built up at high power keeps decaying into xenon — so xenon rises for several hours (the "xenon peak") before decaying away. (A) ignores the iodine source term. (B) equilibrium depends on power, so a step change breaks it. (C) xenon-135 is radioactive with a ~9.2 h half-life; it is not stable.
Containment & structural design · Week 6
14. In the defense-in-depth scheme for a water reactor, which barrier is the final one between fission products and the public?
Answer: C. The containment is the outermost, final barrier, designed to remain intact even if the inner barriers are assumed to have failed. (A) cladding and (B) the pressure boundary are inner barriers. (D) the fuel matrix is the first, innermost barrier, not the last.
15. For a thin-walled cylindrical containment under internal pressure, how does the hoop (circumferential) membrane stress compare with the longitudinal (axial) stress?
Answer: A. σhoop = P·r/t while σlong = P·r/(2t), so hoop is exactly twice longitudinal — the reason a pressurized cylinder tends to split along its length. Designing against the smaller longitudinal value would leave the wall at only half its needed thickness. (B) reverses the ratio. (C) equality holds only for a sphere, not a cylinder. (D) is false — hoop stress is the larger of the two, not zero.
16. How do a PWR large-dry containment, a PWR ice-condenser containment, and a BWR Mark I/II/III containment each limit peak accident pressure?
Answer: C. Large-dry containments use cavernous free volume (and often sprays) to keep the pressure spike down; ice-condenser containments route steam through a melting borated-ice bed, allowing a much smaller building; BWR Mark I/II/III containments are pressure-suppression designs that vent steam into a water pool (torus-shaped in Mark I) where it condenses. (A) and (B) scramble which design uses which mechanism. (D) every containment is explicitly rated to a design-basis peak pressure.
Materials, stress & radiation damage · Week 7
17. A reactor-internals component sees a fast-neutron flux of 5.0×10¹⁰ n/cm²/s with a displacement cross-section of 2.0×10⁻²¹ cm². Over 10 years at a 90% capacity factor, roughly what dpa does it accumulate?
Answer: B. Rate = φ·σd = (5.0×10¹⁰)(2.0×10⁻²¹) = 1.0×10⁻¹⁰ dpa/s. Exposure t = 10 yr × 0.90 × 3.15×10⁷ s/yr ≈ 2.84×10⁸ s. dpa = rate × t ≈ 0.0283 dpa, i.e. ≈ 0.028 dpa. (A), (C), (D) are each off by one power of ten from a dropped or misplaced exponent.
18. As a pressure-vessel steel accumulates neutron dose, its ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT)…
Answer: A. Irradiation hardens and embrittles the steel, raising the DBTT — it becomes brittle at a warmer temperature than when new, which is why pressurized thermal shock (cold water into a hot, irradiated vessel) must be avoided. (B) reverses the real effect. (C) ignores well-documented aging. (D) the transition does not vanish; it shifts.
Instrumentation & control (I&C) · Week 8
19. Why is reactor-trip logic almost always built as two-out-of-four (2oo4) voting rather than a single channel?
Answer: C. Needing two of four channels to agree tolerates any single channel failure in either direction — a failed-high channel alone cannot trip the plant, and a failed-low channel alone cannot mask a real danger. (A) describes 1oo4 logic, which would trip spuriously on any single failed-high channel. (B) describes 4oo4 logic, which would fail to trip if even one channel failed low. (D) is not the engineering rationale.
20. How does a PWR's SCRAM mechanism differ from a BWR's, and why?
Answer: D. PWR control rods enter from the top and drop by gravity in a couple of seconds once their holding magnets are de-energized. A BWR's blades enter from below (the top is occupied by steam separators/dryers), so a SCRAM instead fires hydraulic scram valves that drive the blades upward using accumulator or reactor pressure. Both are fail-safe fast-insertion paths. (A) and (B) scramble or invert the mechanisms. (C) soluble boron is far too slow for a fast trip, and BWRs absolutely have a fast-shutdown system.
21. Adding more identical redundant channels to a trip system eventually stops improving reliability. What is the dominant limiting factor, and the fix?
Answer: D. Identical channels can share a hidden common-cause flaw (same software bug, same drift mode), which sets a reliability floor that more copies cannot remove. Only genuine diversity — a different sensor principle, manufacturer, or technology — lowers that common-cause fraction. (A), (B), (C) are not the limiting statistical mechanism.
Advanced & small modular reactors · Week 9
22. Why are Generation IV reactor concepts classified first by their coolant (gas, sodium, or molten salt)?
Answer: B. The coolant fixes temperature and pressure directly, and whether it moderates neutrons at all — choices that ripple through fuel, structure, and safety approach. (A) is not a real engineering factor. (C) is the opposite of true. (D) is not the reason for the classification scheme.
23. In a "fast reactor," what does the word "fast" refer to?
Answer: C. "Fast" describes the neutron energy spectrum — a fast reactor omits a moderator so neutrons stay near their birth energy, enabling breeding and actinide burning. (A), (B), and (D) all misread the term; it has nothing to do with response time, pump speed, or build schedule.
24. What is the central economic bet behind small modular reactors (SMRs)?
Answer: D. SMRs trade away some of the classic economies of scale in exchange for factory learning, standardization, lower financing risk from faster builds, and the passive safety that a small core makes easier. (A) is the traditional logic SMRs work against. (B) and (C) are false — fuel still has a cost, and safety systems are still required, just often passive ones.
Capstone: reactor system design · Week 10
25. A plant must deliver 250 MWe net electrical power at a net cycle (thermal-to-electric) efficiency of 0.32. What thermal power must the core produce?
Answer: D. Pth = Pe/η = 250/0.32 ≈ 781 MWth, meaning roughly 531 MWth is waste heat rejected to the environment. (A) mistakenly multiplies instead of dividing (250×0.32 = 80). (B) ignores efficiency entirely. (C) uses 1−η in the denominator (250/0.68 ≈ 368), which has no physical basis — always divide the electrical target by η, never by 1−η.
