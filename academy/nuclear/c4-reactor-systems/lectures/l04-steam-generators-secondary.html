Steam Generators & the Secondary Loop

A wall of metal tubing thinner than a coin separates radioactive reactor water from the clean steam that spins the turbine — and keeping that wall intact is one of the most consequential jobs in the whole plant.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a pressurized-water reactor needs a separate secondary loop and what the steam generator does between them.
  • Contrast U-tube (recirculating) and once-through steam generators and the trade-offs that drive the choice.
  • Apply the energy-balance and log-mean-temperature-difference (LMTD) relations to size the heat-transfer area of a steam generator.
  • Trace the secondary steam loop and balance of plant: feedwater, steam, turbine, condenser, and feedwater heating.
  • Describe steam-generator tube integrity as the radioactive/clean boundary and why tube degradation matters for safety.

4.1Why a Second Loop? The Steam Generator as the Great Divide

In Week 3 we sealed the reactor coolant inside a thick pressure vessel and kept it liquid at roughly 155 bar so it could carry heat away without boiling. That hot, high-pressure water is also slightly radioactive: it picks up activated corrosion products (the famous "crud") and traces of fission products that leak from any pinhole in the fuel cladding. We absolutely do not want that water touching the turbine, the condenser, or anything an operator might stand next to.

The solution that defines the entire pressurized-water reactor (PWR) is to split the plant into two hydraulically separate loops joined only by a heat exchanger. The primary loop carries hot radioactive water from the core to the steam generator and back. The secondary loop is a clean, non-radioactive water/steam circuit that picks up the heat, boils, and drives the turbine. The component that transfers heat from one to the other without letting the fluids mix is the steam generator (SG).

This is why the steam generator is sometimes called the heart — or the bottleneck — of a PWR. Every watt the core produces must cross those tube walls. A typical large PWR uses two to four steam generators, each a vessel the height of a small building containing thousands of tubes, transferring on the order of 800–1000 MW of thermal power apiece.

AnalogyThink of a double boiler in a kitchen. You never put chocolate directly on the burner — you set it in a bowl over a pot of simmering water. The hot water (primary) heats the bowl, the bowl heats the chocolate (secondary), and the two never mix. The steam generator is that bowl, scaled up by a factor of a million and built so the "burner side" is allowed to be radioactive while the "chocolate side" stays clean.

Boiling-water reactors (BWRs) skip the second loop entirely — they boil the coolant in the core and send that steam straight to the turbine, so the turbine hall is mildly radioactive during operation. The PWR's extra loop costs an enormous heat exchanger but buys a cleaner, more accessible turbine island. That single design decision ripples through the rest of this lecture.

4.2U-Tube vs. Once-Through: Two Ways to Boil Water

Inside the steam generator, hot primary water flows through thousands of small-diameter tubes (typically near 19 mm outer diameter, with walls around 1 mm thick) while secondary water surrounds them on the shell side. Heat conducts through the tube wall and boils the secondary water. There are two dominant ways to arrange this, and the difference shapes how the whole plant behaves.

U-tube (recirculating) steam generator

The most common design bends each tube into an inverted U: primary water enters the bottom, climbs the hot leg, crosses the top, and descends the cold leg to exit — both ends anchored in a single thick tube sheet at the bottom. On the secondary side, water only partly boils. A swirl-vane moisture separator and steam dryers at the top fling the liquid droplets back down, and that unboiled water recirculates to be heated again. The result is saturated steam at the outlet — wet enough that quality (the steam mass fraction) leaving the tube bundle is only about 25%, with the rest recirculated.

Once-through steam generator (OTSG)

The alternative runs straight tubes from a top tube sheet to a bottom tube sheet. Secondary water enters at the bottom and is fully evaporated and then superheated in a single pass — there is no recirculation and no separator. The payoff is superheated steam (a few tens of degrees above saturation), which improves turbine efficiency and avoids carrying liquid into the turbine. The cost is a tighter, less forgiving design: an OTSG holds far less secondary water, so it responds faster and has less thermal buffer if feedwater is lost.

x = m_steam / m_totalSteam quality x: the mass fraction of vapor. U-tube SG outlet x is near 1.0 after the dryers; once-through goes past x = 1 into superheat.
Common mistakeStudents often assume "the steam generator boils all the water that enters it." In a recirculating U-tube SG that is false — only a quarter or so of the water flowing past the tubes actually boils on each pass; the rest recirculates. The mass flow of steam leaving the SG equals the feedwater mass flow entering it (conservation of mass), but the flow circulating inside is several times larger. Confusing the circulating flow with the steam flow throws off every energy balance.

4.3Sizing the Heat Exchanger: Energy Balance & LMTD

How big must a steam generator be? The design rests on two ideas you can derive from first principles: energy must balance, and heat flows down a temperature difference.

First, energy balance. In steady state, the heat the primary water gives up equals the heat the secondary water absorbs (no heat escapes the shell). On the primary side the water stays liquid, so it simply cools by a temperature drop; on the secondary side the water mostly boils, so it absorbs its latent heat of vaporization:

Q = m_p * c_p * (T_hot - T_cold) = m_s * h_fgQ = power transferred (W). m_p, m_s = primary and steam mass flow (kg/s). c_p = primary specific heat. h_fg = latent heat of vaporization of the secondary water at SG pressure.

Second, the rate of heat transfer through the tube walls is governed by the overall heat-transfer coefficient U, the total tube area A, and the temperature difference between the two fluids. Because that difference changes along the tube, we use the log-mean temperature difference (LMTD), which is the thermodynamically correct average for a heat exchanger:

Q = U * A * LMTDU = overall heat-transfer coefficient (W/m^2K), A = heat-transfer area (m^2). LMTD is the log-mean of the end-to-end temperature differences.
LMTD = (dT1 - dT2) / ln(dT1 / dT2)dT1 and dT2 = primary-to-secondary temperature differences at the two ends of the exchanger.

These three relations let an engineer go from "the core makes Q watts" to "we need A square metres of tube," which fixes the number and length of tubes — and therefore the physical size of the vessel. A real SG carries thousands of tubes precisely because U and LMTD are modest while Q is gigantic.

AnalogyLMTD is like figuring out how fast a hot cup of coffee cools in a room. Near the start the gap to room temperature is big and it cools fast; near the end the gap is small and it barely cools. You cannot just use the average of the two gaps — the right "effective" gap is weighted toward the smaller end, which is exactly what the logarithm in the LMTD formula does. A heat exchanger sees the same shrinking gap from one end of the tube to the other.

4.4Balance of Plant & the Radioactive/Clean Boundary

Once dry steam leaves the SG, it enters the balance of plant (BOP) — everything that converts heat into electricity. The steam expands through the high- and low-pressure turbines, spinning the generator. Spent steam dumps into the condenser, a vacuum vessel cooled by river, sea, or cooling-tower water, where it collapses back to liquid (creating the low back-pressure that lets the turbine extract maximum work). That liquid is the condensate; pumps push it through a string of feedwater heaters that preheat it using steam bled from the turbine, raising overall efficiency, and a feedwater pump returns it to the steam generator at high pressure. Round and round: feedwater in, steam out, condensate back to feedwater.

Now the safety crux. The thin tube wall is the only barrier between radioactive primary water (at ~155 bar) and clean secondary water (at ~70 bar). Because the primary side is at higher pressure, any breach pushes radioactive water into the secondary system — a steam generator tube rupture (SGTR), one of the design-basis accidents every PWR must manage. So tube integrity is paramount.

Tubes degrade through mechanisms you can now name physically: flow-induced vibration that frets tubes against their supports; stress-corrosion cracking in tight crevices; denting and wastage from impurities concentrating where water boils; and fouling by deposits that throttle heat transfer. Operators fight back with rigorous secondary water chemistry (keeping oxygen and chlorides near zero), periodic eddy-current inspection of every tube, and plugging or sleeving any tube that has thinned past its limit. A steam generator that loses too many tubes is eventually replaced wholesale — among the largest maintenance jobs a plant ever undertakes.

Common mistakeDo not assume a tube leak sends clean water into the radioactive primary loop. The pressure gradient runs the other way: primary pressure (~155 bar) exceeds secondary pressure (~70 bar), so a rupture drives radioactive primary water into the clean secondary side, where it can reach the condenser and the environment. That direction is precisely what makes SGTR a radiological concern rather than a mere efficiency loss.
✦ Worked Example — sizing a steam generator's tube area

A steam generator must transfer Q = 900 MW thermal. Primary water enters at T_hot = 320 C and leaves at T_cold = 290 C. The secondary water boils at a constant T_sat = 270 C. The overall heat-transfer coefficient is U = 5000 W/m²K. (a) Find the LMTD. (b) Find the required tube heat-transfer area A. (c) If each tube has outer diameter 19 mm and active length 18 m, how many tubes are needed?

  1. End temperature differences: at the hot end dT1 = 320 − 270 = 50 C; at the cold end dT2 = 290 − 270 = 20 C. (The secondary stays at 270 C because it is boiling at constant pressure.)
  2. LMTD: LMTD = (50 − 20) / ln(50 / 20) = 30 / ln(2.5) = 30 / 0.9163 ≈ 32.7 C.
  3. Required area (part b): A = Q / (U × LMTD) = (900 × 10⁶) / (5000 × 32.7) = 9.0 × 10⁶ / 1.635 × 10⁵ → A ≈ 5500 m².
  4. Area per tube: A_tube = pi × d × L = pi × 0.019 × 18 = 1.074 m² per tube.
  5. Number of tubes (part c): N = 5500 / 1.074 ≈ 5120 tubes.
  6. Sanity check: real large U-tube SGs carry roughly 3000–16000 tubes depending on size; ~5000 tubes for a 900 MW unit is squarely in range. The thousands-of-tubes count is dictated by the modest LMTD (~33 C) and U against a huge Q.

🛠️ Hands-On: size a steam generator and model its tube bundle

This course builds reactor hardware in free CAD/FEA tools — FreeCAD for the geometry, Salome-Meca (with the code_aster solver) for thermal/structural finite-element analysis, and Python + NumPy to script and post-process. If you set up the reactor-env environment back in Week 2 (the install panel lives in Lecture 2), you already have everything you need here — no new tools to install.

Let's turn this lecture's energy balance, LMTD, and tube-count math into code, then sketch the tube bundle in FreeCAD.

# steam_generator.py — Lecture 4 (run in plain Python; CAD block needs FreeCAD)
import numpy as np

Q      = 900e6      # thermal power to transfer, W
T_hot  = 320.0      # primary inlet, C
T_cold = 290.0      # primary outlet, C
T_sat  = 270.0      # secondary saturation temp, C
U      = 5000.0     # overall heat-transfer coeff, W/m^2K

# --- LMTD (constant-T_sat secondary side) ---
dT1 = T_hot  - T_sat        # hot-end gap
dT2 = T_cold - T_sat        # cold-end gap
lmtd = (dT1 - dT2) / np.log(dT1 / dT2)
print(f"LMTD       = {lmtd:.1f} C")          # -> 32.7 C

# --- required tube area and tube count ---
A = Q / (U * lmtd)
d, L = 0.019, 18.0          # tube OD (m), active length (m)
A_tube = np.pi * d * L
N = A / A_tube
print(f"Area       = {A:,.0f} m^2")          # -> ~5500 m^2
print(f"Tubes      = {N:,.0f}")              # -> ~5120

# --- secondary steam flow from latent heat ---
h_fg = 1500e3               # latent heat at ~55 bar, J/kg (approx)
m_steam = Q / h_fg
print(f"Steam flow = {m_steam:.0f} kg/s")    # -> ~600 kg/s

# --- FreeCAD: one straight tube of the bundle ---
import FreeCAD as App, Part
doc = App.newDocument("SGtube")
tube = Part.makeCylinder(d/2 * 1000, L * 1000)   # mm units in FreeCAD
Part.show(tube)
# Array this tube on a triangular pitch to build the full bundle.

Try lowering U to 3000 (representing fouled tubes) and watch the required area — and tube count — climb. That is exactly why fouling and tube plugging steal margin: less effective area means the SG can move less power.

Q1. Why does a PWR use a separate secondary loop instead of sending reactor coolant straight to the turbine?
A. To keep the radioactive primary water out of the turbine and condenser, which stay clean
B. Because water cannot be pumped at high pressure
C. To allow the primary water to boil inside the core
D. Because turbines cannot run on steam
Show answer

A. The second loop isolates the slightly radioactive primary water from the turbine island, keeping it accessible and clean. (B) High-pressure pumping is exactly what reactor coolant pumps do. (C) PWR primary water is deliberately kept from boiling; that is the BWR approach. (D) Turbines run on steam — that is their whole purpose.

Q2. What is the key advantage of a once-through steam generator over a U-tube design?
A. It holds much more secondary water for thermal buffer
B. It produces superheated steam in a single pass, improving turbine efficiency
C. It eliminates the need for any feedwater pump
D. It removes the radioactive/clean boundary
Show answer

B. An OTSG fully evaporates and superheats the secondary water in one pass, giving drier, hotter steam. (A) is backwards — the OTSG holds far less water and has less buffer. (C) Feedwater pumps are still required. (D) The tube wall remains the boundary in any SG.

Q3. A steam generator transfers 800 MW with U = 4000 W/m²K and an LMTD of 40 C. Approximately what tube area is required?
A. ~50 m²
B. ~500 m²
C. ~5000 m²
D. ~50000 m²
Show answer

C. A = Q / (U × LMTD) = 800 × 10⁶ / (4000 × 40) = 8 × 10⁴ / 1.6 × 10⁵ = 5000 m². (A) and (B) are orders of magnitude too small; (D) is ten times too large — a unit-arithmetic slip.

Q4. During a steam generator tube rupture, which way does water flow across the breach, and why?
A. Secondary into primary, because secondary water is hotter
B. No net flow, because both sides are at equal pressure
C. Secondary into primary, because the core sucks water in
D. Primary into secondary, because primary pressure (~155 bar) exceeds secondary pressure (~70 bar)
Show answer

D. The primary side is at higher pressure, so radioactive primary water is driven into the clean secondary system — the radiological hazard of an SGTR. (A) and (C) get the direction backwards; temperature does not drive the flow, pressure does. (B) is false — the loops operate at very different pressures.

Q5. In the secondary loop, what is the primary purpose of the condenser?
A. To boil the feedwater before it enters the steam generator
B. To condense spent turbine steam to liquid and maintain a low turbine back-pressure
C. To remove radioactivity from the steam
D. To pressurize the primary loop
Show answer

B. The condenser collapses the exhaust steam to liquid, creating the vacuum (low back-pressure) that lets the turbine extract maximum work, and supplies condensate for feedwater. (A) Boiling happens in the steam generator, not the condenser. (C) Secondary steam is already clean if tubes are intact. (D) The pressurizer, on the primary loop, does that — not the condenser.
