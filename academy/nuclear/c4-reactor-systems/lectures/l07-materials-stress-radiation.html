Materials, Stress & Radiation Damage

The metals that hold a reactor together are quietly under attack — hammered atom-by-atom by neutrons that harden, embrittle, and swell them, slowly turning a tough steel into something that can crack on a cold day.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the principal reactor structural materials — zirconium alloys and reactor-grade steels — and why each is chosen.
  • Relate stress and strain through the elastic modulus, and distinguish elastic, plastic, and creep deformation.
  • Explain how neutron irradiation produces displacement damage and quantify it with the displacements-per-atom (dpa) metric.
  • Describe irradiation hardening, embrittlement (ductile-to-brittle shift), and void swelling as lifetime-limiting mechanisms.
  • Estimate a component lifetime from a dose rate and an allowable dpa limit.

7.1The Materials Palette: Zircaloy & Reactor Steels

A reactor is built from a surprisingly short list of metals, each chosen against a brutal combination of constraints: it must keep its strength near 300 °C, resist corrosion in hot pressurised water for decades, and — uniquely — it must not waste neutrons by absorbing them. That last requirement quietly rules out most of the periodic table.

For fuel cladding the answer is zirconium alloys (commercially "Zircaloy"). Zirconium's defining virtue is an extremely small neutron-absorption cross-section, so the thin tubes around every fuel pellet (Week 2) barely steal neutrons from the chain reaction, while still being strong and corrosion-resistant in hot water. Its weakness is a high-temperature steam reaction that produces hydrogen — a key accident concern you will revisit.

For the heavy, load-bearing structures — the reactor pressure vessel, the primary piping, the steam-generator shells — the answer is low-alloy and stainless reactor steels. Here neutron economy matters less (these parts sit outside or at the edge of the core), so engineers choose steel for its strength, toughness, weldability, and well-understood behaviour. The pressure vessel in particular is a thick low-alloy steel forging, sometimes clad on the inside with stainless steel for corrosion resistance.

AnalogyChoosing reactor materials is like packing for an expedition where every gram of pack weight is "neutron cost." Near the core, where neutrons are precious, you pick the lightest-absorbing gear you can (zirconium), even if it is fussy. Far from the core you can afford the heavy, dependable steel boots — they cost neutrons you no longer need to hoard.

7.2Stress, Strain, Creep: How Metals Carry Load

Before radiation enters the picture, a reactor component is just a structure carrying load, and it obeys the ordinary mechanics of materials. Pull on a bar of cross-section A with a force F and it feels a stress σ = F/A; it responds by stretching a fractional amount called strain ε = ΔL/L. For small loads the two are proportional through Hooke's law, with the constant of proportionality the material's elastic modulus E (Young's modulus):

sigma = E * epsilonsigma = stress (Pa), epsilon = strain (dimensionless), E = Young's modulus (Pa). Steel E ~ 200 GPa; zirconium ~ 95 GPa.

This elastic regime is reversible — release the load and the metal springs back. Push past the yield strength and deformation becomes plastic: the metal stretches permanently. The yield strength is what the safety factor in last week's vessel sizing protected against. But there is a third, sneakier mode that matters intensely in reactors: creep, the slow, time-dependent deformation of a metal held under load at high temperature — even at a stress well below yield.

Creep matters because a reactor component sits at temperature and load not for a test, but for decades. A fuel cladding tube, internally loaded by fission-gas pressure and externally squeezed by coolant, slowly creeps over years; a bolt slowly relaxes. The governing intuition is that creep rate climbs steeply with both stress and temperature, so a modest increase in either can dramatically shorten component life.

Common mistakeDo not assume "below yield means safe forever." A stress comfortably under the yield strength can still cause failure by creep if it acts for long enough at high temperature, or by fatigue if it cycles. Static yield is only one of several limits; time and temperature (creep) and cycling (fatigue) are independent failure paths a reactor designer must check separately.

7.3Radiation Damage & the dpa Metric

What makes reactor materials special is that they are bombarded. A fast neutron does not just pass through steel — it can slam into an atom hard enough to knock it clean out of its lattice site, and that recoiling atom in turn knocks out dozens more, a tiny cascade of displaced atoms. Each displaced atom leaves behind a vacancy (an empty site) and lodges somewhere as an interstitial. These point defects are the seeds of every radiation-damage effect.

To compare doses across reactors and decades, engineers use a single normalising metric: displacements per atom (dpa) — the average number of times each atom in the material has been knocked from its site over the exposure. One dpa means every atom has, on average, been displaced once; the pressure vessel steel of a power reactor accumulates on the order of a fraction of a dpa to a few dpa over its life, while fast-reactor core materials can see hundreds.

dpa = phi * sigma_d * tphi = fast neutron flux (n/cm^2/s), sigma_d = displacement cross-section (cm^2), t = exposure time (s). Multiply flux by displacement cross-section to get a displacement rate, then by time.

The power of dpa is that it separates the cause (accumulated atomic displacement) from the material: two steels at the same dpa have suffered comparable atomic disruption, even if the neutron spectra differed. It is the irradiation analogue of "mileage" on a car — a single number that lets you predict wear and schedule replacement.

AnalogyImagine a crowd standing in neat rows (the crystal lattice). A neutron is a runner sprinting through, knocking people out of position; each shoved person stumbles into others. After the dust settles, the rows are messier. dpa is simply "how many times, on average, has each person in the crowd been bumped out of their spot." A few bumps and the crowd self-heals; thousands of bumps and the orderly rows are gone for good.

7.4Hardening, Embrittlement & Swelling: The Lifetime Limits

Those accumulated defects do not stay invisible — they cluster and rearrange, and three consequences set how long a component can serve.

Irradiation hardening & embrittlement. Defect clusters pin the dislocations that let a metal deform gracefully. The steel gets stronger (higher yield) but, fatally, less ductile — it loses its ability to bend rather than break. The clearest signature is a rise in the ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT): the temperature below which the steel fractures in a brittle, glass-like way instead of tearing. Irradiation shifts the DBTT upward, so a vessel that was tough at room temperature when new can become brittle at that same temperature after years of dose. This is why operators must avoid pressurised thermal shock — never slam cold water into a hot, irradiated vessel.

Void swelling. At high dose the surplus vacancies can agglomerate into microscopic voids, and the material literally grows — a few percent volume increase at high dpa. Swelling distorts close-fitting core components and is a leading life-limiter in fast-reactor structures.

DBTT_irradiated = DBTT_0 + Delta_T(dpa, fluence)The transition temperature shifts UP with dose. A positive shift means the steel turns brittle at a higher (warmer) temperature than when new.

Together these mean a reactor's allowable stresses and operating rules are not fixed — they evolve as the metal ages under irradiation. The yield strength used in last week's vessel sizing creeps up, the toughness creeps down, and the safe-operating envelope narrows. Plant life extension is, at heart, the engineering argument that the embrittled, swollen, hardened metal still has enough margin left.

Common mistakeIt is tempting to think irradiation "strengthens" steel and is therefore good. The hardening is real but it comes with embrittlement — higher strength, lower toughness. A harder, more brittle vessel is more dangerous, not safer, because it can crack suddenly under a thermal shock instead of yielding gracefully. Always track toughness and DBTT, not just strength.

✦ Worked Example — does a pressure vessel survive 60 years of dose?

A pressure-vessel steel sees a fast-neutron flux of φ = 1.0×10⁹ n/cm²/s at the inner wall, with a displacement cross-section of σd = 1.5×10−²¹ cm² (i.e. ~1500 barns of displacement). The steel is qualified to an end-of-life limit of 0.5 dpa. Will it stay under the limit over a 60-year life (assume ~80% capacity, so 1.5×10⁹ s of exposure)?

  1. Displacement rate: R = φ · σd = (1.0×10⁹)(1.5×10−²¹) = 1.5×10−¹² dpa/s.
  2. Exposure time: 60 yr × 0.80 ≈ 48 effective full-power years × 3.15×10⁷ s/yr ≈ 1.5×10⁹ s.
  3. Accumulated dpa: dpa = R · t = (1.5×10−¹²)(1.5×10⁹) = 0.23 dpa.
  4. Compare to limit: 0.23 dpa < 0.5 dpa qualified limit — passes, with roughly a factor-of-two margin remaining.
  5. Interpret: the wall is fine on dpa, but you must still confirm the embrittlement that 0.23 dpa causes (the DBTT shift) keeps the vessel tough at its lowest credible operating temperature. dpa is the dose; the DBTT shift is the consequence you actually license against.

🛠️ Hands-On: dose accumulation & an embrittlement check in Python

This course's free toolchain is FreeCAD (parametric CAD for vessel and core geometry), Salome-Meca (open-source finite-element analysis), and the Python + NumPy scientific stack for quick calculations. You set all three up in the Week 1 lecture (the install <details> there covers the one-time setup); we reuse that same environment here — no new tools needed.

Below we turn this lecture's physics into code: accumulate dpa from a flux, compare against an allowable limit, and apply a simple embrittlement-shift model to see how the toughness margin narrows with dose.

# radiation_damage.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np

# --- dose accumulation: dpa = phi * sigma_d * t ---
phi     = 1.0e9        # fast neutron flux, n/cm^2/s
sigma_d = 1.5e-21      # displacement cross-section, cm^2
years   = 60
cf      = 0.80         # capacity factor
t       = years * cf * 3.15e7          # effective full-power seconds

rate = phi * sigma_d                    # dpa per second
dpa  = rate * t
print(f"displacement rate : {rate:.2e} dpa/s")
print(f"accumulated dpa   : {dpa:.3f} dpa over {years} yr")
print("dpa check         :", "PASS" if dpa < 0.5 else "FAIL", "(limit 0.5 dpa)")

# --- simple embrittlement: DBTT shifts UP with dpa ---
dbtt0   = -20.0        # as-built transition temp, deg C
k       = 250.0        # shift coefficient, deg C per dpa (illustrative)
dbtt    = dbtt0 + k * dpa
print(f"DBTT (new)        : {dbtt0:.0f} C")
print(f"DBTT (end-of-life): {dbtt:.0f} C  -> stay above this when pressurized")

# displacement rate : 1.50e-12 dpa/s
# accumulated dpa   : 0.227 dpa over 60 yr
# DBTT (end-of-life): 37 C  -> the vessel must be warm before pressurizing

Notice the second result: a vessel that was tough down to −20 °C when new is only guaranteed tough above ≈ 37 °C after 60 years — the physics behind heat-up-before-pressurize rules. Sweep phi to see how a higher-flux location embrittles faster, then feed the end-of-life yield into a FreeCAD/Salome-Meca vessel re-check.

Q1. Why is a zirconium alloy, rather than steel, used for fuel cladding inside the core?
A. It is cheaper than steel
B. It has a very small neutron-absorption cross-section, so it barely steals neutrons
C. It cannot corrode under any conditions
D. It is the strongest metal known
Show answer

B. Zirconium's low neutron absorption preserves neutron economy where neutrons are precious — inside the core. (A) it is not chosen on cost; (C) it does react with high-temperature steam, producing hydrogen; (D) it is not the strongest metal — steel is used where strength, not neutron economy, dominates.

Q2. A steel bar held at high temperature under a stress well below its yield strength slowly elongates over years. What is this called?
A. Elastic strain
B. Plastic yielding
C. Creep
D. Fracture
Show answer

C. Time-dependent deformation under sustained load at high temperature, even below yield, is creep. (A) elastic strain is instantaneous and reversible; (B) plastic yielding requires exceeding yield; (D) fracture is final separation, not slow elongation.

Q3. The metric "displacements per atom" (dpa) measures what?
A. The number of fissions per second in the fuel
B. The electrical power output of the plant
C. The corrosion rate of the cladding in micrometres per year
D. The average number of times each atom has been knocked from its lattice site
Show answer

D. dpa is the accumulated atomic displacement dose — a normalising "mileage" for irradiation. (A) describes fission rate; (B) is plant power; (C) is a chemical corrosion rate, a different damage mechanism.

Q4. As a pressure-vessel steel accumulates neutron dose, its ductile-to-brittle transition temperature (DBTT)…
A. shifts upward, so the steel turns brittle at a higher temperature than when new
B. shifts downward, making the steel tougher
C. stays exactly constant for the plant's life
D. disappears entirely
Show answer

A. Irradiation hardens and embrittles the steel, raising the DBTT — it becomes brittle at a warmer temperature, which is why cold pressurised thermal shock must be avoided. (B) reverses the effect; (C) ignores aging; (D) the transition does not vanish.

Q5. A component sees a displacement rate of 2×10−¹² dpa/s for 1×10⁷ s. Roughly what dose does it accumulate?
A. 0.002 dpa
B. 0.02 dpa
C. 0.2 dpa
D. 2 dpa
Show answer

B. dpa = rate × t = (2×10−¹²)(1×10⁷) = 2×10−⁵ × 10⁷ = 2×10−² = 0.02 dpa. (A), (C), (D) are power-of-ten slips — line up the exponents: −12 + 7 = −5, times the leading 2 gives 2×10−⁵, i.e. 0.02 dpa.
