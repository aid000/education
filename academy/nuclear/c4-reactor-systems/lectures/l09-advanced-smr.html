Advanced & Small Modular Reactors

A new generation of reactors trades the giant one-off plant for something you can build in a factory, truck to site, and trust to cool itself with nothing but gravity and physics when the power goes out.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Place the Generation IV concepts in context and name the leading coolant families: gas, sodium, and molten salt.
  • Explain the appeal of fast reactors — breeding fissile fuel and burning long-lived waste — versus thermal designs.
  • Define a small modular reactor (SMR) and a microreactor and explain the economic logic of factory build.
  • Distinguish active from passive safety and explain why a small core can be cooled by natural circulation alone.
  • Estimate decay-heat removal by natural convection and judge when a core is "walk-away safe."

9.1Generation IV: The Advanced Coolant Families

The water-cooled reactors of the previous eight weeks are the workhorses of today's fleet, but they are not the only way to build a reactor. The Generation IV program is a deliberate rethink, aiming for higher temperatures (more efficiency and industrial heat), better fuel utilisation, and safety that leans on physics rather than pumps. Almost every advanced concept is defined first by its coolant, because the coolant sets the temperature, the pressure, and the neutron spectrum.

Three families dominate. Gas-cooled reactors use helium, which is chemically inert and can run very hot (800 °C+), unlocking high-efficiency cycles and process heat — at the cost of poor heat capacity, so they need large surfaces and robust fuel. Liquid-metal-cooled reactors, usually sodium, stay liquid over a huge temperature range at near-atmospheric pressure (no thick pressure vessel needed) and transfer heat superbly — but sodium burns in air and reacts with water, demanding an intermediate loop. Molten-salt reactors dissolve the fuel itself in a flowing fluoride or chloride salt, running hot and at low pressure, with the unusual option of online refuelling and a fuel that simply freezes solid if it leaks.

AnalogyChoosing a coolant is like choosing what to cook with. Water (today's reactors) is cheap and familiar but boils at a low temperature unless you pressurise it hard — like cooking in a sealed pressure cooker. Helium is a fan oven: clean and can go very hot, but it carries little heat per puff. Liquid sodium is a cast-iron skillet — superb at moving heat, stays usable over a huge range, but you must never let water near it.

9.2Fast Reactors: Breeding & Burning

Most of today's reactors are thermal: they slow neutrons down with a moderator so they fission U-235 efficiently. A fast reactor deliberately leaves the neutrons fast (no moderator), which changes the physics in two powerful ways. First, fast neutrons can breed — they convert abundant, non-fissile U-238 into fissile plutonium-239, so a fast reactor can create more fuel than it consumes, stretching the uranium resource by a factor of fifty or more. Second, fast neutrons can fission the long-lived actinides in spent fuel that thermal neutrons merely capture, shrinking the waste that must be stored for millennia into shorter-lived products.

The key bookkeeping number is the conversion (or breeding) ratio — fissile atoms produced per fissile atom destroyed:

CR = (fissile produced) / (fissile destroyed)CR > 1 is a breeder (net fuel gain); CR = 1 is a converter that sustains itself; CR < 1 is a burner (net fuel consumption, like today's LWRs).

A breeding ratio above 1 is the holy grail of fuel sustainability, but it comes with hard engineering: fast reactors need a high power density and a coolant that does not moderate neutrons (hence sodium or lead, never water), and the high-energy spectrum drives the very void swelling and dpa damage you studied in Week 7 to hundreds of dpa over a core's life. Fast reactors are a bargain in fuel and waste paid for in materials and complexity.

Common mistakeDo not conflate "fast" with "more dangerous" or "fast neutrons move the reactor faster." The word fast refers only to neutron energy (they are not slowed by a moderator), not to how quickly the reactor responds or how risky it is. A well-designed fast reactor can be extraordinarily safe; the term is purely about the neutron spectrum.

9.3Small Modular Reactors & Microreactors

The other revolution is about size and how you build it, not coolant. A small modular reactor (SMR) is defined as a reactor of roughly 300 MWe or less designed to be built in a factory as repeated modules and shipped to site, rather than constructed as a one-off civil-engineering megaproject. A microreactor pushes further — a few to tens of MW, small enough to fit on a truck or in shipping containers, aimed at remote sites, industry, and disaster relief.

The logic is economic and runs against a century of "bigger is cheaper." A traditional gigawatt plant exploits economies of scale: cost grows more slowly than power, so a big reactor is cheaper per kilowatt. SMRs bet on the opposite lever — economies of series production: a factory churning out identical modules learns, standardises, and drives unit cost down with volume, while shorter on-site construction slashes the financing risk that sinks big projects.

Cost ~ C0 * (P / P0)^n, n ~ 0.6The classic scale law: a scaling exponent n below 1 means cost per kW falls as power rises. SMRs trade this away in exchange for factory learning and lower financial risk.

The trade-off is explicit: an SMR gives up some scale efficiency to gain factory quality, faster build, deployment flexibility, and — crucially — the passive safety that only a small core makes possible, which is the subject of the next section.

AnalogyA traditional reactor is a cathedral — built once, on site, by armies of craftsmen over a decade, magnificent but ruinously expensive and never quite repeatable. An SMR is an airliner: still complex, but built on a production line where the hundredth unit is far cheaper and more reliable than the first. The bet is that a fleet of good-enough airliners beats a single perfect cathedral.

9.4Passive Safety: Letting Physics Do the Cooling

Even after a reactor shuts down, the fuel keeps making heat — the decay heat from fission-product radioactivity, which starts at a few percent of full power and decays over hours and days. In a large plant, removing this heat after a shutdown relies on active systems: pumps, diesel generators, operator action. The lesson of past accidents is that those active systems can fail when you need them most.

Passive safety turns the problem over to physics that cannot fail to show up: gravity, natural convection, and stored coolant. When hot fluid rises and cool fluid sinks, a loop circulates with no pump at all — natural circulation. The driving pressure comes from the density difference between the hot and cold legs over the height between heat source and heat sink:

delta_P = (rho_cold - rho_hot) * g * Hdelta_P = buoyant driving head (Pa), rho = coolant densities, g = gravity, H = height between core and heat sink. Taller and hotter -> stronger natural flow.

This is exactly why small cores enable passive safety: a low-power core makes little decay heat, and its modest size means natural circulation and conduction can carry that heat away to the surrounding water or air without any operator action. Some microreactors are small enough to shed decay heat by conduction to the ground alone. A reactor that will cool itself safely with the power off and nobody in the room is called "walk-away safe" — the defining ambition of advanced designs.

Common mistake"Passive" does not mean "no decay heat to remove" — the heat is still there and must still go somewhere. Passive means the mechanism that removes it (gravity, buoyancy, conduction) needs no power, pumps, or human action. A common error is to assume a shut-down reactor is instantly cold; it is not — passive systems exist precisely because decay heat persists for days.

✦ Worked Example — can natural circulation cool an SMR's decay heat?

An SMR runs at Pth = 200 MWth. Just after shutdown the decay heat is about 6% of full power. The passive loop has height H = 12 m between core and heat sink, coolant cp = 5500 J/kg/K, and is allowed a temperature rise of ΔT = 30 K. The hot/cold density difference is Δρ = 40 kg/m³. Find the decay-heat load and the mass flow natural circulation must supply.

  1. Decay heat at shutdown: Q = 0.06 × 200 MW = 12 MWth to remove (it falls further over the following hours).
  2. Required flow: from Q = m_dot·cp·ΔT, m_dot = Q / (cp·ΔT) = 12×10⁶ / (5500 × 30) = 12e6 / 165000 ≈ 73 kg/s.
  3. Buoyant driving head: ΔP = Δρ·g·H = 40 × 9.81 × 12 ≈ 4.7 kPa of pressure, generated for free by the loop geometry.
  4. Judge feasibility: a 73 kg/s flow driven by ~4.7 kPa is achievable in a well-designed wide-bore loop — so this small core can shed its decay heat passively. A 3000 MWth plant would need ~180 MW removed and far more flow than buoyancy alone can drive, which is exactly why small cores unlock walk-away safety.
  5. Takeaway: passive cooling scales against size. Halve the power and you roughly halve the decay-heat load while keeping the same buoyant head — the smaller the core, the easier physics alone can cool it.

🛠️ Hands-On: decay-heat & natural-circulation sizing in Python

This course's free toolchain is FreeCAD (parametric CAD), Salome-Meca (open-source FEA), and the Python + NumPy stack for quick calculations — all installed in the Week 1 setup (the install <details> there covers it). We reuse that same environment here; no new tools needed.

Below we estimate the decay-heat curve, compute the natural-circulation flow needed to remove it, and check the buoyant driving head — the core of a passive-safety argument.

# passive_cooling.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

P_th = 200e6        # full thermal power, W
cp   = 5500.0       # coolant specific heat, J/kg/K
dT   = 30.0         # allowed loop temperature rise, K
H    = 12.0         # core-to-sink height, m
drho = 40.0         # hot/cold density difference, kg/m^3
g    = 9.81

# decay-heat fraction ~ 0.066 * t^-0.2 (rough Wigner-Way form), t in s
def decay_frac(t):  return 0.066 * t**-0.2

for t in [1, 10, 100, 3600, 86400]:
    Q    = decay_frac(t) * P_th
    mdot = Q / (cp * dT)
    print(f"t={t:6d}s  decay={Q/1e6:5.1f} MW  flow={mdot:5.1f} kg/s")

dP = drho * g * H                       # buoyant driving head, Pa
print(f"buoyant head : {dP/1e3:.1f} kPa (pump-free)")

# t=     1s  decay= 13.2 MW  flow= 80.0 kg/s
# t=  3600s  decay=  6.3 MW  flow= 38.3 kg/s
# t= 86400s  decay=  3.4 MW  flow= 20.4 kg/s
# buoyant head : 4.7 kPa (pump-free)

Note how the required flow falls with time as decay heat decays — the hardest moment is right after shutdown. Drop P_th to a microreactor's few MW and the flow becomes trivial, which is why the smallest cores can be cooled by conduction alone. Then sketch the loop in FreeCAD and run a buoyancy-driven thermal study in Salome-Meca.

Q1. Why are advanced reactors usually classified first by their coolant (gas, sodium, molten salt)?
A. The coolant determines the colour of the reactor building
B. The coolant sets the operating temperature, pressure, and neutron spectrum
C. Coolant choice has no effect on the reactor design
D. Regulators require coolant to be named first
Show answer

B. The coolant fixes temperature, pressure, and whether neutrons are moderated — the choices that cascade into the whole design. (A) is irrelevant; (C) is the opposite of the truth; (D) is not the reason for the classification.

Q2. In a fast reactor, what does the word "fast" refer to?
A. How quickly the reactor can change power
B. How fast the coolant pumps run
C. The high energy (speed) of the neutrons, which are not slowed by a moderator
D. The construction schedule
Show answer

C. "Fast" describes the neutron energy spectrum — fast reactors omit a moderator so neutrons stay energetic. (A), (B), (D) all misread the term; it has nothing to do with response time, pumps, or build time.

Q3. A reactor with a breeding (conversion) ratio greater than 1…
A. produces more fissile material than it consumes
B. consumes more fissile material than it produces
C. cannot sustain a chain reaction
D. has stopped fissioning entirely
Show answer

A. CR > 1 means net fissile gain — a breeder, converting fertile U-238 into fissile Pu-239 faster than it burns fuel. (B) describes a burner (CR < 1); (C) and (D) are unrelated to the conversion ratio.

Q4. What is the central economic bet behind small modular reactors?
A. That bigger single reactors are always cheaper per kilowatt
B. That fuel will become free
C. That no safety systems are needed at small size
D. That factory series-production and shorter on-site build can offset the loss of economies of scale
Show answer

D. SMRs trade some scale efficiency for factory learning, standardisation, and reduced financing risk from faster construction. (A) is the traditional logic SMRs work against; (B) and (C) are false — fuel still costs money and safety is still required.

Q5. A reactor is called "walk-away safe" primarily because…
A. it produces no decay heat after shutdown
B. its decay heat is removed passively — by gravity, natural circulation, or conduction — with no pumps or operator action
C. operators can physically walk inside the core during operation
D. it never needs refuelling
Show answer

B. Walk-away safety means physics removes the decay heat with no active systems or human intervention. (A) is wrong — decay heat persists; that is precisely why passive removal matters. (C) and (D) are unrelated claims.
