The Reactor Core & Fuel Assembly Design

A power reactor's heart is a precision lattice of millions of ceramic pellets — each thinner than a fingertip — whose geometry quietly decides both how the neutrons multiply and how the heat escapes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Build the core from the bottom up: pellet → cladding → fuel rod → assembly → lattice core.
  • Explain why fuel is a sintered ceramic (UO₂) pellet inside a thin zirconium-alloy tube, and what each layer is for.
  • Define enrichment and burnup, and use them to estimate the energy a fuel load delivers.
  • Describe how grid spacers locate the rods and why the rod pitch sets the moderator-to-fuel ratio.
  • Connect assembly geometry to both neutronics (multiplication) and thermal-hydraulics (heat removal).

2.1From a Pellet to a Core: the Nested Hierarchy

A reactor core looks monolithic from the outside, but it is built from one tiny repeated unit. Start at the smallest piece. The fuel is a cylindrical ceramic pellet of uranium dioxide (UO₂), roughly the size of a pencil eraser — about 8 mm in diameter and 10 mm tall. The uranium is pressed as a powder and sintered (baked near 1700 °C) into a dense, hard ceramic. Ceramic, not metal, because UO₂ keeps its shape up to a melting point near 2800 °C and chemically locks in the radioactive fission products instead of letting them wander.

Stack a few hundred pellets into a thin sealed tube and you have a fuel rod (or fuel pin), typically about 1 cm across and 3.5–4 m long. The tube — the cladding — is a zirconium alloy chosen because it is nearly transparent to neutrons (it does not waste them) yet survives high temperature and pressure. The cladding is the first engineered barrier between the fission products and the coolant.

Now arrange a square grid of rods — a 17×17 array holds 264 fuel rods plus guide tubes — and bundle them with structural hardware into a fuel assembly. Finally, stand dozens to a couple hundred assemblies upright side by side, and that regular repeating pattern is the reactor core lattice. The whole power plant exists to extract heat from this stack of pellets.

pellet -> fuel rod -> assembly (17x17 lattice) -> core (100-200+ assemblies)Each level is a tightly toleranced repeat of the one below; the core is a lattice of lattices.
AnalogyThink of the core like a city built from a single brick. The pellet is the brick, the fuel rod is a column of bricks, the assembly is a city block of columns, and the core is the whole grid of blocks. Change the brick spacing on one block and you change traffic flow across the entire city — exactly how rod pitch ripples out to govern the whole reactor.

2.2Enrichment: Tuning the Fissile Fraction

Natural uranium is almost all uranium-238, which a slow (thermal) neutron will not split. Only about 0.72% is the fissile isotope uranium-235. That is too lean for most water-cooled reactors, because ordinary water absorbs enough neutrons that a 0.72% mixture cannot sustain a chain reaction. Enrichment is the process of raising the U-235 fraction — typically to 3–5% for commercial light-water reactors.

Enrichment is a mass fraction of the fissile isotope in the uranium:

enrichment e = mass(U-235) / mass(U-235 + U-238)Natural: 0.72%. LWR fuel: ~3-5%. Research/naval cores can go higher; weapons-grade is >90% (out of scope).

Why does this matter for the assembly designer? More U-235 per pellet means more fissions per neutron and a hotter, more reactive lattice. But enrichment is expensive and tightly regulated, so designers use the minimum enrichment that still lets the core run a full operating cycle. Within a single assembly, designers even vary enrichment rod-to-rod — lower enrichment near the edges — to flatten the power so no single rod runs too hot. Enrichment, in short, is the first knob you turn to set how reactive the core will be.

Common mistakeEnrichment is not the same as how radioactive or how "powerful" the fuel is in a bomb sense. Commercial 5%-enriched fuel cannot explode like a weapon — the geometry, enrichment, and assembly are all deliberately wrong for that. Enrichment simply sets the fissile fraction; the chain reaction is still controlled entirely by the lattice geometry, moderator, and control rods.

2.3Burnup: How Much Energy a Fuel Load Delivers

Once fuel is loaded, you want to know how much energy you can wring out of it before it must be replaced. The metric is burnup: the thermal energy extracted per unit mass of heavy metal (uranium) in the fuel. The standard unit is gigawatt-days per tonne of heavy metal:

burnup B = (thermal energy produced) / (initial mass of heavy metal) [GWd / tHM]tHM = tonne of heavy metal (the uranium charged into the core). Typical LWR discharge: 45-60 GWd/tHM.

Burnup is the fuel's "odometer." As the reactor runs, U-235 is consumed and neutron-absorbing fission products build up, so the lattice slowly loses reactivity. When an assembly can no longer hold the chain reaction efficiently, it is discharged — usually after about 4–6 years and a burnup of 45–60 GWd/tHM. Higher enrichment buys higher achievable burnup, which means fewer refueling outages and more energy per assembly. The link is direct: roughly, each 1% of U-235 fissioned corresponds to about 9.4 GWd/tHM, so 5% fuel can reach far higher burnup than the old 3% fuel.

Burnup ties the whole assembly-design problem together: enrichment sets how much fissile material you start with, geometry sets how evenly it burns, and the cladding must survive the entire accumulated burnup without failing. A good design burns the fuel deeply and evenly.

AnalogyBurnup is the fuel tank gauge of a car expressed as miles-per-tank rather than fuel remaining. Enrichment is like the octane rating — higher octane (enrichment) lets you drive farther (higher burnup) before refueling. And just as you don't want one cylinder running far hotter than the rest, you want every fuel rod to burn at a similar rate.

2.4Lattice Geometry: Where Neutronics Meets Thermal-Hydraulics

The single most consequential geometric choice in the assembly is the rod pitch — the center-to-center spacing between neighboring fuel rods. Holding the rods at exactly that pitch, all the way up a 4-metre column, is the job of the grid spacers: thin egg-crate lattices welded along the assembly at intervals. They locate every rod, resist flow-induced vibration, and often carry small vanes that swirl the coolant to mix it and improve heat transfer.

Pitch matters because the gap between rods is filled with the moderator/coolant (water, in a light-water reactor). Water both slows fast neutrons down to thermal energies (where U-235 fissions best) and carries heat away. The ratio that captures this is the moderator-to-fuel ratio, well approximated by the ratio of water volume to fuel volume in the unit cell:

Vm / Vf = (p^2 - pi*r^2) / (pi*r^2)p = rod pitch, r = fuel-pellet radius, in a square lattice unit cell. The water occupies the area left over after the rod.

Here is the design tension. Spread the rods apart (large pitch → more water): neutrons are well moderated and there is plenty of coolant for heat removal — but too much water also absorbs neutrons and wastes core volume. Pack them tight (small pitch → less water): you fit more fuel in a compact core, but neutrons are under-moderated and the narrow channels struggle to remove heat. Real LWRs are deliberately designed slightly under-moderated, so that if the water heats up and thins out, reactivity drops — a built-in negative feedback that makes the core self-stabilizing.

This is the key idea of the whole lecture: one geometric parameter — the pitch — simultaneously governs the neutronics (how well neutrons are moderated and multiplied) and the thermal-hydraulics (how much coolant flows and how hot the rods get). You cannot optimize one without the other; the assembly is where physics and heat engineering are forced to agree.

✦ Worked Example — moderator-to-fuel ratio and energy per assembly

A 17×17 assembly uses fuel pellets of radius r = 0.41 cm on a square pitch p = 1.26 cm. (a) Find the moderator-to-fuel volume ratio of the unit cell (ignore cladding for a first estimate). (b) If the assembly holds about 460 kg of uranium and is discharged at a burnup of 50 GWd/tHM, how much thermal energy did it deliver?

  1. Fuel area in the cell: A_fuel = pi r² = pi × (0.41)² = pi × 0.1681 = 0.5281 cm².
  2. Cell area: A_cell = p² = (1.26)² = 1.5876 cm².
  3. Water (moderator) area: A_water = 1.5876 − 0.5281 = 1.0595 cm².
  4. Moderator-to-fuel ratio: Vm/Vf = 1.0595 / 0.5281 ≈ 2.0. A ratio near 2 is typical of an under-moderated LWR lattice — exactly where we want the negative feedback.
  5. Energy delivered (part b): 460 kg = 0.460 tHM. Energy = burnup × mass = 50 GWd/tHM × 0.460 tHM = 23 GW·days (thermal).
  6. Put it in familiar units: 23 GWd × 24 h/day = 552 GWh = 5.52 × 10&sup5; MWh of thermal energy from a single assembly — enough to run a small town for months, all from ~460 kg of uranium.

Verify: run python -c "import numpy; print('ok')" and launch FreeCAD once — its Python console printing FreeCAD means you're set for the whole course.

Let's script the unit-cell geometry and confirm the moderator-to-fuel ratio from the worked example, then sketch the rod in FreeCAD.

# unit_cell.py — Lecture 2 (run in FreeCAD's Python console or plain Python for the math)
import numpy as np

r_fuel = 0.41      # pellet radius, cm
pitch  = 1.26      # rod center-to-center spacing, cm

A_fuel  = np.pi * r_fuel**2          # fuel cross-section
A_cell  = pitch**2                   # square unit cell
A_water = A_cell - A_fuel            # leftover = moderator
ratio   = A_water / A_fuel

print(f"Vm/Vf = {ratio:.2f}")       # -> 2.01  (under-moderated LWR)

# --- FreeCAD geometry: extrude a 4 m fuel rod ---
import FreeCAD as App, Part
doc = App.newDocument("FuelRod")
clad_or = 0.475                      # cladding outer radius, cm
H = 400.0                            # active length, cm
clad   = Part.makeCylinder(clad_or, H)
pellet = Part.makeCylinder(r_fuel,  H)
Part.show(clad,   "Cladding")
Part.show(pellet, "FuelStack")
print("rod volume (cm^3):", round(clad.Volume, 1))

The math reproduces Vm/Vf ≈ 2.0 from the worked example. Try sweeping pitch from 1.1 to 1.5 cm and plotting the ratio — you'll see directly how tightening the lattice under-moderates the core. The FreeCAD block builds a real solid rod you can later mesh in Salome-Meca for a thermal run.

Q1. Why is reactor fuel made as a sintered UO₂ ceramic pellet rather than as metallic uranium?
A. Ceramic is cheaper than metal to manufacture
B. The high-melting-point ceramic keeps its shape and chemically retains fission products at reactor temperatures
C. Ceramic conducts neutrons better than metal
D. Ceramic is radioactive while metal uranium is not
Show answer

B. UO₂ has a ~2800 °C melting point and locks fission products into its lattice, the first containment barrier. (A) ceramic is not chosen for cost. (C) "conducting neutrons" is not a thing, and UO₂ actually has poor thermal conductivity. (D) both forms of uranium are radioactive.

Q2. Natural uranium is about 0.72% U-235. Commercial light-water reactor fuel is typically enriched to roughly…
A. 0.72% (unchanged)
B. over 90%
C. 3–5%
D. exactly 50%
Show answer

C. LWR fuel is enriched to about 3–5% U-235 — enough to sustain a chain reaction in a water lattice. (A) 0.72% is too lean for most LWRs. (B) >90% is weapons-grade, deliberately not used in commercial power fuel. (D) 50% has no basis.

Q3. Burnup (e.g. 50 GWd/tHM) is best described as…
A. the temperature at which the fuel melts
B. the rod center-to-center spacing in the lattice
C. the enrichment fraction of the fresh fuel
D. the thermal energy extracted per unit mass of heavy metal in the fuel
Show answer

D. Burnup measures energy produced per tonne of heavy metal (GWd/tHM) — the fuel's "odometer." (A) is melting point, (B) is pitch, (C) is enrichment — all different quantities, though enrichment and burnup are linked (more U-235 enables more burnup).

Q4. In a square unit cell, the moderator-to-fuel volume ratio is Vm/Vf = (p² − πr²)/(πr²). If you decrease the rod pitch p (pack the rods tighter), the lattice becomes…
A. more under-moderated, with less coolant per rod
B. more over-moderated, with more coolant per rod
C. unaffected, since pitch does not appear in the ratio
D. unable to reach criticality under any circumstances
Show answer

A. Smaller p shrinks the water area (p² − πr²), lowering Vm/Vf — less moderation and less coolant per rod. (B) is the opposite direction. (C) is wrong: p² is explicitly in the formula. (D) is overstated — tighter lattices still operate; they just shift along the moderation curve.

Q5. Why are commercial LWR lattices deliberately designed to be slightly under-moderated?
A. To make the fuel cheaper by using less uranium
B. So that if the coolant heats and thins, reactivity drops — a self-stabilizing negative feedback
C. To eliminate the need for control rods entirely
D. Because under-moderation maximizes reactivity at all temperatures
Show answer

B. On the under-moderated side, heating the water reduces its density and moderation, so k falls — passive negative feedback that helps the core self-stabilize. (A) confuses moderation with fuel cost. (C) control rods are still required for startup, shutdown, and shaping power. (D) is backwards — peak reactivity is at the optimum, not under-moderated.
