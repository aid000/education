Capstone: A Reactor System Design Study

Everything from ten weeks — core, primary loop, secondary loop, safety, and a single stressed bolt — finally clicks together into one plant you can defend on a single sheet of paper.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Size a reactor core from a target electrical output, working backward through thermal efficiency, power density, and fuel inventory.
  • Connect the core to a primary coolant loop and a secondary steam loop into one self-consistent plant heat balance.
  • Perform a first-order stress check on a single pressure-bearing component and decide whether it meets an allowable-stress margin.
  • Assemble the design into a one-page concept with key parameters and a defensible, defense-in-depth safety rationale.
  • Frame and deliver the capstone: what reviewers expect, how to present trade-offs, and how to justify every number.

10.1From Target Power to Core: Sizing the Heart of the Plant

A reactor design never starts with the core. It starts with a requirement — "deliver 300 MW of electricity to the grid" — and works backward through the plant until it arrives at a lump of fuel. This is the single most important habit of a systems engineer: the customer buys electricity, not neutrons, so every choice traces back to that output number.

Electrical output Pe is the thermal power Pth the core produces, multiplied by the thermal efficiency η of the power-conversion cycle. A light-water plant runs a Rankine steam cycle limited by coolant temperature to roughly η ≈ 0.33; a high-temperature gas system can reach η ≈ 0.45. Invert that relationship and the required core power falls out immediately:

P_th = P_e / etaP_e = electrical output (MW), eta = net cycle efficiency (dimensionless), P_th = reactor thermal power (MWth).

Once you know Pth, the core's size follows from how hard you are willing to push each cubic metre of fuel — the average power density q''' (MW per m³). Push too hard and the fuel centreline overheats; push too gently and the vessel becomes uneconomically large. The active core volume is simply the thermal power divided by the power density:

V_core = P_th / q'''V_core = active fuel volume (m^3), q''' = average volumetric power density (MW/m^3).

From the volume you pick an aspect ratio (height-to-diameter near 1 minimises neutron leakage, a result you proved back in the criticality weeks), count the fuel assemblies, and finally fix the fissile inventory from the enrichment and the burnup target. Notice the chain: a single grid requirement has cascaded all the way down to kilograms of uranium-235. That cascade is systems engineering.

AnalogyDesigning a plant from its power target is like buying a car by deciding the trip first. You don't start at the engine — you say "I need to haul 1 tonne up a 6 % grade at 100 km/h," and only that tells you the horsepower, which tells you the displacement, which tells you the fuel tank. Reverse the order and you end up with an engine in search of a purpose.

10.2Closing the Loop: Primary & Secondary Heat Balance

The core makes heat; the rest of the plant's job is to move that heat to a turbine without ever letting the fuel melt or the radioactive coolant escape. In a typical pressurised water plant this is done with two loops. The primary loop circulates pressurised water through the core (kept liquid at high temperature by ~15 MPa pressure) and dumps its heat into a steam generator. The secondary loop boils clean, non-radioactive water on the other side of that steam generator and drives the turbine. The two never mix — that physical separation is itself a safety barrier.

Sizing the primary loop is a single conservation-of-energy statement. The heat the core deposits must equal the heat the coolant carries away, which is the mass flow rate times the specific heat times the temperature rise across the core:

P_th = m_dot * c_p * (T_hot - T_cold)m_dot = coolant mass flow (kg/s), c_p = specific heat (J/kg/K), (T_hot - T_cold) = core temperature rise (K).

This one equation sets the pump size. Fix the temperature rise (limited by how hot you dare run the fuel and how cold the steam generator returns the water), and the required flow rate — and therefore the coolant-pump power — drops straight out. The secondary loop is then governed by the same logic in reverse: the steam generator must transfer that same Pth into the steam, and the turbine converts the fraction η into shaft work while a condenser rejects the remainder, (1 − η)Pth, to the ultimate heat sink (a river, the sea, or a cooling tower).

The whole plant must balance: every watt is accounted for. A design that "loses" energy somewhere in the heat balance is simply wrong, and a reviewer will find it in thirty seconds. Drawing the full balance — in, through, and out — is the single most powerful check you have on a system concept.

Common mistakeStudents routinely confuse electrical and thermal megawatts. A 300 MWe plant is not a 300 MWth core — at η = 0.33 the core makes roughly 909 MWth, and about 609 MWth (two thirds of the total!) is dumped to the environment as waste heat, never electricity. Sizing your steam generator or condenser to the electrical number undersizes it by a factor of three. Always carry the "e" or "th" subscript.

10.3A Single Stressed Component: First-Order CAD & Stress Check

A plant concept is not credible until at least one part is shown to physically survive. You will not analyse the whole vessel for the capstone — that is a career, not a course — but you will take one pressure-bearing component and prove it holds. The classic choice is the reactor pressure vessel wall, a thin-walled cylinder under internal pressure. For a cylinder whose wall is thin compared with its radius, the dominant hoop stress (circumferential, the stress that tries to split the cylinder lengthwise) is:

sigma_hoop = p * r / tp = internal pressure (Pa), r = inner radius (m), t = wall thickness (m). Valid when t < r/10.

The hoop stress is twice the axial (lengthwise) stress, which is why pressurised pipes split along their length rather than popping their ends — the seam always fails the way the higher stress points. Design then becomes a single inequality: the working stress must stay below the material's allowable stress S, which is the yield (or ultimate) strength reduced by a safety factor n that absorbs corrosion, weld defects, irradiation embrittlement, and plain uncertainty:

sigma_hoop <= S = sigma_yield / nS = allowable stress, sigma_yield = material yield strength, n = safety factor (often 1.5 to 3 for nuclear pressure boundaries).

In a free CAD tool you draw the cylinder, then in a free finite-element solver you apply the pressure load and read back the peak stress — which near the smooth wall should match the hand formula, while near nozzles and welds it spikes (a stress concentration) and tells you exactly where to add material. The hand calculation is the sanity check on the software; the software finds the corners the hand formula cannot. You always do both.

AnalogyHoop stress is why an over-inflated balloon or a boiling sausage splits along a line down its side, never around its waist. The skin is pulled hardest around the circumference, so that is where it tears. A reactor vessel obeys the identical physics — just with steel instead of latex and 15 MPa instead of your lungs.

10.4The Deliverable: One Page, Every Number Defended

The capstone is due this week, and the deliverable is deliberately compact: a one-page design concept plus a short defence. Reviewers do not want a novel; they want to see that your numbers are self-consistent and that you understand why each choice is safe. A strong submission has four parts.

1. The key-parameter table. Power (MWe and MWth), efficiency, coolant and pressure, core dimensions, fissile inventory, primary flow, and the stress margin on your chosen component. Every number must be traceable to one of the equations above — a reviewer should be able to recompute any cell from the others.

2. The system sketch. The two-loop diagram of §10.2 with your numbers on it: the heat balance must close.

3. The safety rationale. This is where you earn the grade. Frame it around defense in depth — multiple independent barriers (fuel matrix, cladding, primary boundary, containment) so that no single failure releases radioactivity — and around inherent feedback: your design should have a negative temperature coefficient so that a power excursion is self-limiting, the physics you proved in the reactivity weeks. Name the worst-case event and show what stops it.

4. The honest trade-offs. Every real design sacrifices something. Higher efficiency wants higher temperature, which embrittles steel faster; higher power density shrinks the vessel but shortens the thermal margin to fuel melting. State the tension and defend where you landed.

Common mistakeThe fatal capstone error is an unbalanced or untraceable number — a core power that does not equal Pe/η, a condenser sized to MWe, or a stress margin quoted with no safety factor. Reviewers check consistency first and elegance second. A modest design whose numbers all close beats a clever design that silently violates conservation of energy.
✦ Worked Example — sizing a 300 MWe plant end to end

Design target: 300 MWe net, pressurised-water plant. Use η = 0.33, average power density q''' = 50 MW/m³, coolant cp = 5500 J/kg/K, core temperature rise (Thot − Tcold) = 35 K. The vessel is a thin cylinder, inner radius r = 2.0 m, wall t = 0.18 m, internal pressure p = 15.5 MPa, steel yield σyield = 345 MPa. Find core power, core volume, primary flow, and the vessel safety factor.

  1. Thermal power: Pth = Pe / η = 300 / 0.33 = 909 MWth. (So 609 MWth is waste heat.)
  2. Core volume: V = Pth / q''' = 909 / 50 = 18.2 m³ of active fuel.
  3. Primary flow: m_dot = Pth / [cp(Thot−Tcold)] = 909×10⁶ / (5500 × 35) = 909e6 / 192500 ≈ 4720 kg/s.
  4. Hoop stress: σhoop = p·r/t = (15.5×10⁶ × 2.0) / 0.18 = 31.0e6 / 0.18 ≈ 172 MPa. (Check thin-wall: t/r = 0.09 < 0.1 ✓.)
  5. Safety factor: n = σyield / σhoop = 345 / 172 ≈ 2.0 — comfortably above 1.5, so the wall passes on yield. (You would still check fatigue and irradiation embrittlement before signing.)

🛠️ Hands-On: a one-script plant sizer + vessel check

This course's design stack is all free: FreeCAD to draw the vessel, Salome-Meca (the open-source Code_Aster front end) for the finite-element stress run, and Python + NumPy for the system arithmetic — installed back in the Week 1/2 setup (the install <details> lives in those early lectures). Below, NumPy does the whole capstone heat-balance and stress check in one pass, so every number in your one-pager is traceable to code.

# capstone_sizer.py — Lecture 10: size a plant from its grid target
import numpy as np

# --- design inputs ---
P_e   = 300e6        # target electrical power, W
eta   = 0.33         # net cycle efficiency
q3    = 50e6         # average power density, W per m^3
cp    = 5500.0       # coolant specific heat, J/kg/K
dT    = 35.0         # core temperature rise, K

# --- vessel inputs (thin cylinder) ---
p     = 15.5e6       # internal pressure, Pa
r     = 2.0          # inner radius, m
t     = 0.18         # wall thickness, m
sy    = 345e6        # steel yield strength, Pa

# --- the requirement cascade ---
P_th  = P_e / eta              # core thermal power, W
P_rej = P_th - P_e             # waste heat to the sink, W
V     = P_th / q3              # active core volume, m^3
mdot  = P_th / (cp * dT)       # primary coolant flow, kg/s

# --- vessel stress check ---
sigma = p * r / t              # hoop stress, Pa
n     = sy / sigma             # safety factor on yield

print(f"Thermal power : {P_th/1e6:6.1f} MWth")
print(f"Waste heat    : {P_rej/1e6:6.1f} MWth to sink")
print(f"Core volume   : {V:6.1f} m^3")
print(f"Primary flow  : {mdot:6.0f} kg/s")
print(f"Hoop stress   : {sigma/1e6:6.1f} MPa")
print(f"Safety factor : {n:6.2f}  ->", "PASS" if n >= 1.5 else "FAIL")

# Thermal power :  909.1 MWth
# Waste heat    :  609.1 MWth to sink
# Core volume   :   18.2 m^3
# Primary flow  :   4723 kg/s
# Hoop stress   :  172.2 MPa
# Safety factor :   2.00  -> PASS

Next, build the cylinder in FreeCAD, load it at 15.5 MPa in Salome-Meca, and confirm the smooth-wall stress lands near 172 MPa — then watch it spike at a nozzle cutout. The script is the hand-check; the FEM finds the corners. Together they are your defensible vessel result.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A plant must deliver 600 MWe at a net cycle efficiency of 0.40. What thermal power must the core produce?
A. 240 MWth
B. 600 MWth
C. 1500 MWth
D. 960 MWth
Show answer

C. Pth = Pe/η = 600/0.40 = 1500 MWth. (A) multiplies instead of divides (600×0.40). (B) ignores efficiency entirely. (D) uses 1−η in the denominator, which has no physical basis.

Q2. In the heat balance Pth = m_dot·cp·(Thot−Tcold), if you must raise core power but cannot increase the coolant temperature rise, what must increase?
A. The condenser rejection temperature
B. The mass flow rate of the coolant
C. The thermal efficiency of the turbine
D. The vessel wall thickness
Show answer

B. With cp and ΔT fixed, Pth is proportional to m_dot, so a bigger pump is required. (A) sets the cold-end of the secondary cycle, not the core balance. (C) efficiency relates Pe to Pth, not the core flow. (D) wall thickness is a structural choice, unrelated to heat transport.

Q3. For a thin-walled cylindrical pressure vessel, how does the hoop (circumferential) stress compare with the axial (lengthwise) stress?
A. They are equal
B. Hoop stress is half the axial stress
C. Hoop stress is twice the axial stress
D. Axial stress is always zero
Show answer

C. σhoop = pr/t and σaxial = pr/2t, so hoop is twice axial — which is why pressurised cylinders split along their length. (A) and (B) get the ratio wrong. (D) is false; the end caps carry a real axial load.

Q4. A vessel sees a hoop stress of 200 MPa in a steel with 360 MPa yield. What is the safety factor on yield, and does it clear a 1.5 threshold?
A. 1.8 — passes
B. 0.56 — fails
C. 1.5 — exactly marginal
D. 2.4 — passes
Show answer

A. n = σyield/σworking = 360/200 = 1.8, above 1.5, so it passes. (B) inverts the ratio (200/360). (C) and (D) are arithmetic errors — 360/200 is neither 1.5 nor 2.4.

Q5. A reviewer reads your one-page capstone. Which single property of the design will they almost certainly check first?
A. Whether the color scheme of the diagram is appealing
B. Whether the fuel vendor's brand is named
C. Whether the design uses the newest possible technology
D. Whether the heat balance closes and every key number is internally consistent
Show answer

D. Self-consistency — Pth=Pe/η, the condenser sized to waste MWth, a stress margin with a stated safety factor — is the first thing a reviewer verifies. (A) is cosmetic. (B) is irrelevant and not a design property. (C) novelty never substitutes for a balance that closes.
