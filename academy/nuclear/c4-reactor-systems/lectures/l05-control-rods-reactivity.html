Control Systems: Rods, Reactivity & Poisons

A reactor's power is balanced on a knife-edge of neutron bookkeeping — and the operator's whole job is to add and subtract tiny fractions of a percent of reactivity without ever letting the balance run away.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define reactivity ρ in terms of keff and explain why operators trade in units of pcm and dollars.
  • Describe control-rod worth, differential and integral worth curves, and why rods are grouped into banks.
  • Explain how soluble boron acts as a chemical shim to compensate for fuel burnup over a cycle.
  • Show how burnable poisons hold down beginning-of-cycle reactivity and flatten the power profile.
  • Define shutdown margin and explain the xenon-135 transient and why it complicates control after a power change.

5.1Reactivity: The Currency of Reactor Control

By now you know the multiplication factor keff: the ratio of neutrons in one fission generation to the number in the generation before it. When keff = 1 the chain reaction exactly sustains itself — the reactor is critical and power holds steady. When keff > 1 power climbs; when keff < 1 it falls. Controlling a reactor means nudging keff by minuscule amounts and holding it at one for months on end.

keff itself is awkward to work with because the useful range is so close to 1.0000. Operators instead speak in reactivity, the fractional departure from criticality:

rho = (k_eff - 1) / k_effrho = 0 is critical; rho > 0 is supercritical (power rising); rho < 0 is subcritical (power falling).

Reactivity is dimensionless but the numbers are tiny, so the field uses scaled units. pcm ("per cent mille") is 10-5 of reactivity: a 100 pcm change is Δρ = 0.001. Even more revealing is the dollar, which measures reactivity in units of the delayed-neutron fraction β (about 0.0065 for uranium-235):

rho [$] = rho / betaOne dollar of reactivity = beta. Below $1 the reactor is controllable on delayed neutrons; at or above $1 it is "prompt critical" and uncontrollable.

That dollar scale is not bookkeeping vanity — it is the dividing line between a reactor you can steer and one you cannot. Recall from earlier weeks that a handful of fission products emit neutrons seconds to minutes after fission. These delayed neutrons stretch the effective generation time from microseconds to about a tenth of a second, slowing power changes enough for mechanical control. As long as the reactivity you insert stays well below $1, the reactor's response is governed by those slow delayed neutrons and an operator (or control system) has ample time to react.

AnalogyThink of reactivity as the throttle of a car whose accelerator is incredibly sensitive and whose engine has tremendous momentum. keff = 1 is cruising at constant speed. A few pcm is a feather-touch on the pedal. The delayed neutrons are like a long delay between pressing the pedal and the engine actually revving — annoying for a sports car, but a lifesaver for a reactor, because it gives the driver time to ease off before the speed runs away. Push past $1 of reactivity and the delay vanishes: now the car responds in microseconds and no human can steer it.

5.2Control Rods: Worth, Banks & the S-Curve

The fastest, most direct way to move reactivity is the control rod: a rod of strongly neutron-absorbing material — typically a silver-indium-cadmium alloy or boron carbide — that slides into a channel in the core. Where the rod sits, it swallows neutrons that would otherwise cause fission, locally driving down keff. Withdraw the rod and those neutrons are returned to the chain reaction, adding positive reactivity.

The total reactivity a fully inserted rod or bank removes is its integral worth. But the worth is not spread evenly along the rod's travel, because reactivity change is proportional to the neutron flux where the rod tip moves. Flux in a bare core is roughly cosine-shaped — peaked in the center, low at the ends — so a rod near the top or bottom of the core moves through weak flux and changes little reactivity per centimeter, while a rod crossing the high-flux midplane changes a great deal. This sensitivity is the differential rod worth:

differential worth = d(rho)/dx proportional to phi(x)^2x = rod position; phi(x) = neutron flux at the rod tip. Worth per cm is largest at the core midplane and small at the ends.

Integrate that bell-shaped differential curve over position and you get the characteristic S-shaped integral worth curve: shallow at the top of travel, steep through the middle, shallow again at the bottom. Operators read these curves constantly to know exactly how much reactivity a given rod motion will add.

A large core has dozens of rods. Moving them individually would distort the power shape, so they are wired into banks (groups) that move together, and banks are sequenced with overlap so that as one bank reaches the bottom of its high-worth region another is entering it. The aim is a smooth, nearly linear relationship between total bank position and inserted reactivity, and a power distribution that stays flat. A few rods or banks are held fully withdrawn in reserve purely for fast shutdown.

Common mistakeDo not assume a control rod's reactivity worth is constant along its stroke. Pulling a rod 10 cm out of the bottom of the core barely changes reactivity, while pulling it 10 cm through the midplane can change it many times more. Treating worth as linear leads to badly mis-estimating the reactivity of a rod motion — and in the high-worth midplane region that error can be the difference between a small adjustment and an unintended power excursion.

5.3Soluble Boron & Burnable Poisons: Slow Reactivity Control

Control rods are excellent for fast, local adjustments, but they are a poor tool for the slow, core-wide reactivity loss that happens as fuel burns. A fresh core is loaded with far more fissile material than it needs for one critical configuration — enough excess reactivity to run 12 to 18 months. If you held all that excess down with inserted rods, the rods would badly distort the power shape and waste neutrons. Two chemistry-based tools handle the slow trend instead.

Soluble boron: the chemical shim

In a pressurized-water reactor the coolant doubles as the moderator and circulates everywhere, so dissolving a neutron absorber in it spreads the absorption uniformly across the whole core without distorting the flux shape. Boric acid is exactly that absorber, and the dissolved-boron concentration is called the chemical shim. At the start of a cycle the boron concentration is high — perhaps 1500 to 2000 parts per million — holding down the large excess reactivity of fresh fuel. As the fuel burns and reactivity slowly drops, operators dilute the boron a few ppm at a time to keep keff at exactly one, with rods nearly fully withdrawn:

boron worth ~ -10 pcm per ppm (typical)Roughly linear: removing about 10 ppm of boron adds about +100 pcm of reactivity. Concentration falls from ~1800 ppm to near 0 over a cycle.

Burnable poisons

Boron alone cannot hold down all of beginning-of-cycle excess — too much dissolved boron can drive the moderator temperature coefficient positive, which is unsafe. So designers also build fixed burnable poisons — gadolinium or boron compounds blended into selected fuel rods or as discrete absorber rods. These absorb neutrons strongly when fresh, suppressing early reactivity, then burn out (transmute into weak absorbers) at roughly the same rate the fuel depletes. The net effect is to flatten the reactivity-versus-time curve and let designers shape the local power distribution, putting extra poison where the fuel is freshest and hottest.

AnalogyManaging a cycle's reactivity is like flying a long route on a single tank of fuel. The control rods are the yoke — quick, precise, for immediate maneuvers. Soluble boron is the throttle trim you ease open as the plane burns off weight and wants to climb. Burnable poisons are ballast that slowly evaporates: heavy at takeoff to counter a full fuel load, gone by the time the tanks are nearly empty. All three together keep you level for the entire flight without fighting the controls.

5.4Shutdown Margin & the Xenon Transient

Two harder ideas round out reactor control. The first is a safety requirement; the second is the most notorious dynamic poison in reactor physics.

Shutdown margin

It is not enough that the reactor can be shut down — it must be guaranteed to stay shut down even in the worst plausible case. Shutdown margin (SDM) is the amount of negative reactivity by which the core is subcritical after a full scram (all rods dropped) with the single highest-worth rod assumed stuck fully out. That "stuck-rod" assumption is the conservative heart of the definition:

SDM = |rho_subcritical| with most-reactive rod stuck outMust remain more negative than a required minimum (e.g. 1000-2000 pcm) at all times. Also accounts for cooldown adding positive reactivity.

SDM must hold across the whole cycle and must allow for the positive reactivity added when the core cools down and xenon decays away. If a planned condition would violate the minimum SDM, the plant cannot proceed to it. This is why operators continuously track rod positions and boron concentration: both feed the running SDM calculation.

The xenon-135 transient

Xenon-135 is the single strongest neutron absorber that arises naturally in an operating core, with an absorption cross-section of roughly 2.6 million barns. It is produced two ways: directly from fission (a little) and, more importantly, from the beta decay of iodine-135 (half-life ~6.7 h), which is itself a fission product. Xenon-135 is destroyed two ways: by its own beta decay (half-life ~9.2 h) and by absorbing a neutron and transmuting to xenon-136. The competition between these production and loss paths is what makes xenon so troublesome:

I-135 --(beta, ~6.7 h)--> Xe-135 --(beta, ~9.2 h)--> Cs-135 Xe-135 + n -> Xe-136 (burnup, fast only while flux is high)At steady high power, neutron burnup removes Xe-135 quickly. Drop the power and that burnup path shuts off while iodine keeps feeding xenon.

Here is the trap. When a reactor running at high power suddenly reduces power, the neutron flux drops, so the burnup path that was destroying xenon nearly shuts off. But the large inventory of iodine-135 built up at high power keeps decaying into more xenon. For several hours the xenon concentration rises even though power has fallen — a "xenon peak" that adds a large slug of negative reactivity. To stay critical the operator must withdraw rods or dilute boron to compensate. Then, hours later, as the iodine source depletes and xenon decays, the negative reactivity unwinds and the operator must reinsert reactivity. After a full shutdown the peak can be so deep that there is not enough excess reactivity to restart for many hours — the reactor is xenon-precluded (in operator slang, a "xenon dead time").

Common mistakeDo not assume xenon poisoning goes down right after you reduce power. The opposite happens: xenon first rises for several hours because the iodine-135 already in the core keeps decaying into xenon while the neutron-burnup removal path has been switched off by the lower flux. Mistaking the direction of the transient is exactly the kind of error that, combined with operator actions that defeated safety features, contributed to the most severe reactor accidents in history.
✦ Worked Example — reactivity of a rod withdrawal, in pcm and dollars

A reactor is exactly critical (keff = 1.00000). An operator withdraws a control bank from a region of its travel where the integral worth curve has a local slope of 8 pcm/cm, moving it 15 cm. Take the delayed-neutron fraction β = 0.0065. Find the inserted reactivity in pcm, in absolute ρ, and in dollars, and state whether the reactor is prompt critical.

  1. Reactivity added (pcm): 8 pcm/cm × 15 cm = 120 pcm of positive reactivity (rods out = +ρ).
  2. Convert to absolute ρ: 1 pcm = 10-5, so Δρ = 120 × 10-5 = 0.00120.
  3. Convert to dollars: ρ[$] = Δρ / β = 0.00120 / 0.0065 ≈ $0.185, or about 18.5 cents.
  4. Prompt-critical check: $0.185 is far below $1.00, so the reactor stays well within the delayed-neutron-controlled regime — the power rise is slow and easily managed. It is not prompt critical.
  5. New keff (approx): from ρ = (k−1)/k with ρ = 0.00120, k ≈ 1/(1−0.00120) ≈ 1.00120 — a tiny supercritical step, exactly the size of adjustment routine control demands.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate the xenon transient in Python

This course's design work runs on a free stack — FreeCAD and Salome-Meca for geometry and structural/thermal models, plus Python and NumPy for the physics. (See the Week 1–2 lectures for the one-time install of the full stack.) The iodine-xenon dynamics are just two coupled ordinary differential equations, so plain NumPy is all we need here — no extra tools to install.

# xenon_transient.py — Lecture 5
# Iodine-135 / Xenon-135 inventory after a step power reduction.
import numpy as np

# Decay constants (1/s)
lam_I  = np.log(2) / (6.7 * 3600.0)    # I-135 half-life 6.7 h
lam_Xe = np.log(2) / (9.2 * 3600.0)    # Xe-135 half-life 9.2 h

# Fission yields and cross sections (representative)
gamma_I  = 0.061      # I-135 yield per fission
gamma_Xe = 0.003      # direct Xe-135 yield per fission
sigma_a  = 2.6e-18    # Xe-135 absorption (cm^2 ~ 2.6e6 barns)
Sigma_f  = 0.07       # macroscopic fission cross section (1/cm)

def flux(t):
    # 100% power until t=0, then step down to 20%
    return 3.0e13 if t < 0 else 0.6e13   # n/cm^2/s

def derivs(I, Xe, phi):
    dI  = gamma_I * Sigma_f * phi - lam_I * I
    dXe = (gamma_Xe * Sigma_f * phi + lam_I * I
           - lam_Xe * Xe - sigma_a * phi * Xe)
    return dI, dXe

# Start from the steady state at full power
phi0 = 3.0e13
I  = gamma_I * Sigma_f * phi0 / lam_I
Xe = (gamma_Xe * Sigma_f * phi0 + lam_I * I) / (lam_Xe + sigma_a * phi0)

dt = 10.0                          # s
Xe0 = Xe
peak = Xe; t_peak = 0.0
for step in range(int(40 * 3600 / dt)):   # 40 hours
    t = step * dt
    phi = flux(t)
    dI, dXe = derivs(I, Xe, phi)
    I  += dI  * dt
    Xe += dXe * dt
    if Xe > peak:
        peak = Xe; t_peak = t / 3600.0

print(f"Xe peak / initial = {peak / Xe0:.2f}  at  t = {t_peak:.1f} h")
# Xe peak / initial = 1.40  at  t = 7.6 h
# Xenon RISES ~40% and peaks ~7-8 h AFTER the power cut, then decays.

The result captures the whole lesson of 5.4: after the power drop, xenon climbs for hours before it falls. Multiply the xenon excess by sigma_a and the flux to turn it into a reactivity penalty, and you have the negative-reactivity slug the operator must chase with rods or boron.

Q1. A reactor has keff = 1.0020. Approximately what is its reactivity in pcm?
A. About 200 pcm
B. About 20 pcm
C. About 2000 pcm
D. About 0.002 pcm
Show answer

A. ρ = (k−1)/k = 0.0020/1.0020 ≈ 0.001996 ≈ 200 pcm (since 1 pcm = 10-5). (B) and (C) are off by a factor of ten; (D) confuses ρ itself (0.002) with pcm.

Q2. A control rod has its greatest differential worth (most reactivity per centimeter of travel) when its tip is…
A. at the very top of the core, just entering
B. at the very bottom of the core
C. near the core midplane, where the neutron flux is highest
D. the same everywhere; rod worth is constant
Show answer

C. Differential worth tracks the local flux (roughly phi squared), which peaks at the midplane, giving the steep middle of the S-curve. (A) and (B) are the low-flux ends where worth per cm is small; (D) is the classic mistake — worth is strongly position-dependent.

Q3. What is the primary role of soluble boron (chemical shim) in a pressurized-water reactor?
A. To provide fast, localized reactivity changes for load-following
B. To compensate uniformly for the slow loss of reactivity as fuel burns over a cycle
C. To act as the primary fast-shutdown system in place of rods
D. To increase the moderating power of the coolant
Show answer

B. Dissolved boron spreads absorption uniformly and is diluted slowly to track fuel depletion over months. (A) describes control rods; (C) boron is far too slow to add/remove for fast shutdown — that is the rods' job; (D) boron absorbs neutrons, it does not improve moderation.

Q4. Shutdown margin is conservatively defined assuming which condition?
A. All control rods successfully insert on a scram
B. The boron concentration is at its cycle maximum
C. Xenon is at its peak negative-reactivity value
D. The single highest-worth control rod is stuck fully withdrawn
Show answer

D. SDM assumes the most reactive rod fails to insert — the worst single failure — and still requires the core to be subcritical by a set minimum. (A) is the non-conservative best case, not the design basis; (B) and (C) would actually add negative reactivity and make shutdown easier, so they are not the limiting assumption.

Q5. Several hours after a reactor steps down from full power to a lower power, what does the xenon-135 concentration do, and why?
A. Falls immediately, because less fission means less xenon is produced
B. Stays constant, because xenon is in equilibrium at any power
C. Rises forever, because xenon is a stable end product
D. Rises, then later falls, because neutron burnup of xenon drops while iodine-135 keeps decaying into xenon
Show answer

D. Lower flux nearly shuts off the neutron-burnup loss path, while the iodine-135 inventory built up at high power keeps decaying into xenon — so xenon rises for hours (the peak), then decays away. (A) ignores the iodine source; (B) equilibrium depends on power, so a step breaks it; (C) xenon-135 is radioactive (~9.2 h) and decays, it is not stable.
