The Primary Coolant System & Pressure Vessel

Squeeze water to 155 times atmospheric pressure and you can heat it past 300 °C without ever letting it boil — that single trick is the beating heart of every pressurized water reactor.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace the flow of coolant around the primary loop and name each major component it passes through.
  • Explain the dual role of the reactor pressure vessel as both a structural barrier and a fission-product boundary.
  • Describe how the pressurizer holds the primary system subcooled, and compute the saturation margin from steam tables.
  • Account for the duty of the reactor coolant pumps using the heat-balance and pump-power relations.
  • Justify why a PWR runs near 155 bar and ~300 °C rather than at lower pressure.

3.1The Primary Loop: A Closed Circle of Hot Water

In a pressurized water reactor (PWR) the primary coolant system is a sealed, high-pressure loop of ordinary water that does exactly two jobs: it carries fission heat out of the core, and it acts as a leak-tight boundary keeping radioactive material away from the rest of the plant. Picture a closed circle. Water enters the reactor pressure vessel at the bottom around 290 °C, flows up through the fuel assemblies absorbing roughly 3 GW of thermal power, and leaves the top around 325 °C. It then travels through a steam generator, where it hands that heat to a separate water loop, and a reactor coolant pump shoves it back to the vessel to do it all again.

A crucial fact: the primary water never boils. It stays liquid the whole way around. The boiling happens only on the far side of the steam generator, in the secondary loop (next week's topic). Keeping the primary single-phase is what makes its heat transfer predictable and its chemistry controllable — and doing that at 325 °C is precisely why the loop must be pressurized so hard.

Real PWRs split the primary into two, three, or four parallel loops, each with its own steam generator and pump, all fed from the one shared reactor vessel. Parallel loops add redundancy and divide the enormous flow — a single large plant circulates on the order of 20,000 kg of water every second.

AnalogyThe primary loop is a closed central-heating system on a giant scale. A boiler (the core) heats water, a pump pushes it through a radiator (the steam generator), and the cooled water returns to the boiler. The twist: instead of an open vent, the whole circuit is sealed and squeezed so the "boiler water" can run far hotter than 100 °C while staying liquid.

3.2The Reactor Pressure Vessel: Barrier and Boundary

The reactor pressure vessel (RPV) is the steel forging that contains the core and the pressurized water around it. In a large PWR it is a cylinder roughly 4–5 m across and 12 m tall, with walls of low-alloy steel about 20 cm thick, clad on the inside with a thin layer of stainless steel for corrosion resistance. It is the single most safety-critical and least replaceable component in the plant: every other part can in principle be swapped out, but the vessel is welded shut around the core for the reactor's whole life.

The wall thickness is set by the pressure it must hold. For a thin-walled cylinder the hoop (circumferential) stress in the wall is

sigma_hoop = p * r / tp = internal pressure, r = inner radius, t = wall thickness. The hoop stress is twice the axial stress, so it governs the design.

This is the formula that turns "155 bar" into "20 cm of steel." Push the pressure up and you must thicken the wall in proportion; that is one reason engineers do not simply raise the pressure without limit. The vessel must keep this stress comfortably below the steel's yield strength and survive decades of fast-neutron bombardment, which gradually embrittles the steel — a phenomenon you will quantify in the materials weeks.

The RPV is also the second of the three fission-product barriers. The first is the fuel cladding (Lecture 2); the vessel plus the rest of the sealed primary loop is the second; the concrete containment building is the third. Defense in depth means that even if one barrier fails, the others still keep radioactivity contained.

Common mistakeThe hoop-stress formula sigma = p·r/t uses the inner radius r and the wall thickness t — not the diameter. Students routinely plug in the diameter and get a number twice too small, or forget that pressure must be in pascals (1 bar = 10⁵ Pa) to come out in pascals of stress. Keep SI units throughout and check that your answer lands in the hundreds of MPa, not in bar.

3.3The Pressurizer: Keeping the Water Subcooled

How do you hold an entire loop of 300 °C water at a precise pressure when it is constantly heating and cooling, expanding and contracting? You give it a cushion. The pressurizer is a tall tank, partly full of water and partly full of steam, connected to the hot leg. The steam bubble at the top acts like a spring: it sets the system pressure, and it absorbs the volume swings of the rest of the loop.

Pressure is controlled actively. Electric heaters at the bottom boil a little water to raise pressure; spray nozzles at the top inject cool water to condense steam and lower it. By holding the pressurizer at the saturation pressure for its own temperature (~345 °C, ~155 bar), the cooler bulk coolant elsewhere in the loop sits well below its own boiling point. The gap between the operating temperature and the local saturation temperature is the subcooling margin:

Delta T_sub = T_sat(p) - T_coolantT_sat(p) is the boiling temperature at the system pressure p; T_coolant is the actual local water temperature. A positive margin means no boiling.

At 155 bar water boils at about 345 °C. With the hottest coolant near 325 °C, the loop carries roughly 20 °C of subcooling — enough to guarantee single-phase liquid even at the hottest spot, with margin for transients. This is the central safety logic of a PWR: keep the water liquid, keep it covering the core, and you keep removing heat.

AnalogyThe pressurizer is the expansion tank and pressure cap on a car's cooling system, scaled up enormously. The cap lets the coolant run hotter than 100 °C without boiling, and the small air/steam space swallows the expansion as the engine warms. Squeeze the cap tighter (more pressure) and you raise the boiling point — exactly what 155 bar buys the reactor.

3.4Reactor Coolant Pumps, Loop Layout & Why So Much Pressure

The reactor coolant pumps (one per loop) are massive single-stage centrifugal pumps, each drawing on the order of several megawatts of electrical power. Their job is to push enough water through the core to carry away the heat without the coolant overheating. The flow rate needed follows directly from an energy balance: the heat picked up by the coolant equals its mass flow times its heat capacity times its temperature rise.

Q = m_dot * c_p * (T_hot - T_cold)Q = core thermal power (W), m_dot = mass flow rate (kg/s), c_p = specific heat (~5.5 kJ/(kg.K) for hot pressurized water), Delta T = hot-minus-cold leg.

Rearranging gives the flow the pumps must deliver: m_dot = Q / (c_p · ΔT). A small temperature rise across the core (typically 30–40 °C) keeps the fuel from getting too hot and keeps the whole loop comfortably subcooled — but a small ΔT demands a large mass flow, which is why the pumps are so big. The pump power itself scales with the volumetric flow and the pressure rise it must supply against friction in the loop:

P_pump = (m_dot / rho) * Delta_p_loop / etaDelta_p_loop = frictional pressure drop around the loop, rho = coolant density, eta = pump efficiency. Note: this Delta_p is the few-bar friction loss, NOT the 155 bar system pressure.

Now the headline question: why 155 bar? A reactor wants the hottest possible coolant, because a hotter primary side makes hotter, higher-pressure steam in the secondary loop, and a hotter steam supply means a more efficient turbine (Carnot again). But if you heat water past 100 °C at atmospheric pressure it just boils. Bulk boiling in the core is dangerous: steam carries heat far worse than liquid, so a boiling hot spot can let the fuel temperature run away. The fix is to raise the pressure until water's boiling point climbs above your operating temperature. To run near 325 °C with a safe margin, you need about 155 bar. The pressure is not the goal — it is the price you pay to run hot while staying liquid.

Common mistakeThe reactor coolant pumps do not create the 155 bar system pressure — the pressurizer does. The pumps only overcome the few-bar friction as water circulates. Confusing the loop's frictional head (a handful of bar) with the absolute system pressure (155 bar) leads to pump-power estimates that are off by more than a factor of thirty.
✦ Worked Example — core flow rate and subcooling margin

A PWR core produces Q = 3000 MW thermal. The coolant enters at T_cold = 290 °C and leaves at T_hot = 325 °C; take c_p = 5.5 kJ/(kg·K). The system runs at 155 bar, where water saturates at 345 °C. Find (a) the required mass flow rate and (b) the subcooling margin at the core outlet.

  1. Temperature rise across the core: ΔT = 325 − 290 = 35 K.
  2. Rearrange the heat balance: m_dot = Q / (c_p · ΔT).
  3. Plug in (SI): Q = 3.0×10⁹ W, c_p = 5500 J/(kg·K), ΔT = 35 K, so m_dot = 3.0×10⁹ / (5500 × 35) = 3.0×10⁹ / 192500 ≈ 15,600 kg/s.
  4. Subcooling margin at outlet: ΔT_sub = T_sat − T_hot = 345 − 325 = 20 K. Positive, so even the hottest exit coolant stays liquid.
  5. Sanity check: ~15.6 tonnes of water per second is right for a 3 GW core split across three or four loops (~4–5 tonnes/s each). And 20 K of subcooling matches the design intent of Section 3.3 — no boiling, with room for transients.

🛠️ Hands-On: size the loop and check the vessel wall in Python

We use the same free Python scientific stack set up in Week 1 (NumPy, Jupyter, matplotlib) — see the install panel in Lecture 1 if you need it. For the 3-D vessel geometry and later finite-element stress work this course also uses FreeCAD and Salome-Meca (both free); a one-time install note for those lives in the Week 2 lecture. Here we only need NumPy to turn this lecture's two formulas into numbers.

# primary_system.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

# --- 1. Core flow rate from the heat balance Q = m_dot * c_p * dT ---
Q      = 3.0e9        # core thermal power, W
cp     = 5500.0       # specific heat of hot pressurized water, J/(kg.K)
T_hot  = 325.0        # hot-leg temperature, C
T_cold = 290.0        # cold-leg temperature, C

dT     = T_hot - T_cold
m_dot  = Q / (cp * dT)
print(f"core flow rate   = {m_dot:8.0f} kg/s")     # ~15600

# --- 2. Subcooling margin at the core outlet ---
T_sat_155bar = 345.0   # boiling temp of water at 155 bar, C (steam tables)
margin = T_sat_155bar - T_hot
print(f"subcooling margin = {margin:6.1f} K")       # 20.0

# --- 3. Hoop stress in the vessel wall: sigma = p*r/t ---
p = 155e5             # system pressure, Pa  (155 bar)
r = 2.2              # inner radius, m
t = 0.20             # wall thickness, m
sigma = p * r / t
print(f"hoop stress      = {sigma/1e6:6.0f} MPa")   # ~171 MPa
# Low-alloy RPV steel yields near 350-450 MPa, so 171 MPa keeps a healthy margin.

Try sweeping the pressure from 70 to 155 bar and watch both the required wall thickness (to hold a fixed stress) and the saturation temperature climb together — that coupling is exactly the design trade-off behind choosing 155 bar.

💬 Discussion

A PWR runs near 155 bar and 325 °C, accepting a thick, expensive, hard-to-inspect pressure vessel as the cost of staying liquid and running hot. A boiling water reactor (BWR), by contrast, deliberately lets the coolant boil in the core at a much lower pressure (~70 bar), feeding steam straight to the turbine and skipping the steam generator entirely. If lower pressure means a thinner, cheaper vessel and a simpler loop, why has the PWR's high-pressure, no-boiling design remained so dominant? What does the subcooling margin buy you that boiling-in-the-core gives up? Argue the trade-off in terms of safety, heat transfer, and system complexity.

Show a model response

The PWR pays for its high pressure but earns three things in return. First, single-phase predictability. Keeping the primary subcooled means heat transfer in the core stays in the well-understood liquid regime; there is no risk of a boiling crisis (departure from nucleate boiling) suddenly insulating the fuel and letting its temperature run away. The ~20 °C subcooling margin is a direct, monitorable safety reserve — a cushion that boiling-in-the-core simply does not have, because the BWR already operates at saturation.

Second, isolation of radioactivity. Because the PWR primary water never reaches the turbine, the radioactive coolant stays inside the sealed primary loop and containment; the turbine hall is conventional and accessible. A BWR sends slightly activated steam directly to the turbine, so more of the plant must be treated as a radiation zone.

Third, clean separation of control. Pressure (set by the pressurizer) and reactivity (set in the core) are decoupled, which simplifies control and transient response. The cost is real — a thick forged vessel, an extra heat exchanger, large pumps, and the high-pressure boundary itself — and the BWR's simplicity and lower pressure are genuine advantages. The honest answer is that both designs work; the PWR's dominance reflects a historical preference for a robust subcooling margin and a fully isolated primary loop over the BWR's lower-pressure simplicity.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer should:

  • Name the subcooling margin and tie it to avoiding a boiling crisis / DNB.
  • Note that PWR primary radioactivity stays out of the turbine hall.
  • Acknowledge the real cost (thick vessel, steam generator, big pumps) — not treat high pressure as free.
  • Recognize both designs are viable rather than declaring one strictly "better."

Q1. In a normally operating PWR, where does the primary coolant water boil?
A. In the core, as it absorbs fission heat
B. In the cold leg as it leaves the pump
C. In the pressurizer water space at the bottom
D. Nowhere — the primary coolant stays liquid all the way around the loop
Show answer

D. The primary loop is held subcooled so the water never boils; boiling happens only in the secondary loop. (A) describes a BWR, not a PWR. (B) The cold leg is the coolest point — furthest from boiling. (C) The pressurizer boils a little water at its top via heaters, but that is a deliberate steam bubble, not the bulk primary coolant.

Q2. What sets and maintains the ~155 bar system pressure in a PWR primary loop?
A. The reactor coolant pumps
B. The steam generator tubes
C. The pressurizer, via its steam bubble plus heaters and spray
D. The thickness of the vessel wall
Show answer

C. The pressurizer's steam space acts as a pressure-setting cushion, trimmed by heaters (raise) and spray (lower). (A) The pumps only overcome a few bar of friction, not the absolute pressure. (B) The steam generator transfers heat; it does not set primary pressure. (D) The wall withstands the pressure but does not create it.

Q3. A core produces 3000 MW with c_p = 5.5 kJ/(kg·K) and a 35 K rise across the core. Roughly what mass flow rate is required?
A. ~160 kg/s
B. ~1,600 kg/s
C. ~15,600 kg/s
D. ~156,000 kg/s
Show answer

C. m_dot = Q/(c_p·ΔT) = 3.0×10⁹/(5500×35) ≈ 15,600 kg/s. (A) and (B) are 100× and 10× too small (dropped powers of ten), and (D) is 10× too large. Tracking units in SI prevents these slips.

Q4. The subcooling margin in a PWR primary loop is defined as…
A. T_sat(p) − T_coolant, the gap between the local boiling point and the actual water temperature
B. T_hot − T_cold across the core
C. the system pressure minus atmospheric pressure
D. the difference between primary and secondary loop temperatures
Show answer

A. Subcooling is how far below boiling the water sits: T_sat(p) minus the actual temperature. (B) is the core temperature rise, a different quantity. (C) is a pressure difference, not a temperature margin. (D) describes the steam-generator approach temperature, not subcooling.

Q5. Why is a PWR primary loop pressurized to roughly 155 bar?
A. To force the water through the core faster than the pumps could alone
B. To raise water's boiling point above the ~325 °C operating temperature so the coolant runs hot yet stays liquid
C. To compress the uranium fuel and increase its density
D. To keep air dissolved in the coolant for chemistry control
Show answer

B. High pressure lifts the saturation temperature (~345 °C at 155 bar) above the operating temperature, allowing a hot, efficient cycle with no bulk boiling. (A) Flow is set by the pumps, not the static pressure. (C) Pressure does not meaningfully compress solid fuel. (D) PWR chemistry deliberately removes oxygen; it does not keep air dissolved.
