Reactor Types & Plant Overview

A nuclear power plant is just an extraordinarily exotic kettle — the entire art is moving the heat from a fissioning core out to a spinning turbine without ever letting the two touch.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the major power-reactor families (PWR, BWR, heavy-water/CANDU, gas-cooled, sodium fast) and state what coolant and moderator each uses.
  • Describe the primary, secondary, and tertiary loops and explain why a plant is built around heat-exchange barriers.
  • Trace energy from fission in the core, through the steam cycle, to electricity on the grid, and estimate the thermal efficiency.
  • Contrast a PWR and a BWR on pressure, where steam is made, and how radioactivity is contained.
  • Read a simplified plant schematic and identify the reactor vessel, steam generator, turbine, condenser, and pump.

1.1What Every Power Reactor Has in Common

Strip away the engineering and a power reactor does exactly one thing: it sustains a controlled fission chain reaction so that the heat released — roughly 200 MeV per uranium-235 fission — appears at a steady, useful rate. Every commercial design then faces the same three jobs, and the differences between reactor types are almost entirely how they solve these three jobs.

1) Moderate the neutrons (in thermal reactors). Fission neutrons are born fast (~2 MeV), but uranium-235 fissions far more readily when struck by slow ("thermal") neutrons. A moderator — light water, heavy water, or graphite — slows neutrons by elastic collisions without absorbing too many of them. Fast reactors deliberately skip this step and run on fast neutrons.

2) Remove the heat. A coolant flows through the core and carries away the fission heat. The coolant choice (ordinary water, heavy water, helium gas, or liquid sodium) shapes the whole plant: its pressure, its temperature, and ultimately its efficiency.

3) Control the reaction. Neutron-absorbing control rods (and dissolved boron in many water reactors) let operators raise, lower, and shut down the chain reaction on demand.

P_thermal = N_fission/s * E_fission , E_fission ~ 200 MeV ~ 3.2e-11 JReactor thermal power = fissions per second times the energy released per fission. One gram of U-235 fully fissioned releases about 1 MW-day of heat.
AnalogyThink of a wood stove heating a house. The fire is the core; the damper that throttles the air is the control system; and the water pipes you run past the firebox to carry heat to the radiators are the coolant loop. You never pipe the smoke itself into the living room — you transfer its heat through a barrier. A reactor does the same with radioactivity instead of smoke.

1.2The Major Reactor Families

Almost every reactor on the grid belongs to one of five families. What distinguishes them is the combination of coolant, moderator, and fuel enrichment.

Pressurized Water Reactor (PWR). Ordinary ("light") water is both coolant and moderator. The water is held at very high pressure (~15.5 MPa, about 155 atmospheres) so it stays liquid even at ~325 °C and never boils in the core. It is the most common power reactor in the world. Fuel is low-enriched uranium (~3–5% U-235).

Boiling Water Reactor (BWR). Also light water for coolant and moderator, but at a lower pressure (~7 MPa) so the water is allowed to boil right inside the core. The steam goes straight to the turbine — simpler, but the turbine then sees mildly radioactive steam.

Heavy-Water Reactor (CANDU-type). Uses heavy water (D₂O, deuterium oxide) as moderator. Heavy water absorbs so few neutrons that the reactor can run on natural (unenriched) uranium, and it can be refuelled while running.

Gas-Cooled Reactor. Uses graphite as the moderator and a gas — carbon dioxide or helium — as the coolant. Gas can reach very high temperatures, which raises efficiency and opens the door to process-heat applications.

Sodium-Cooled Fast Reactor. Has no moderator: it runs on fast neutrons, cooled by liquid sodium (an excellent heat-transfer fluid that does not slow neutrons much). Fast reactors can "breed" new fuel and burn long-lived waste.

Common mistakeDo not assume the coolant and the moderator must be two different substances. In a PWR and a BWR the same light water does both jobs at once. This coupling has a profound safety upside: if the water boils away or leaks, the moderator vanishes too, neutrons stay fast, and the chain reaction tends to shut itself down (a negative coolant-void/temperature feedback). Treating "coolant" and "moderator" as always-separate parts will lead you to mis-read every water-reactor safety argument.

1.3Plant Layout: Primary, Secondary & Tertiary Loops

A nuclear plant is organized as a chain of fluid loops connected by heat exchangers. Each loop hands its heat to the next through a metal wall — never by mixing — so that radioactivity is confined to the innermost loop while the outer loops remain clean. This is the single most important architectural idea in the course.

Primary loop (radioactive). Coolant flows through the reactor core, picks up fission heat, and carries it to a heat exchanger called the steam generator. In a PWR this water is pressurized so it never boils. The primary loop is sealed inside the containment building because its coolant becomes slightly radioactive from passing through the core.

Secondary loop (clean steam). On the other side of the steam-generator wall, lower-pressure feedwater boils into steam. That steam spins the turbine, which turns the generator to make electricity. The secondary loop never touches the core, so its steam is non-radioactive — a worker can stand beside the PWR turbine.

Tertiary loop (cooling water). After the turbine, the steam must be condensed back to liquid in the condenser. A third loop of cooling water — drawn from a river, the sea, or a cooling tower — carries the leftover waste heat to the environment. This is the plume you see rising from cooling towers: it is plain water vapor, not smoke.

core --[primary]--> steam generator --[secondary]--> turbine --> condenser --[tertiary]--> environmentHeat cascades loop to loop through walls. Only the primary loop is radioactive; the secondary and tertiary loops stay clean.

1.4PWR vs BWR: One Loop or Two?

The clearest way to lock in the loop concept is to compare the two dominant light-water designs head to head. They use the same fuel and the same water-as-moderator trick, but they answer one question differently: where is the steam made?

In a PWR, the primary water is kept under such high pressure that it cannot boil in the core. Steam is made only in a separate steam generator, on the clean secondary side. So a PWR has two water loops, and the turbine never sees radioactive water. The cost is a heavy, thick-walled pressure vessel and the extra steam generators.

In a BWR, the pressure is lower and the water is allowed to boil right in the core. The steam produced there goes directly to the turbine — there is no separate steam generator, so the BWR has effectively one primary steam loop. This is mechanically simpler and a bit more efficient, but the turbine and its building must be treated as a radiation area because the steam came straight from the core.

We can quantify how much steam a boiling core makes with the thermal balance: the heat added equals the mass flow times the enthalpy rise. To boil water you must supply its latent heat of vaporization:

Q = m_dot * h_fgQ = boiling power (W), m_dot = steam mass flow (kg/s), h_fg = latent heat of vaporization (~1500 kJ/kg of saturated water at 7 MPa).

The same physics — Q = ṁ · Δh — governs the secondary side of a PWR's steam generator. The difference is purely architectural: the PWR puts a wall between the boiling water and the core; the BWR does not.

AnalogyA BWR is a covered pot on a stove: the water boils where the heat is and the steam rises straight off. A PWR is a double boiler: the inner pot (primary) stays pressurized and never boils, gently heating water in an outer pot (secondary) that does the boiling. The double boiler needs more hardware, but the food in the outer pot never touches the burner — just as the PWR turbine never touches core water.
✦ Worked Example — from fission rate to grid megawatts

A PWR core runs at a steady thermal power of 3000 MW_thermal. Each U-235 fission releases about 200 MeV of recoverable energy (3.2×10⁻¹⁷ J). The plant's overall thermal-to-electric efficiency is 33%. Find the fission rate and the electrical output.

  1. Energy per fission in joules: 200 MeV × 1.602×10⁻¹³ J/MeV = 3.2×10⁻¹⁷ J per fission.
  2. Fission rate: Ṅ = P_thermal / E_fission = (3.0×10⁹ W) / (3.2×10⁻¹⁷ J) ≈ 9.4×10¹⁹ fissions per second.
  3. Electrical power: P_electric = η × P_thermal = 0.33 × 3000 MW = 990 MW_electric (about 1 GWe — a typical large unit).
  4. Waste heat: the other 67% — 3000 − 990 = 2010 MW — must be dumped by the tertiary loop to the river, sea, or cooling tower.
  5. Sanity check: 3000 MW_thermal at 33% giving ~1 GWe matches real large PWRs. The 1500-or-so MW of "lost" heat is exactly why those cooling towers are so enormous — most of the energy never reaches the grid.

🛠️ Hands-On: sketch the plant heat balance in Python

This course's free toolchain is FreeCAD (parametric CAD for vessel and core geometry), Salome-Meca (open-source finite-element analysis for thermal/structural studies), and the Python + NumPy scientific stack for the quick calculations we do every week. Week 1 setup: install once below, then reuse it for every lecture and the design project.

FreeCAD and Salome-Meca are downloaded as free installers; the Python side installs with pip into a clean virtual environment:

python3 -m venv reactor-env

FreeCAD ships its own bundled Python with NumPy, so scripts you write here also run inside FreeCAD's macro console. Salome-Meca (the Code_Aster FEA bundle) is a separate free download you unpack and launch directly.

Verify: run python -c "import numpy; print('ok')" — printing ok means the Python side is ready for the whole course.

Let's turn this lecture's plant balance into code: from thermal power and efficiency, get the electrical output, the waste heat, and the cooling-water flow the tertiary loop must carry.

# plant_balance.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

P_thermal = 3000e6        # reactor thermal power, W
eta       = 0.33          # thermal-to-electric efficiency
E_fission = 3.2e-11       # recoverable energy per fission, J (~200 MeV)

N_fission = P_thermal / E_fission          # fissions per second
P_elec    = eta * P_thermal                # electrical power, W
Q_waste   = P_thermal - P_elec             # heat rejected by tertiary loop, W

# cooling water needed if it warms by dT = 10 K (cp = 4180 J/kg/K)
cp, dT = 4180.0, 10.0
m_dot_cool = Q_waste / (cp * dT)           # kg/s

print(f"fission rate : {N_fission:.2e} /s")
print(f"electrical   : {P_elec/1e6:.0f} MWe")
print(f"waste heat   : {Q_waste/1e6:.0f} MW")
print(f"cooling flow : {m_dot_cool:.0f} kg/s")

# fission rate : 9.38e+19 /s
# electrical   : 990 MWe
# waste heat   : 2010 MW
# cooling flow : 48086 kg/s   (~48 cubic metres of water per second!)

That 48 m³/s of cooling water is why plants sit on big rivers or coastlines. Try lowering eta to 0.30 (an older plant) and watch the waste heat and cooling flow climb — efficiency directly sets how much river you need.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a pressurized water reactor (PWR), what is the role of the ordinary light water flowing through the core?
A. It is the coolant only; a separate graphite moderator slows the neutrons
B. It serves as both the coolant and the moderator
C. It is the moderator only; the fuel is cooled by helium gas
D. It neither cools nor moderates; it only shields radiation
Show answer

B. In light-water reactors the same water both removes heat and slows neutrons. (A) describes a gas-cooled/graphite design, not a PWR. (C) a PWR is not helium-cooled. (D) the water is essential to both cooling and moderation, not merely shielding.

Q2. Which loop in a PWR plant carries the radioactive coolant?
A. The secondary loop, because that is where steam is made
B. The tertiary loop, because it touches the environment
C. The primary loop, which flows through the reactor core
D. All three loops are equally radioactive
Show answer

C. Only the primary loop passes through the core and becomes radioactive; it is sealed inside containment. (A) the secondary steam is clean because the steam-generator wall separates it from core water. (B) the tertiary loop is clean cooling water. (D) the whole point of the loop architecture is that the secondary and tertiary loops stay non-radioactive.

Q3. The key architectural difference between a BWR and a PWR is that…
A. a BWR boils water in the core and sends that steam directly to the turbine, with no separate steam generator
B. a BWR uses heavy water while a PWR uses light water
C. a BWR has no moderator and runs on fast neutrons
D. a PWR boils water in the core while a BWR keeps it liquid
Show answer

A. A BWR boils in the core and pipes the steam straight to the turbine (one loop); a PWR keeps the primary liquid and makes steam in a separate steam generator (two loops). (B) both use light water. (C) a BWR is a thermal reactor with a water moderator, not a fast reactor. (D) this is backwards — it is the BWR that boils in the core.

Q4. A reactor produces 3000 MW of thermal power at an overall efficiency of 33%. Roughly how much waste heat must the tertiary (cooling) loop reject to the environment?
A. About 330 MW
B. About 990 MW
C. Essentially zero — efficient plants reject no heat
D. About 2010 MW
Show answer

D. Electrical output = 0.33 × 3000 = 990 MWe, so waste heat = 3000 − 990 = 2010 MW. (B) 990 MW is the electrical output, not the waste heat. (A) 330 MW corresponds to a misplaced factor. (C) the second law forbids zero rejection — most of the heat is always dumped.

Q5. Why can a heavy-water (CANDU-type) reactor run on natural, unenriched uranium while a PWR needs 3–5% enriched fuel?
A. Heavy water is a far better coolant, so the fuel runs cooler
B. Heavy water absorbs very few neutrons, so the neutron economy supports a chain reaction even at natural enrichment
C. Natural uranium contains more U-235 than enriched uranium
D. Heavy-water reactors do not need a chain reaction at all
Show answer

B. Deuterium in heavy water captures neutrons far less than the hydrogen in light water, so more neutrons survive to cause fission and the reactor can sustain criticality on natural uranium. (A) better cooling does not improve the neutron economy. (C) natural uranium has only ~0.7% U-235, far less than enriched fuel. (D) every power reactor needs a sustained chain reaction.
