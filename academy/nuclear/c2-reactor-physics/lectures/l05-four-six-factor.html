The Four- and Six-Factor Formulas

Follow a single neutron around one full life cycle — birth in fission, slowing down, capture, leakage — and you can predict whether an entire reactor lives, dies, or runs forever in perfect balance.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace the neutron life cycle and explain why a single multiplication number determines a reactor's fate.
  • State and interpret the four-factor formula k_inf = eta · f · p · epsilon for an infinite medium.
  • Give the physical meaning of each factor — reproduction, thermal utilization, resonance escape, fast fission — and how it can be near, above, or below 1.
  • Extend to a finite reactor with the fast and thermal non-leakage probabilities to obtain the six-factor formula for k_eff.
  • Identify which knobs a reactor designer turns to raise or lower each factor and bring k_eff to exactly 1.

5.1The Neutron Life Cycle & the Multiplication Factor

In Lecture 4 we defined the effective multiplication factor k_eff as the ratio of neutrons in one generation to those in the previous generation, and saw that k = 1 means a self-sustaining chain reaction. That definition is correct but blunt — it tells us whether the chain survives, not why. To design a reactor we need to open up that single number and see the chain of physical events hiding inside it.

Picture one generation of neutrons born in fission. To produce the next generation, each neutron must run a gauntlet. It is born fast (around 2 MeV). It may cause a fast fission before it slows. It must slow down (moderate) through the dangerous resonance energies where fertile nuclei love to swallow it. Once thermal (around 0.025 eV), it must be absorbed in fuel rather than in coolant, cladding, or structure — and that absorption must actually cause fission rather than a sterile capture. The fraction of neutrons surviving each hurdle, multiplied together, is exactly k.

The genius of the four-factor formula is that it factors this life cycle into four probabilities (well-defined for an infinite medium with no leakage), each capturing one stage of the journey:

k_inf = eta * f * p * epsilonk_inf = infinite-medium multiplication factor; the product of reproduction (eta), thermal utilization (f), resonance escape (p), and fast-fission (epsilon).
AnalogyThink of a relay race run by every newborn neutron. There are four batons to hand off cleanly: "make enough new runners" (eta), "hand off to a teammate not a rival" (f), "survive the hurdles while slowing down" (p), and a small head start from runners who score early (epsilon). The team only sustains itself if the product of all the hand-off success rates is at least 1. Drop any baton too often and the race — the chain reaction — dies out.

5.2The Four Factors, One at a Time

Each factor is a ratio with a clean physical story. We follow the cycle in the order a neutron actually experiences it, then write them in the conventional product order.

eta — the reproduction factor

When a thermal neutron is absorbed in the fuel, it does not always cause fission — sometimes the fuel nucleus just captures it. eta is the average number of fast neutrons produced per thermal neutron absorbed in the fuel:

eta = nu * Sigma_f / Sigma_a(fuel) = nu * Sigma_f / (Sigma_f + Sigma_c)nu = neutrons emitted per fission (~2.43 for U-235); Sigma_f = fission cross section, Sigma_c = capture, Sigma_a = Sigma_f + Sigma_c, all for the fuel only.

Because some fuel absorptions are sterile captures, eta is always less than nu. For natural-uranium fuel eta is around 1.33; enrichment raises it because U-235 has a far higher fission-to-capture ratio than U-238.

f — the thermal utilization factor

A thermal neutron about to be absorbed somewhere in the core might land in the fuel, or be wasted in moderator, coolant, cladding, control material, or fission products. f is the fraction of all thermal absorptions that occur in the fuel:

f = Sigma_a(fuel) / Sigma_a(total) = Sigma_a(fuel) / (Sigma_a(fuel) + Sigma_a(other))Higher fuel loading raises f; neutron poisons, excess moderator, and control rods lower it.

p — the resonance escape probability

As a fast neutron slows through the intermediate (epithermal) range, U-238 presents enormous, narrow resonance absorption peaks. p is the probability a neutron slows all the way to thermal energies without being captured in one of these resonances. Good moderation — large energy loss per collision, fuel finely separated from moderator (heterogeneity) — raises p.

epsilon — the fast-fission factor

A small bonus: some fast neutrons cause fission in U-238 (which fissions only above ~1 MeV) before they slow down, adding extra neutrons. epsilon is the ratio of total fissions (fast + thermal) to thermal fissions alone, so it is slightly greater than 1, typically about 1.02–1.03 in a thermal reactor.

Common mistakeDo not confuse eta with nu. nu is the raw neutron yield per fission; eta is neutrons produced per thermal neutron absorbed in the fuel, which is smaller because not every fuel absorption fissions. Plugging nu (~2.43) into the four-factor formula where eta (~2.0 for enriched fuel, ~1.33 for natural U) belongs will overpredict k by a wide margin — a classic exam-killing slip.

5.3Adding Leakage: The Six-Factor Formula

The four-factor formula assumes a reactor of infinite size, where every neutron stays in the multiplying medium forever. Real reactors are finite, so some neutrons simply leak out across the boundary and are lost. Leakage happens in two energy regimes, and each gets its own survival probability.

While neutrons are fast and slowing down, some escape the core: the fast non-leakage probability P_FNL is the fraction that do not. After they thermalize, some still diffuse out before being absorbed: the thermal non-leakage probability P_TNL is the fraction that survive that stage too. Inserting both into the cycle gives the six-factor formula:

k_eff = eta * f * p * epsilon * P_FNL * P_TNLk_eff = effective multiplication factor of a finite reactor; the two extra factors are the fast and thermal non-leakage probabilities, each between 0 and 1.

The relationship to k_inf is clean and worth memorizing — leakage simply scales down the infinite-medium result:

k_eff = k_inf * P_FNL * P_TNLBecause P_FNL and P_TNL are each less than 1, a finite reactor always has k_eff < k_inf. A core must over-design k_inf to overcome leakage.

Both non-leakage probabilities grow toward 1 as the reactor gets larger, because the surface-to-volume ratio shrinks — a big core "loses" a smaller fraction of its neutrons through its skin. This is exactly why there is a critical size: a chunk of fuel too small leaks too much (P_FNL · P_TNL too low) for k_eff to reach 1, no matter how good its four factors are.

AnalogyImagine heating a room with a fixed number of heaters (the four factors set how much heat is generated). Leakage is heat escaping through the walls. A tiny shed loses heat through its walls faster than a giant warehouse, because the shed has lots of wall per unit of interior. Make the core big enough and the "walls" become a negligible fraction of the whole — non-leakage approaches 1 and k_eff approaches k_inf.

5.4Tuning the Factors: How Reactors Are Actually Designed

The six-factor formula is not just bookkeeping — it is the designer's control panel. Every choice of fuel, moderator, geometry, and control material moves one or more factors. The goal is a core whose factors multiply to k_eff slightly above 1 at startup (so there is reserve reactivity to burn), held at exactly 1 in operation by control elements.

The knobs, factor by factor

eta — set mainly by fuel enrichment. More U-235 (or plutonium) raises the fission-to-absorption ratio in the fuel. This is the single most powerful lever in most designs.

f — raised by packing in more fuel and minimizing parasitic absorbers; lowered deliberately by inserting control rods and by burnable poisons and accumulating fission products like xenon-135. Operators trim f in real time.

p — improved by an effective moderator and by lumping the fuel (heterogeneous lattices) so fast neutrons do most of their slowing down in the moderator, away from the U-238 resonance traps in the fuel.

epsilon — largely fixed by the lattice geometry and fuel; close-packed fuel and higher U-238 content give a slightly larger fast-fission bonus, but it is a minor knob.

P_FNL, P_TNL — raised by making the core bigger and by surrounding it with a reflector that bounces escaping neutrons back in, effectively boosting both non-leakage probabilities without adding fuel.

There is also a crucial safety dimension. Because f and p depend on moderator density and temperature, they give the reactor built-in feedback: if a water-moderated core overheats and the water expands (or boils), p and f shift so that k_eff drops — a negative temperature coefficient that we will quantify in Week 7. The six-factor formula is the language in which that self-regulation is expressed.

✦ Worked Example — from four factors to k_eff

A thermal core is characterized by eta = 1.65, f = 0.71, p = 0.87, epsilon = 1.03, and non-leakage probabilities P_FNL = 0.97, P_TNL = 0.99. Find k_inf and k_eff, and decide the reactor's state.

  1. Four-factor product (k_inf): first eta × f = 1.65 × 0.71 = 1.1715. Then × p: 1.1715 × 0.87 = 1.01921. Then × epsilon: 1.01921 × 1.03 = k_inf ≈ 1.0498.
  2. Combine the leakage terms: P_FNL × P_TNL = 0.97 × 0.99 = 0.9603 (about 4% of neutrons leak out overall).
  3. Six-factor product (k_eff): k_eff = k_inf × 0.9603 = 1.0498 × 0.9603 ≈ 1.0081.
  4. Interpret: k_eff > 1, so the reactor is supercritical as built — it carries roughly 0.8% excess reactivity. Control rods would be inserted to lower f until k_eff = 1 for steady operation.
  5. Sanity check: notice k_inf (1.05) is only just above 1, and leakage shaved off ~4%; without that small reserve the core could not have reached criticality. This is why designers always build k_inf comfortably above 1.

🛠️ Hands-On: estimate the four factors from cross sections in Python

We continue with the free Python + OpenMC stack set up in Week 1/2 (see the one-time install details block in the early lectures). Here we don't even need a full transport run — the four factors are definitions in terms of macroscopic cross sections, so a few lines of NumPy turn lecture physics into numbers. Later you can cross-check these against a real lattice tally in OpenMC.

# four_factor.py — Lecture 5
# Estimate k_inf from macroscopic cross sections of a simple thermal lattice.
import numpy as np

nu = 2.43            # neutrons per U-235 fission

# Macroscopic cross sections (1/cm), thermal group, illustrative values
Sig_f_fuel  = 0.070  # fission, fuel
Sig_c_fuel  = 0.018  # radiative capture, fuel
Sig_a_fuel  = Sig_f_fuel + Sig_c_fuel
Sig_a_other = 0.029  # absorption in moderator, clad, structure

eta     = nu * Sig_f_fuel / Sig_a_fuel              # reproduction
f       = Sig_a_fuel / (Sig_a_fuel + Sig_a_other)   # thermal utilization
p       = 0.87        # resonance escape (from slowing-down theory)
epsilon = 1.03        # fast-fission factor

k_inf = eta * f * p * epsilon
print(f"eta={eta:.3f}  f={f:.3f}  p={p:.3f}  eps={epsilon:.3f}")
print(f"k_inf = {k_inf:.4f}")

# Add leakage for a finite core -> six-factor formula
P_FNL, P_TNL = 0.97, 0.99
k_eff = k_inf * P_FNL * P_TNL
print(f"k_eff = {k_eff:.4f}  ->",
      "supercritical" if k_eff > 1 else "subcritical or critical")

# eta=2.295  f=0.752  p=0.870  eps=1.030
# k_inf = 1.5466   k_eff = 1.4848  (this lattice needs control to tame it)

Try sweeping Sig_a_other upward (adding a neutron poison) and watch f — and therefore k_eff — fall. That single line is, in miniature, exactly what a control rod does.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the four-factor formula k_inf = eta · f · p · epsilon, which factor is the average number of fast neutrons produced per thermal neutron absorbed in the fuel?
A. eta (reproduction factor)
B. f (thermal utilization)
C. p (resonance escape)
D. epsilon (fast-fission factor)
Show answer

A. eta = nu · Sigma_f / Sigma_a counts neutrons produced per thermal absorption in the fuel. (B) f is the fraction of thermal absorptions that occur in fuel; (C) p is the probability of escaping resonance capture while slowing down; (D) epsilon is the small fast-fission bonus.

Q2. A reactor has k_inf = 1.25, P_FNL = 0.96, and P_TNL = 0.98. What is k_eff?
A. 1.25
B. 1.18
C. 1.30
D. 0.94
Show answer

B. k_eff = k_inf · P_FNL · P_TNL = 1.25 × 0.96 × 0.98 = 1.176 ≈ 1.18. (A) ignores leakage entirely; (C) wrongly multiplies upward; (D) confuses k_eff with the overall leakage fraction.

Q3. Inserting control rods into an operating core primarily lowers k_eff by reducing which factor?
A. epsilon, the fast-fission factor
B. eta, the reproduction factor
C. f, the thermal utilization factor
D. P_FNL, the fast non-leakage probability
Show answer

C. Control rods are strong thermal absorbers in non-fuel material, so they steal thermal neutrons from the fuel and reduce f. (A) epsilon is fixed by lattice geometry; (B) eta is set by fuel composition; (D) leakage is governed by core size and reflector, not rods.

Q4. Lumping the fuel into discrete rods rather than mixing it uniformly with the moderator mainly improves which factor, and why?
A. It raises epsilon, because rods concentrate fast fissions
B. It raises f, because rods simply hold more fuel
C. It lowers eta, because rods capture more neutrons
D. It raises p, because neutrons slow down in the moderator away from the U-238 resonances in the fuel
Show answer

D. Heterogeneous (lumped) fuel lets neutrons do most of their slowing down in the moderator, so fewer pass through the U-238 resonance energies while inside the fuel — raising the resonance escape probability p. (A) the effect on epsilon is minor; (B) lumping does not change the total fuel amount; (C) lumping does not lower eta.

Q5. Why is k_eff always less than k_inf for a real, finite reactor?
A. Because eta is always less than nu
B. Because epsilon is greater than 1
C. Because the fast and thermal non-leakage probabilities are each less than 1
D. Because finite reactors cannot reach criticality at all
Show answer

C. k_eff = k_inf · P_FNL · P_TNL, and both leakage factors lie between 0 and 1, so the product is smaller than k_inf. (A) is true but is already baked into k_inf, not the cause of the k_inf-to-k_eff gap; (B) epsilon > 1 would raise k, not lower it; (D) is false — finite reactors reach criticality routinely.
