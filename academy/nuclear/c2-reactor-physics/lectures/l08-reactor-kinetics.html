Week 8 · Lecture 8

Reactor Kinetics & Delayed Neutrons

Fewer than one neutron in a hundred arrives late — yet that tiny tardy fraction is the only reason a human hand on a control rod can steer a fission chain reaction instead of being outrun by it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write down the point-kinetics equations and explain what each term physically represents.
  • Distinguish prompt from delayed neutrons and define the delayed-neutron fraction beta.
  • Explain the prompt jump and why an instantaneous reactivity step causes a sudden, finite power change.
  • Compute the stable reactor period from reactivity and relate it to how fast power rises or falls.
  • Explain why delayed neutrons make a reactor controllable, and why prompt criticality is catastrophic.

8.1From Steady State to Kinetics: Tracking Power in Time

Through Weeks 4 to 7 we treated the reactor as a steady, balanced system: in a critical reactor, exactly one neutron from each fission survives to cause the next fission, so the chain reaction holds at constant power. The multiplication factor k equals 1, and the neutron population neither grows nor decays. That static picture answers "is the reactor balanced?" but not the question an operator actually lives with: when I move a control rod, how fast does the power change? That is the subject of reactor kinetics — the time evolution of the neutron population.

The natural measure of imbalance is reactivity, the fractional departure of k from criticality:

rho = (k - 1) / krho = reactivity (dimensionless). rho = 0 is exactly critical; rho > 0 is supercritical (power rising); rho < 0 is subcritical (power falling).

The other key timescale is the prompt neutron generation time Λ (capital lambda) — the average time between a neutron's birth and the moment it triggers the next fission. In a thermal reactor Λ is heartstoppingly short: roughly 10⁻⁴ to 10⁻⁵ seconds, because a neutron need only slow down, diffuse a little, and be absorbed. If every neutron were prompt, the population would respond on this timescale. Imagine a reactor a mere 0.1% supercritical (rho = 0.001) with Λ = 10⁻⁴ s and only prompt neutrons: the population would multiply by a factor of e roughly every 0.1 s, climbing about twenty-thousand-fold in a single second. No mechanical control rod could ever keep up. The whole art of reactor control rests on one saving grace, which the rest of this lecture builds up: a small fraction of neutrons are not prompt.

AnalogyThink of the neutron population like a bank balance under continuous compound interest. The reactivity rho is the interest rate, and the generation time Λ is how often interest compounds. With purely prompt neutrons the account compounds tens of thousands of times per second — a runaway you could never manage. Delayed neutrons act like a slow-release portion of every deposit that only clears days later, dramatically stretching out the effective compounding time and giving you room to act.

8.2Prompt vs Delayed Neutrons & the Fraction beta

When a uranium-235 nucleus fissions, most of the 2-3 neutrons it emits appear within about 10⁻¹⁴ s of the split — essentially instantaneously. These are prompt neutrons. But the fission fragments are unstable and neutron-rich, and a handful of specific fragments — the classic examples are bromine-87 and iodine-137 — decay by beta emission into excited daughter nuclei that immediately "boil off" a neutron. Because this neutron must wait for the parent fragment to beta-decay first, it appears seconds to minutes after the fission. These are delayed neutrons, and the fragments that produce them are delayed-neutron precursors.

The fraction of all fission neutrons that are delayed is the delayed-neutron fraction β (beta). For uranium-235 it is famously small:

beta approx 0.0065 (about 0.65% for U-235)Only ~1 fission neutron in 150 is delayed; the other ~99.35% are prompt. Pu-239 has a smaller beta approx 0.0021.

The precursors are conventionally grouped into six families (delayed groups), each with its own decay constant λi and yield fraction βi, with half-lives ranging from a fraction of a second up to about 55 seconds for the longest-lived group. These half-lives are set by the beta decay of the precursor, not by anything neutronic. The crucial number is not the prompt generation time Λ (10⁻⁴ s) but the population-weighted average delay — the mean generation time including delayed neutrons — which works out to around 0.08-0.1 s for U-235. That hundred-millisecond effective delay, thousands of times longer than Λ, is what slows the reactor down to a controllable pace.

Common mistakeBeta is not the fraction of neutrons that are "lost" or "wasted." Delayed neutrons cause fission just like prompt ones — they are fully counted in k. Beta is purely the fraction that arrive late. A related error is treating beta as a fixed universal constant: it depends on the fissile nuclide. Plutonium-239 has roughly a third of U-235's beta, which is exactly why plutonium-fueled and high-burnup cores have a narrower margin to prompt criticality and demand tighter control.

8.3The Point-Kinetics Equations

To track power versus time we collapse the spatial detail of the core into a single number — the total neutron population, or equivalently the power level n(t) — and write a balance equation. This point-reactor kinetics approximation assumes the shape of the neutron distribution stays fixed while only its amplitude changes. Folding the six precursor groups into one effective group (with combined fraction β and average decay constant λ) for clarity, the equations are:

dn/dt = [(rho - beta) / Lambda] * n + lambda * Cn = neutron population (power). The bracket is the net prompt growth rate; lambda*C is the delayed-neutron feed from decaying precursors.
dC/dt = (beta / Lambda) * n - lambda * CC = precursor concentration. Fission stocks precursors at rate (beta/Lambda)*n; they decay away at rate lambda*C, releasing delayed neutrons.

Read the first equation physically. Of the neutrons produced per generation, the prompt fraction (1−β) is available immediately; combined with the reactivity this gives the (rho−β)/Λ prompt growth term. The delayed fraction β is not released right away — it is instead deposited into the precursor pool C and dribbles back out later as the λC term. The precursors behave like a capacitor or a reservoir: fission charges them up, and their radioactive decay slowly discharges neutrons back into the chain.

The single most important thing to notice is the sign of the prompt term (rho − β). As long as the reactivity rho stays below β, this term is negative: on the prompt-only timescale the population would actually decay, and the chain is sustained only by the slow trickle of delayed neutrons from C. The reactor's response is therefore governed by the leisurely precursor decay, not by the lightning-fast Λ. This is the mathematical heart of why a reactor is controllable.

8.4Prompt Jump, the Reactor Period & Prompt Criticality

Now we extract the two results an operator actually uses. Suppose the reactor is critical and steady, then at t = 0 we insert a small positive reactivity step rho (with rho < β). Two things happen on two very different timescales.

The prompt jump

Almost instantly — on the Λ timescale — the power leaps to a new, higher level and then stops, because the precursor pool C has not yet had time to change. Setting the fast transient to its quasi-equilibrium gives the prompt jump (or prompt-drop) ratio:

n_after / n_before = beta / (beta - rho)The instantaneous power multiplier right after a reactivity step rho < beta. As rho -> beta the denominator -> 0 and the jump blows up.

For example a step of rho = 0.5β gives a jump factor of β/(β−0.5β) = 2: power doubles essentially at once, then climbs more slowly. The reactor does not run away because the prompt term (rho−β) is still negative — the jump is bounded and finite.

The stable reactor period

After the jump, the power settles into a slow exponential rise governed by the precursors, n(t) ∝ et/T, where T is the stable reactor period — the time for power to change by a factor of e. For small reactivity a useful approximation is:

T approx (beta - rho) / (lambda * rho)T = stable period (seconds). Larger rho -> shorter period -> faster power rise. Operators watch the period meter closely.

With realistic numbers (lambda ≈ 0.08 s⁻¹), a modest rho of a few tenths of a percent gives periods of tens of seconds — slow enough that a human or an automatic system comfortably trims the rods. The period, not the raw power, is the quantity reactor instrumentation alarms on.

Common mistakeDo not confuse supercritical with dangerous. Every reactor is briefly supercritical (rho > 0) whenever it raises power — that is normal and fully controlled, because delayed neutrons set the pace. The genuine danger line is rho = β, not rho = 0.

Prompt criticality: the cliff at rho = beta

Look again at the prompt term (rho−β)/Λ. If reactivity reaches rho = β, that term becomes zero and then positive — meaning the prompt neutrons alone are now enough to sustain and grow the chain, with no help from the delayed pool. The reactor is prompt critical, and its response time collapses from the precursor timescale (~0.1 s) back to the brutal prompt scale Λ (~10⁻⁴ s). Power then escalates on a period of milliseconds — far faster than any rod can move. Because rho = β is so pivotal, reactivity is often measured in dollars: $1 of reactivity is defined as β, so prompt criticality is exactly "one dollar." Reactor designs and operating limits are built to keep reactivity insertions safely below a dollar with large margin.

✦ Worked Example — prompt jump and reactor period from a reactivity step

A U-235-fueled thermal reactor is critical and steady. An operator withdraws a control rod, inserting a positive reactivity step of rho = +0.0020 (i.e. +200 pcm). Take β = 0.0065, an effective one-group precursor decay constant λ = 0.08 s⁻¹, and generation time Λ = 1.0×10⁻⁴ s. Find the prompt jump and the stable reactor period.

  1. Check we are below prompt critical: rho = 0.0020 and β = 0.0065, so rho < β. In dollars, rho = 0.0020 / 0.0065 = $0.31 — safely under $1. Good: the response is delayed-neutron-controlled.
  2. Prompt jump ratio: n_after / n_before = β / (β − rho) = 0.0065 / (0.0065 − 0.0020) = 0.0065 / 0.0045 ≈ 1.44. Power jumps ~44% almost instantly, then keeps rising.
  3. Stable period (approx): T ≈ (β − rho) / (λ · rho) = 0.0045 / (0.08 × 0.0020) = 0.0045 / 0.00016 ≈ 28 s.
  4. Interpret the period: power then rises by a factor of e (about 2.7×) every 28 s. Over 60 s it climbs by e60/28 ≈ e2.1 ≈ 8× — brisk, but easily trimmed by reinserting the rod.
  5. Sanity check on the danger line: had the operator instead inserted rho = +0.0065 = $1.00, the prompt-jump denominator (β−rho) would be zero — the formula diverges, signaling prompt criticality and a runaway on the Λ-scale of ~10⁻⁴ s. The 200 pcm step leaves a comfortable margin to that cliff.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate the point-kinetics equations in Python

We use the same free scientific stack from Week 1/2 — NumPy and matplotlib (the Week-1 install conda install -c conda-forge openmc numpy matplotlib jupyter already covers these). Point-kinetics is a small but stiff ODE system (the Λ and λ timescales differ by ~10³), so we lean on SciPy's solve_ivp with a stiff solver. SciPy ships in the same conda-forge channel; if it is missing, conda install -c conda-forge scipy adds it.

Let's reproduce the worked example: insert rho = +0.0020 into a critical reactor and watch the prompt jump and slow rise emerge from the equations themselves.

# point_kinetics.py — Lecture 8 (one-group precursors)
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

beta   = 0.0065        # delayed-neutron fraction (U-235)
lam    = 0.08          # effective precursor decay constant, 1/s
Lambda = 1.0e-4        # prompt generation time, s
rho    = 0.0020        # step reactivity inserted at t=0

# State y = [n, C]; start critical and steady (dn/dt = dC/dt = 0)
n0 = 1.0
C0 = beta * n0 / (Lambda * lam)       # steady-state precursor stock

def pke(t, y):
    n, C = y
    dn = ((rho - beta) / Lambda) * n + lam * C
    dC = (beta / Lambda) * n - lam * C
    return [dn, dC]

sol = solve_ivp(pke, [0, 120], [n0, C0], method="BDF",
                dense_output=True, rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-10)

t = np.linspace(0, 120, 600)
n = sol.sol(t)[0]
print(f"prompt jump (t~0.1 s): {sol.sol(0.1)[0]:.2f}  "
      f"(theory beta/(beta-rho) = {beta/(beta-rho):.2f})")
print(f"power at 60 s: {n[t.searchsorted(60)]:.1f} x initial")

# prompt jump (t~0.1 s): 1.44   (theory beta/(beta-rho) = 1.44)
# power at 60 s: ~8 x initial  -> matches period T ~ 28 s

The numerical jump (1.44) lands right on the analytic β/(β−rho), and the 60-second growth matches the ~28 s period. Now try the experiment that defines safety: set rho = 0.0065 (exactly $1) and watch the solver strain as the period collapses toward Λ — a numerical fingerprint of prompt criticality.

Q1. Roughly what fraction of fission neutrons from U-235 are delayed (the value of beta)?
A. About 65% (0.65)
B. About 6.5% (0.065)
C. About 0.65% (0.0065)
D. Exactly 0% — all fission neutrons are prompt
Show answer

C. beta ≈ 0.0065, about 1 neutron in 150. (A) and (B) overstate it by factors of 100 and 10. (D) is false — the small delayed fraction is precisely what makes control possible, so it cannot be zero.

Q2. In the point-kinetics equation dn/dt = [(rho−beta)/Lambda]·n + lambda·C, what does the term lambda·C represent?
A. The leakage of neutrons out of the core
B. The rate at which decaying precursors release delayed neutrons into the chain
C. The prompt neutron production rate
D. The rate of control-rod insertion
Show answer

B. C is the precursor concentration and lambda its decay constant, so lambda·C is the delayed-neutron feed rate. (A) leakage is folded into rho via k, not this term. (C) the prompt production is the (rho−beta)/Lambda term. (D) rod motion changes rho, not this source term.

Q3. A critical reactor receives a step reactivity insertion of rho = 0.5β. By roughly what factor does power change in the near-instant "prompt jump"?
A. It doubles (factor of 2)
B. It is unchanged (factor of 1)
C. It halves (factor of 0.5)
D. It increases a thousandfold
Show answer

A. Prompt jump = beta/(beta−rho) = beta/(beta−0.5beta) = beta/(0.5beta) = 2. (B) ignores the jump entirely. (C) is the reciprocal. (D) would require approaching prompt criticality (rho near beta), not rho = 0.5beta.

Q4. A reactor becomes "prompt critical" when reactivity reaches which value?
A. rho = 0, the moment it first becomes supercritical
B. rho = 1, when k becomes infinite
C. Any rho > 0, since any supercriticality is prompt critical
D. rho = beta (one dollar), where prompt neutrons alone sustain the chain
Show answer

D. At rho = beta the prompt term (rho−beta)/Lambda turns non-negative, so prompt neutrons alone sustain the chain on the ~10⁻⁴ s scale — a runaway. (A) and (C) confuse ordinary, controllable supercriticality with the danger line. (B) rho approaches 1 only as k -> infinity, which is unphysical and not the prompt-critical condition.

Q5. Why do delayed neutrons make a reactor controllable?
A. They absorb excess prompt neutrons and damp the chain
B. Their seconds-to-minutes delay stretches the reactor's effective response time from ~10⁻⁴ s to ~0.1 s, slow enough to control
C. They carry far more energy, so fewer are needed
D. They are released only when a control rod moves
Show answer

B. When rho < beta the precursor decay timescale dominates the response, giving periods of tens of seconds. (A) delayed neutrons cause fission, they do not absorb. (C) delayed neutrons are actually slightly lower in energy, and their count, not their energy, is what matters. (D) they come from precursor decay, independent of rods.
