Week 2 · Lecture 2

Neutron Diffusion Theory

Track a trillion neutrons not one by one but as a flowing crowd — and a single elegant equation tells you where they pile up, where they leak away, and whether the reactor lives or dies.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State Fick's law for neutrons and explain why net current flows down the flux gradient: J = −D ∇φ.
  • Interpret the diffusion coefficient D physically and relate it to the transport mean free path.
  • Derive the steady-state diffusion equation as a neutron balance: production = absorption + leakage.
  • List the assumptions baked into diffusion theory and predict where they break down.
  • Recognize how diffusion theory underpins fast nodal core simulators used to license real power reactors.

2.1From Tracking Neutrons to Tracking a Crowd

In Week 1 you met the full neutron transport equation — a seven-dimensional monster that follows the population through position, energy, direction of travel, and time. It is exact, but it is also brutal. For a real reactor with billions of fuel pins and a continuous energy spectrum, solving it head-on means Monte Carlo simulations that can run for hours on a supercomputer.

Diffusion theory makes a bargain. We give up detailed knowledge of which direction each neutron is heading and keep only how many neutrons are present and how that number varies in space. The central quantity becomes the scalar flux φ(r), the total path-length traveled per unit volume per second by all neutrons regardless of direction:

phi(r) = n(r) * vn = neutron number density (neutrons/cm^3), v = neutron speed (cm/s). Units of flux: neutrons/(cm^2 s).

The flux is what every reaction rate depends on. The number of fissions, captures, or scatters per cubic centimeter per second is always Σ φ, the product of a macroscopic cross section and the flux. So if we can find φ(r) everywhere in the core, we can find the power distribution, the burnup, and the reactivity — without ever tracking a single neutron's angle. That is the prize diffusion theory chases.

AnalogyImagine a crowded room slowly emptying through doors. You could film every individual person and reconstruct their path — that is transport theory. Or you could install people-counters and just watch the density of people thin out near the exits. The second approach loses the choreography but captures the flow with almost no effort. Diffusion theory is the people-counter view of the neutron population: it works beautifully when the crowd is dense and bumps into walls (scatters) far more often than it slips out a door (gets absorbed or leaks).

2.2Fick's Law: Neutrons Flow Down the Gradient

The heart of diffusion theory is a single intuition borrowed from heat conduction and chemical diffusion. Wherever neutrons are densely packed, more of them happen to be wandering away from the crowd than wandering back into it — purely by the statistics of random scattering. The net result is a flow of neutrons from high-flux regions toward low-flux regions, just as heat flows from hot to cold and a drop of ink spreads through still water.

We capture this with the net neutron current J, a vector giving the net number of neutrons crossing a unit area per second. Fick's law states that this current is proportional to, and points opposite to, the spatial gradient of the flux:

J = -D grad(phi)J = net current vector (neutrons/cm^2 s); D = diffusion coefficient (cm); grad(phi) = spatial gradient of the flux.

The minus sign is the whole physics: neutrons drift down the flux hill, from where they are crowded to where they are sparse. A steeper gradient drives a larger current. Where the flux is flat (gradient zero, as at the symmetric center of a bare core) there is no net current even though neutrons are streaming in every direction — the leftward and rightward streams exactly cancel.

The proportionality constant D is the diffusion coefficient, and it has a clean physical meaning. To leading order it is set by how far a neutron travels between course-changing collisions:

D = 1 / (3 * Sigma_tr) = lambda_tr / 3Sigma_tr = transport (macroscopic) cross section; lambda_tr = 1/Sigma_tr = transport mean free path (cm). Larger D means neutrons spread more easily.

Read this physically. If neutrons scatter often (large Σtr, short mean free path), they shuffle along slowly and D is small — a strong scatterer like water "holds" the neutron flux in place. If collisions are rare (long mean free path), D is large and neutrons spread far. The transport cross section also corrects for the fact that scattering off heavy nuclei tends to be forward-peaked, so a neutron "remembers" its old direction; lighter moderators scatter more isotropically. This is exactly why D appears, not the bare total cross section.

Common mistakeFick's law is an approximation, not a law of nature like the transport equation. It assumes the angular distribution of neutrons is nearly isotropic — only slightly tilted toward the low-flux direction. Do not trust J = −D∇φ within a couple of mean free paths of a strong absorber, a vacuum boundary, or a localized source, where the flux is sharply anisotropic. Students who apply Fick's law right up to a control-rod surface get currents that can be physically impossible (even exceeding the free-streaming limit).

2.3The Diffusion Equation: A Neutron Balance Sheet

Fick's law tells us how neutrons flow; to find the flux we need a bookkeeping rule that says neutrons are conserved. Consider any small volume of the core in steady state — the number of neutrons inside is not changing in time. Whatever is produced inside must be exactly balanced by what is lost. There are exactly three terms on the ledger:

Production — neutrons appear from sources S (fission, an external source) at a rate S per cm³ per second. Absorption — neutrons vanish by capture or fission at the rate Σaφ. Leakage — neutrons stream out across the surface of the volume; by the divergence theorem the net leakage per unit volume is ∇·J.

production = absorption + leakage
S = Sigma_a * phi + div(J)Steady-state neutron balance per unit volume. Every neutron born is either absorbed or leaks out.

Now substitute Fick's law, J = −D∇φ. The leakage term becomes ∇·(−D∇φ) = −D∇²φ when D is uniform. Rearranging gives the celebrated steady-state neutron diffusion equation:

-D * grad^2(phi) + Sigma_a * phi = Sgrad^2 is the Laplacian (d2/dx2 + d2/dy2 + d2/dz2). This is a single linear PDE for the flux phi(r).

This compact equation is the workhorse of classical reactor physics. The first term is leakage (curvature of the flux drains or fills a region), the second is absorption, and the right side is the source. Dividing through by D and defining the diffusion length L through L² = D/Σa puts it in its cleanest source-free form:

grad^2(phi) - phi / L^2 = -S / D, L = sqrt(D / Sigma_a)L = diffusion length (cm): the characteristic distance a neutron migrates from birth to absorption. Large L -> neutrons roam far before being absorbed.

The diffusion length L is the single most useful number that falls out of the theory. It measures how far, on average, a neutron wanders from where it is born to where it is finally absorbed. A small L (heavy absorber) keeps the flux tightly localized; a large L (clean moderator) lets neutrons spread across the whole core and is exactly why moderators must be low-absorption materials. In the next lecture this same equation, with the source set to fission, becomes the criticality condition that decides whether a reactor is subcritical, critical, or supercritical.

2.4The Assumptions — and Where Diffusion Theory Breaks

Diffusion theory is fast and intuitive precisely because it threw away information. It is worth knowing exactly what we assumed, because that tells us where the answers can be trusted and where they will lie. Fick's law and the diffusion equation rest on a handful of approximations:

(1) Near-isotropic flux. The angular distribution of neutrons is assumed to be at most linearly anisotropic — a gentle tilt toward the low-flux side. This is the core assumption that lets a single scalar φ stand in for the full angular detail.

(2) Scattering dominates absorption. Neutrons must collide and change direction many times before being absorbed (Σs » Σa), so the population genuinely "diffuses" like a gas rather than streaming straight to its fate.

(3) No localized sources or sinks, slowly varying medium. The flux and material properties should change little over one mean free path, so the gradient that drives Fick's law is well defined.

When these hold, diffusion theory is remarkably accurate — typically within a few percent of full transport in the bulk of a large, well-moderated core. When they fail, it fails predictably:

Where it breaks downNear boundaries. At the edge of the reactor neutrons leak into vacuum and cannot come back, so the flux is sharply forward-directed — anisotropic. Diffusion theory patches this with the extrapolated boundary, forcing the flux to zero a short distance (about 0.71 λtr) outside the physical surface rather than at it. Near strong absorbers (control rods, burnable poisons) the flux is heavily depressed and anisotropic; Fick's law overpredicts the current and underpredicts the local dip. Near localized sources and at material interfaces (fuel/water boundaries, voids) the flux can change over less than a mean free path, violating assumption (3). And in fast or sparsely moderated systems where Σa is not small, the whole "diffusion" picture weakens.

In modern practice these limits are handled, not ignored. Production codes run diffusion theory cheaply over the whole core, then correct it with discontinuity factors and few-group constants that are pre-computed by an expensive transport calculation on small representative regions. The result keeps diffusion theory's speed while restoring much of transport theory's accuracy — which is why diffusion solvers, decades old, still sit at the heart of the simulators that license commercial power reactors.

✦ Worked Example — diffusion length and leakage current in a moderator

A block of graphite is bombarded so that the thermal flux varies as φ(x) = φ₀ e−x/L with φ₀ = 1.0×10¹² n/(cm² s). For thermal neutrons in graphite take the diffusion coefficient D = 0.84 cm and the macroscopic absorption cross section Σa = 0.00024 cm⁻¹. Find (a) the diffusion length L and (b) the net current J at x = 0.

  1. Diffusion length: L = √(D/Σa) = √(0.84 / 0.00024) = √3500 ≈ 59.2 cm. Thermal neutrons in graphite migrate roughly half a meter before capture — graphite is a famously clean moderator, which is exactly why L is so large.
  2. Set up Fick's law in 1-D: J(x) = −D dφ/dx. With φ(x) = φ₀ e−x/L, the derivative is dφ/dx = −(φ₀/L) e−x/L.
  3. Combine: J(x) = −D · (−φ₀/L) e−x/L = (Dφ₀/L) e−x/L. The current is positive (flowing toward +x, away from the high-flux face) as expected.
  4. Evaluate at x = 0: J(0) = Dφ₀/L = (0.84 × 1.0×10¹²) / 59.2 ≈ 1.42×10¹⁰ n/(cm² s).
  5. Sanity check: The current is far below the free-streaming limit (φ₀/4 = 2.5×10¹¹ n/cm²s for one-sided flow), confirming the flux is only weakly anisotropic — diffusion theory is valid here, deep inside a good moderator and away from any boundary.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the 1-D diffusion equation numerically

Verify: run python -c "import numpy, openmc; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set for the whole course.

We discretize −Dφ'' + Σaφ = S on a slab with a source on the left, using a finite-difference Laplacian. This is the bones of every diffusion solver — OpenMC will later give us the transport "truth" to compare against.

# diffusion_1d.py — Lecture 2: steady-state 1-D diffusion in a slab
import numpy as np

D      = 0.84        # diffusion coefficient, cm (graphite, thermal)
Sig_a  = 0.00024     # macroscopic absorption cross section, 1/cm
width  = 200.0       # slab width, cm
N      = 400         # number of mesh cells
dx     = width / N

# build the tridiagonal matrix for  -D phi'' + Sig_a phi = S
A = np.zeros((N, N))
S = np.zeros(N)
for i in range(N):
    A[i, i] = 2*D/dx**2 + Sig_a          # diagonal: leakage + absorption
    if i > 0:     A[i, i-1] = -D/dx**2    # coupling to left neighbor
    if i < N-1:   A[i, i+1] = -D/dx**2    # coupling to right neighbor

S[0] = 1.0e12 * D / dx**2   # fixed high flux on the left face (source side)

phi = np.linalg.solve(A, S)              # solve A phi = S for the flux profile

L = np.sqrt(D / Sig_a)                   # diffusion length, cm
print(f"diffusion length L = {L:.1f} cm")
print(f"peak flux = {phi.max():.3e}, edge flux = {phi[-1]:.3e}")
# Flux decays roughly as exp(-x/L); L ~ 59 cm matches the worked example.

Change Sig_a to a larger value (say 0.02, like a borated absorber) and watch L shrink and the flux collapse near the source — the same physics that lets a control rod carve a local dip in the core flux.

Q1. In Fick's law for neutrons, J = −D∇φ, what does the minus sign tell you?
A. Neutrons are absorbed wherever the flux is highest
B. The net current flows down the flux gradient, from high flux to low flux
C. The diffusion coefficient D is always negative
D. Neutrons travel faster where the flux is lower
Show answer

B. The minus sign means the net current points opposite to the gradient — neutrons drift from crowded (high-flux) to sparse (low-flux) regions. (A) confuses current with absorption. (C) D is a positive material property. (D) flux is density×speed, not a statement about local speed.

Q2. The diffusion coefficient is D = 1/(3Σtr). A neutron in a strongly scattering moderator (large Σtr, short mean free path) therefore has a diffusion coefficient that is…
A. small — the neutron shuffles along slowly and spreads little
B. large — frequent scattering flings the neutron far
C. zero — scattering stops all net motion
D. negative — strong scattering reverses the current
Show answer

A. D is inversely proportional to Σtr, so a large transport cross section (short mean free path) gives a small D and slow spreading. (B) inverts the relationship. (C) net motion is driven by the gradient, not eliminated. (D) D is never negative.

Q3. The steady-state diffusion equation −D∇²φ + Σaφ = S expresses what physical principle?
A. Conservation of energy per fission
B. Newton's second law applied to each neutron
C. Conservation of neutrons: production = absorption + leakage
D. The mass-energy relation E = mc²
Show answer

C. It is a steady-state neutron balance per unit volume: the source equals absorption (Σaφ) plus leakage (−D∇²φ from ∇·J). (A) and (D) concern energy, not neutron number. (B) diffusion theory abandons individual trajectories entirely.

Q4. The diffusion length L = √(D/Σa) physically represents…
A. the speed of a thermal neutron
B. the time between two fissions
C. the energy released per absorption
D. the characteristic distance a neutron migrates from birth to absorption
Show answer

D. L sets the scale over which the flux decays and measures how far a neutron wanders before being absorbed; a clean moderator gives a large L. (A) is a velocity, (B) a time, (C) an energy — none has units of length, and L is a length.

Q5. Which situation is LEAST suited to diffusion theory, because its assumptions break down?
A. The bulk interior of a large, well-moderated thermal core
B. A region many mean free paths from any boundary or absorber
C. The thin layer right next to a strong control-rod absorber
D. A uniform moderator with weak absorption and slow flux variation
Show answer

C. Next to a strong absorber the flux is sharply depressed and anisotropic, violating the near-isotropic assumption behind Fick's law. (A), (B), and (D) all describe exactly the conditions — isotropic flux, scattering » absorption, slow variation — where diffusion theory is accurate.
