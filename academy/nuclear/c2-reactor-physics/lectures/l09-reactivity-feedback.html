Week 9 · Lecture 9

Reactivity Feedback & Temperature Coefficients

The deepest safety feature of a reactor isn't a control rod or a computer — it's the physics that makes a too-hot core quietly turn itself down before anyone touches a thing.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define reactivity rho = (k − 1)/k and explain why it, not k alone, measures the “departure from critical.”
  • Describe how a change in core conditions feeds back on k through cross sections and neutron leakage.
  • Explain the fuel (Doppler) temperature coefficient and why it acts instantly and is always negative in oxide fuel.
  • Distinguish the moderator temperature coefficient from the void coefficient, and compute reactivity from a coefficient and a temperature change.
  • Argue why an overall negative power coefficient gives inherent stability — and why a positive void coefficient is dangerous.

9.1Reactivity: Measuring the Departure from Critical

By now you know the multiplication factor k: the ratio of neutrons in one fission generation to the number in the generation before. When k = 1 the chain reaction exactly sustains itself (critical); k > 1 means the population grows (supercritical); k < 1 means it dies away (subcritical). That single number tells you the direction the reactor is heading, but it is an awkward yardstick when you want to talk about how far from balance you are, or to add up several independent effects.

So reactor physicists work instead with reactivity, defined as the fractional excess of k above 1:

rho = (k - 1) / krho = 0 at critical; rho > 0 supercritical; rho < 0 subcritical. Dimensionless, but quoted in special units below.

Reactivity is the natural language of perturbations. If you nudge the core — insert a control rod, heat the fuel, boil a little coolant — each nudge shifts k by some small amount, and the corresponding reactivity changes simply add up. That additivity is exactly why we change variables from k to rho: feedback effects layer cleanly on top of one another.

Because the numbers are tiny (a few thousandths), reactivity is usually quoted in per cent mille (pcm), where 1 pcm = 10−5, or in dollars, where one dollar equals the delayed-neutron fraction beta (about 650 pcm for a uranium core). A reactor held one dollar supercritical would be prompt critical — the runaway regime you met in Week 8. Keeping reactivity well below a dollar is what makes a reactor controllable.

AnalogyThink of a thermostat-controlled room. The temperature setpoint is “critical” (rho = 0). Reactivity is the error signal — how far above or below setpoint you are right now. Just as a good heating system responds to that error and pushes the room back toward setpoint, a well-designed reactor responds to positive reactivity by generating negative reactivity that cancels it. The whole lecture is about where that self-correcting error signal comes from.

9.2Reactivity Feedback: The Core Talks Back

Here is the central idea. The multiplication factor k is not a fixed property stamped on the reactor at the factory — it depends on the physical state of the core right now: how hot the fuel is, how dense the coolant is, how the neutron spectrum is shaped. When the reactor's power changes, those conditions change, and k changes with them. That loop — power changes the state, the state changes k, the changed k changes power — is reactivity feedback.

The microscopic levers are the same ones you have been tracking all course: the cross sections that govern absorption and fission, and the leakage of neutrons out of the core. A feedback mechanism is any chain of cause and effect that ties a state variable (temperature, density) to one of these and so to k.

We package each mechanism as a reactivity coefficient: the change in reactivity per unit change in some state variable. For a temperature T,

alpha_T = d(rho) / dTalpha_T < 0 means heating the core adds negative reactivity (self-stabilizing). Units: pcm per kelvin (pcm/K).

The sign is everything. If alpha_T is negative, then any rise in temperature inserts negative reactivity, pushing k back down toward 1 and opposing the disturbance — negative feedback. If alpha_T is positive, heating adds more positive reactivity, which raises power, which raises temperature further: a self-reinforcing spiral. Reactors are designed so that the sum of all feedback coefficients — the overall power coefficient — is negative across the whole operating range.

9.3The Fuel (Doppler) Temperature Coefficient

The single most important feedback in a thermal reactor lives in the fuel itself, and it is breathtakingly fast. When fission power rises, the fuel pellets heat up within milliseconds — long before that heat has even reached the coolant. Whatever feedback the hot fuel provides therefore acts almost instantly, which is precisely the speed you want from a safety mechanism.

That feedback is the Doppler effect. Uranium-238 (the bulk of the fuel) has sharp resonance peaks in its absorption cross section at intermediate neutron energies — narrow energies where a passing neutron is almost certain to be captured and lost. As the fuel heats, the U-238 nuclei vibrate faster with thermal motion. A neutron of a fixed lab energy now sees a spread of relative velocities, so the sharp resonance is smeared out: it gets shorter and broader. Crucially, the area under the resonance is roughly conserved, but the broadened wings reach out to energies that were previously “in the shadow” of the tall narrow peak (where self-shielding had protected neutrons). Net effect: more neutrons get captured in U-238 resonances when the fuel is hot.

T_fuel up -> U-238 resonances broaden -> more resonance capture -> k down -> rho downThis is “Doppler broadening.” alpha_Doppler < 0, and it responds in milliseconds because it lives in the fuel.

Because more parasitic capture in U-238 means fewer neutrons survive to cause fission, k falls and the inserted reactivity is negative. The Doppler coefficient is therefore always negative in standard oxide fuel — and, vitally, it cannot be switched off, delayed, or jammed. It is pure physics built into the fuel. This is the feedback that arrested the power excursion in landmark safety tests of inherently-safe reactor designs, where operators deliberately yanked all the rods and the reactor simply leveled off and cooled on its own.

AnalogyPicture the U-238 resonance as a tall, skinny goalpost that neutrons mostly fly past on either side. Cold fuel = a narrow goalpost: easy to miss. Heating the fuel is like shaking the goalpost rapidly side to side — to a neutron it now looks wider and blurrier, much harder to slip past. More neutrons are “caught,” so the chain reaction throttles itself the instant the fuel gets hot.
Common mistakeStudents often say Doppler broadening makes the resonance “bigger” so it absorbs more. The peak actually gets shorter, not taller — its height drops while its width grows, keeping the integrated area nearly constant. The extra absorption comes from defeating self-shielding: a tall narrow peak shields the inner fuel because the outer layer mops up all neutrons at the peak energy, but a broadened peak captures over a wider energy band that the shielding cannot fully cover. “Taller peak = more absorption” is the wrong mechanism even if it lands on the right sign.

9.4Moderator Temperature & Void Coefficients

The second family of feedbacks lives in the moderator/coolant — the water (in a light-water reactor) that both slows neutrons to thermal energies and carries heat away. These feedbacks are slower than Doppler because the coolant heats only after the fuel has passed its heat across, but they are just as important to overall stability.

Moderator temperature coefficient (MTC)

When water heats up it expands and becomes less dense. Fewer water molecules per unit volume means fewer scattering collisions, so neutrons are moderated less effectively and a hotter neutron spectrum results. In a well-designed light-water reactor this reduces k — partly through poorer moderation and increased leakage, and partly through a harder spectrum that shifts neutrons into U-238 resonances. The MTC is engineered to be negative.

T_mod up -> water density down -> weaker moderation + more leakage -> k downTrue when the lattice is “under-moderated.” Soluble boron in the coolant can push the MTC positive — hence boron limits.

The catch: if there is too much absorbing boron dissolved in the coolant, losing water density also removes boron (a poison), which adds reactivity. That can flip the MTC positive. This is why beginning-of-cycle boron concentration is capped — to guarantee the MTC stays negative.

The void coefficient

Push the moderator heating to its extreme and the coolant boils, forming steam voids — pockets of vapor that are essentially empty of moderator. The void coefficient measures the reactivity change per percent of void formed. In a light-water reactor, voiding removes moderator, neutrons stay too fast to fission efficiently, and k drops: the void coefficient is negative, a powerful safety feature. Boiling-water reactors even use it to control power by adjusting coolant flow.

void coefficient = d(rho) / d(void fraction)Negative in light-water designs. A POSITIVE void coefficient means boiling raises power — the hallmark of an unstable design.

But the sign is not guaranteed by nature — it is a consequence of design. In a reactor that uses a separate solid moderator (such as graphite) while water acts mainly as a coolant and neutron absorber, boiling the water removes an absorber without removing the moderation. Then voiding adds reactivity: a positive void coefficient. Combined with a slow control system, that is the feedback geometry behind the most catastrophic reactor accident in history. Modern safety standards forbid a large positive void coefficient precisely for this reason.

Common mistakeDo not assume “less water always lowers reactivity.” The sign of the void coefficient depends entirely on what the water is doing. If water is the primary moderator (light-water reactor), losing it is bad for the chain reaction (negative void coefficient — safe). If water is mainly an absorber sitting beside a solid moderator, losing it helps the chain reaction (positive void coefficient — dangerous). Same physics, opposite sign, decided by design.
✦ Worked Example — adding up feedback during a power rise

A pressurized-water reactor sits exactly critical (rho = 0) at low power. The operator withdraws a control rod, inserting +120 pcm of reactivity. As power rises, the fuel heats by 300 K and the moderator heats by 8 K. The Doppler coefficient is −2.5 pcm/K and the moderator temperature coefficient is −15 pcm/K. Where does the reactor settle, and is it stable?

  1. Doppler feedback (fuel): rho_Doppler = alpha_D × ΔT_fuel = (−2.5 pcm/K)(300 K) = −750 pcm.
  2. Moderator feedback: rho_mod = alpha_M × ΔT_mod = (−15 pcm/K)(8 K) = −120 pcm.
  3. Sum the reactivities (this is why we use rho — they add): rho_total = +120 − 750 − 120 = −750 pcm.
  4. Interpret: the total is strongly negative, so by the time the core has heated this far the net reactivity is well below zero. In practice the temperatures don't climb that far — heating stops as soon as the negative feedback exactly cancels the +120 pcm rod withdrawal. The reactor finds a new, slightly hotter steady state and holds there.
  5. Stability check: the feedback opposed the disturbance (the +120 pcm was met by hundreds of pcm of negative reactivity per modest heat-up). A small step in reactivity produced a small, self-limiting rise in temperature, not a runaway — this is exactly what an inherently stable, negative-power-coefficient core does. Convert to dollars (beta ≈ 650 pcm): the +120 pcm insertion is only ~0.18 $, far below prompt critical.

🛠️ Hands-On: measure the Doppler coefficient in OpenMC

We use the same free stack as all course — OpenMC for Monte Carlo neutron transport, plus NumPy and matplotlib. If you set up the environment in the Week 1/2 install step you're ready; if not, the one-time install details lives in the early lectures. The key new idea here: OpenMC reads temperature-dependent cross sections, so we can run the same fuel pin at two fuel temperatures and read off the reactivity change directly.

# doppler_coefficient.py — Lecture 9
# Run one fuel pin at two fuel temperatures; compute alpha_Doppler.
import openmc
import numpy as np

def run_pin(fuel_temp_K):
    # UO2 fuel enriched to 4%, with U-238 carrying the resonances
    fuel = openmc.Material(name="UO2")
    fuel.add_nuclide("U235", 0.04)
    fuel.add_nuclide("U238", 0.96)
    fuel.add_nuclide("O16", 2.0)
    fuel.set_density("g/cm3", 10.4)
    fuel.temperature = fuel_temp_K        # <-- the variable we sweep

    water = openmc.Material(name="water")
    water.add_nuclide("H1", 2.0)
    water.add_nuclide("O16", 1.0)
    water.set_density("g/cm3", 0.74)
    water.add_s_alpha_beta("c_H_in_H2O")

    mats = openmc.Materials([fuel, water])
    mats.export_to_xml()

    # simple infinite pin-cell lattice (reflective boundaries)
    r = openmc.ZCylinder(r=0.41)
    box = openmc.model.RectangularPrism(1.26, 1.26, boundary_type="reflective")
    pin = openmc.Cell(fill=fuel, region=-r)
    mod = openmc.Cell(fill=water, region=+r & -box)
    openmc.Geometry([pin, mod]).export_to_xml()

    settings = openmc.Settings()
    settings.batches = 120
    settings.inactive = 20
    settings.particles = 20000
    settings.temperature = {"method": "interpolation"}  # enable Doppler
    settings.export_to_xml()

    openmc.run(output=False)
    with openmc.StatePoint(f"statepoint.120.h5") as sp:
        return sp.keff.n, sp.keff.s   # k_eff value, std-dev

k_cold, s_cold = run_pin(600.0)     # 600 K fuel
k_hot,  s_hot  = run_pin(900.0)     # 900 K fuel

# reactivity from each k, then the coefficient (pcm per K)
rho = lambda k: (k - 1.0) / k
alpha = (rho(k_hot) - rho(k_cold)) / (900.0 - 600.0) * 1e5
print(f"k(600K) = {k_cold:.5f}   k(900K) = {k_hot:.5f}")
print(f"Doppler coefficient = {alpha:.2f} pcm/K")
# Expect k to DROP as fuel heats -> alpha is NEGATIVE (~ -2 to -3 pcm/K).

Heating only the fuel isolates the Doppler effect from moderator feedback. You should find k_hot < k_cold and a negative coefficient. Try sweeping four temperatures and fitting alpha = d(rho)/dT — and watch the U-238 resonance integral grow when you tally capture vs. energy.

💬 Discussion

A negative Doppler coefficient acts in milliseconds; the moderator and void coefficients act over seconds; xenon poisoning (which you'll meet later) acts over hours. Suppose you were arguing the safety case for a brand-new reactor design to a skeptical review panel. Why is the speed of a feedback mechanism just as important as its sign? Could a reactor with the “right” signs on all its coefficients still be unsafe? Defend your position with the physics from this lecture.

Show a model response

Speed matters because feedback only protects you against disturbances it can outrun. A power excursion driven by a fast reactivity insertion can grow on a timescale of milliseconds. The Doppler coefficient is the only feedback fast enough to catch such a transient, because it lives in the fuel and responds the instant fission heat appears — no heat transfer delay. Moderator and void feedback lag by seconds because the coolant must first receive heat from the fuel; xenon feedback lags by hours. So a reactor whose only negative coefficient were a slow moderator term could still suffer a damaging prompt excursion before that feedback engaged: the signs would all be “right,” yet the reactor would be unsafe against fast transients. This is exactly why a prompt, negative Doppler coefficient is treated as a non-negotiable design requirement, and why prompt-critical operation (rho > one dollar) is forbidden — past prompt critical, power doubles faster than any feedback can respond. A complete safety case must therefore match each credible disturbance to a feedback that is both correctly signed and fast enough to act before damage occurs.

✓ Self-check rubric: (1) States that fast feedback (Doppler, ms) outpaces slow feedback (moderator/void seconds, xenon hours). (2) Connects feedback speed to the timescale of the disturbance it must counter. (3) Gives a concrete failure mode — a fast transient outrunning a slow-only feedback. (4) Ties the argument to prompt criticality / the one-dollar limit. A strong answer hits at least three of these.

Q1. A reactor has k = 1.002. What is its reactivity, approximately?
A. −200 pcm (subcritical)
B. About +200 pcm (supercritical)
C. Exactly 0 (critical)
D. +1002 pcm
Show answer

B. rho = (k−1)/k = 0.002/1.002 ≈ 0.001996 ≈ +200 pcm, and k > 1 means supercritical. (A) has the wrong sign. (C) holds only when k = 1. (D) confuses k itself with reactivity and uses the wrong scale.

Q2. The fuel (Doppler) temperature coefficient acts within milliseconds. What makes it so fast?
A. The control rods drop automatically when fuel heats
B. It depends on the coolant reaching boiling temperature
C. It originates in the fuel itself, which heats instantly when fission power rises — before any heat transfer
D. Delayed neutrons carry the feedback signal
Show answer

C. Doppler broadening of U-238 resonances happens in the fuel the moment it heats, with no heat-transfer delay. (A) is an external engineered action, not the Doppler effect. (B) describes void feedback, which is slower. (D) confuses delayed neutrons (kinetics) with temperature feedback.

Q3. As fuel temperature rises, how does Doppler broadening change the U-238 absorption resonances and k?
A. Resonances broaden and shorten; resonance capture increases, so k decreases
B. Resonances grow taller; capture increases, so k increases
C. Resonances vanish entirely, so capture drops and k rises
D. Nothing changes; cross sections are temperature-independent
Show answer

A. Thermal motion smears each resonance shorter and wider; defeating self-shielding raises net resonance capture in U-238, so k falls (negative feedback). (B) gets the wrong mechanism (peaks shorten, not grow) AND the wrong sign for k. (C) resonances broaden, they don't disappear. (D) cross sections are strongly temperature-dependent — that is the whole effect.

Q4. In a standard light-water reactor, what is the sign of the void coefficient, and why?
A. Positive — steam absorbs fewer neutrons, raising k
B. Positive — voids concentrate the fuel
C. Zero — voids have no effect on a water-cooled core
D. Negative — voids remove moderator, so neutrons stay too fast to fission efficiently and k drops
Show answer

D. In a light-water reactor the water is the primary moderator; boiling it away removes moderation, hardens the spectrum, and lowers k — a safe, negative void coefficient. (A) and (B) describe a positive coefficient, which is the dangerous geometry of designs where water is mainly an absorber beside a solid moderator. (C) ignores the strong moderation role of water.

Q5. A core sits critical, then a +100 pcm rod withdrawal heats the fuel by 200 K with a Doppler coefficient of −2.0 pcm/K. What is the net reactivity from these two effects?
A. −300 pcm
B. +500 pcm
C. +100 pcm, since feedback never cancels rod motion
D. −100 pcm
Show answer

A. Doppler reactivity = (−2.0 pcm/K)(200 K) = −400 pcm; net = +100 − 400 = −300 pcm. Reactivities add, so the negative feedback overwhelms the insertion — the core is self-limiting. (B) adds with the wrong sign. (C) is false — that is exactly what feedback does. (D) forgets to multiply the coefficient by the full 200 K (it uses only −200 pcm).
