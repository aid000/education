Week 4 · Lecture 4

Criticality & the Multiplication Factor

A reactor lives or dies on a single dimensionless number — push it a hair above 1 and the neutron population blooms exponentially; nudge it below and the chain reaction quietly dies.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the effective multiplication factor k_eff as production over loss (absorption plus leakage), and classify subcritical, critical, and supercritical states.
  • Track a single generation of neutrons through a finite reactor and see why leakage makes k_eff smaller than the infinite-medium value k_inf.
  • State the criticality condition as material buckling equal to geometric buckling, and explain what each buckling measures.
  • Compute the critical size of a bare reactor from its material composition and shape.
  • Connect critical size to critical mass, and explain why squeezing or reflecting a core changes both.

4.1The Multiplication Factor: Bookkeeping a Neutron Generation

Everything a reactor does — idling at zero power, ramping to full output, or scramming to shutdown — comes down to one question asked of each generation of neutrons: do the neutrons born in this generation produce more, fewer, or exactly as many neutrons in the next? The answer is a single ratio, the effective multiplication factor k_eff.

Picture the entire neutron population at one instant as a "generation." Those neutrons fly around, scatter, and eventually meet one of three fates: they are absorbed in fuel (often causing fission and releasing new neutrons), they are absorbed in non-fuel material (coolant, structure, control rods) where they vanish without producing offspring, or they leak out through the surface of the core and are lost forever. k_eff is simply the ratio of neutrons produced to neutrons lost:

k_eff = neutrons produced (this generation) / neutrons lost (absorption + leakage)A pure ratio of one generation to the next. Dimensionless. The single most important number in reactor physics.

The three regimes follow immediately. If k_eff = 1 the population exactly replaces itself generation after generation — the reactor is critical and runs at steady power. If k_eff < 1 each generation is smaller than the last and the chain reaction dies away — subcritical. If k_eff > 1 each generation is larger and the population grows exponentially — supercritical. A power reactor spends nearly all its life poised infinitesimally above and below 1, the operator nudging reactivity to hold it on the knife edge.

It is useful to separate two effects. In an imagined infinite medium there is no surface, so nothing leaks; the multiplication factor there is called k_inf and depends only on the material. A real, finite reactor loses some neutrons through its boundary, so we multiply k_inf by the fraction of neutrons that do not leak out, the non-leakage probability P_NL:

k_eff = k_inf * P_NLk_inf is set by the material recipe; P_NL (between 0 and 1) is set by the size and shape. Bigger core -> less surface per volume -> P_NL closer to 1.
AnalogyThink of a rumor spreading in a room. Each person who hears it tells, on average, k other people before losing interest. If k > 1 the rumor sweeps the whole room (supercritical); if k < 1 it fizzles out (subcritical); if k = 1 it circulates at a steady hum (critical). "Leakage" is people leaving the room before they can pass it on — a small, crowded room loses fewer people out the door per conversation, exactly like a large reactor leaks proportionally fewer neutrons.

4.2Leakage, Diffusion, and the Birth of Buckling

Where does leakage come from, and how do we put a number on it? Recall from Week 3 that the slow-neutron flux φ(r) in a bare reactor obeys the one-group diffusion equation. In a critical reactor, production from fission must balance absorption plus the net leakage described by the diffusion (Laplacian) term:

D * grad^2(phi) - Sigma_a * phi + (1/k) * nu*Sigma_f * phi = 0D = diffusion coefficient, Sigma_a = macroscopic absorption, nu*Sigma_f = neutrons produced per cm by fission. At criticality k = 1.

Divide through and rearrange, and this becomes a beautifully simple eigenvalue equation. The flux shape must satisfy:

grad^2(phi) + B^2 * phi = 0The flux is an eigenfunction of the Laplacian; B^2 is the eigenvalue. This single equation governs every flux shape we met last week.

The quantity B² is called buckling — and the name is literal. It measures how sharply the flux curves (buckles) in space. A large B² means a steeply curved flux that drops quickly to zero at the boundary; that steep gradient drives a large leakage current out of the core. A small B² means a flat, gently curving flux with little leakage. Buckling is the geometric fingerprint of leakage.

Here is the subtle and powerful part. The equation above can be read in two completely different ways, giving two distinct bucklings that must agree for a steady reactor:

Material buckling B²_m comes from the physics of the material. Solving the balance for the value of B² that the neutron economy demands gives:

B^2_m = (nu*Sigma_f - Sigma_a) / D = (k_inf - 1) / L^2L^2 = D / Sigma_a is the diffusion area. B^2_m is positive only when k_inf > 1 — i.e. only fuel that multiplies in an infinite medium can ever go critical.

Geometric buckling B²_g comes from the shape and size of the core. It is the smallest eigenvalue of grad²φ + B²φ = 0 for that geometry with the flux forced to (nearly) zero at the surface. For a few standard bare shapes:

sphere R: B^2_g = (pi / R)^2 slab a: B^2_g = (pi / a)^2cylinder (R, H): B^2_g = (2.405 / R)^2 + (pi / H)^2. Bigger dimensions -> smaller B^2_g -> less leakage.
Common mistakeStudents often think buckling is "a property of the fuel" or "a property of the box," but it is both — there are two bucklings. B²_m is fixed once you choose the material recipe; B²_g is fixed once you choose the size and shape. They are independent until you demand criticality, which forces them to be equal. Treating B² as a single given number, rather than the meeting point of material and geometry, is the number-one conceptual error in this topic.

4.3The Criticality Condition: Material Buckling = Geometric Buckling

Now we collect the payoff. The neutron economy (material) supplies a buckling B²_m; the geometry (size and shape) supplies a buckling B²_g. A reactor is exactly critical when the curvature the material wants matches the curvature the geometry provides:

CRITICAL <=> B^2_m = B^2_gThe criticality condition. Material physics and core geometry meet at a single shared buckling value.

This compact statement contains the whole story of k_eff. Using the non-leakage form, for a bare core the non-leakage probability is P_NL = 1 / (1 + B²_g L²), so:

k_eff = k_inf / (1 + B^2_g * L^2)Make the core bigger -> B^2_g shrinks -> denominator -> 1 -> k_eff rises toward k_inf. Make it tiny -> B^2_g huge -> leakage dominates -> k_eff -> 0.

Read it as a tug-of-war. The material pulls k_eff up toward k_inf; leakage, encoded in B²_g L², drags it back down. Setting k_eff = 1 in this expression reproduces exactly B²_m = B²_g — the two views are the same statement. The practical consequence is enormous: a given fuel recipe has one critical size. Below it, leakage wins and the reactor cannot sustain a chain reaction no matter how perfect the fuel; above it, the reactor is supercritical and must be controlled.

AnalogyImagine heating a pot of water on a stove. The burner adds heat (production); the pot's surface radiates heat away (leakage). A tiny espresso cup loses heat through its walls faster than a small flame can supply it — it never boils. A big stockpot has so much volume per unit surface that the same flame brings it to a rolling boil. "Critical size" is the pot just big enough that supply finally balances loss. Shrink it and the chain (the boil) cannot hold.

4.4Critical Size and Critical Mass

Once the criticality condition fixes the critical size, the critical mass follows immediately: it is just the mass of fissile material contained in a core of that size and composition. Multiply the critical volume by the fuel density (and fissile fraction), and you have the minimum amount of material that can sustain a chain reaction in that configuration.

M_critical = rho * V_critical = rho * (4/3) * pi * R_critical^3 (bare sphere)The sphere is the optimum shape: it has the least surface area per volume, hence the least leakage and the smallest critical mass of any geometry.

Two levers change the critical mass dramatically, and both are central to real engineering and safety:

Density. Critical size scales roughly with the neutron mean free path, which is inversely proportional to density. Compress the fuel and the same number of atoms occupies a smaller, less-leaky sphere — the critical mass falls. This is precisely why criticality safety rules forbid concentrating or compressing fissile solutions, and it is why a fixed amount of material can be safely subcritical when spread out but dangerously supercritical when packed.

Reflection. Surround the core with a reflector — a layer of moderator like graphite, beryllium, or water — and many neutrons that would have leaked out are instead scattered back in. P_NL rises, so a smaller, lighter core can reach k_eff = 1. A good reflector can cut the bare critical mass by roughly half. Every power reactor uses this deliberately; criticality-safety engineers must also account for accidental reflectors (a flooded room, a concrete wall) that can turn a "safe" amount of material critical.

The same logic explains why fuel geometry matters in storage and processing. A long thin pipe of fissile solution has enormous surface-to-volume and leaks profusely, staying subcritical; pour the identical solution into a compact tank and it can go critical. Reactor cores are engineered fat and reflected to minimize leakage; storage arrays are engineered thin and separated to maximize it.

✦ Worked Example — critical radius of a bare spherical reactor

A bare spherical core has an infinite-medium multiplication factor k_inf = 1.20 and a diffusion area L² = 60 cm². Treating it with one-group bare-reactor theory, find (a) the material buckling, (b) the critical radius, and (c) the corrected physical radius if the flux extrapolates to zero a distance d = 2.0 cm beyond the surface.

  1. Material buckling: B²_m = (k_inf − 1) / L² = (1.20 − 1) / 60 = 0.20 / 60 = 0.003333 cm⁻².
  2. Criticality condition: set geometric buckling equal to material buckling. For a bare sphere B²_g = (π / R)², so (π / R)² = 0.003333 cm⁻².
  3. Solve for R: π / R = √0.003333 = 0.05774 cm⁻¹, so R = π / 0.05774 = 3.1416 / 0.05774 ≈ 54.4 cm (this is the extrapolated radius where the flux hits zero).
  4. Extrapolation correction: the flux vanishes a distance d beyond the physical surface, so the physical radius is R_phys = R − d = 54.4 − 2.0 ≈ 52.4 cm. The reactor's actual sphere is slightly smaller than the bare estimate.
  5. Sanity check: with R = 54.4 cm, B²_g = (π/54.4)² = (0.05775)² = 0.003335 cm⁻² ≈ B²_m. The two bucklings match — the core is critical. A core much smaller than ~52 cm would be subcritical; much larger would be supercritical and require control absorbers to hold k_eff at 1.

🛠️ Hands-On: find the critical radius two ways in Python

We use the same free stack from the Week 1/2 setup — NumPy and Matplotlib for the analytic curve, and OpenMC (open-source Monte Carlo transport) to confirm it on a real geometry. The full one-time install lives in the early lectures' install panel; nothing new is needed here. First, the pure one-group formula:

# critical_radius.py — Lecture 4 (one-group bare sphere)
import numpy as np

k_inf = 1.20            # infinite-medium multiplication factor
L2    = 60.0            # diffusion area L^2, cm^2

B2_m = (k_inf - 1.0) / L2                # material buckling, cm^-2
R_crit = np.pi / np.sqrt(B2_m)           # since (pi/R)^2 = B2_m for a sphere
print(f"material buckling  = {B2_m:.6f} cm^-2")
print(f"critical radius    = {R_crit:.2f} cm")

# k_eff as a function of radius: k_inf / (1 + B2_g * L2)
def k_eff(R):
    B2_g = (np.pi / R) ** 2
    return k_inf / (1.0 + B2_g * L2)

for R in (30, 54.4, 80):
    print(f"R = {R:>5} cm -> k_eff = {k_eff(R):.3f}")

# material buckling  = 0.003333 cm^-2
# critical radius    = 54.41 cm
# R =    30 cm -> k_eff = 0.724   (subcritical)
# R =  54.4 cm -> k_eff = 1.000   (critical)
# R =    80 cm -> k_eff = 1.098   (supercritical)

Now confirm with a real Monte Carlo eigenvalue calculation. OpenMC reports k_eff directly from a k-eigenvalue run, so we can sweep the sphere radius and watch it cross 1:

# crit_search_openmc.py — sweep sphere radius, read k_eff
import openmc

def keff_for_radius(R_cm):
    fuel = openmc.Material()
    fuel.add_nuclide('U235', 1.0)
    fuel.set_density('g/cm3', 18.7)        # compact metal core

    sph  = openmc.Sphere(r=R_cm, boundary_type='vacuum')  # bare: neutrons leak out
    cell = openmc.Cell(fill=fuel, region=-sph)
    geom = openmc.Geometry([cell])

    settings = openmc.Settings()
    settings.run_mode = 'eigenvalue'       # OpenMC solves for k_eff
    settings.batches, settings.inactive, settings.particles = 120, 20, 5000
    model = openmc.Model(geom, openmc.Materials([fuel]), settings)
    sp_path = model.run()
    with openmc.StatePoint(sp_path) as sp:
        return sp.keff.nominal_value

for R in (7.0, 8.5, 10.0):                 # bare metal critical radius ~ 8-9 cm
    print(f"R = {R} cm -> k_eff = {keff_for_radius(R):.3f}")
# Watch k_eff rise through 1.0 as R grows: the Monte Carlo critical size.

The one-group formula and the Monte Carlo run won't give the same radius (the diffusion model is approximate, and real cross sections are energy-dependent) — but both show the same physics: k_eff rises monotonically through 1 as the core grows. Try changing boundary_type to 'reflective' to approximate a perfect reflector and watch the critical radius shrink.

Q1. A reactor has k_eff = 0.97. What is its state and what happens to the neutron population?
A. Supercritical; the population grows each generation
B. Critical; the population holds exactly steady
C. Subcritical; each generation is smaller and the chain dies out
D. Prompt critical; the population is undefined
Show answer

C. k_eff < 1 means each generation produces fewer neutrons than the last, so the population decays — subcritical. (A) describes k_eff > 1. (B) requires exactly k_eff = 1. (D) "prompt critical" is a kinetics threshold above 1, not 0.97, and the population is well defined.

Q2. Why is k_eff always smaller than k_inf for a real, finite reactor built from the same material?
A. Because fission produces fewer neutrons per event in small cores
B. Because a finite core loses some neutrons by leakage through its surface, so P_NL < 1
C. Because k_inf already includes leakage and double-counts it
D. Because the diffusion coefficient is negative
Show answer

B. k_eff = k_inf · P_NL, and the non-leakage probability P_NL is always less than 1 for a finite core. (A) is false — ν, the neutrons per fission, is a material property independent of size. (C) is backwards: k_inf is the infinite-medium value with zero leakage by definition. (D) the diffusion coefficient is positive.

Q3. The criticality condition for a bare reactor is best stated as:
A. Material buckling equals geometric buckling, B²_m = B²_g
B. The diffusion length equals the core radius
C. Absorption equals leakage
D. k_inf = 1
Show answer

A. At criticality the curvature the material demands (B²_m) exactly matches the curvature the geometry provides (B²_g). (B) is unrelated — L and R are not forced equal. (C) is wrong: at criticality production equals absorption plus leakage, not absorption equal to leakage. (D) k_inf = 1 would make B²_m = 0, requiring an infinite core.

Q4. Compressing a fissile metal sphere to higher density, with the same number of atoms, changes the critical mass how?
A. Critical mass rises, because density has no effect on leakage
B. Critical mass is unchanged, since the number of atoms is fixed
C. Critical mass becomes infinite
D. Critical mass falls, because higher density shortens the mean free path and reduces leakage
Show answer

D. Higher density shortens the neutron mean free path, shrinks the critical volume, and lowers the critical mass — the basis of criticality-safety rules against compression. (A) reverses the physics. (B) ignores that critical mass depends on geometry and density, not atom count alone. (C) has no physical basis.

Q5. A bare core has k_inf = 1.25 and L² = 50 cm². If its geometric buckling is B²_g = 0.004 cm⁻², what is k_eff, and is it critical?
A. k_eff ≈ 1.04, supercritical
B. k_eff ≈ 1.00, exactly critical
C. k_eff ≈ 1.25, supercritical
D. k_eff ≈ 0.83, subcritical
Show answer

A. k_eff = k_inf / (1 + B²_g·L²) = 1.25 / (1 + 0.004·50) = 1.25 / 1.20 ≈ 1.04, which is > 1, so supercritical. (B) would require B²_m = B²_g, but B²_m = (1.25−1)/50 = 0.005 > 0.004, so the core is larger than critical. (C) ignores leakage entirely. (D) inverts the formula.
