Week 1 · Lecture 1

The Neutron Transport Problem

A reactor core is a stadium full of neutrons in flight — and predicting whether their numbers grow, hold steady, or fade away is the single bookkeeping problem at the heart of every reactor ever built.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the neutron-balance principle — that the population in any region is governed by gains minus losses.
  • Define the neutron density, the scalar flux, and the angular flux, and explain how they differ.
  • Identify the four bookkeeping terms — source, leakage, scattering, and absorption — that make up the balance.
  • Write the steady-state neutron transport (Boltzmann) equation and read each term physically.
  • Explain why exact transport theory is so hard to solve, motivating the diffusion approximation used next week.

1.1The Neutron-Balance Idea: Gains = Losses

Strip away all the engineering and a nuclear reactor is one thing: a box full of neutrons that are constantly being born, bouncing around, and dying. A neutron is "born" in a fission event, "dies" when it is absorbed, and "escapes" when it leaks out through the surface. Reactor physics is the science of counting these neutrons — and the whole subject rests on a single, almost childishly simple principle: bookkeeping must balance.

Pick any region of the core and any band of neutron speeds. Over a short time, the number of neutrons in that region can only change for a handful of reasons. Neutrons arrive (from fission, or by streaming in from neighboring regions, or by scattering into the speed band). Neutrons leave (by being absorbed, by leaking out across the boundary, or by scattering out of the band). The rate of change of the population is simply the difference:

rate of change = (gains) - (losses)The fundamental neutron balance. Every transport equation in this course is just this sentence written out term by term.

When gains exactly equal losses, the population holds steady and we say the reactor is critical — this is the state every operating power plant is held in, second by second. When gains exceed losses the population grows (the reactor is supercritical); when losses win it dies away (subcritical). Almost every quantity an engineer cares about — power level, control-rod worth, how much fuel to load — is ultimately a statement about this balance.

AnalogyThink of the neutron population like the water level in a bathtub. The faucet pours water in (fission source plus in-scattering); the drain and the splashing over the rim pull water out (absorption plus leakage). The level rises, holds, or falls depending only on whether the faucet out-runs the drains. "Criticality" is the setting where inflow exactly matches outflow and the level sits perfectly still — and the operator's job is to keep the taps balanced.

1.2Neutron Flux and Angular Flux: How We Describe the Population

To do the bookkeeping we first need the right variables. The most basic is the neutron density n — the number of neutrons per cubic centimeter at a point. But density alone is misleading: a slow neutron and a fast neutron both count as "one neutron" yet the fast one sweeps through far more material per second and so causes far more reactions. What actually drives reaction rates is how much track length neutrons lay down. That motivates the central quantity of the whole field, the scalar flux:

phi = n * vphi = scalar flux (neutrons per cm^2 per s), n = neutron density (per cm^3), v = neutron speed (cm/s).

The flux is best understood as a track-length density: it is the total distance flown per second by all the neutrons in one cubic centimeter. That is exactly the right object, because a reaction rate is simply the flux multiplied by a macroscopic cross section: reaction rate = Sigma × phi. The flux is, quite literally, the currency of reactor physics.

But the scalar flux throws away one crucial piece of information: direction. Neutrons near a control rod or a reflector are not flying equally in all directions — they stream preferentially. To capture that, we keep the full directional detail with the angular flux:

psi(r, E, Omega) -> integrate over all Omega -> phi(r, E)psi = angular flux: track-length density of neutrons at position r, energy E, heading in direction Omega. Summing psi over every direction Omega recovers the scalar flux phi.

The angular flux is the most complete classical description we have. It lives in a space of seven variables: three for position (r), one for energy (E), two for direction (Omega), and one for time. The scalar flux is just the angular flux with the direction information averaged away. Holding onto Omega is what makes transport theory exact — and, as we will see in §1.4, what makes it so expensive to solve.

Common mistakeDo not confuse flux with current. The scalar flux phi adds up neutron travel regardless of direction — two neutrons crossing in opposite directions both add to the flux. The net current J counts direction with sign, so those same two neutrons cancel. A spot can have huge flux but zero net current (neutrons buzzing every which way with no net drift). Treating "flux" as if it were a directional flow is the classic first-week error.

1.3The Four Terms: Source, Leakage, Scattering, Absorption

Now we make the balance of §1.1 quantitative. For neutrons of a given energy in a given region, the loss and gain mechanisms come in exactly four flavors. Three are losses or transfers, one is a true source.

Absorption removes a neutron from the population entirely — it is captured by a nucleus (e.g. by control-rod or coolant material) or it triggers fission. Its rate per unit volume is the absorption cross section times the flux:

absorption rate = Sigma_a * phiSigma_a = macroscopic absorption cross section (per cm). A pure loss term.

Leakage is the net escape of neutrons across the boundary of the region. It is the divergence of the neutron current — neutrons streaming out faster than they stream in. Leakage is why a small reactor is harder to keep critical than a large one: a small core has a lot of surface relative to its volume, so it bleeds neutrons out the edges.

leakage rate = div( J )J = net neutron current vector. The surface-to-volume ratio makes leakage dominate in small cores.

Scattering does not destroy neutrons but moves them — in direction and, crucially, in energy. A fast fission neutron bounces off light nuclei (the moderator) and loses energy step by step until it is "thermalized." For a single energy band, scattering is both a loss (neutrons scatter out to other energies) and a gain (neutrons scatter in from other energies).

The source is where new neutrons are born. In a power reactor the dominant source is fission itself: each fission releases on average about ν ≈ 2.4 neutrons. This is what makes the problem self-feeding — the flux produces the source that produces the flux, a loop that either grows, holds, or decays.

fission source = nu * Sigma_f * phinu = neutrons per fission (~2.4 for U-235), Sigma_f = macroscopic fission cross section. The gain that closes the chain-reaction loop.

Put the four together and the steady-state balance reads, in words: leakage out + absorption out + scatter-out = scatter-in + fission source. That single sentence, written for every position, energy, and direction at once, is the neutron transport equation.

1.4Why Exact Transport Is Hard — and Why Diffusion Beckons

Writing the balance for the angular flux at every point, energy, and direction simultaneously gives the neutron transport equation, a form of the linear Boltzmann equation. In steady state it reads:

Omega . grad(psi) + Sigma_t * psi = integral[ Sigma_s * psi' dOmega' dE' ] + SStreaming + total-collision losses on the left; scatter-in (integral over incoming directions/energies) + fission source S on the right. psi = psi(r, E, Omega).

Read it term by term and it is exactly the four-term balance of §1.3. The first term, Omega · grad(psi), is streaming/leakage; Sigma_t · psi is all collisions (absorption plus scatter-out); the integral on the right gathers every neutron that scatters into this direction and energy from any other; and S is the fission source. It is rigorous and essentially exact. It is also brutal to solve.

The difficulty is dimensionality. The unknown ψ depends on seven variables at once — three space, two angle, one energy, one time. The scatter-in term is an integral over all the angles and energies you are simultaneously solving for, coupling every direction to every other. Add realistic geometry (fuel pins, gaps, control rods) and cross sections that wiggle violently with energy across hundreds of resonances, and there is no hope of a pencil-and-paper solution except in toy cases. In practice engineers attack it two ways: Monte Carlo, which simulates millions of individual neutron histories (the OpenMC approach we will use), or deterministic methods that discretize all seven dimensions on a grid.

This cost is exactly what motivates an approximation. If neutrons collide so often that their directional distribution becomes nearly smooth — almost the same in every direction with only a slight drift — then we can stop tracking direction entirely and keep only the scalar flux φ. The angular integral collapses, the seven variables shrink toward four, and the transport equation simplifies into the far friendlier diffusion equation. That single simplification, valid deep inside large reactors away from strong absorbers, is the workhorse of classical reactor design — and it is exactly where next week begins.

AnalogyTracking the angular flux is like forecasting a crowd by recording every single person's exact heading at every instant — complete, but overwhelming. The diffusion approximation is like ignoring individual headings and only tracking the crowd's overall drift toward the exits. It loses fine detail near doorways (strong absorbers and boundaries), but in the packed middle of the room — where everyone is bumping into everyone and there is no preferred direction — it captures the flow beautifully and at a tiny fraction of the effort.
✦ Worked Example — a non-multiplying balance in a slab

A block of material has scalar flux φ = 5 × 1012 neutrons/cm²/s, macroscopic absorption cross section Σa = 0.020 cm⁻¹, and net leakage rate ∇·J = 6 × 1010 neutrons/cm³/s out of the region. There is no fission. What constant external source S (neutrons/cm³/s) keeps the population steady?

  1. Write the steady balance: with no fission and treating one energy group, gains = losses means S = (absorption) + (leakage). Scatter-in and scatter-out cancel within a single group, so they drop out.
  2. Absorption rate: Sigma_a × phi = 0.020 cm⁻¹ × 5 × 1012 = 1.0 × 1011 neutrons/cm³/s.
  3. Leakage rate: given directly as ∇·J = 6 × 1010 neutrons/cm³/s.
  4. Solve for the source: S = 1.0 × 1011 + 0.6 × 1011 = 1.6 × 1011 neutrons/cm³/s.
  5. Interpret: the source must replace every neutron lost to absorption and every neutron that leaks away. Cut the source below this and the flux decays (subcritical-like); raise it above and the flux climbs. This is the bathtub from §1.1, in numbers.

Before we drive OpenMC in later weeks, let's feel the physics ourselves: a bare-bones Monte Carlo that walks neutrons through a slab and counts how many leak, scatter, or are absorbed — the four-term balance, simulated one history at a time.

# neutron_walk.py — Lecture 1: tally the balance by simulation
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
SIGMA_S, SIGMA_A = 0.30, 0.10      # scatter & absorption cross sections (1/cm)
SIGMA_T = SIGMA_S + SIGMA_A         # total = collision rate per cm
SLAB = 10.0                         # slab thickness (cm); leak if x exits [0, SLAB]
N = 200_000

leak = absorbed = 0
for _ in range(N):
    x, mu = 0.0, 1.0                # start at left face, moving +x
    while True:
        x += -np.log(rng.random()) / SIGMA_T * mu   # free-flight to next collision
        if x < 0 or x > SLAB:       # streamed out a boundary -> LEAKAGE
            leak += 1; break
        if rng.random() < SIGMA_A / SIGMA_T:         # ABSORPTION ends the history
            absorbed += 1; break
        mu = 2*rng.random() - 1     # SCATTER: pick a new direction cosine

print(f"leaked:    {leak/N:.3f}")
print(f"absorbed:  {absorbed/N:.3f}")
print(f"check sum: {(leak+absorbed)/N:.3f}")   # every neutron must leak OR be absorbed -> 1.000

Every neutron ends its life exactly once — by leaking or being absorbed — so the two fractions must sum to 1.000. That is the balance enforcing itself. Try thickening the slab (SLAB = 30) to watch leakage plummet and absorption rise: the surface-to-volume effect from §1.3, seen directly.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A reactor is held at constant power. In terms of the neutron balance, this means the reactor is…
A. supercritical — gains exceed losses
B. subcritical — losses exceed gains
C. critical — gains exactly equal losses
D. source-free — no neutrons are being produced
Show answer

C. Constant power means the neutron population is steady, so gains equal losses — the definition of critical. (A) supercritical means a growing population (rising power); (B) subcritical means a decaying one; (D) is false — fission is continuously producing neutrons, the gains and losses are merely in balance.

Q2. The scalar flux is defined as φ = n·v. Why is flux, not density n, the quantity that drives reaction rates?
A. Because flux counts only the fast neutrons and ignores slow ones
B. Because reaction rate = Σφ, and flux is the track length swept per second that exposes neutrons to nuclei
C. Because density cannot be measured but flux can
D. Because flux already includes the fission source term
Show answer

B. Flux is the track-length density (n times speed v), and a reaction rate equals the macroscopic cross section times flux. A faster neutron sweeps more track per second and so causes more reactions, which density alone misses. (A) is wrong — flux weights all speeds, fast or slow. (C) is false — both are inferable. (D) confuses flux with the source.

Q3. Which list correctly gives the GAIN terms in the steady-state neutron balance for one energy group?
A. Fission source and scatter-in
B. Absorption and leakage
C. Leakage and scatter-out
D. Absorption and fission source
Show answer

A. Gains add neutrons to the band: the fission source (νΣfφ) and scatter-in from other energies. (B) and (C) list only losses. (D) mixes a loss (absorption) with a gain — absorption removes neutrons, so it is never a gain.

Q4. Why does leakage make a small reactor core harder to keep critical than a large one of the same material?
A. Small cores have higher absorption cross sections
B. Small cores produce fewer neutrons per fission
C. Leakage does not depend on core size at all
D. Small cores have a larger surface-to-volume ratio, so a bigger fraction of neutrons escapes across the boundary
Show answer

D. Leakage is a surface effect while production is a volume effect; a small core has proportionally more surface, so it loses a larger fraction of its neutrons to escape. (A) cross sections are material properties, not size-dependent. (B) ν is set by the nuclide, not core size. (C) is exactly backwards — leakage is strongly size-dependent.

Q5. What is the chief reason the exact neutron transport equation is so hard to solve, motivating the diffusion approximation?
A. Neutrons travel faster than light, breaking the math
B. The angular flux depends on many variables (space, energy, direction, time) and the scatter-in integral couples all directions and energies together
C. Cross sections are exactly constant, leaving no equation to solve
D. The strong nuclear force has no known mathematical form
Show answer

B. The unknown ψ lives in seven dimensions and the in-scattering term integrates over all directions and energies, coupling everything to everything — intractable except by simulation or heavy discretization. (A) is false physics. (C) is backwards — cross sections vary wildly with energy across resonances, adding difficulty. (D) is irrelevant; transport theory uses measured cross sections, not the force law directly.
