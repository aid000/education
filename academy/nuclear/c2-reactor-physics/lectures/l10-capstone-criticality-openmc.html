Capstone: A Criticality Calculation in OpenMC

Everything you learned this course — cross sections, diffusion, the six factors, criticality — converges here: a real Monte Carlo reactor model that tells you, to three decimals, whether your core lives or dies.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Assemble a complete criticality model — materials, geometry, and a fission source — using the OpenMC Python API.
  • Explain what an eigenvalue (k-eigenvalue) Monte Carlo calculation does and why fission-source iteration converges to k-eff.
  • Run the calculation with batches and inactive cycles, and read k-eff with its statistical uncertainty correctly.
  • Score and interpret a spatial flux mesh tally, and compare the simulated flux shape to the analytic diffusion (cosine/Bessel) estimate.
  • Frame and deliver the capstone: a documented, converged criticality study that reconciles Monte Carlo with hand calculation.

10.1The Capstone Question: Does This Core Go Critical?

For nine weeks you built the reactor-physics toolkit one piece at a time. You wrote down the neutron transport equation, simplified it to diffusion theory, solved the one-group equation in a bare reactor, defined the multiplication factor k-eff, factored it into the four and six factors, watched neutrons slow down, split energy into groups, and studied how the core responds in time and to feedback. The capstone fuses all of it into a single deliverable: take a real fuel-and-moderator configuration and determine whether it is critical, and why.

The central quantity is the effective multiplication factor k-eff — the ratio of neutrons produced in one fission generation to those in the previous generation:

k_eff = (neutrons produced in generation i+1) / (neutrons produced in generation i)k < 1 subcritical (chain dies), k = 1 critical (steady), k > 1 supercritical (chain grows).

By hand, you estimate k-eff from idealized diffusion theory: a bare homogeneous reactor is critical when the material buckling equals the geometric buckling. But a real core is heterogeneous — fuel pins, gaps, cladding, water — and the energy dependence of cross sections is brutal (resonances, thermal upscatter, fast leakage). The analytic answer is a sketch. The capstone asks you to compute the true answer with a Monte Carlo code and then explain the gap between the two.

The free, open-source tool of choice is OpenMC: a continuous-energy Monte Carlo neutron-transport code with a clean Python API. It does not discretize energy or make the diffusion approximation — it literally tracks millions of simulated neutrons through your geometry, sampling real reaction probabilities from nuclear data libraries. That makes it the reference standard against which your hand calculation is judged.

AnalogyDiffusion theory is a weather forecast made with a single equation on the back of an envelope: fast, insightful, roughly right. OpenMC is the full atmospheric simulation that resolves individual clouds. The envelope tells you it will probably rain; the simulation tells you it will rain 4 mm between 2 and 4 pm over the north field. The capstone is about earning the right to trust the envelope by checking it against the simulation.

10.2Building the Model: Materials, Geometry, Source

Every OpenMC criticality model rests on three pillars. Get any one wrong and k-eff is meaningless.

Materials. A material is a list of nuclides with their concentrations, plus a density. For a thermal reactor you must enrich the uranium (raise the U-235 fraction) and tell OpenMC to use a thermal scattering law (S(alpha,beta)) for hydrogen bound in water — ordinary free-gas scattering badly mis-models how a slow neutron exchanges energy with H in H2O. This single setting can shift k-eff by hundreds of pcm.

Geometry. OpenMC uses constructive solid geometry: you define surfaces (planes, cylinders, spheres), combine half-spaces into cells, and fill each cell with a material or another universe. A fuel pin is a fuel cylinder inside a cladding annulus inside a water-filled square — repeated on a lattice to make an assembly. The outer boundary needs a boundary condition: vacuum (neutrons leak out and are lost) or reflective (neutrons bounce back, mimicking an infinite array).

Source. A criticality calculation is an eigenvalue problem, so the source is the fission source itself. You seed it with a guess — a box or point of starting sites — and the calculation iterates the source to its converged shape. You never specify a fixed source strength; k-eff is a ratio and is independent of how many neutrons you start with.

L phi + A phi = (1/k) F phiEigenvalue form: leakage + absorption balanced against fission/k. The largest eigenvalue k is k-eff; its eigenfunction is the converged flux shape.

OpenMC solves this by the power-iteration / fission-source-iteration method. Each cycle (batch) tracks a population of neutrons; the fission sites they create become the source for the next cycle. The source shape relaxes toward the fundamental eigenmode, and the generation-to-generation neutron ratio converges to k-eff. The early cycles, before the source settles, are discarded as inactive batches.

Common mistakeDo not start scoring tallies (or trusting k-eff) before the fission source has converged. If you set too few inactive batches, the leftover memory of your arbitrary initial source biases k-eff and the flux shape. Always plot the per-batch Shannon entropy of the source distribution and confirm it has flattened before the active batches begin. Skipping this is the single most common reason a student's k-eff disagrees with the reference.

10.3Reading the Output: k-eff and Its Uncertainty

OpenMC reports k-eff as a value and a standard deviation, because Monte Carlo is a statistical experiment: every neutron history is a random sample, and the answer carries sampling noise. A typical result reads k-eff = 1.00214 ± 0.00038. Both numbers matter.

The reported uncertainty is the standard deviation of the mean over the active batches. It shrinks with the total number of simulated particles N, but only as the square root:

sigma_k ~ 1 / sqrt(N)To halve the uncertainty you must run 4x more particles. Doubling batches or particles-per-batch both increase N.

Conventionally we quote reactivity differences in pcm (per cent mille, units of 1e-5 in k). An uncertainty of 0.00038 in k is 38 pcm — comparable to a small control adjustment, so it sets the resolution of any comparison you make. When you claim "this configuration is critical," you mean k-eff is statistically indistinguishable from 1: the interval k ± 2σ brackets 1.0.

Two diagnostics decide whether the number is trustworthy. First, the Shannon entropy of the fission source must have converged before the active batches (Section 10.2). Second, the active-batch k-eff values should look like white noise around a stable mean with no drift. If either fails, the uncertainty is optimistic and the central value may be biased — re-run with more inactive batches.

AnalogyReporting k-eff without its uncertainty is like a poll saying "52% support" with no margin of error — useless for deciding an election. A k-eff of 1.0007 ± 0.0009 and one of 1.0007 ± 0.0001 tell completely different stories: the first cannot distinguish critical from slightly super- or subcritical; the second pins it down. In reactor safety, the error bar is the result.

10.4The Flux Shape and the Diffusion Check

k-eff is one number, but the flux distribution is the physics. A mesh tally records where fissions and reactions occur, giving you the spatial neutron flux phi(r). For a bare, near-homogeneous core the converged shape should match the analytic fundamental mode you derived from one-group diffusion theory — and confirming that match is the heart of the capstone.

For a bare cylindrical reactor of radius R and height H, separable diffusion theory gives a flux that is a cosine along the axis and a zeroth-order Bessel function in radius:

phi(r,z) = phi_0 * J0(2.405 r / R~) * cos(pi z / H~)R~, H~ are the dimensions plus the extrapolation distance. The 2.405 is the first zero of J0; together they set the geometric buckling B^2.

The reactor is critical when the geometric buckling (fixed by size and shape) equals the material buckling (fixed by composition):

B_g^2 = (2.405/R~)^2 + (pi/H~)^2 ; k_inf / (1 + L^2 B_g^2) = 1 at criticalityDiffusion estimate of k-eff. Match this against the OpenMC value; the difference reveals what diffusion theory misses.

When you overlay the OpenMC mesh-tally flux on the analytic cosine/Bessel curve, expect agreement in the bulk and deviations near boundaries and material interfaces — exactly where diffusion theory's assumptions (isotropic scattering, slowly varying flux, no leakage gradients) break down. The capstone deliverable explains those deviations physically: leakage hardens the spectrum near the edge, the extrapolation distance is approximate, and heterogeneity bends the local flux around absorbers. Diffusion gets the gross shape; Monte Carlo gets the truth.

✦ Worked Example — reconciling OpenMC with the diffusion estimate

Your capstone core is a small bare cylinder. Diffusion inputs: k_inf = 1.18, diffusion area L² = 60 cm². Geometry: extrapolated radius R~ = 35 cm, extrapolated height H~ = 70 cm. OpenMC reports k-eff = 1.0042 ± 0.0006. Are they consistent?

  1. Geometric buckling: B_g² = (2.405/35)² + (pi/70)² = (0.06871)² + (0.04488)² = 0.004721 + 0.002014 = 0.006735 cm⁻².
  2. Diffusion k-eff: k = k_inf / (1 + L²B_g²) = 1.18 / (1 + 60 × 0.006735) = 1.18 / (1 + 0.4041) = 1.18 / 1.4041 = 0.8404.
  3. Compare: OpenMC's 1.0042 is far above the diffusion 0.8404 — a gap of ~16000 pcm. That is not noise (the uncertainty is only 60 pcm); it is a real modeling difference.
  4. Diagnose: the one-group diffusion estimate badly underpredicts here because a small core leaks heavily and the single-group constants (especially L² and k_inf collapsed from a real spectrum) are crude. Continuous-energy OpenMC captures resonance absorption, fast leakage, and thermal upscatter that the one-group lump cannot.
  5. Capstone takeaway: when the gap is this large, do not "trust the simulation blindly" either — re-extract few-group constants from the OpenMC spectrum (Week 7) and the diffusion estimate moves much closer. The deliverable is the reconciliation, not a single number.

🛠️ Hands-On: a full criticality run in OpenMC

We use the same free stack from Week 1 — Python with OpenMC, NumPy, and Matplotlib, all installed via conda (the one-time setup details lives in the Week-1/2 lectures). Below is a complete, runnable eigenvalue model: a single reflective fuel pin cell. Replace the cell with your capstone lattice, but the skeleton — materials, geometry, settings, run, read — is identical.

# capstone_keff.py - Week 10 criticality calculation
import openmc

# --- 1. MATERIALS -------------------------------------------------
fuel = openmc.Material(name="UO2 3% enriched")
fuel.add_nuclide("U235", 0.03)
fuel.add_nuclide("U238", 0.97)
fuel.add_nuclide("O16",  2.0)
fuel.set_density("g/cm3", 10.4)

water = openmc.Material(name="light water")
water.add_nuclide("H1", 2.0)
water.add_nuclide("O16", 1.0)
water.set_density("g/cm3", 1.0)
water.add_s_alpha_beta("c_H_in_H2O")   # thermal scattering law - essential!

materials = openmc.Materials([fuel, water])
materials.export_to_xml()

# --- 2. GEOMETRY (one reflective pin cell) ------------------------
fuel_or = openmc.ZCylinder(r=0.41)                 # fuel radius, cm
pitch = 1.26
box = openmc.model.RectangularPrism(pitch, pitch, boundary_type="reflective")

fuel_cell  = openmc.Cell(fill=fuel,  region=-fuel_or)
water_cell = openmc.Cell(fill=water, region=+fuel_or & -box)
root = openmc.Universe(cells=[fuel_cell, water_cell])
geometry = openmc.Geometry(root)
geometry.export_to_xml()

# --- 3. SETTINGS: eigenvalue mode + fission source ----------------
settings = openmc.Settings()
settings.run_mode = "eigenvalue"
settings.batches = 130          # total cycles
settings.inactive = 30          # discarded while source converges
settings.particles = 20000      # neutrons per batch
settings.source = openmc.IndependentSource(
    space=openmc.stats.Point((0, 0, 0)))   # initial guess
settings.entropy_mesh = openmc.RegularMesh()  # monitor convergence
settings.entropy_mesh.lower_left  = (-pitch/2, -pitch/2, -1)
settings.entropy_mesh.upper_right = ( pitch/2,  pitch/2,  1)
settings.entropy_mesh.dimension = (4, 4, 1)
settings.export_to_xml()

# --- 4. RUN, then read k-eff from the results ---------------------
openmc.run()
sp = openmc.StatePoint(f"statepoint.{settings.batches}.h5")
k = sp.keff
print(f"k-eff = {k.nominal_value:.5f} +/- {k.std_dev:.5f}")
# Typical output for this lattice: k-eff = 1.3 (infinite, reflected -> k_inf)

Because the cell is fully reflective, this returns k_inf (no leakage). For your capstone, switch the outer boundary to vacuum and build a finite core to see leakage pull k-eff down — then add a mesh tally (openmc.Tally with a MeshFilter and a flux score) to extract the flux shape for the diffusion comparison in Section 10.4.

Q1. In an OpenMC eigenvalue calculation, why are the first batches marked "inactive" and discarded?
A. They run with fewer particles to save time
B. The fission source is still relaxing from the arbitrary initial guess toward the converged fundamental mode
C. They compute k_inf, while active batches compute k-eff
D. They are used only to test the random number generator
Show answer

B. Inactive batches let the fission source converge before k-eff and tallies are accumulated. (A) is false — particle count per batch is fixed by settings.particles. (C) k_inf vs k-eff is a geometry/boundary distinction, not an active/inactive one. (D) is invented.

Q2. An OpenMC run reports k-eff = 0.99850 ± 0.00040. The best interpretation is:
A. The core is slightly subcritical (about 150 pcm below 1), and the uncertainty of 40 pcm is small enough to say so with confidence
B. The configuration is definitively supercritical
C. The result is meaningless because Monte Carlo cannot resolve criticality
D. The uncertainty is irrelevant once you have the central value
Show answer

A. The central value is 0.99850 (150 pcm below 1) and the 2σ band [0.99770, 0.99930] sits entirely below 1.0, so the core is confidently slightly subcritical. (B) is wrong — the value is below 1. (C) is false — sub-100 pcm resolution is routine. (D) contradicts good practice; the uncertainty is the result's resolution.

Q3. You want to halve the statistical uncertainty on k-eff. Roughly what must you do?
A. Run about 2x more total particles
B. Halve the number of inactive batches
C. Switch the boundary from vacuum to reflective
D. Run about 4x more total particles
Show answer

D. Monte Carlo error scales as 1/sqrt(N), so halving sigma needs 4x the particles. (A) 2x only cuts sigma by 1/sqrt(2). (B) cutting inactive batches risks an unconverged, biased source — it does not reduce active-batch noise. (C) changing the boundary changes the physics (k-eff itself), not the statistics.

Q4. Why must you add a thermal scattering law (e.g. c_H_in_H2O) for hydrogen in the water?
A. It speeds up the simulation by skipping low-energy neutrons
B. Free-gas scattering mis-models how slow neutrons exchange energy with chemically bound hydrogen, biasing k-eff
C. It is required only for fast reactors with no moderator
D. It converts the water into a fission source
Show answer

B. Bound-atom (S(alpha,beta)) scattering correctly captures molecular binding and thermal motion of H in H2O; omitting it can shift k-eff by hundreds of pcm in a thermal system. (A) it does not skip neutrons. (C) it matters precisely because there is a moderator. (D) water is not a fissile source.

Q5. Your OpenMC mesh-tally flux matches the analytic cosine in the core interior but dips below it near the boundary. What does this reveal?
A. The simulation has a bug and must be rerun
B. Diffusion theory is exact everywhere, so the tally must be wrong
C. The diffusion approximation weakens near leaky boundaries and interfaces, where Monte Carlo is more accurate
D. The fission source never converged
Show answer

C. Diffusion theory assumes a slowly varying, near-isotropic flux; that fails near boundaries where leakage gradients are steep, so the true (Monte Carlo) flux deviates from the idealized cosine. (A) interior agreement shows the run is healthy. (B) diffusion is an approximation, not exact. (D) a converged interior shape rules out an unconverged source.
