Neutron Energy Spectra & Multigroup Methods

Reactor neutrons are born at millions of electron-volts and absorbed a fraction of an eV later — multigroup theory is how engineers tame that ten-million-fold energy spread into a handful of solvable numbers.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Sketch the neutron energy spectrum of a thermal reactor and name its fast, epithermal, and thermal regions.
  • Explain the multigroup approximation: why we chop a continuous energy range into discrete groups.
  • Define group constants and show how continuous cross-sections are flux-weighted ("collapsed") into group averages.
  • Write and interpret the coupled two-group diffusion equations and identify each term physically.
  • Explain why two-group theory is the workhorse of practical reactor analysis and where it breaks down.

7.1The Neutron Energy Spectrum: Fast, Epithermal, Thermal

A reactor is, at heart, a population of neutrons being continuously born, slowed, and absorbed. But these neutrons are not all alike — they differ enormously in energy. A neutron freshly emitted in fission carries on the order of 2 MeV; by the time it is finally absorbed in a thermal reactor it may be wandering at about 0.025 eV, the energy of room-temperature thermal motion. That is a span of nearly eight orders of magnitude. Because cross-sections (Week 5) swing wildly with energy, you simply cannot treat all neutrons as the same. The full description is the neutron flux spectrum φ(E): how many neutrons, per unit energy, are flying around at each energy.

In a typical thermal reactor the spectrum has three recognizable regions:

Fast region (~0.1–10 MeV). This is where neutrons are born. The fission process emits them with a smooth distribution called the fission (Watt) spectrum χ(E), peaking near 0.7 MeV with an average around 2 MeV. Fast neutrons travel far between collisions and barely interact with fuel until they slow down.

Epithermal / slowing-down region (~1 eV–0.1 MeV). As neutrons scatter off the moderator (Week 6) they lose energy in roughly equal logarithmic steps. In a clean moderator with little absorption, the flux here follows the classic 1/E slowing-down spectrum — a long, slowly descending shelf. This is also where dangerous resonance absorption in uranium-238 lurks.

Thermal region (< ~1 eV). Once neutrons reach equilibrium with the jiggling moderator atoms, they stop losing energy on average and settle into a Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution peaked near 0.025 eV. Most fission in a thermal reactor is driven by these slow neutrons, because the fission cross-section of uranium-235 is hundreds of times larger here than in the fast region.

phi(E): chi(E) -> 1/E shelf -> Maxwellian peakBorn fast (fission spectrum), slowed through the 1/E region, thermalized to a Maxwellian near kT = 0.025 eV.
AnalogyPicture a crowded water slide. Neutrons are dumped in at the top moving fast (the fission spectrum), race down the long chute losing speed at each bump (the 1/E slowing-down shelf), and finally mill around in the shallow pool at the bottom (the thermal Maxwellian). The whole reactor design problem is really about managing traffic at every stage of that slide.

7.2The Multigroup Approximation

The exact neutron transport (or diffusion) equation is a function of energy and space and direction and time — a continuous beast in every variable. Solving it with the full continuous φ(E) at every point of a reactor is hopeless by hand and expensive even for a computer. The breakthrough idea, used in essentially every production reactor code, is the multigroup approximation: chop the continuous energy range into a finite number of contiguous energy groups and treat all the neutrons inside a group with one effective set of cross-sections.

Group 1 is conventionally the highest-energy group (fast), and the group index increases as energy decreases, so the last group is thermal. We integrate the flux over the energy width of group g to get the group flux:

phi_g = integral over [E_g, E_(g-1)] of phi(E) dEThe group flux is the total flux of neutrons with energies inside group g's energy band.

The energy variable is now a discrete label g = 1, 2, ..., G. Instead of a continuous integro-differential equation in E, we get a coupled set of G balance equations — one per group — that talk to each other through scattering and fission. A neutron can leave its group by being absorbed, by leaking out of the reactor, or by scattering down into a lower-energy group (and, rarely, up). Fission removes a neutron and creates new ones, almost all of which are born back in the top (fast) groups.

The number of groups G is a modeling choice. Fast-reactor lattice codes may use dozens or hundreds of fine groups to resolve resonances; whole-core analysis often collapses those down to just two groups. The art is choosing group boundaries so that each group is internally well-behaved — for example, putting one boundary near 1 eV to cleanly separate "thermal" from "everything above."

Common mistakeThe multigroup approximation does not assume the flux is constant within a group. The group flux φg is an integral over the band, and the cross-sections are averaged using the real shape of φ(E) inside the group. Treating φ(E) as flat inside a group only works if your groups are extremely fine — exactly why coarse-group constants must be generated from a realistic spectrum first.

7.3Group Constants: Collapsing the Cross-Sections

For the multigroup equations to be useful, each group needs a single effective cross-section — a group constant. But how do you boil down a cross-section σ(E) that varies over orders of magnitude into one number for a whole energy band? The guiding principle is reaction-rate preservation: the group constant must reproduce the same total reaction rate that the true energy-dependent cross-section would, given the actual flux shape. Reaction rate is Σ(E)φ(E), so we flux-weight:

Sigma_x,g = [ integral Sigma_x(E) phi(E) dE ] / [ integral phi(E) dE ]Group constant = flux-weighted average of the macroscopic cross-section over group g. The denominator is just phi_g.

This is the central act of "collapsing." The numerator is the true reaction rate in the group; dividing by the group flux gives the average cross-section that yields that exact rate when multiplied by φg. The same recipe collapses absorption, fission, scattering, and the diffusion coefficient. Group-to-group scattering gets its own constant Σs,g→g', the average rate at which neutrons scatter from group g into group g'.

The catch — and the whole reason reactor physics is subtle — is that you need the flux to compute the group constants, but you need the group constants to compute the flux. In practice this is broken by a two-step workflow: a fine-group lattice calculation over a small repeating fuel cell first estimates a realistic φ(E), uses it to collapse cross-sections down to a few groups, and then a coarse-group core calculation uses those constants over the whole reactor. This "lattice-to-core" pipeline is exactly how industrial reactor codes work today.

D_g chosen so that D_g * grad(phi_g) preserves the group leakage rateThe group diffusion coefficient is likewise flux-weighted so the leakage current is reproduced.

7.4Two-Group Theory: The Workhorse

The coarsest useful version of multigroup theory keeps just two groups: a fast group (group 1, everything above ~1 eV) and a thermal group (group 2, below ~1 eV). This deceptively simple model captures the essential life story of a neutron in a thermal reactor — born fast, slowed down, then thermalized and absorbed — and is accurate enough for an astonishing amount of real design and safety work.

Writing the steady-state two-group diffusion balance, with all fission neutrons born into the fast group (χ1 = 1) and using k to scale fission to criticality:

Fast: -D1 grad^2 phi1 + (Sigma_a1 + Sigma_s12) phi1 = (1/k)(nu Sigma_f1 phi1 + nu Sigma_f2 phi2)Fast neutrons are lost to absorption and to down-scattering; the source is all fission neutrons, born fast.
Thermal: -D2 grad^2 phi2 + Sigma_a2 phi2 = Sigma_s12 phi1Thermal neutrons are gained ONLY by slowing down from the fast group, and lost to absorption.

Read these equations as bookkeeping. In the fast equation: leakage (the −D∇²φ term) plus removal (absorption + scattering to thermal) on the left, balanced by the fission source on the right. The crucial coupling term is Σs,1→2φ1 — neutrons leaving the fast group by slowing down. That same term reappears on the right of the thermal equation as the only way thermal neutrons are created. The two groups are stitched together by slowing-down. Solve them together (with the leakage geometry from Week 4's buckling) and you extract keff and the spatial flux shapes φ1(r), φ2(r).

Why is two-group the workhorse? Because in a thermal reactor the physics genuinely is two-natured: a fast population that mostly leaks and slows down, and a thermal population that does most of the fissioning. Two groups put a number on both the fast non-leakage and thermal utilization pieces of the six-factor formula (Week 4) and run in milliseconds, making them ideal for control-rod worth, burnup sweeps, and reactor-kinetics models.

AnalogyTwo-group theory is like modeling a city's population as just "working-age adults" and "retirees" instead of tracking every individual age. You lose fine detail, but you capture the two flows that actually drive the budget — exactly as the fast and thermal groups capture the two flows that drive a reactor.
Common mistakeTwo-group theory works because down-scattering dominates: in the slowing-down direction neutrons cascade from fast to thermal. Do not assume it applies blindly to a fast reactor, where there is no large thermal population, the spectrum is harder, and resonance effects demand many more groups. Picking the wrong group structure for the wrong reactor is a classic blunder.
✦ Worked Example — collapsing a two-group thermal absorption constant

Suppose a fine-group lattice run gives, in the thermal group, three energy bins with macroscopic absorption cross-sections and fluxes as below. Collapse them into one thermal-group constant Σa2 using flux weighting.

  1. The data (bin: Σa in cm⁻¹, flux φ in arbitrary units): bin 1: Σa = 0.40, φ = 100;  bin 2: Σa = 0.20, φ = 300;  bin 3: Σa = 0.10, φ = 600.
  2. Numerator = sum of reaction rates Σaφ: 0.40×100 + 0.20×300 + 0.10×600 = 40 + 60 + 60 = 160.
  3. Denominator = total group flux φ2: 100 + 300 + 600 = 1000.
  4. Collapse: Σa2 = 160 / 1000 = 0.16 cm⁻¹.
  5. Sanity check: the result (0.16) is pulled toward the low-Σ, high-flux bin (0.10), not the plain arithmetic mean of the cross-sections (which would be 0.233). That is exactly right — the bins carrying the most neutrons must dominate the effective cross-section, which is the whole point of flux weighting.

🛠️ Hands-On: collapse a continuous-energy spectrum into group constants with OpenMC

We use the same free stack from Week 1 — OpenMC for Monte Carlo transport plus NumPy and matplotlib (full install details live in the Week 1/2 lectures). OpenMC tallies the continuous-energy flux and reaction rates inside energy bins, and its mgxs module collapses them into multigroup constants for you — the lattice-to-core pipeline of Section 7.3, in code.

# collapse_groups.py — Lecture 7: flux-weighted two-group constants
import openmc
import openmc.mgxs as mgxs

# --- assume model, materials, and geometry already built (Weeks 5-6) ---
# Define a 2-group structure: thermal boundary at 1 eV, fast top at 20 MeV
groups = mgxs.EnergyGroups([0.0, 1.0, 20.0e6])   # eV, ascending edges

# Ask OpenMC to flux-weight absorption + fission + scattering over a cell
fuel_cell = list(model.geometry.get_all_cells().values())[0]
absorb  = mgxs.AbsorptionXS(domain=fuel_cell, groups=groups)
fission = mgxs.FissionXS(domain=fuel_cell, groups=groups, nu=True)
scatter = mgxs.ScatterMatrixXS(domain=fuel_cell, groups=groups)

tallies = openmc.Tallies()
for lib in (absorb, fission, scatter):
    tallies += lib.tallies.values()
model.tallies = tallies

sp_path = model.run()                # Monte Carlo: tally flux & reaction rates

with openmc.StatePoint(sp_path) as sp:
    for lib in (absorb, fission, scatter):
        lib.load_from_statepoint(sp)

# The collapsed, flux-weighted 2-group constants:
print("Sigma_a    (fast, thermal):", absorb.get_xs())
print("nu*Sigma_f (fast, thermal):", fission.get_xs())
print("Scatter 1->2 (down-scatter):", scatter.get_xs(in_groups=[1], out_groups=[2]))

OpenMC has done the integral ∫Σ(E)φ(E)dE / ∫φ(E)dE for you over each group. The printed numbers are exactly the Sigma_a,g, nu*Sigma_f,g, and Sigma_s,1->2 you would drop straight into the two-group diffusion equations of Section 7.4. Try refining to 8 groups and watch the thermal constant barely change while the fast group splits into resolved resonances.

Q1. In a thermal reactor, where are most neutrons born and where do most fissions ultimately occur?
A. Born thermal, fission fast
B. Born fast (~2 MeV), fission thermal (~0.025 eV)
C. Both born and fission in the epithermal region
D. Born and fission both at exactly 1 eV
Show answer

B. Fission emits neutrons fast (the χ(E) spectrum, average ~2 MeV); after slowing down they drive most fissions as thermal neutrons because U-235's fission cross-section is huge there. (A) reverses the physics. (C) the epithermal region is mostly slowing-down, not the main fission or birth zone. (D) is an arbitrary single energy, not how the spectrum works.

Q2. What does the multigroup approximation actually do to the energy variable?
A. Assumes all neutrons share exactly one energy
B. Ignores neutron energy entirely
C. Treats energy as continuous but discretizes only space
D. Replaces continuous energy with a finite set of discrete energy groups
Show answer

D. Multigroup chops the continuous energy range into G contiguous groups and writes one balance equation per group. (A) is the one-group/monoenergetic special case, not the general method. (B) is false — energy structure is the whole point. (C) describes spatial discretization, a separate approximation.

Q3. Group constants are computed by averaging the cross-section over a group weighted by…
A. the neutron flux φ(E), to preserve reaction rate
B. the total volume of the reactor
C. a simple unweighted arithmetic mean over energy
D. the temperature of the coolant
Show answer

A. The collapse uses Σx,g = ∫Σxφ dE / ∫φ dE — flux weighting preserves the true reaction rate. (C) an unweighted mean ignores where the neutrons actually are and gives the wrong rate (see the worked example). (B) and (D) are not the weighting function for energy collapsing.

Q4. In the standard two-group equations, how are thermal-group neutrons created?
A. Directly by fission, since most fission neutrons are born thermal
B. By absorption events in the fuel
C. By down-scattering of fast neutrons into the thermal group (Σs,1→2φ1)
D. They are not created; they only leak in from outside
Show answer

C. The coupling term Σs,1→2φ1 is the only source on the right of the thermal equation. (A) is wrong — fission neutrons are born fast (χ1 = 1). (B) absorption removes, not creates, neutrons. (D) leakage is a loss term, not a source.

Q5. Why is two-group theory NOT a safe default for a fast reactor?
A. A fast reactor lacks a large thermal population and keeps a hard spectrum, so two coarse groups miss the resonance-rich fast physics
B. Fast reactors contain no neutrons above 1 eV
C. Two-group theory only works at zero power
D. Fast reactors cannot be modeled with diffusion theory at all
Show answer

A. Two-group structure assumes a dominant thermal population fed by down-scattering; a fast reactor's spectrum stays hard, so accurate work needs many more groups to resolve resonances. (B) is false — fast reactors are almost entirely above 1 eV. (C) power level is unrelated to group structure. (D) is an overstatement; fast reactors are routinely modeled, just with finer group structures.
