Neutron Moderation & Slowing-Down

A fission neutron is born fast and useless for a thermal chain — moderation is the billiards game of elastic collisions that drags it down to thermal energy, and how few collisions it takes decides what a reactor can be built from.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe elastic-scatter kinematics and why a light nucleus steals far more energy per collision than a heavy one.
  • Define lethargy and the average logarithmic energy loss ξ, and use them to count collisions to thermal.
  • Rank moderators with the moderating power ξΣs and moderating ratio ξΣs/Σa.
  • Read the slowing-down density q(E) as the flow of neutrons down the energy axis.
  • Explain resonance escape probability p and how it ties moderation back to the four-factor formula.
  • Derive the Fermi age equation and use it to obtain the fast non-leakage probability P_FNL ≈ 1/(1+B²τ).

6.1Why Slow Neutrons Down at All — and the Billiards of Elastic Scatter

Fission neutrons are born fast, around 2 MeV. But the fission cross section of U-235 is hundreds of times larger at thermal energies (~0.025 eV) than at birth. A thermal reactor only works if those fast neutrons are first dragged down across eight orders of magnitude in energy — a process called moderation or slowing-down. The job is done almost entirely by elastic scattering off light nuclei, the same physics as billiard balls colliding.

The key result of elastic kinematics: a neutron of mass 1 hitting a stationary nucleus of mass number A can lose, at most, a fixed fraction of its energy in a single head-on hit. That maximum fractional loss is governed by the collision parameter α:

alpha = ((A - 1) / (A + 1))², E_min = alpha * E0After one collision a neutron's energy lies anywhere between αE₀ (head-on) and E₀ (grazing). For hydrogen (A=1), α = 0 — a single head-on hit can stop the neutron dead.

The lighter the target, the larger the possible energy loss. Hydrogen (A=1) can take all of a neutron's energy in one collision; carbon (A=12) takes at most ~28%; uranium (A=238) barely nudges it. This is why moderators are light elements — hydrogen in water, deuterium in heavy water, carbon in graphite — and why slamming a neutron into the heavy fuel does almost nothing to slow it.

AnalogyThrow a ping-pong ball at a bowling ball and it bounces back with nearly all its speed — almost no energy transferred. Throw it at another ping-pong ball and it can stop dead, handing over everything. A neutron meeting a hydrogen nucleus (nearly equal mass) is the second case; meeting uranium is the first. Moderation works only when the target is light enough to "catch" the neutron's energy.

6.2Lethargy and ξ: Counting Collisions to Thermal

Because each collision multiplies the energy by a random factor (not subtracts a fixed amount), it is far more natural to work on a logarithmic energy scale. Define the lethargy u as the log of how far the neutron has slowed from a reference energy:

u = ln(E0 / E)Lethargy increases as the neutron slows. A neutron born at E₀ starts at u = 0; "more tired" = more lethargy = lower energy.

The beauty of lethargy is that the average gain in lethargy per collision is a constant, independent of energy, depending only on the target mass. This constant is the average logarithmic energy loss ξ (xi):

xi = 1 + (alpha / (1 - alpha)) ln(alpha) ≈ 2 / (A + 2/3)ξ = mean lethargy gained per collision, per nuclide. For hydrogen (A=1) ξ = 1.000; for pure deuterium (A=2) ξ = 0.725; carbon ξ = 0.158; U-238 ξ ≈ 0.0084.

Since each collision adds ξ on average, the number of collisions to slow a neutron from fission energy to thermal is just the total lethargy gap divided by ξ:

n_collisions ≈ ln(E_fast / E_thermal) / xiFrom 2 MeV to 0.025 eV the lethargy gap is ln(2e6/0.025) ≈ 18.2. Divide by ξ: ~18 collisions in light water, ~36 in heavy water (D₂O), ~115 in graphite.

So hydrogen thermalizes a neutron in under 20 collisions while graphite needs over a hundred. Fewer collisions means a more compact core — one reason light-water reactors are far smaller than graphite-moderated ones.

Common mistakeDon't quote ξ = 0.725 (pure deuterium's per-collision value, A=2) as if it were the number for heavy water. A D₂O molecule also contains oxygen, and diluting deuterium's collisions with oxygen's much weaker ones pulls the molecule's effective ξ down to about 0.509 (the standard literature value) — not 0.725. That drops heavy water's collision count to thermal from the naive ~25 (pure D, A=2 alone) to the correct ~35–36 for real D₂O. Twenty-five collisions is a bare-deuterium number, not a heavy-water number.

6.3Moderating Power and Moderating Ratio: the Two Competing Demands

Counting collisions only tells half the story. A good moderator must slow neutrons in few collisions — but it must also do so without eating them. Hydrogen is the champion slower-downer, yet ordinary water also absorbs a meaningful number of neutrons, which is exactly why light-water reactors need enriched fuel. Two figures of merit capture this tension.

The moderating power measures how fast a material slows neutrons per unit path — collisions per cm times energy lost per collision:

moderating power = xi * Sigma_sΣs = macroscopic scatter cross section. High when the material both scatters often and steals a lot per hit. Water beats graphite on this measure.

But slowing-down power alone would crown a strong absorber that also scatters well. What we really want is to slow neutrons down relative to how many it absorbs. That is the moderating ratio:

moderating ratio = xi * Sigma_s / Sigma_aThe true quality metric. Heavy water (~5700) trounces graphite (~170) and light water (~62) because deuterium barely absorbs neutrons.

This is the source of the design tension flagged in the discussion: light water has high moderating power (compact cores) but a mediocre moderating ratio (it absorbs, demanding enrichment). Heavy water has a superb ratio (can run on natural uranium) but lower power (bigger cores) and costs a fortune to make. Every reactor concept is a negotiated settlement between these two numbers.

Common mistakeDon't conflate moderating power with moderating ratio. A material can be excellent at slowing neutrons (high ξΣs) yet a poor moderator overall because it also absorbs them (low ξΣs/Σa). The ratio, not the power, is what decides whether you can sustain a chain reaction on natural uranium.

6.4Slowing-Down Density and Resonance Escape

To track moderation as a continuous process, define the slowing-down density q(E): the rate at which neutrons slow past a given energy E, per cm³ per second. Think of it as the current of neutrons flowing downhill along the energy axis. In a clean, non-absorbing moderator at steady state, q is constant — every neutron that starts down eventually reaches thermal.

q(E) = rate neutrons slow past energy EIn a pure moderator with source S fast neutrons/cm³/s, q = S at every energy down to thermal — nothing is lost on the way down.

Real cores are not clean. As neutrons slow through the epithermal range (~1 eV to ~1 keV) they pass through the towering capture resonances of U-238 — narrow energies where the absorption cross section spikes by orders of magnitude. A neutron unlucky enough to be sitting at a resonance energy is captured and lost before it ever reaches thermal. The fraction that thread this gauntlet and make it to thermal is the resonance escape probability p — the very factor we met in last week's four-factor formula:

p = q_thermal / q_fast = (neutrons reaching thermal) / (neutrons that started down)p < 1 because U-238 resonances capture some slowing-down neutrons. Better moderation (more energy lost per collision) jumps neutrons across resonances faster, raising p.

Here moderation and resonance escape lock together: a good moderator with large ξ makes neutrons take big leaps down the energy scale, so they spend less time lingering at any resonance energy and are more likely to escape capture. This is also why fuel-moderator geometry matters — lumping fuel into pins rather than mixing it homogeneously lets neutrons do most of their slowing in the moderator, away from the U-238 resonances, raising p. It is the physics that makes a heterogeneous lattice outperform a homogeneous mixture.

AnalogyResonances are like potholes spread along a downhill road, each able to swallow a slow-rolling marble. A strong moderator gives the marble big bounces that clear several potholes in one hop; a weak one lets it creep along, far likelier to drop into a hole. And keeping the potholes (fuel) separated from the slope (moderator) means the marble does most of its rolling on clear pavement — the heterogeneous-lattice advantage.

6.5Fermi Age Theory & the Fast Non-Leakage Probability

Section 6.4 tracked slowing-down only in energy, as if the core were infinite. But a real core leaks, and some neutrons leak out while still slowing down — before they ever reach thermal. To put a number on that leakage we need to know not just how a neutron's energy changes but how far it wanders in space while it does. That is the job of Fermi age theory, and it is where the fast non-leakage probability P_FNL you used without proof back in Week 5 actually comes from.

Combine the diffusion approximation at each lethargy with the slowing-down balance of §6.4, and change variables from lethargy u to a new quantity τ(u), the Fermi age:

tau(u) = integral_0^u [ D(u') / (xi * Sigma_s(u')) ] du'τ has units of cm², not time — a "fictitious time" borrowed from the mathematics of heat conduction.

With this substitution, the spatial-and-energy balance for the slowing-down density q(r,u) takes on exactly the form of the time-dependent heat/diffusion equation, with τ standing in for time:

grad^2 q(r, tau) = dq(r, tau) / d(tau)Fermi's age equation. Neutrons "diffuse" through space as they "age" down the energy axis, exactly as heat diffuses through a solid over time.

For a point fast source in an infinite medium this has the same Gaussian solution as a point heat pulse: the slowing-down density spreads out in r as a 3-D Gaussian with mean-square migration distance ⟨r²⟩ = 6τ. Physically, τ is one-sixth the mean-square straight-line distance a neutron travels between being born fast and slowing to a given energy.

From the age equation to P_FNL

Now use the age equation in a finite, critical core. The fundamental spatial mode of a bare reactor satisfies ∇²φ = −B²φ (Week 3), so insert that same curvature into the age equation in place of the Laplacian:

-B^2 * q(tau) = dq(tau)/d(tau) => q(tau) = q(0) * exp(-B^2 * tau)Every unit of age accumulated while slowing down costs a leakage factor exp(−B² dτ) — the same mathematical form as radioactive decay, but in "age" rather than time.

q(0) is the fast source rate and q(τth) is the rate that actually arrives at thermal without leaking, where τth — the age to thermal — is τ(u) accumulated over the whole slowing-down range. Their ratio is exactly the fast non-leakage probability:

P_FNL = exp(-B^2 * tau_th) ~ 1 / (1 + B^2 * tau_th)The exponential is the exact age-theory result; the 1/(1+x) form is the standard engineering approximation, accurate to within a percent or two for the small B²τth (∼0.03–0.1) typical of thermal power reactors — this is the P_FNL formula from Week 5.

That second form is the one worth remembering, because it has exactly the same shape as the thermal non-leakage probability from Week 4, P_TNL = 1/(1 + B²L²). Multiplying the two and keeping the leading term in B² gives the single most useful leakage formula in one-speed-plus-age reactor physics:

P_NL = P_FNL * P_TNL ~ 1 / (1 + B^2 * M^2), M^2 = tau_th + L^2M² is the migration area — the total mean-square "wandering distance" from birth to thermal absorption, fast slowing-down (τth) plus thermal diffusion (L²). This upgrades Week 4's one-group P_NL = 1/(1+B²L²) into the two-group ("age-diffusion") result.
AnalogyPicture a neutron's life as a two-leg journey: first a fast, headlong dash while slowing down (wandering radius set by τ), then a short thermal shuffle before capture (wandering radius set by L²). Because the two legs are statistically independent random walks laid end to end, their mean-square distances simply add — M² = τ + L² is the Pythagorean sum. A bigger core (smaller B²) tames the leakage from both legs at once.

Typical light-water numbers make this concrete: τth ≈ 27 cm² against L² ≈ 8 cm², so M² ≈ 35 cm² — most of the migration area is the fast leg, not the thermal one. That is why taming fast leakage (a bigger core, a good reflector) matters at least as much as the thermal non-leakage probability emphasized in Week 5.

✦ Worked Example — collisions to thermal in graphite vs. water

A 2.0 MeV fission neutron must slow to 0.025 eV thermal. Graphite has ξ = 0.158; light water has an effective ξ ≈ 0.92. How many collisions does each need, and what does the comparison say about core size?

  1. Lethargy gap: u = ln(Efast/Ethermal) = ln(2.0×10⁶ / 0.025) = ln(8.0×10⁷) ≈ 18.2.
  2. Graphite collisions: n = u/ξ = 18.2 / 0.158 ≈ 115 collisions.
  3. Water collisions: n = u/ξ = 18.2 / 0.92 ≈ 20 collisions.
  4. Interpret size: a graphite neutron makes ~6× more collisions, each carrying it a small distance, so it wanders much farther before thermalizing — graphite cores are large. Water thermalizes neutrons close to where they were born, allowing compact cores.
  5. The catch: water's high ξ comes with hydrogen's appreciable absorption, so despite the small core it needs enriched fuel — the moderating-ratio penalty of §6.3 in action.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulating slowing-down in Python

Reuse the Week 1 reactor environment (NumPy). We Monte-Carlo a neutron bouncing off a single nuclide, applying the elastic-scatter energy law E' = E×(uniform between α and 1), and count collisions to thermal — then compare to the u/ξ estimate.

# slowing_down.py — Lecture 6: collisions to thermal by Monte Carlo
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
A = 12.0                                  # graphite; try 1.0 for hydrogen
alpha = ((A - 1)/(A + 1))**2
xi = 1 + (alpha/(1 - alpha))*np.log(alpha) if A > 1 else 1.0

E_fast, E_th = 2.0e6, 0.025               # eV
counts = []
for _ in range(20000):
    E, n = E_fast, 0
    while E > E_th:
        E *= alpha + (1 - alpha)*rng.random()   # elastic-scatter energy law
        n += 1
    counts.append(n)

print(f"A={A:.0f}  xi={xi:.3f}")
print(f"mean collisions (sim):  {np.mean(counts):.1f}")
print(f"estimate u/xi:          {np.log(E_fast/E_th)/xi:.1f}")

The simulated mean lands within a collision or two of the u/ξ estimate — confirming that ξ really is the average lethargy gained per hit. Switch A = 1.0 for hydrogen and watch the collision count crash from ~115 to ~18, the §6.2 result reproduced from first principles.

Model response

The tension is between two different figures of merit that both depend on the same material property, but in opposite ways. Moderating power ξΣs rewards a nuclide that scatters often (large Σs) and steals a lot of energy per hit (large ξ) — hydrogen wins this contest outright, because A ≈ 1 lets α→0 and a single collision can nearly stop a neutron. But nothing in that figure of merit cares whether the material also absorbs neutrons. The moderating ratio ξΣs/Σa is the figure of merit that does care: it rewards slowing neutrons down relative to how many you lose to parasitic capture along the way. Hydrogen's absorption cross section, while small in absolute terms, is not negligible next to deuterium's — so light water scores brilliantly on power but only middling on ratio.

Real reactors resolve the tension by picking which number they can afford to sacrifice. Light-water reactors (PWRs, BWRs) take the moderating-power side of the trade: hydrogen's huge ξΣs thermalizes neutrons in ~18 collisions, giving a compact, economical core, but hydrogen's absorption means the fuel must be enriched above natural uranium to overcome the loss — the "price" of choosing power over ratio. Heavy-water reactors (CANDU) take the opposite side: deuterium's minuscule absorption gives a moderating ratio roughly 90× better than light water, so the reactor can run on cheap, unenriched natural uranium — but it needs ~36 collisions instead of ~18 and a much larger, more expensive core, plus the cost of producing the heavy water itself. Graphite-moderated designs sit at a third point: a very good ratio (low absorption) but a poor power (small ξ, ~115 collisions), trading an even larger core for natural-uranium fuel without heavy water's expense.

Q1. Why are moderators made of light elements like hydrogen, deuterium, or carbon?
A. Light nuclei absorb neutrons more strongly
B. A neutron transfers a much larger fraction of its energy when it hits a light, near-equal-mass nucleus
C. Light elements have higher fission cross sections
D. Heavy nuclei cannot scatter neutrons at all
Show answer

B. Elastic kinematics let a neutron lose up to (1−α) of its energy per hit, and α → 0 as A → 1, so light targets steal the most energy. (A) good moderators must absorb little. (C) moderators aren't chosen for fission. (D) heavy nuclei scatter but transfer almost no energy.

Q2. The quantity ξ (average logarithmic energy loss) is useful because…
A. it equals the neutron's absolute energy
B. it is the same for every nuclide
C. it is the average lethargy gained per collision and is constant with energy, so collisions-to-thermal = (lethargy gap)/ξ
D. it measures absorption, not scattering
Show answer

C. Because energy loss is multiplicative, the average logarithmic loss per collision is constant, so dividing the total lethargy gap by ξ counts collisions. (A) ξ is dimensionless lethargy, not energy. (B) ξ depends strongly on mass number A. (D) ξ describes elastic scattering.

Q3. Heavy water has a far higher moderating ratio than light water primarily because…

Show answer

A. The moderating ratio ξΣs/Σa rewards low absorption, and deuterium's tiny absorption cross section makes Σa very small — letting heavy-water reactors run on natural uranium. (B) deuterium actually needs more collisions than hydrogen. (C) density isn't the dominant factor. (D) moderators don't fission.

Q4. The resonance escape probability p is less than 1 because…

D. p is the fraction surviving the epithermal U-238 capture resonances on the way down. (A) that is leakage (the non-leakage factors), a separate effect. (B) that concerns η/ν. (C) upscatter is negligible at these energies.
