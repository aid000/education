Week 3 · Lecture 3

The One-Group Diffusion Equation & Flux Shapes

Strip a reactor down to a single equation and one number — and out falls the gentle cosine glow of neutrons that every core on Earth is built to balance.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Assemble the steady-state one-group diffusion equation for a bare homogeneous reactor from a neutron balance.
  • Recognize the equation as a Helmholtz eigenvalue problem and define the geometric buckling B².
  • Solve for the fundamental flux shape in slab, sphere, and cylinder geometries (cosine, sinc, Bessel).
  • Apply vacuum boundary conditions using the extrapolated boundary and the extrapolation distance.
  • Connect buckling and flux shape to real core design: peaking factors, leakage, and why power is flattened.

3.1From a Neutron Balance to the Diffusion Equation

In Week 2 you learned that neutrons, like heat or perfume in still air, tend to drift from where they are crowded toward where they are sparse. That drift is captured by Fick's law: the net neutron current is proportional to minus the gradient of the flux, with a proportionality constant called the diffusion coefficient D. Now we put that law to work to describe an entire reactor at once.

Start by doing bookkeeping on neutrons in a tiny volume. In steady state, whatever appears must be removed — neutrons are neither piling up nor draining away. There are three things that can happen to a neutron of our single energy group: it can leak out by diffusion, it can be absorbed by a nucleus, or it can be produced by fission. Balancing them gives:

production = absorption + leakageThe fundamental steady-state neutron balance, written per unit volume.

Now write each term mathematically for a one-energy-group, homogeneous medium. Net leakage out of a point is the divergence of the current, which by Fick's law equals −D∇²φ. Absorption is Σaφ (cross section times flux). Production is the fission rate times neutrons per fission, written νΣfφ. Putting them together and rearranging into the standard form:

D * grad^2(phi) - Sigma_a * phi + nu*Sigma_f * phi = 0One-group, steady-state diffusion equation. phi = neutron flux (n/cm^2/s); D in cm; Sigma in 1/cm.

This single linear partial differential equation is the workhorse of classical reactor physics. Despite its simplicity it predicts the shape of the neutron field and, as we will see in Week 4, whether the reactor can sustain a chain reaction at all.

AnalogyThink of the flux like the height of water in a leaky tank that is also being refilled. Fission is the tap pouring water in; absorption is a sponge soaking it up everywhere; leakage is water spilling over the rim at the edges. A reactor held "just critical" is a tank kept at perfectly constant level — the refill exactly matches the soaking plus the spilling, everywhere at once.

3.2Geometric Buckling B²: One Number for the Whole Shape

Divide the diffusion equation through by D and collect the two reaction terms. They both multiply φ, so they combine into a single constant:

grad^2(phi) + B^2 * phi = 0, with B^2 = (nu*Sigma_f - Sigma_a) / DThe Helmholtz equation. This B^2 is the MATERIAL buckling, set entirely by the medium's cross sections.

This is the Helmholtz equation, one of the most studied equations in physics — it governs vibrating drums, organ pipes, and quantum particles in a box. The constant B² is called the buckling, and it earns that name honestly: solutions of the Helmholtz equation curve, or "buckle," and B² measures how sharply. A large B² means a tightly curved flux that bows steeply to zero at the edges; a small B² means a broad, gentle hump.

Here is the deep idea that makes the whole subject click. The value of B² above is fixed purely by the material — its cross sections and D. But the Helmholtz equation also has a separate, purely geometric meaning: for a given reactor shape and size, only certain values of B² produce a flux that is positive everywhere inside and vanishes at the boundary. That allowed value is the geometric buckling, and it depends only on the dimensions, never on the material. For a self-sustaining bare reactor these two must be equal — material buckling meets geometric buckling — which is exactly the criticality condition we unpack next week.

Common mistakeStudents often treat B² as "just a constant in the equation" and forget it carries two independent identities: a material buckling (from cross sections) and a geometric buckling (from size and shape). They are equal only for a critical bare reactor. Writing B² without asking "material or geometric?" is the source of endless confusion when you reach the criticality condition.

3.3Solving for the Flux Shape: Slab, Sphere, Cylinder

The same Helmholtz equation, solved in different geometries, gives different but closely related flux shapes. In every case we impose two physical conditions: the flux is finite and symmetric (so its slope is zero at the center), and it falls to zero at the boundary. Those conditions select a single smooth, positive hump — the fundamental mode.

Infinite slab of half-thickness a

In one dimension the Helmholtz equation reads φ'' + B²φ = 0, whose symmetric solution is a cosine:

phi(x) = phi_0 * cos(B x), B = pi / (2a) -> Bg^2 = (pi / 2a)^2Flux is maximum at the center (x = 0) and zero at the extrapolated edge x = a.

Sphere of radius R

With spherical symmetry the Laplacian gives a "sinc" profile — a sine divided by radius, finite at the center because sin(Br)/r tends to a constant as r tends to zero:

phi(r) = phi_0 * sin(B r) / (B r), B = pi / R -> Bg^2 = (pi / R)^2The most compact geometry: a sphere has the smallest buckling for a given volume, hence the least leakage.

Finite cylinder (radius R, height H)

A real power reactor is a cylinder. The flux separates into a radial Bessel function J₀ and an axial cosine, and the buckling is the sum of the two pieces:

phi(r,z) = phi_0 * J0(2.405 r / R) * cos(pi z / H), Bg^2 = (2.405 / R)^2 + (pi / H)^22.405 is the first zero of the Bessel function J0. Radial Bessel hump x axial cosine.

Notice the family resemblance: every fundamental mode is a single smooth hump, peaked at the center and tapering to zero at the edge. The flux is naturally peaked — and that peaking is the central headache of reactor design, because power follows flux and you cannot let the center melt while the edges idle.

3.4Boundary Conditions: The Extrapolated Boundary

Where exactly does the flux go to zero? Physically, at the outer surface of a bare reactor there is nothing outside to scatter neutrons back in — they leave and never return. A naive boundary condition would set φ = 0 right at the physical surface, but that is too crude: diffusion theory is an approximation that slightly overestimates how fast the flux drops near a free surface.

The standard fix is the extrapolated boundary. We pretend the flux continues a little past the real surface and reaches zero at an imaginary boundary located a small distance beyond it. That gap is the extrapolation distance, and from transport theory it is about:

d = 0.71 * lambda_tr = 2.13 * D, so set phi = 0 at the EXTRAPOLATED edgelambda_tr = transport mean free path; the extrapolated half-thickness is the physical one plus d.

So in our slab solution the cosine reaches zero not at the physical half-thickness but at the extrapolated half-thickness ã = a + d. For a large power reactor d is a centimeter or two — small compared with a core that is meters across — so it is often neglected for back-of-envelope work but matters for precise critical-size calculations and for small, leaky systems.

AnalogyImagine a crowd thinning out at the open edge of a plaza. If you measured only where the density "looks like zero" and drew a straight line, you would over-predict the thinning rate right at the wall. The extrapolated boundary is like saying, "the crowd really runs out a couple of steps past the visible edge" — a small correction that makes the simple straight-line model match what people actually do.
Common mistakeDo not plug the physical dimension into Bg² = (pi/2a)² and its cousins. The buckling formulas use the extrapolated dimensions. Using the physical size makes a reactor look more leaky (larger Bg²) than it really is, and for small systems the error is significant. Always add the extrapolation distance d before computing geometric buckling.
✦ Worked Example — critical size and peak-to-average flux of a bare spherical reactor

A bare homogeneous reactor has material buckling B² = 0.0025 cm⁻² and a diffusion coefficient D = 0.80 cm. Treat it as a sphere. Find the extrapolated and physical critical radius, and the peak-to-average flux ratio.

  1. Match material to geometric buckling. Criticality demands Bg² = B². For a sphere Bg² = (pi / R̃)², so (pi / R̃)² = 0.0025, giving R̃ = pi / √0.0025 = pi / 0.05 = 62.8 cm (extrapolated radius).
  2. Subtract the extrapolation distance. d = 2.13 D = 2.13 × 0.80 = 1.70 cm. Physical radius R = R̃ − d = 62.8 − 1.7 ≈ 61.1 cm.
  3. Write the flux shape. φ(r) = φ0 sin(B r) / (B r), peaked at the center where the sinc tends to φ0.
  4. Average flux over the sphere. For the sinc mode the volume average works out to φavg = φ0 · (3 / pi²) ≈ 0.304 φ0.
  5. Peak-to-average ratio. φpeak / φavg = pi² / 3 ≈ 3.29. The center runs more than three times hotter than the core average — a severe peaking factor that, in a real core, forces designers to flatten the flux with zoning and control.

🛠️ Hands-On: plot the three flux shapes and check buckling in Python

We use the same free stack from Weeks 1–2 — NumPy, matplotlib, and OpenMC, plus SciPy for the Bessel function and its first zero. The full one-time install lives in the Week-1 lecture's setup box; if you skipped it, the only addition here is SciPy: conda install -c conda-forge scipy. Below we plot the analytic modes and verify the buckling numerically by feeding the flux back through the Laplacian.

# flux_shapes.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from scipy.special import j0, jn_zeros

a = 50.0                      # slab extrapolated half-thickness, cm
R = 50.0                      # sphere / cylinder extrapolated radius, cm
H = 100.0                     # cylinder extrapolated height, cm

# --- fundamental flux shapes (normalized to phi_0 = 1 at center) ---
x  = np.linspace(-a, a, 400)
slab = np.cos(np.pi * x / (2 * a))                   # cos(Bx)

r  = np.linspace(1e-6, R, 400)
B_sph  = np.pi / R
sphere = np.sin(B_sph * r) / (B_sph * r)             # sin(Br)/Br

nu0 = jn_zeros(0, 1)[0]                               # 2.405
cyl = j0(nu0 * r / R)                                 # radial Bessel part

# --- geometric bucklings ---
Bg2_slab = (np.pi / (2 * a))**2
Bg2_sph  = (np.pi / R)**2
Bg2_cyl  = (nu0 / R)**2 + (np.pi / H)**2
print(f"slab   Bg^2 = {Bg2_slab:.5f} 1/cm^2")
print(f"sphere Bg^2 = {Bg2_sph:.5f} 1/cm^2")
print(f"cyl    Bg^2 = {Bg2_cyl:.5f} 1/cm^2")

# --- numerical check: -phi''/phi should equal Bg^2 for the slab ---
d2 = np.gradient(np.gradient(slab, x), x)
print("slab -phi''/phi (mid) =", -np.median((d2 / slab)[150:250]))

plt.plot(x / a, slab, label="slab cos")
plt.xlabel("x / a"); plt.ylabel("flux / phi_0"); plt.legend()
plt.savefig("flux_shapes.png", dpi=120)

# slab Bg^2 = 0.00099 1/cm^2  ->  matches -phi''/phi ~ 0.00099

The numerical second derivative of the cosine, divided by the cosine, recovers Bg² to four digits — direct confirmation that the flux shape really does solve the Helmholtz equation. Later you can replace these analytic shapes with an OpenMC flux tally in a modeled core and watch the cosine emerge from first-principles Monte Carlo transport.

💬 Discussion

The fundamental flux shapes we derived all peak sharply at the center — the spherical mode runs about 3.3 times hotter at the core center than its volume average. Yet real power reactors are engineered to have a much flatter radial power profile. Why is a peaked flux a problem for a real core, and what physical levers could a designer pull to flatten it without changing the overall size or shutting the reactor down? Think about heat removal, fuel utilization, and what the diffusion equation says once the medium is no longer perfectly homogeneous.

Show a model response

A peaked flux means a peaked power density, because the local power tracks the fission rate Σfφ. The hottest spot — the center — sets the limit for the entire reactor: the coolant must remove the centerline heat without the fuel exceeding its melting or cladding-failure temperature. If the center peaks at 3.3 times the average, total power must be throttled to keep that one spot safe, so most of the fuel runs far below its limit and is underutilized. Flattening the profile lets you raise total power for the same peak temperature — better economics and a larger safety margin.

Our derivation assumed a bare, homogeneous core, but designers deliberately break both assumptions. Levers include: (1) fuel zoning / enrichment grading — lower-enriched fuel at the center and higher at the edges raises the edge flux relative to the center; (2) burnable poisons concentrated centrally that suppress the central flux early in life; (3) partially inserted control rods or chemical shim shaping the axial and radial profile; and (4) adding a reflector, which scatters leaking neutrons back and lifts the edge flux, raising the average without enlarging the core. Each makes the medium spatially varying, so B² becomes position-dependent and the clean analytic cosine gives way to a flatter, engineered shape — exactly the kind of problem the numerical diffusion and transport tools in later weeks are built to solve.

✓ Self-check rubric. A strong answer (1) connects flux peaking to power and the limiting hot-spot temperature; (2) explains that the peak caps total power and wastes the rest of the fuel; (3) names at least two concrete flattening levers (zoning, burnable poison, control shaping, reflector); and (4) recognizes that flattening abandons the homogeneous assumption, so B² becomes a function of position.

Q1. The one-group diffusion equation D∇²φ − Σaφ + νΣfφ = 0 expresses which physical statement in steady state?
A. Production = absorption + leakage, per unit volume
B. The flux is constant everywhere in the reactor
C. Neutrons travel in straight lines without scattering
D. Energy is conserved in each fission event
Show answer

A. The three terms are leakage (D∇²φ), absorption (Σaφ), and production (νΣfφ); their balance is production = absorption + leakage. (B) is false — the flux is peaked, not constant. (C) describes free streaming, the opposite of diffusion. (D) is a different conservation law, not what this equation states.

Q2. What distinguishes the geometric buckling from the material buckling?
A. They are always equal for any reactor of any size
B. Geometric buckling depends only on size and shape; material buckling only on cross sections
C. Geometric buckling depends on temperature; material buckling on pressure
D. Material buckling is always negative
Show answer

B. Geometric buckling Bg² comes from the dimensions and shape; material buckling comes from D and the cross sections. (A) is wrong — they are equal only for a critical bare reactor. (C) confuses unrelated variables. (D) is false; for a multiplying medium νΣf > Σa, so material B² is positive.

Q3. For a bare sphere of extrapolated radius R, the fundamental flux shape and geometric buckling are:
A. φ = φ0 cos(πr/R), Bg² = (π/R)²
B. φ = φ0 J₀(2.405 r/R), Bg² = (2.405/R)²
C. φ = φ0 sin(πr/R)/(πr/R), Bg² = (π/R)²
D. φ = φ0 e−r/R, Bg² = 1/R²
Show answer

C. The spherical Helmholtz solution that is finite at r = 0 is the sinc, sin(Br)/(Br), with B = π/R so Bg² = (π/R)². (A) is the cosine — the slab/axial form, which would have nonzero slope and a kink at the center for a sphere. (B) is the cylinder's radial part, not a sphere. (D) a decaying exponential does not solve ∇²φ + B²φ = 0 in a multiplying core.

Q4. Why do we use an extrapolated boundary instead of setting φ = 0 at the physical surface?
A. Because neutrons are reflected back at a bare surface
B. Because the flux is exactly zero a full meter inside the surface
C. Because diffusion theory slightly mispredicts the flux near a free surface, so the linear extrapolation hits zero a small distance d ≈ 2.13D beyond it
D. Because the buckling must be made negative at the edge
Show answer

C. Diffusion theory over-predicts how fast the flux falls near a vacuum boundary; transport theory shows the linear extrapolation reaches zero a distance d ≈ 0.71λtr ≈ 2.13D outside the surface. (A) describes a reflector, not a bare surface. (B) the distance is centimeters, not a meter. (D) the buckling is not made negative; the extrapolation is a geometric correction to where φ = 0.

Q5. A bare reactor's fundamental flux peaks sharply at the center. Why does this matter for real core design?
A. It does not matter; only the average flux affects safety
B. A peaked flux automatically makes the reactor subcritical
C. The peak occurs at the edge, where cooling is easiest, so it is harmless
D. The peak power density at the center sets the thermal limit, forcing total power to be throttled and the fuel to be flattened
Show answer

D. Power tracks Σfφ, so the central peak is the hottest spot; the coolant must keep it below its limit, which caps total power and wastes the cooler outer fuel — hence flux flattening. (A) ignores the hot spot, which is exactly what limits the design. (B) peaking is unrelated to criticality. (C) the fundamental mode peaks at the center, not the edge.
