Neutron transport & diffusion theory · Weeks 1–2
1. Why is the scalar flux φ, not the neutron density n alone, the quantity that drives reaction rates?
Answer: A. φ = n·v is the total distance flown per second by all neutrons in a cm³, so a reaction rate is exactly Σφ. (B) φ weights all speeds, fast or slow. (C) that describes the net current J, not the flux — flux adds up travel regardless of direction. (D) φ includes the speed factor v, which density alone lacks.
2. A region has a thermal-neutron density n = 1.0×10&sup8; cm³ and neutron speed v = 2.2×10⁵ cm/s. The scalar flux φ = n·v is approximately…
Answer: B. φ = n·v = (1.0×10&sup8;)(2.2×10⁵) = 2.2×10¹³ n/cm²/s. (A) drops five orders of magnitude. (C) doubles the correct value. (D) uses the wrong leading digit.
3. Fick's law for neutrons, J = −D∇φ, carries a minus sign. What does that sign mean physically?
Answer: C. Neutrons drift from crowded (high-φ) regions to sparse (low-φ) regions, exactly like heat flowing from hot to cold. (A) D is a positive material property. (B) confuses current direction with a separate reaction (absorption). (D) flux is density×speed, not a statement about local neutron speed.
4. A moderator has diffusion coefficient D = 1.2 cm and macroscopic absorption cross section Σa = 0.003 cm⁻¹. Its diffusion length L = √(D/Σa) is…
Answer: D. L = √(1.2/0.003) = √400 = 20 cm. (A) inverts the ratio. (B) is D/Σa itself, forgetting the square root. (C) does not correspond to the correct ratio.
One-group diffusion & flux shapes · Week 3
5. What exactly is the criticality condition for a bare, homogeneous reactor, in terms of buckling?
Answer: A. B² has two independent identities — one set by the material (cross sections, D), one set by the geometry (size/shape) — and a bare reactor is critical exactly when they coincide. (B) L and the core size are not forced equal. (C) at criticality production = absorption + leakage, not absorption alone equal to leakage. (D) the extrapolation distance is a small geometric correction, not driven to zero.
6. A bare slab reactor has extrapolated half-thickness a = 75 cm. Its geometric buckling B²g = (π/2a)² is approximately…
Answer: B. B²g = (π/150)² = (0.02094)² ≈ 0.000439 cm⁻². (A) is π/2a itself — forgetting to square it. (C) uses (π/a)² instead of (π/2a)², dropping the factor of 2. (D) is off by a factor of ten.
Criticality & the multiplication factor · Week 4
7. Why is keff always smaller than kinf for a real, finite reactor built from the same material?
Answer: C. keff = kinf·PNL, and PNL is strictly less than 1 whenever the core has a finite surface to leak through. (A) ν is a nuclide property, independent of size. (B) kinf is defined with zero leakage, not double-counted leakage. (D) D is always positive.
8. A bare core has kinf = 1.15, diffusion area L² = 45 cm², and geometric buckling B²g = 0.0035 cm⁻². Using keff = kinf/(1 + B²gL²), what is keff and the reactor's state?
Answer: D. B²gL² = 0.0035×45 = 0.1575, so keff = 1.15/1.1575 ≈ 0.9935 ≈ 0.99 < 1 — subcritical; this core is slightly too small or too leaky to sustain the chain as built. (A) simply reports kinf, skipping leakage entirely. (B) and (C) do not match the arithmetic.
9. Compressing a fissile metal sphere to higher density, keeping the same number of atoms, changes the critical mass how?
Answer: A. Higher density shortens the mean free path, shrinking the critical volume and lowering critical mass — exactly why criticality-safety rules forbid compressing or concentrating fissile material. (B) reverses the physics. (C) ignores that critical mass depends on density and geometry, not atom count alone. (D) has no physical basis.
Four- & six-factor formulas · Week 5
10. What is the key distinction between η (the reproduction factor) and ν (neutrons per fission) in the four-factor formula?
Answer: B. η = νΣf/Σa(fuel); since Σa(fuel) = Σf + Σc, η < ν whenever there is any parasitic capture in the fuel. Plugging ν into the four-factor formula where η belongs overpredicts k. (C) ν is a nuclear property of the fissioning nuclide, not the moderator. (D) η is typically well below 2.4 (e.g. ~1.33 for natural uranium).
11. A thermal lattice has η = 1.80, f = 0.68, p = 0.92, ε = 1.02, PFNL = 0.98, and PTNL = 0.97. What is keff?
Answer: C. kinf = η·f·p·ε = 1.80×0.68×0.92×1.02 ≈ 1.1486. keff = kinf·PFNL·PTNL = 1.1486×0.98×0.97 ≈ 1.092. (A) stops at kinf and forgets leakage. (B) stops after only two of the four factors. (D) does not match the arithmetic.
12. Inserting control rods into an operating core primarily lowers keff by reducing which factor, and why?
Answer: D. Control rods are deliberately strong thermal-neutron absorbers, so they reduce the fraction of thermal absorptions that occur in the fuel — the thermal utilization factor f. (A) ε is set by lattice geometry, not control rods. (B) η depends on fuel composition. (C) p is set during slowing-down, well before the already-thermal rod absorption occurs.
Neutron moderation & slowing-down · Week 6
13. Why are good neutron moderators made of light elements such as hydrogen, deuterium, or carbon?
Answer: A. Elastic kinematics let a neutron lose up to (1−α) of its energy per hit; α→0 as the target mass approaches the neutron's own mass, so hydrogen can absorb nearly all the energy in one collision. (B) good moderators must absorb little, not much. (C) moderators are not chosen for fission. (D) heavy nuclei do scatter, just with almost no energy transfer.
14. A moderator has average logarithmic energy loss ξ = 0.20. How many collisions, approximately, does it take to slow a neutron from 1.0 MeV to 0.025 eV thermal (n ≈ ln(Efast/Eth)/ξ)?
Answer: B. The lethargy gap is u = ln(1.0×10⁶/0.025) ≈ 17.5; dividing by ξ = 0.20 gives n ≈ 87.5 ≈ 88 collisions. (A) is the hydrogen-like count for a much larger ξ (≈0.92-1.0). (C) is the graphite count for ξ ≈ 0.158 from a 2 MeV start. (D) does not match this ξ and energy range.
15. What is the difference between a material's "moderating power" and its "moderating ratio"?
Answer: C. Light water has excellent moderating power (compact cores) but only a mediocre moderating ratio because hydrogen absorbs a non-trivial fraction of neutrons, which is exactly why light-water reactors need enriched fuel. (A) conflates two distinct figures of merit. (B) both quantities apply to any moderator. (D) is backwards — power ignores absorption entirely, which is why the ratio is needed.
Neutron energy spectra & multigroup methods · Week 7
16. A multigroup "group constant" Σx,g is computed by averaging the continuous cross section Σx(E) over group g weighted by…
Answer: D. Σx,g = ∫Σx(E)φ(E)dE / ∫φ(E)dE — flux-weighting is the only choice that reproduces the correct reaction rate when multiplied by the group flux. (A) and (C) are not the weighting variable for an energy collapse. (B) an unweighted mean ignores where the neutrons actually sit in energy and gives the wrong rate.
17. In the standard two-group diffusion equations, how are thermal-group (group 2) neutrons created?
Answer: A. With χ1 = 1 (all fission neutrons born fast), the only source term on the right of the thermal-group equation is the down-scatter coupling Σs,1→2φ1. (B) is wrong — fission neutrons are born fast, not thermal. (C) absorption removes neutrons, it does not create them. (D) leakage is a loss mechanism at the boundary, not a thermal-group source.
Reactor kinetics & delayed neutrons · Week 8
18. Why do delayed neutrons make a reactor controllable, when they are only about 0.65% of all fission neutrons for U-235?
Answer: B. As long as ρ < β, the reactor's behavior is governed by the slow precursor-decay timescale (tens of seconds to minutes), not the brutally fast prompt generation time Λ. (A) delayed neutrons cause fission like any other, they do not act as absorbers. (C) their count, not their energy, is what matters, and they are actually lower in energy on average. (D) precursor decay is independent of rod motion.
19. A critical reactor receives a step reactivity insertion of ρ = 0.0015, with β = 0.0075. The prompt jump ratio nafter/nbefore = β/(β−ρ) is…
Answer: C. β/(β−ρ) = 0.0075/(0.0075−0.0015) = 0.0075/0.0060 = 1.25 — power jumps about 25% almost instantly, then rises more slowly on the precursor timescale. (A), (B), and (D) do not match this arithmetic.
20. A reactor becomes "prompt critical" — power runs away on the millisecond, Λ-limited timescale — at which reactivity value?
Answer: D. At ρ = β the prompt term (ρ−β)/Λ turns from negative to zero/positive, so the chain no longer needs any help from delayed neutrons — the response collapses to the ~10⁻⁴ s prompt scale. (A) ordinary, controlled supercriticality starts at ρ = 0, far below the danger line. (B) ρ → 1 only as k → ∞, which is unphysical. (C) a negative reactivity describes a subcritical, decaying reactor, the opposite situation.
Reactivity feedback, temperature coefficients & the fuel cycle · Week 9
21. As fuel temperature rises, what does Doppler broadening do to the U-238 absorption resonances, and why is the resulting feedback so fast?
Answer: A. Thermal motion smears each resonance shorter and wider; the broadened wings reach energies that were previously shielded, so net capture rises and k falls — and because this happens in the fuel itself, it acts in milliseconds, long before any heat reaches the coolant. (B) gets the mechanism backwards (peaks shorten, not grow taller). (C) resonances broaden, they do not disappear. (D) Doppler feedback is independent of coolant boiling, which is the separate void-coefficient mechanism.
22. What determines the sign of a reactor's void coefficient?
Answer: B. In a light-water reactor, water is the primary moderator, so boiling it away removes moderation and lowers k — a safe, negative void coefficient. But in a design where water mainly absorbs neutrons beside a separate solid moderator (e.g. graphite), losing water removes an absorber without losing moderation, and voiding can add reactivity — a dangerous, positive void coefficient. (A) and (C) ignore this crucial design dependence. (D) pressure is not the deciding factor.
23. How does xenon-135's behavior after a reactor shutdown differ from samarium-149's?
Answer: C. Xenon's story is a race between production, its own ~9.2 h beta decay, and neutron burnout; tripping the reactor kills the burnout term but I-135 keeps feeding fresh Xe-135, so the concentration overshoots before finally decaying. Samarium-149 is stable, so once formed it only grows (fed by Pm-149 decay) and is removed solely by neutron absorption at power — it never decays away on its own. (A) and (D) are directly false; (B) swaps the two nuclides' roles.
Capstone: criticality in OpenMC · Week 10
24. In an OpenMC k-eigenvalue run, why must a number of "inactive" batches be discarded before scoring keff and tallies?
Answer: D. Fission-source iteration starts from an arbitrary guess; scoring before the source's Shannon entropy has flattened lets that arbitrary starting shape bias both keff and the flux tallies. (A) is not the physical reason. (B) there is no such universal hard-coded minimum — the right number depends on the problem. (C) geometry is fixed for the whole run, not redefined mid-simulation.
25. OpenMC's Monte Carlo statistical uncertainty on keff scales as σk ∼ 1/√N, where N is the total number of simulated neutron histories. To cut σk in half, you must…
Answer: A. Since σk ∝ 1/√N, halving σk requires N to increase by a factor of (2)² = 4. (B) doubling N only shrinks σk by √2 ≈ 1.41×, not 2×. (C) running fewer histories makes the uncertainty worse, not better. (D) a different seed resamples the same noise level; it does not reduce it systematically.
