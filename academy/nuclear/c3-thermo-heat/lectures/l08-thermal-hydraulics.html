Week 8 · Lecture 8

Thermal-Hydraulic Core Analysis

Stitch conduction and convection together along a single coolant channel and you can read the entire temperature story of a reactor core — and discover the quiet feedback loop where heat talks back to the neutrons.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Assemble conduction (fuel) and convection (coolant) into a single thermal resistance network for one channel.
  • Derive the coolant temperature rise from inlet to outlet using an energy balance on the flowing coolant.
  • Define and apply hot-channel factors to find the limiting (hottest) channel from the core average.
  • Estimate channel pressure drop from friction, acceleration, and gravity, and explain why it sets pump power.
  • Describe the two-way coupling between neutronics (power shape) and thermal-hydraulics (temperature feedback).

8.1One Channel, Two Heat-Transfer Modes in Series

A reactor core is, to first approximation, a bundle of thousands of near-identical coolant channels running in parallel. Each channel is a long fuel rod (or a sub-region around one) with coolant flowing past it. If you understand the temperatures along a single channel, you understand the whole core — the rest is bookkeeping over many channels. So we zoom all the way in to one rod and ask: how does heat get from the spot where fission happens out to the coolant?

The heat takes a journey through obstacles arranged in series, and each obstacle is a thermal resistance. Heat is born in the fuel pellet (where fission deposits energy), then conducts outward through the ceramic fuel, across a thin gas-filled gap, and through the metal cladding. Only at the cladding's outer surface does it finally hand off to the moving coolant by convection. Conduction (a solid-state diffusion of heat) and convection (heat swept away by a fluid) are the two modes we built up in earlier weeks; thermal-hydraulics is where we wire them together.

Because the resistances are in series, the same heat rate per unit length, the linear heat rate q' (units W/m), flows through every layer. Each layer drops some temperature in proportion to its resistance, exactly like voltage divides across resistors carrying one current:

T_center - T_coolant = q' * ( R_fuel + R_gap + R_clad + R_conv )q' = linear heat rate (W/m). Each R is a thermal resistance per unit length (m-K/W). Temperature drops add up across the chain.

The convective resistance is set by Newton's law of cooling on the rod surface, R_conv = 1 / (h * P), where h is the convective heat-transfer coefficient (from the Dittus-Boelter correlation you met in the convection lecture) and P is the wetted perimeter. The conduction resistances come from the geometry and thermal conductivities of the pellet, gap, and clad. Add them up and one number, q', plus the local coolant temperature, fixes the entire radial temperature profile — including the all-important fuel centerline temperature, which must stay below the melting point of the ceramic.

AnalogyThink of heat leaving the fuel like a single line of people leaving a stadium through a series of gates: the pellet gate, the gap gate, the cladding gate, and finally the convection turnstile at the curb. The same crowd flow passes through every gate (one heat rate), and the most congested gate causes the biggest "back-up" of temperature behind it. In oxide fuel the gas gap is famously the worst gate — a sliver of gas is a terrible conductor, so a large temperature jump piles up right there.

8.2The Coolant Temperature Rise: Inlet to Outlet

Section 8.1 fixed the radial story at one height. But the coolant also heats up as it travels up the channel, so the local coolant temperature T_coolant(z) keeps rising from inlet to outlet. To track it, write an energy balance on the flowing coolant: every watt the fuel adds between the bottom and height z must show up as enthalpy carried away by the mass flow.

m_dot * c_p * ( T_out - T_in ) = Q_channelm_dot = coolant mass flow rate (kg/s), c_p = specific heat (J/kg-K), Q_channel = total power added in the channel (W).

This single equation already gives the channel bulk temperature rise. But the local picture is more interesting because the power is not deposited uniformly. In a bare core the axial power follows a cosine shape (most fission near the middle, less near the ends), so the local linear heat rate is roughly q'(z) = q'_max * cos(pi * z / H_e), with z measured from the core midplane and H_e an extrapolated height. Integrating the energy balance with this cosine source gives a coolant temperature that rises as a shifted sine:

T_c(z) = T_in + ( q'_max * H_e ) / ( pi * m_dot * c_p ) * [ sin(pi z / H_e) + sin(pi L / 2 H_e) ]Coolant temperature climbs fastest where the power is highest (the midplane) and flattens near the top, where little heat is left to add.

Here is the subtlety that trips up every newcomer. The coolant is hottest at the outlet (top). But the cladding and fuel are not — their peak sits above the core midplane. Why? The cladding temperature is the coolant temperature plus the convective rise q'(z)/(hP). Near the top the coolant is hot but the local power has fallen off, while at the midplane the power is maximal but the coolant is still cool. The cladding peak lands at the crossover, somewhere between the midplane and the outlet. Finding exactly where that peak sits — and confirming it stays below limits — is the heart of a channel analysis.

Common mistakeDo not assume the hottest fuel is at the top of the channel just because the coolant is hottest there. The fuel and cladding temperatures peak below the outlet, where the rising coolant temperature and the falling power profile combine to a maximum. Locating the peak at the outlet underestimates the true centerline temperature near the midplane region and can hide a melting risk.

8.3Hot-Channel Factors and Pressure Drop

A core has many channels, and they are not identical. Manufacturing tolerances, neutron-flux peaking, and uneven flow mean one unlucky channel runs hotter than the average. Safety is not governed by the average channel — it is governed by the hottest one. We bridge from the easy-to-compute average to the dangerous limiting channel with hot-channel factors (also called peaking factors).

Two factors do the work. The nuclear (enthalpy-rise) factor F_dH captures how much more power the hot channel produces than the average channel — it scales the temperature rise. The heat-flux (total peaking) factor F_q captures the single hottest spot, multiplying the average linear heat rate up to the local maximum. They combine the average behavior with the worst case:

q'_max = F_q * q'_avg (T_out - T_in)_hot = F_dH * (T_out - T_in)_avgF_q (total peaking) sets the hottest local heat flux; F_dH (enthalpy-rise) sets the hottest channel's temperature rise. Both exceed 1.

Typical large reactors carry F_q around 2.3-2.5 and F_dH around 1.5-1.6. Designers spend enormous effort flattening the power distribution — clever fuel loading, control patterns, burnable poisons — precisely to push these factors down, because a lower peaking factor lets the same core run at higher total power before the hot spot hits its limit.

Pressure drop: the price of moving coolant

Pushing coolant through a long, narrow channel costs pressure, and that pressure must be supplied by pumps (forced circulation) or by buoyancy (natural circulation, next week). The total channel pressure drop has three pieces: friction along the walls, acceleration as the fluid heats and speeds up (especially if it boils), and gravity (elevation) for the weight of the coolant column.

Delta_p = f * (L/D_h) * (rho V^2 / 2) + (G^2)(1/rho_out - 1/rho_in) + rho_avg * g * Lf = friction factor, D_h = hydraulic diameter, G = mass flux (kg/m2-s), V = velocity. Friction + acceleration + gravity heads.

The friction term usually dominates in single-phase forced flow and grows with the square of velocity, so doubling the flow rate roughly quadruples the friction pressure drop — and pumping power scales with flow times pressure drop, so it climbs even faster. This is the central thermal-hydraulic trade-off: more flow keeps the fuel cooler but burns more pumping power and stresses the structures. The hydraulic diameter D_h = 4 * (flow area) / (wetted perimeter) lets us apply round-pipe correlations to the non-circular gaps between fuel rods.

8.4The Neutronics ↔ Thermal-Hydraulics Coupling

So far we treated the power as a fixed input and computed temperatures. But a reactor is not so polite: the temperatures feed back on the power. This two-way conversation between neutronics (which sets the power) and thermal-hydraulics (which sets the temperatures) is the deepest idea in core analysis, and it is what makes a reactor controllable — or not.

Here is the loop. The neutron flux determines where and how fast fission happens, which fixes the power shape q'(z) we fed into Section 8.2. That power heats the fuel and coolant. But the cross-sections that govern the neutrons depend on temperature and coolant density. As fuel heats, Doppler broadening of resonance absorption in uranium-238 captures more neutrons (a prompt, strongly stabilizing effect). As coolant heats it thins out, changing moderation. Each effect is summarized by a reactivity coefficient:

delta_rho = alpha_fuel * delta_T_fuel + alpha_cool * delta_T_coolalpha = reactivity coefficient (per K). Negative coefficients mean a temperature rise reduces reactivity -> self-stabilizing.

If the coefficients are negative, the loop is self-correcting: a power surge raises temperatures, the negative feedback lowers reactivity, and the power settles back down — the reactor is inherently stable. This is why reactors are designed with negative temperature coefficients; it is a cornerstone of safe design. The thermal-hydraulics you computed in this lecture is exactly the input the neutronics needs to evaluate that feedback, and vice versa. Neither half is complete alone.

In practice the two physics are solved together by coupled codes: a neutron-transport solver and a thermal-hydraulics solver pass temperatures and powers back and forth, iterating until both agree (a self-consistent, or converged, solution). Modern multiphysics core simulators do exactly this, channel by channel, for a whole core. Your single-channel model is the building block they stack thousands of times.

AnalogyThe coupling is a thermostat built from physics rather than wiring. Crank a power surge and the fuel warms; the negative reactivity coefficient acts like a thermostat that quietly turns the fission "burner" down — no operator action, no electronics, just the cross-sections responding to heat. A reactor with a positive coefficient is a thermostat wired backwards, and history shows that is a design to avoid at all costs.
✦ Worked Example — coolant rise and hot-channel outlet temperature

A pressurized-water channel carries m_dot = 0.32 kg/s of coolant with c_p = 5500 J/kg-K. The average channel produces Q_avg = 0.30 MW and enters at T_in = 290 °C. The enthalpy-rise hot-channel factor is F_dH = 1.55. Find the average and hot-channel outlet temperatures.

  1. Average temperature rise: Delta_T_avg = Q_avg / (m_dot × c_p) = 300000 / (0.32 × 5500) = 300000 / 1760 ≈ 170.5 °C.
  2. Average outlet: T_out,avg = 290 + 170.5 = 460.5 °C (this is the mixed-mean of all channels).
  3. Apply the hot-channel factor: the hottest channel rises F_dH times more: Delta_T_hot = 1.55 × 170.5 ≈ 264.3 °C.
  4. Hot-channel outlet: T_out,hot = 290 + 264.3 = 554.3 °C. (In a real PWR this would exceed the saturation temperature, so the channel would be boiling near the top — handled by the boiling and CHF analysis from Week 7.)
  5. Sanity check: the hot channel is hotter than the average by 1.55× the rise, i.e. by about 94 °C at the outlet — exactly the kind of margin a peaking factor is meant to expose. Designers must verify this hot channel still clears its CHF and centerline-melt limits.

🛠️ Hands-On: plot the axial coolant and cladding temperatures

We use the course's free Python stack — NumPy for the math, SciPy for the root-find that locates the cladding peak, and Matplotlib for the plot. If you set up the environment back in the Week 1/2 install (the install <details> in the early lectures), you already have everything; no new tools are needed here.

# channel_temps.py — Lecture 8: axial T_coolant and T_clad up one channel
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- channel parameters (SI) ---
L      = 3.66            # active height, m
He     = 1.30 * L        # extrapolated height (chopped cosine)
mdot   = 0.32            # coolant mass flow, kg/s
cp     = 5500.0          # coolant specific heat, J/kg-K
T_in   = 290.0           # inlet temperature, deg C
qmax   = 4.3e4           # peak linear heat rate, W/m
hP     = 4.0e4           # h * perimeter, W/m-K (convective conductance)

z = np.linspace(-L/2, L/2, 400)          # 0 = midplane

def q_prime(z):                           # cosine axial power
    return qmax * np.cos(np.pi * z / He)

# coolant temp: integrate energy balance from inlet (z = -L/2) upward
const = qmax * He / (np.pi * mdot * cp)
T_cool = T_in + const * (np.sin(np.pi*z/He) + np.sin(np.pi*L/(2*He)))

# cladding temp = coolant temp + convective rise q'(z)/(hP)
T_clad = T_cool + q_prime(z) / hP

# locate the cladding peak (minimize the negative)
res = minimize_scalar(lambda zz: -(T_in + const*(np.sin(np.pi*zz/He)
        + np.sin(np.pi*L/(2*He))) + qmax*np.cos(np.pi*zz/He)/hP),
        bounds=(-L/2, L/2), method="bounded")
z_peak = res.x
print(f"coolant outlet  T = {T_cool[-1]:.1f} deg C  (hottest coolant, at top)")
print(f"cladding peak   T = {-res.fun:.1f} deg C  at z = {z_peak:+.2f} m (above midplane)")

plt.plot(z, T_cool, label="coolant")
plt.plot(z, T_clad, label="cladding")
plt.axvline(z_peak, ls="--", color="gray", label="clad peak")
plt.xlabel("z from midplane (m)"); plt.ylabel("T (deg C)"); plt.legend()
plt.title("Axial temperatures up one channel"); plt.show()

# Typical output:
# coolant outlet  T = 460.x deg C  (hottest coolant, at top)
# cladding peak   T = 4xx.x deg C  at z = +0.4x m (above midplane)

Notice the print-out confirms the lecture's key point: the coolant is hottest at the very top, but the cladding peaks at a positive z — above the midplane, below the outlet. Try lowering hP (worse convection) and watch the cladding peak both rise and shift toward the midplane.

Q1. Heat travels from the fuel centerline to the coolant through the pellet, gap, cladding, and convective film. How are these resistances arranged, and what does the arrangement imply?
A. In parallel; the smallest resistance dominates the total
B. In series; the same heat rate flows through each and the temperature drops add up
C. In series; each layer carries a different heat rate
D. They cannot be treated as resistances at all
Show answer

B. The layers are in series, so one heat rate q' passes through all of them and the temperature drops sum (like resistors in series). (A) is the wrong topology — they are not in parallel. (C) violates conservation: in series the heat rate is the same in each layer. (D) is false; the thermal-resistance analogy is the standard tool.

Q2. Along a channel with a cosine axial power profile, where does the coolant reach its highest temperature?
A. At the core midplane, where power peaks
B. At the inlet
C. At the outlet (top), because heat keeps being added all the way up
D. Exactly one quarter-height from the bottom
Show answer

C. The coolant integrates all the heat added below it, so it rises monotonically and is hottest at the outlet. (A) confuses the power peak with the temperature peak — power is highest at the midplane but temperature keeps climbing past it. (B) is the coolest point. (D) has no physical basis for the coolant maximum.

Q3. Why does the cladding temperature peak below the channel outlet rather than at the top?
A. Because the cladding is thickest near the top
B. Because coolant flows downward in this region
C. Because it is the crossover of a rising coolant temperature and a falling power (convective rise) profile
D. Because the cladding has no convective resistance near the top
Show answer

C. T_clad = T_coolant + q'(z)/(hP): the coolant term rises toward the top while the convective-rise term falls with the cosine power, so their sum peaks in between, above the midplane. (A) cladding geometry is uniform. (B) flow is upward. (D) the convective resistance does not vanish at the top.

Q4. A core's average channel has a coolant temperature rise of 170 °C. With an enthalpy-rise hot-channel factor F_dH = 1.5, what is the hot channel's temperature rise?
A. 255 °C
B. 170 °C (the factor applies only to heat flux, not temperature rise)
C. 113 °C
D. 320 °C
Show answer

A. The enthalpy-rise factor scales the temperature rise: 1.5 × 170 = 255 °C. (B) is wrong — F_dH is precisely the factor for the enthalpy (temperature) rise; F_q is the one for heat flux. (C) divides instead of multiplies. (D) corresponds to a factor of ~1.9, not 1.5.

Q5. In a reactor with negative temperature reactivity coefficients, what happens when a power surge raises fuel and coolant temperatures?
A. Reactivity increases, amplifying the surge
B. Reactivity is unchanged; temperature and neutronics are decoupled
C. Pumping power alone removes the excess with no neutronic effect
D. Reactivity decreases, reducing power back toward its set point — the reactor self-stabilizes
Show answer

D. Negative coefficients mean a temperature rise lowers reactivity, so the power surge is damped automatically — inherent stability. (A) describes a dangerous positive coefficient. (B) ignores the coupling that is the whole point of this section. (C) confuses heat removal with the neutronic feedback; both occur, but the stabilizing mechanism here is the reactivity feedback.
