Week 1 · Lecture 1

Energy, Heat & the First Law

Every megawatt a reactor delivers is just bookkeeping on a single conserved quantity — energy — as it slips from fissioning fuel into flowing water and out as electricity.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a thermodynamic system, its boundary, and the difference between properties (state) and path quantities.
  • Distinguish internal energy, heat, and work, and identify the sign conventions used in reactor energy balances.
  • Apply the first law dU = Q − W to a closed system and trace where the fission energy goes.
  • Define enthalpy H = U + pV and specific heat, and use them to relate temperature rise to heat added.
  • Write the steady-flow energy equation and use it to size the temperature rise of coolant through a reactor core.

1.1Systems, Boundaries & Properties

Thermodynamics begins with a deceptively simple act: drawing a box. We pick a region of the universe to study — the system — and everything outside it is the surroundings. The imaginary surface separating them is the boundary. In a reactor, the system might be the water inside one fuel channel, the entire primary coolant loop, or just a single lump of fuel pellet. Choosing the boundary wisely is half the battle; every energy balance in this course is really an accounting of what crosses that boundary.

Boundaries come in three flavors. A closed system lets energy in and out but no mass — like the sealed working fluid in an idealized piston. An open system (a control volume) lets mass flow across the boundary too; the reactor core, with coolant streaming through it, is the canonical example. An isolated system exchanges neither mass nor energy — a useful idealization, never quite real.

To describe the state of the fluid inside our box, we use properties: pressure p, temperature T, volume V, density, internal energy, and so on. A crucial split divides the world of thermodynamics in two. A property (also called a state variable) depends only on the current condition of the system, not on how it got there. Heat and work are different — they are path quantities, energy in transit across the boundary, meaningful only during a process.

state property: depends only on the present state
path quantity (Q, W): depends on the route takenPressure, temperature, internal energy are properties. Heat and work are not properties — they describe transfers, not contents.
AnalogyThink of a reservoir behind a dam. The water level is a property: it tells you the present state regardless of history. The rain that fell and the water released through the turbines are path quantities — they describe transfers in and out, not what the lake "contains." You would never say the lake "holds 3 inches of rain"; rain is a flow, just as heat and work are flows of energy, not stored content.

1.2Internal Energy, Heat & Work

Drill down into the fluid in our box and you find ceaseless motion: molecules translating, rotating, and vibrating, plus the potential energy stored in the forces between them. Summed over every molecule, this microscopic mechanical energy is the internal energy U. It is a genuine property — a definite number for a given state — and for most fluids it rises with temperature. When a reactor heats its coolant, it is pumping internal energy into the water.

Energy crosses the boundary in exactly two ways. Heat Q is energy transferred because of a temperature difference — the disordered, molecule-by-molecule handoff from hot fuel cladding to cooler water. Work W is energy transferred by an organized force acting through a distance — a pump pushing fluid, or steam expanding against a turbine blade. Both are energy in transit; once they cross the boundary and settle in, they become indistinguishable contributions to U.

We need a sign convention, and this course uses the classical engineering one: heat added to the system is positive, and work done by the system is positive. With that choice, energy a reactor receives from fission shows up as +Q, and energy a turbine extracts shows up as +W.

Q > 0 : heat INTO the system
W > 0 : work done BY the systemEngineering sign convention. A pump doing work ON the fluid contributes W < 0.
Common mistakeDo not say a system "contains heat" or "has so much work." Heat and work exist only while energy is crossing the boundary — they are verbs, not nouns. What the system stores is internal energy (and enthalpy, kinetic, potential energy). Writing "the water holds 500 kJ of heat" is meaningless; the water holds internal energy, and heat is how some of it got there.

1.3The First Law: dU = Q − W

The first law of thermodynamics is the principle of energy conservation, sharpened into an equation. For a closed system, the change in internal energy equals the heat added minus the work done by the system:

dU = Q − W (closed system)Energy cannot be created or destroyed — only moved across the boundary as heat or work, or stored as internal energy.

Read it as an accounting statement. Put heat in (+Q) and U goes up. Let the system do work on the surroundings (+W) and U goes down, because the system spent some of its store pushing on the outside world. The first law simply insists the books balance: whatever crosses the boundary must show up as a change in what is stored inside.

For a reactor channel treated momentarily as closed — say a slug of stagnant coolant being heated by the fuel before the pump moves it — the work term is often negligible and nearly all the fission heat goes straight into internal energy and thus temperature. But the moment we let fluid flow through, work matters: the fluid pushed in at the inlet does work on our system, and our system does work pushing fluid out at the outlet. Handling that flow work cleanly is exactly why we will invent enthalpy in the next section.

1.4Enthalpy, Specific Heat & the Steady-Flow Energy Equation

When fluid flows through an open system, pushing it across the boundary takes work. To shove a parcel of volume V into a region at pressure p costs pV of flow work. Rather than track this separately every time, thermodynamics bundles it into a new property called enthalpy:

H = U + pV (specific: h = u + pv)Enthalpy bundles internal energy with the flow work pV. It is a property because U, p, and V all are.

Enthalpy is the natural energy currency for anything that flows, which is why reactor coolant tables list h, not U. To connect heat to temperature, we use specific heat — the energy needed to raise one kilogram of substance by one kelvin. At constant pressure (the relevant case for flowing coolant) it is c_p; at constant volume it is c_v. For a small temperature change the heat absorbed is:

Q = m * c_p * (T_out − T_in)m = mass, c_p = specific heat at constant pressure. For liquid water near reactor conditions, c_p is roughly 5 to 6 kJ/(kg*K).

Now combine everything for a control volume with fluid flowing steadily through it — a reactor core, a heat exchanger, a pump. In steady state nothing inside accumulates, so the rate of energy in must equal the rate out. Writing it per unit mass flowing, and including kinetic and potential energy, gives the steady-flow energy equation (SFEE):

q − w_s = (h_out − h_in) + (V_out^2 − V_in^2)/2 + g*(z_out − z_in)q = heat per kg, w_s = shaft work per kg, h = specific enthalpy, V = flow speed, z = height. Energy balance for a steady open system.

This single equation is the workhorse of reactor thermal-hydraulics. For a core with no shaft work (w_s = 0) and negligible changes in speed and height, it collapses to the beautifully simple statement that the heat added per kilogram equals the rise in enthalpy: q = h_out − h_in. Multiply by the mass flow rate and you have the reactor's thermal power. The whole job of the primary loop is to carry enthalpy from the core to the steam generator.

✦ Worked Example — coolant temperature rise across a reactor core

A pressurized-water reactor core produces 3000 MW of thermal power. The primary coolant (liquid water, c_p ≈ 5.5 kJ/(kg·K) at operating pressure) flows through at a mass flow rate of m_dot = 15,000 kg/s and enters at 290 °C. Treat the core as a steady-flow control volume with no shaft work. Find the coolant outlet temperature.

  1. Apply the SFEE for the core: with w_s = 0 and negligible kinetic/potential changes, q = h_out − h_in. Multiplying by mass flow rate, the thermal power is Q_dot = m_dot · c_p · (T_out − T_in).
  2. Solve for the temperature rise: Delta T = Q_dot / (m_dot · c_p).
  3. Plug in numbers: Delta T = (3000 × 10⁶ W) / (15,000 kg/s × 5500 J/(kg·K)) = (3.0 × 10⁹) / (8.25 × 10⁷) ≈ 36.4 K.
  4. Outlet temperature: T_out = 290 °C + 36.4 °C ≈ 326 °C.
  5. Sanity check: a rise of roughly 36 °C across the core is exactly the order of magnitude real PWRs run (inlet ~290 °C, outlet ~325 °C). Pressurizing the loop to ~155 bar keeps the water liquid at these temperatures — a theme we return to with phase change in later weeks.

Let's turn the worked example into reusable code and sweep the mass flow rate to see how it trades against temperature rise.

# core_energy_balance.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

def core_outlet_temp(power_W, mdot_kg_s, cp_J_kgK, T_in_C):
    # Steady-flow energy balance, no shaft work:
    # Q_dot = mdot * cp * (T_out - T_in)  ->  solve for T_out
    dT = power_W / (mdot_kg_s * cp_J_kgK)
    return T_in_C + dT

power = 3000e6        # thermal power, W
cp    = 5500.0        # J/(kg*K), pressurized liquid water
T_in  = 290.0         # inlet temperature, deg C

T_out = core_outlet_temp(power, 15000.0, cp, T_in)
print(f"Outlet temperature: {T_out:.1f} C  (rise {T_out - T_in:.1f} K)")
# Outlet temperature: 326.4 C  (rise 36.4 K)

# Sweep mass flow: faster flow -> smaller temperature rise
for mdot in (12000, 15000, 18000):
    To = core_outlet_temp(power, mdot, cp, T_in)
    print(f"mdot={mdot:6d} kg/s -> dT = {To - T_in:5.1f} K")
# mdot= 12000 kg/s -> dT =  45.5 K
# mdot= 15000 kg/s -> dT =  36.4 K
# mdot= 18000 kg/s -> dT =  30.3 K

Notice the inverse relationship: doubling the flow halves the temperature rise for the same power. That trade-off — pump harder to keep the core cooler — is a central design lever we will return to in the heat-transfer weeks.

Q1. Which of the following is a path quantity, not a property of the system?
A. Pressure
B. Internal energy
C. Heat
D. Temperature
Show answer

C. Heat is energy in transit across the boundary — it depends on the process path, not the current state. (A) Pressure, (B) internal energy, and (D) temperature are all state properties that describe the present condition regardless of history.

Q2. Using the engineering sign convention, a pump does work ON the reactor coolant. In the first law dU = Q − W, the work term W for this is…
A. negative, because work is done on the system, not by it
B. positive, because any work always raises U
C. zero, because pumps do no thermodynamic work
D. equal to the heat Q by definition
Show answer

A. W is defined as work done by the system, so work done on it (by a pump) makes W negative, which raises U via dU = Q − W. (B) confuses sign and ignores the convention; (C) pumps absolutely do work on the fluid; (D) there is no general relation W = Q.

Q3. Why is enthalpy h = u + pv, rather than internal energy alone, the natural quantity for analyzing flowing coolant?
A. Enthalpy is always larger, so the numbers look better
B. It automatically bundles in the flow work pv needed to push fluid across the boundary
C. Internal energy is not a property but enthalpy is
D. Enthalpy ignores pressure, simplifying the math
Show answer

B. For an open system, pushing fluid in/out costs flow work pv; enthalpy folds that into one property so the steady-flow balance stays clean. (A) is irrelevant; (C) both u and h are properties; (D) enthalpy explicitly includes the pv term, it does not ignore pressure.

Q4. A core delivers 3000 MW to coolant flowing at 15,000 kg/s with c_p = 5.5 kJ/(kg·K). The temperature rise across the core is closest to…
A. 3.6 K
B. 110 K
C. 36 K
D. 360 K
Show answer

C. Delta T = Q_dot/(m_dot·c_p) = 3.0×10⁹ / (15000 × 5500) ≈ 36 K. (A) and (D) are off by factors of ten (decimal-place slips); (B) would result from using a wrong c_p of about 1.8 kJ/(kg·K).

Q5. For a reactor core treated as a steady-flow control volume with no shaft work and negligible changes in flow speed and height, the heat added per kilogram of coolant equals…
A. the change in kinetic energy only
B. zero, since steady state means no energy change
C. the work done by the pump
D. the rise in specific enthalpy, h_out − h_in
Show answer

D. The SFEE collapses to q = h_out − h_in when w_s = 0 and kinetic/potential changes are negligible. (A) kinetic change is exactly what we neglected; (B) steady state means nothing accumulates inside, not that no energy crosses the boundary; (C) there is no pump in the core itself, and w_s = 0 here.
