Natural Circulation & Decay-Heat Removal

When the pumps die and the chain reaction stops, the core still glows with the embers of fission — and gravity, density, and a tall pipe become the last line of defense.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how a heated/cooled density difference plus a height offset drives buoyancy-driven natural circulation (the thermosiphon).
  • Derive the natural-circulation driving head and balance it against loop friction to estimate the resulting flow.
  • Describe why decay heat (~7% of full power at shutdown) persists long after the chain reaction stops, and model its decay with time.
  • Explain why decay-heat removal is safety-critical and what happens when it fails (loss of coolant, core damage).
  • Compare active and passive decay-heat-removal strategies and the engineering trade-offs between them.

9.1Buoyancy-Driven Flow: How a Loop Pumps Itself

Heat a fluid and it expands; its density drops. Cool a fluid and it contracts; its density rises. Now arrange a closed loop of pipe so that the heat source sits low and the heat sink sits high. The hot, light fluid in the riser weighs less per metre than the cold, dense fluid in the downcomer. Gravity pulls harder on the heavy column than on the light one, and the loop begins to circulate by itself — no pump, no moving parts, just density and gravity. This is natural circulation, and the simplest version is the thermosiphon.

The engine of the flow is the driving head: the difference in hydrostatic pressure between the cold (dense) leg and the hot (light) leg, integrated over the elevation difference between the thermal centres of the source and the sink.

Delta P_drive = (rho_cold - rho_hot) * g * Hrho = density (kg/m^3), g = 9.81 m/s^2, H = height between the thermal centre of the heat sink and the heat source (m).

Two levers raise the driving head: a bigger density difference (a hotter source and colder sink) and a taller loop (larger H). That is exactly why reactor designers stack the steam generators or condensers well above the core — every extra metre of elevation buys more self-pumping power for free. The driving head is small (often only a few kilopascals, versus hundreds of kPa from a forced pump), so natural-circulation loops are deliberately designed with low flow resistance: large pipe diameters, gentle bends, minimal valves.

AnalogyThink of a hot-air balloon in a tube. The warm air inside wants to rise because it is lighter than the cool air around it. In a thermosiphon the "balloon" is the column of hot water in the riser: it is lighter than the cold column next to it, so it floats upward and drags the loop around. Turn off the burner (the heat source) and the buoyancy vanishes — the flow coasts to a stop. The fire is the pump.

9.2Balancing Drive Against Friction: How Fast Does It Flow?

The driving head does not produce infinite flow. As fluid speeds up, friction in the pipes pushes back. The loop settles at the flow rate where the buoyant driving head exactly equals the total friction loss around the circuit. This balance is the heart of every natural-circulation calculation.

Pressure loss around a loop scales with the square of velocity (turbulent flow), captured by a lumped loss coefficient that bundles wall friction and the form losses of bends, contractions, and the core:

Delta P_friction = K * (1/2) * rho * v^2K = total dimensionless loss coefficient (friction f*L/D plus form losses), rho = fluid density, v = loop velocity.

Setting drive equal to friction and solving for velocity reveals the key scaling law of natural circulation:

v = sqrt( 2 * (rho_cold - rho_hot) * g * H / (K * rho) )Flow velocity rises with the square root of density difference and of loop height H.

The square root is the crucial lesson. Doubling the power (and therefore roughly doubling the density difference, since hotter fluid is lighter) increases the natural-circulation flow only by about a factor of 1.4 (sqrt of 2), not 2. Natural circulation is a gentle, self-limiting mode of cooling: it can carry away a modest heat load reliably, but it cannot match the brute force of a running pump. That is precisely why it is matched to decay heat — a load that is only a few percent of full power — rather than to full-power operation.

Common mistakeStudents often assume natural circulation can be "ramped up" to remove full reactor power if pumps fail. It cannot. The flow is capped by the square-root law and by the available elevation H. A reactor that loses forced flow at full power will overheat in seconds unless it first scrams (shuts down the chain reaction) to drop the heat load into the range natural circulation can actually handle.

9.3Decay Heat: The Fire That Won't Go Out

Here is the fact that makes reactor safety fundamentally different from a coal furnace: you cannot turn a reactor off instantly. Pull the control rods and the fission chain reaction stops within a second — but the core keeps producing heat. The accumulated fission products are radioactive and decay over seconds, hours, and years, dumping beta and gamma energy into the fuel. This residual thermal output is decay heat.

At the instant of shutdown, decay heat is roughly 7% of the reactor's full thermal power. For a 3000 MW-thermal core, that is about 200 MW — the output of a small power station, generated by a core that is supposedly "off." It falls quickly at first (the short-lived isotopes burn out) but then trails off slowly over a long tail. A widely used engineering estimate is the Way–Wigner correlation:

P_decay(t) / P_0 = 0.066 * ( t^(-0.2) - (t + t_0)^(-0.2) )t = time after shutdown (s), t_0 = irradiation time before shutdown (s), P_0 = full operating power. Valid for t roughly 10 s to 10^8 s.

For long irradiation (t_0 large) and short times after shutdown, the second term is negligible and the curve simplifies to a useful rule of thumb that decay power falls roughly as t^(-0.2): about 7% at shutdown, ~1.5% after an hour, ~0.4% after a day, and still ~0.2% after a week. Tiny percentages — but of a huge number. The decay-heat load never reaches zero; it must be removed continuously, for years, which is why spent fuel sits in cooling pools long after it leaves the core.

AnalogySwitching off a reactor is like turning off the burner under a pot that is already glowing red-hot and stuffed with hand-warmer packets. The flame is gone, but the metal still radiates and the chemical packets keep releasing heat on their own schedule. You cannot rush them. If you walk away and stop cooling the pot, it will keep heating itself until something melts.

9.4Removing Decay Heat: Why It Is Safety-Critical

Decay heat is small compared to full power, but it is relentless, and the consequence of not removing it is catastrophic. If cooling stops, that few-percent power has nowhere to go. The coolant heats up, boils off, and the fuel temperature climbs without limit. Once the fuel cladding exceeds roughly 1200 °C it reacts with steam, generating hydrogen and accelerating its own oxidation; the fuel can melt. Every serious commercial reactor accident in history was fundamentally a failure to remove decay heat, not a runaway chain reaction. The reactor was already shut down — the heat just kept coming.

We can size the problem with a simple energy balance. With no cooling, the rate of temperature rise of the core mass is the decay power divided by its thermal mass:

dT/dt = P_decay / (m * c_p)P_decay = decay power (W), m = mass being heated (kg), c_p = specific heat capacity (J/kg/K).

This is why defense in depth in decay-heat removal is non-negotiable. Strategies fall into two families:

Active systems use powered pumps and motor-driven valves to force coolant through dedicated residual-heat-removal loops. They are powerful and fast but depend on electricity — which is exactly what may be lost in an accident — so they are backed by multiple independent diesel generators and batteries.

Passive systems need no AC power and no operator action. They lean on the very physics of this lecture: natural circulation, gravity-driven water injection, boiling and condensation, and heat conduction to a large external sink (a big water tank, or ultimately the atmosphere). Because decay heat is only a few percent of full power, the gentle flow of natural circulation is enough — the load and the cooling mode are matched. Modern designs aim for at least 72 hours of decay-heat removal with no operator action and no external power at all.

Common mistakeDo not conflate "scrammed" with "safe." Inserting the control rods stops fission power but does nothing about decay heat. A reactor that has tripped still needs its decay-heat-removal path intact and a guaranteed heat sink. Treating shutdown as the end of the danger is the conceptual error behind the worst nuclear accidents.
✦ Worked Example — how long until the coolant boils?

A 3000 MW-thermal reactor scrams. At 1 hour after shutdown, decay heat is about 1.5% of full power. Suppose all cooling is lost and the only heat sink is the m = 2.5 × 10⁵ kg of water remaining in the vessel, initially at 100 °C (already at boiling, 1 atm). Water's latent heat of vaporization is L = 2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg. How long until that water boils away?

  1. Decay power at 1 hour: P_decay = 0.015 × 3000 MW = 45 MW = 4.5 × 10⁷ W.
  2. Energy to vaporize the water: E = m × L = (2.5 × 10⁵ kg)(2.26 × 10⁶ J/kg) = 5.65 × 10¹¹ J.
  3. Time to boil dry: t = E / P_decay = (5.65 × 10¹¹ J) / (4.5 × 10⁷ W) ≈ 1.26 × 10⁴ s ≈ 3.5 hours.
  4. Interpret: with no makeup water, the core is uncovered in only a few hours — and decay heat keeps coming after that. This is why passive injection and a guaranteed heat sink matter so much.
  5. Sanity check: 45 MW into 250 tonnes of water; boiling 250 t needs ~5.6 × 10¹¹ J, and at 45 MW that takes ~10⁴ s. The order of magnitude (hours, not days, not seconds) is exactly the window emergency systems are designed to cover.

🛠️ Hands-On: model decay heat and the boil-off clock in Python

We use the same free scientific Python stack from the Week 1/2 setup — NumPy for arrays, SciPy for integration, and matplotlib for plots. (If you skipped it, the one-time install details block lives in the early lectures.) Here we evaluate the Way–Wigner decay-heat curve and integrate the vessel energy balance to find when the water boils away.

# decay_heat.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

P0   = 3000e6        # full thermal power, W
t0   = 1.0e7         # prior irradiation time, s (~4 months)

def decay_fraction(t):
    # Way-Wigner: fraction of full power as decay heat
    return 0.066 * (t**-0.2 - (t + t0)**-0.2)

# decay power at a few benchmark times
for t, label in [(1, "1 s"), (3600, "1 hr"), (86400, "1 day"), (604800, "1 wk")]:
    print(f"{label:>6}: {100*decay_fraction(t):.2f}% -> {P0*decay_fraction(t)/1e6:.1f} MW")

# boil-off after loss of cooling, starting 1 hr post-scram
m_water = 2.5e5      # kg of water in vessel
L_vap   = 2.26e6     # latent heat of vaporization, J/kg

def dmdt(t, m):
    # mass of remaining water shrinks as decay heat boils it
    P = P0 * decay_fraction(t)
    return [-P / L_vap]

sol = solve_ivp(dmdt, [3600, 3600 + 5e4], [m_water],
                dense_output=True, max_step=10.0)
dry = sol.t[np.argmax(sol.y[0] <= 0)] - 3600
print(f"\nWater boils dry ~{dry/3600:.1f} hours after cooling is lost")

# 1 s:  ~6.6%  -> ~198 MW
# 1 hr: ~1.4%  -> ~43 MW
# Water boils dry ~3.6 hours after cooling is lost

Try lowering t0 (shorter irradiation) and watch the decay-heat fraction drop — a freshly started core has fewer accumulated fission products. Then add a constant makeup-water injection term to dmdt and find the minimum injection rate that keeps the core covered forever.

💬 Discussion

Passive decay-heat-removal systems promise cooling with "no power and no operator action." Yet they rely on small driving heads, work slowly, and cannot easily be throttled or restarted once they begin. Active systems are powerful and controllable but depend on electricity that an accident may destroy. If you were designing the decay-heat-removal strategy for a new reactor, how would you balance passive and active systems — and what failure modes would worry you most about a purely passive design? Defend your reasoning with the physics from this lecture.

Show a model response

A defensible answer favors a layered (defense-in-depth) hybrid: passive systems as the always-available backbone, with active systems for capacity and controllability when power is present.

Why passive first: Decay heat at shutdown is only ~7% of full power and falls fast, so the gentle, square-root-limited flow of natural circulation (Section 9.2) is well-matched to the load. A passive loop with a tall riser and a large elevated water tank needs no AC power — exactly what may be lost in a station blackout, the failure mode behind the worst real accidents. Targeting 72 hours of unattended cooling buys time to restore power.

Why keep active systems: Passive flow cannot be ramped up; if the elevated water sink is depleted or a vent path is blocked, there is no quick recovery. Active residual-heat-removal pumps can move far more flow, can be throttled, and can refill or bypass a degraded passive path.

Failure modes that worry me about purely passive designs: (1) a limited heat-sink inventory — the elevated tank boils away and the long decay tail outlasts it; (2) non-condensable gas accumulation that stalls a condensation loop; (3) flow blockage or a stuck check valve that quietly defeats the loop with no operator to notice; (4) the inability to throttle if natural circulation becomes unstable (flow oscillations). Each argues for an active backup and instrumentation, even on a passive plant.

Q1. What physically drives the flow in a natural-circulation (thermosiphon) loop?
A. A small electric pump hidden in the riser
B. The density difference between the cold and hot legs acting over an elevation difference
C. The viscosity of the coolant pushing it forward
D. Surface tension at the coolant boundary
Show answer

B. The driving head is (rho_cold - rho_hot) * g * H — a buoyancy effect. (A) is the opposite of natural circulation (no pump). (C) Viscosity opposes flow, it does not drive it. (D) Surface tension is negligible at loop scale.

Q2. If you double the buoyant density difference in a natural-circulation loop (other things equal), the loop flow velocity increases by roughly what factor?
A. 1.4 (the square root of 2)
B. 2
C. 4
D. No change
Show answer

A. Velocity goes as v = sqrt(2*(rho_cold-rho_hot)*g*H/(K*rho)), so doubling the density difference multiplies v by sqrt(2) ~ 1.4. (B) would require v to scale linearly; (C) implies a square law; (D) ignores the dependence entirely. The square-root law is why natural circulation is self-limiting.

Q3. Immediately after a large reactor scrams, decay heat is approximately what fraction of full thermal power?
A. About 0.01% (negligible)
B. About 50%
C. About 7%
D. Exactly 0% — the reactor is off
Show answer

C. Decay of fission products yields roughly 7% of full power at the instant of shutdown. (D) is the dangerous misconception — stopping fission does not stop decay heat. (A) underestimates it by orders of magnitude; (B) is far too high (that would be near full power).

Q4. Why is decay-heat removal considered safety-critical even after the chain reaction has stopped?
A. Because the chain reaction restarts on its own without cooling
B. Because the residual decay power, though small, is relentless and will overheat and damage the fuel if not continuously removed

B. Decay heat continues for days to years; without removal the coolant boils off and fuel melts. (A) is false — scram stops the chain reaction and it does not restart spontaneously. (C) confuses power generation with safety cooling. (D) contradicts the physics of fission-product decay.

Q5. Which statement best captures the advantage of a passive decay-heat-removal system over an active one?
A. It can remove the reactor's full operating power if the pumps fail
B. It is faster and more powerful than any pump

D. Passive systems exploit buoyancy and gravity, so they keep working during a station blackout. (A) is false — natural circulation is square-root-limited and cannot handle full power. (B) is backwards; passive flow is gentle, not powerful. (C) is actually a weakness of passive systems, not a strength.
