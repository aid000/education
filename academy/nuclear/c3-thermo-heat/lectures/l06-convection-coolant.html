Week 6 · Lecture 6

Convection & Coolant Heat Transfer

Conduction carries the heat to the fuel-rod surface — but it is the river of coolant sweeping past that surface, and the thin clinging film right against it, that decides whether a reactor runs cool or melts.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain convective heat transfer as conduction across a thin boundary layer swept away by bulk fluid motion.
  • Apply Newton's law of cooling, q'' = h(T_s − T_f), and interpret each term physically.
  • Describe what the convective heat-transfer coefficient h depends on, and why forced convection beats natural convection in a reactor.
  • Use the Dittus-Boelter correlation to link the Nusselt, Reynolds, and Prandtl numbers and estimate h in a coolant channel.
  • Compute the film temperature drop between a fuel-rod surface and the bulk coolant.

6.1What Convection Really Is: Conduction Plus Motion

In Week 5 we tracked heat as it diffused outward through solid fuel and cladding by conduction — energy hopping between jostling atoms with no net movement of material. But once that heat reaches the surface of a fuel rod, conduction alone cannot carry it away fast enough. The trick a reactor uses is to wash the surface with a moving fluid that physically carries the hot molecules off and replaces them with cold ones. That bulk transport of thermal energy by a moving fluid is convection.

Here is the subtlety that confuses almost everyone: right at the solid wall, the fluid is not moving at all. Friction pins the fluid to the surface — the no-slip condition — so the layer of fluid touching the wall has zero velocity. Heat must first cross that stagnant film by conduction. Convection is therefore conduction through a thin thermal boundary layer, after which the bulk flow sweeps the warmed fluid downstream. The faster and more turbulent the flow, the thinner that boundary layer, and the steeper the temperature gradient across it — which means more heat removed.

We distinguish two regimes. In natural (free) convection, the only thing moving the fluid is buoyancy: warm fluid expands, becomes less dense, and rises on its own. In forced convection, a pump or fan drives the fluid past the surface at high speed. Reactors overwhelmingly rely on forced convection — large pumps push coolant through the core at several meters per second — because forced flow removes orders of magnitude more heat than buoyancy ever could.

AnalogyBlow on a spoonful of hot soup. You are not changing the soup's conductivity — you are forcibly stripping away the thin layer of cooled air sitting on the surface and replacing it with fresh cool air, steepening the temperature gradient right at the liquid surface. That is exactly forced convection: thinning the boundary layer to drag heat out faster. A reactor coolant pump is just a very large, very persistent breath.

6.2Newton's Law of Cooling & the Coefficient h

Rather than resolve the messy boundary layer in detail every time, engineers fold all of its complexity into a single number, the convective heat-transfer coefficient h, and write the heat flux leaving a surface with the deceptively simple Newton's law of cooling:

q'' = h ( T_s - T_f )q'' = heat flux (W/m^2), h = convective coefficient (W/m^2 K), T_s = surface temperature, T_f = bulk fluid temperature.

Read it as a statement of proportionality: the heat flux pulled off a surface is proportional to how much hotter the surface is than the bulk fluid sweeping past. Double the temperature difference and you double the heat removed. The whole art lives inside h, which is not a property of the fluid alone — it bundles together the fluid's conductivity, its speed, its viscosity, the channel geometry, and the flow regime (laminar vs turbulent). A higher h means the surface can dump the same heat at a smaller temperature excess, keeping the fuel cooler.

To get the total heat rate off a surface of area A, multiply the flux by the area:

Q = h A ( T_s - T_f )Q = total heat rate (W). This is the workhorse equation of every heat exchanger and reactor channel.

Typical magnitudes tell the story. Natural convection of air gives h of only a few to tens of W/m²K; forced air, hundreds; forced liquid water in a reactor channel, tens of thousands; and water that is actively boiling, hundreds of thousands. That enormous range is exactly why reactors pump pressurized water hard through the core: to push h — and therefore heat removal at a given temperature margin — as high as possible.

Common mistakeDo not treat h as a fixed material property you can look up like thermal conductivity. The same water in the same channel can have an h that differs by a factor of ten between slow laminar flow and fast turbulent flow. h must be computed for the actual flow conditions — that is precisely what the Dittus-Boelter correlation in 6.3 does. Plugging a generic "h for water" into a design calculation is a classic and dangerous error.

6.3Dittus-Boelter: Predicting h from Nu, Re & Pr

So how do we actually get h for a real coolant channel? We use dimensionless numbers — the universal grammar of heat transfer — which let one experiment speak for thousands of geometries and fluids. Three of them matter here.

The Reynolds number Re compares inertial forces to viscous forces and tells us whether flow is smooth (laminar) or chaotic (turbulent):

Re = rho v D / mu = v D / nurho = density, v = bulk velocity, D = hydraulic diameter, mu = dynamic viscosity, nu = kinematic viscosity. Pipe flow turns turbulent above Re ~ 2300; reactor channels run Re ~ 100000-500000.

The Prandtl number Pr is a pure fluid property comparing how fast momentum diffuses to how fast heat diffuses:

Pr = mu c_p / k = nu / alphac_p = specific heat, k = fluid conductivity, alpha = thermal diffusivity. Water Pr ~ 1-7; liquid metals Pr ~ 0.01 (heat outruns momentum hugely).

The Nusselt number Nu is the prize — it is the ratio of actual convective heat transfer to pure conduction across the same fluid layer, and it packages h itself:

Nu = h D / k -> h = Nu k / DNu = 1 means "no better than still-fluid conduction"; Nu >> 1 means convection dominates. Once we know Nu, we know h.

The Dittus-Boelter correlation is the famous empirical bridge between them for turbulent flow in a smooth tube. It says Nu rises with both Reynolds and Prandtl numbers raised to fixed powers:

Nu = 0.023 Re^0.8 Pr^nn = 0.4 when the fluid is being heated (reactor case), n = 0.3 when cooled. Valid for Re > 10000, 0.7 < Pr < 160, smooth tubes, fully developed turbulent flow.

Read the physics in the exponents. The strong Re0.8 dependence is why pumping harder helps so much: an eightfold increase in flow speed roughly quintuples h. The Pr0.4 term explains why ordinary water is a fine coolant (Pr near 1, balanced) while liquid sodium needs entirely different correlations — its Pr near 0.01 falls outside Dittus-Boelter's range, because heat diffuses through it far faster than momentum.

AnalogyThink of Nu as a "convection score" graded against a baseline of doing nothing. A score of 1 means the moving fluid removed heat no better than a stagnant puddle would by conduction alone. A reactor channel scores Nu in the hundreds — convection is hundreds of times more effective than still-fluid conduction. Dittus-Boelter is simply the empirical formula that predicts your score from how hard you stir (Re) and what you are stirring (Pr).

6.4The Film Temperature Drop

Now we close the loop with the quantity that designers truly care about: the film temperature drop, the temperature difference between the hot cladding surface and the bulk coolant that the convection has to "spend" to push the heat across the boundary layer. Rearranging Newton's law of cooling:

Delta T_film = T_s - T_f = q'' / hThe hotter the surface must run above the coolant to drive the required flux. Smaller is safer: it keeps the cladding cool.

This single relation governs reactor thermal margins. The core must remove a fixed heat flux q'' set by the fission power. If h is large, the surface only needs to sit a modest amount above the coolant — the cladding stays cool and far from failure. If h drops (a pump trips, flow stalls, the boundary layer thickens), the same q'' now forces a much larger Delta T_film, and the cladding temperature climbs toward dangerous territory. The entire safety case for forced cooling is the demand that q''/h stay small.

Stacking this on Week 5's conduction drops gives the full thermal picture: the fuel centerline sits hottest, then temperature falls by conduction through the fuel pellet, across the fuel-clad gap, through the cladding wall, and finally drops by Delta T_film across the convective film into the coolant. The coolant itself is the lowest temperature in the chain — and convection is the last, decisive handoff that sets how hot everything upstream must run.

Common mistakeStudents often assume that raising the coolant flow rate lowers the bulk coolant temperature T_f. It does not, directly — T_f is set by how much energy the coolant absorbs as it travels up the channel (an energy balance). What faster flow does is raise h, which shrinks the film drop q''/h, pulling the surface temperature T_s down toward the bulk T_f. Confusing "lowering T_f" with "shrinking the film drop" leads to wrong conclusions about why pumps protect the fuel.
✦ Worked Example — h and film drop in a PWR coolant channel

Pressurized water flows through a coolant subchannel with hydraulic diameter D = 0.012 m at v = 4.5 m/s. Take water properties at operating conditions: density rho = 720 kg/m³, dynamic viscosity mu = 9.0×10⁻⁵ Pa·s, specific heat c_p = 5700 J/kg·K, conductivity k = 0.56 W/m·K. The surface heat flux is q'' = 1.0×10⁶ W/m². Find h and the film temperature drop. The water is being heated, so n = 0.4.

  1. Reynolds number: Re = rho v D / mu = (720 × 4.5 × 0.012) / 9.0e-5 = 38.88 / 9.0e-5 ≈ 4.32×10⁵. Well above 10⁴, so turbulent — Dittus-Boelter applies.
  2. Prandtl number: Pr = mu c_p / k = (9.0e-5 × 5700) / 0.56 = 0.513 / 0.56 ≈ 0.916. Inside the valid range (0.7–160).
  3. Nusselt number: Nu = 0.023 × Re^0.8 × Pr^0.4. Re^0.8 = (4.32e5)^0.8 ≈ 31700; Pr^0.4 = (0.916)^0.4 ≈ 0.965. So Nu ≈ 0.023 × 31700 × 0.965 ≈ 704.
  4. Heat-transfer coefficient: h = Nu k / D = 704 × 0.56 / 0.012 ≈ 3.28×10⁴ W/m²K. Tens of thousands — exactly the forced-liquid range.
  5. Film temperature drop: Delta T_film = q'' / h = 1.0e6 / 3.28e4 ≈ 30.5 K. The cladding surface runs about 30 K above the bulk coolant — a comfortable margin. If a pump trip halved the flow, h would fall by ~2^0.8 ≈ 1.74×, pushing the film drop to ~53 K and the surface hotter.

🛠️ Hands-On: sweep the coolant velocity and watch the film drop fall

We use the same free Python scientific stack set up in Weeks 1–2 — NumPy for the arithmetic and matplotlib for the plot. (If you skipped the early install, the one-time setup details live in the Week 1 lecture.) Here we turn the Dittus-Boelter chain into code and sweep the pump speed to see how the film temperature drop responds.

# convection_channel.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- water properties at PWR conditions ---
rho = 720.0      # kg/m^3
mu  = 9.0e-5     # Pa.s
cp  = 5700.0     # J/kg.K
k   = 0.56       # W/m.K
D   = 0.012      # hydraulic diameter, m
qpp = 1.0e6      # surface heat flux, W/m^2

def film_drop(v):
    Re = rho * v * D / mu
    Pr = mu * cp / k
    Nu = 0.023 * Re**0.8 * Pr**0.4   # Dittus-Boelter, heated (n=0.4)
    h  = Nu * k / D
    return h, qpp / h                # h (W/m^2K), film drop (K)

v = np.linspace(1.0, 8.0, 50)        # coolant speed sweep, m/s
h, dT = np.vectorize(film_drop)(v)

print(f"At 4.5 m/s:  h = {film_drop(4.5)[0]:.3e} W/m2K,  dT_film = {film_drop(4.5)[1]:.1f} K")

plt.plot(v, dT)
plt.xlabel("coolant velocity (m/s)")
plt.ylabel("film temperature drop (K)")
plt.title("Faster flow -> larger h -> smaller film drop")
plt.grid(True); plt.show()

# At 4.5 m/s:  h = 3.28e+04 W/m2K,  dT_film = 30.5 K
# Doubling speed cuts the film drop by ~2^0.8 = 1.74x.

The curve falls steeply at low speed and flattens at high speed — the Re^0.8 exponent means the first pumps buy the most cooling, with diminishing returns later. Try swapping in liquid-sodium properties (Pr ~ 0.005) and you'll see Dittus-Boelter under-predict badly: that's your cue that low-Pr metals need their own correlations, covered in the advanced reading.

🔗 Where this is heading

The film temperature drop you just computed assumed the coolant stays comfortably liquid. But push the heat flux high enough and the wall gets hot enough to boil the water clinging to it — and boiling changes everything, dramatically boosting h until, suddenly and catastrophically, it doesn't. Week 7 takes on boiling heat transfer and the critical heat flux, the cliff edge where a vapor blanket chokes off convection and surface temperatures spike — the single most important safety limit in a water-cooled core. Later in the course, these channel-level coefficients feed full thermal-hydraulic system models, and tie back to the reactor-physics power distributions from the C2 course that set q'' in the first place.

💬 Discussion

A reactor operator notices the fuel cladding surface temperature is creeping upward over several minutes, even though the reactor power (and therefore the surface heat flux q'') has held perfectly steady on the instruments. The bulk coolant inlet temperature is also unchanged. Using only the physics of this lecture — Newton's law of cooling, the convective coefficient h, and the Dittus-Boelter correlation — propose what could be happening, and explain how you would reason it out. Could the danger be growing even while the power meter looks fine?

Show a model response

Since q'' is fixed but the surface temperature T_s is rising, the film temperature drop Delta T_film = T_s − T_f = q''/h must be increasing. With q'' constant, the only way the film drop grows is if h is falling. Newton's law of cooling pins this down: nothing else in q'' = h(T_s − T_f) can explain a rising T_s at constant q'' and constant T_f.

What lowers h? Through Dittus-Boelter, Nu = 0.023 Re^0.8 Pr^0.4, and h = Nu·k/D, so h tracks Re^0.8 — it drops if the coolant flow rate is decreasing (a pump degrading, a partial flow blockage, or gas/voids forming in the channel and reducing effective mass flow). A flow reduction shows up nowhere on a power meter, yet it steadily starves convection and forces the cladding hotter to drive the same flux. The danger is indeed growing invisibly: the thermal margin to boiling or cladding limits is shrinking even though "power" looks fine.

A good answer would also note an alternative: fouling or a deposit (crud) on the surface adds a thermal resistance in series, which mimics a reduced effective h. The reasoning path — fix q'', watch Delta T_film, infer h, then ask what physically lowers h via Re — is the key skill.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong response (1) recognizes Delta T_film = q''/h must be rising; (2) concludes h is falling since q'' and T_f are fixed; (3) connects falling h to a drop in Re (flow rate) via Dittus-Boelter, or to added surface resistance from fouling; and (4) states clearly that the safety margin can erode while the power reading stays constant.

Q1. In Newton's law of cooling q'' = h(T_s − T_f), if the surface-to-fluid temperature difference doubles while h is unchanged, the heat flux removed…
A. doubles
B. is unchanged
C. halves
D. quadruples
Show answer

A. The law is linear in (T_s − T_f), so doubling the difference doubles q''. (B) ignores the proportionality; (C) reverses it; (D) would require a square dependence, which Newton's law does not have.

Q2. Why is the convective coefficient h not a fixed property you can simply look up for a given fluid?
A. Because h depends only on temperature
B. Because h bundles flow speed, viscosity, geometry, and flow regime together, not just the fluid
C. Because h is always exactly equal to the fluid's thermal conductivity
D. Because h never changes between laminar and turbulent flow
Show answer

B. h depends on velocity, viscosity, conductivity, channel geometry, and whether flow is laminar or turbulent — it must be computed for the actual conditions. (A) is far too narrow; (C) confuses h with k (h = Nu·k/D); (D) is false — the regime change can swing h by an order of magnitude.

Q3. In the Dittus-Boelter correlation Nu = 0.023 Re^0.8 Pr^n, what does the Nusselt number physically represent?
A. The ratio of buoyancy to viscous forces
B. The ratio of momentum diffusivity to thermal diffusivity
C. The ratio of convective heat transfer to pure conduction across the same fluid layer
D. The total heat rate in watts
Show answer

C. Nu = hD/k compares actual convective transfer to conduction alone; Nu >> 1 means convection dominates. (A) describes a Grashof/Rayleigh-type ratio; (B) is the Prandtl number; (D) confuses a dimensionless number with a dimensional heat rate.

Q4. A coolant pump partially fails, halving the flow speed while the reactor's surface heat flux q'' stays constant. What happens to the film temperature drop T_s − T_f?
A. It drops to zero because flow still exists
B. It is unaffected because q'' did not change
C. It decreases, cooling the surface
D. It increases, because lower flow lowers h and Delta T_film = q''/h
Show answer

D. Slower flow lowers Re, hence Nu and h (h ~ Re^0.8). With q'' fixed, Delta T_film = q''/h rises, pushing the surface hotter. (A) and (C) get the direction backwards; (B) wrongly assumes the film drop depends only on q'' — it depends on the ratio q''/h.

Q5. Why do reactors rely on forced convection rather than natural (free) convection for normal cooling?
A. Forced convection drives much higher flow speeds, raising h by orders of magnitude over buoyancy-driven flow
B. Natural convection moves fluid faster than any pump
C. Forced convection eliminates the boundary layer entirely
D. Natural convection has a higher h than forced convection in all cases
Show answer

A. Pumped flow gives h of tens of thousands of W/m²K versus a few to tens for natural convection — vastly more heat removal at a given temperature margin. (B) and (D) are backwards; (C) is false — forced flow thins the boundary layer but never removes it (no-slip always holds).
