10.1The Hot Channel: Stacking Ten Weeks Into One Model

For ten weeks you have collected the pieces. Week 4 gave you volumetric heat generation q''' from fissioning fuel. Week 5 turned that into a conduction problem inside a solid fuel pellet. Week 6 handed you convection to a flowing coolant through Newton's law of cooling. This capstone wires them together along the length of one fuel rod and its surrounding coolant channel — the hot channel, the single most heavily loaded channel in the whole core.

A reactor core is a bundle of thousands of identical fuel rods, each sitting in its own slice of coolant. Power is not uniform: a rod near the center of the core runs hotter than one at the edge, and within a rod the fission rate peaks near the middle and tapers at the ends, tracing the neutron flux shape. The safety case for the entire plant is decided by the worst rod — the hot channel — so that is what we model. If the hottest pin stays safe, every cooler pin does too.

We treat the channel as one-dimensional in the axial direction z, running from the bottom (inlet, z = 0) to the top (outlet, z = L). Heat is born inside the fuel, conducts radially outward through pellet and cladding, crosses into the coolant by convection, and is swept upward by the flow. The axial power profile of a bare core is well approximated by a cosine peaked at the midplane:

q'(z) = q'_max * cos( pi * (z - L/2) / L_e )q'(z) = linear heat rate [W/m] at height z; q'_max = peak value at midplane; L_e = extrapolated length (slightly > L). The cosine falls toward zero at the ends.

The linear heat rate q' (watts per meter of rod) is the natural currency here: multiply by a slice height dz and you get the power deposited in that slice. Integrating q'(z) over the whole rod gives the rod's total power. From this single profile, three temperatures follow in sequence — coolant, then cladding surface, then fuel centerline — each built on the previous one.

AnalogyThink of the coolant climbing the channel like a hiker walking up a trail past a row of campfires. Each campfire (a slice of fuel) hands the hiker some heat as they pass. The hiker (coolant) gets steadily warmer the higher they climb — even after passing the hottest fire near the middle, because every fire after it still adds some warmth. That is why the coolant keeps heating all the way to the top, while the fire intensity peaked back at the midplane.

10.2Building the Coolant Temperature Profile

Start with the coolant, because everything else is referenced to it. The flow has a fixed mass flow rate m_dot [kg/s] and specific heat c_p [J/(kg·K)]. As it climbs from the inlet at temperature T_in, it absorbs the heat shed by the fuel below it. This is the first law applied to a control volume (Week 1): the enthalpy the coolant gains equals the heat added.

m_dot * c_p * dT_c/dz = q'(z)Energy balance on a coolant slice. Left side = rate enthalpy rises; right side = heat handed over by the fuel at that height.

Integrating from the inlet to a height z gives the coolant temperature as a running total of all the heat added below that point:

T_c(z) = T_in + (1 / (m_dot * c_p)) * integral_0^z q'(z') dz'The coolant temperature is a cumulative integral — it only ever rises, reaching its maximum at the outlet z = L.

For the cosine power shape, that integral is a sine, so the coolant temperature rises slowly near the bottom (little power yet), steeply through the middle (peak power), and slowly again near the top — an S-shaped climb that maxes out at the outlet. The total rise across the core, T_out − T_in, is just the rod's total power divided by m_dot·c_p.

Common mistakeDo not assume the coolant is hottest where the power is highest. The coolant temperature is the integral of the power, not the power itself. The coolant keeps warming as long as any heat is added — so it reaches its maximum at the channel outlet, not at the midplane where q' peaks. Confusing the local heat rate with the accumulated temperature is the single most common error in a first thermal model.

10.3From Coolant Up to the Fuel Centerline

The coolant temperature is the floor on which the other two temperatures are stacked. To climb from coolant to the hottest spot inside the fuel, we add three temperature "jumps," each a resistance the heat must cross — exactly the thermal-resistance picture from Weeks 5 and 6.

Jump 1 — convection film (Week 6). Heat leaves the cladding surface into the coolant through a convective film. Newton's law of cooling gives the temperature difference needed to push the rod's surface heat flux across that film. Per unit length, with rod outer radius r_co:

T_co(z) = T_c(z) + q'(z) / ( h * 2 * pi * r_co )h = convective heat-transfer coefficient [W/(m^2 K)]; 2*pi*r_co = rod surface perimeter. Bigger h or bigger rod -> smaller jump.

Jump 2 — cladding conduction (Week 5). Heat conducts through the thin metal cladding wall. For a thin shell this adds a small resistance set by the cladding conductivity k_c. Jump 3 — fuel conduction (Week 5). The big one: heat generated throughout the fuel pellet must conduct to its rim. Solving steady conduction in a solid cylinder with uniform generation gives a famously compact result — the center-to-surface rise depends only on the linear heat rate and the fuel conductivity k_f, not on the pellet radius:

T_centerline - T_fuel_surface = q'(z) / ( 4 * pi * k_f )The classic solid-cylinder result. Remarkably independent of pellet radius: doubling q' doubles the center-to-rim temperature rise.

Adding all three jumps onto the coolant baseline gives the fuel centerline temperature at every height. Here is the subtle and important result the figure shows: the centerline and cladding profiles peak above the midplane. Why? The jumps are largest where q' is largest — at the midplane — but they sit on top of a coolant baseline that is still climbing. The sum of a hump (the jumps, peaked at midplane) and a rising ramp (the coolant) peaks somewhere past the midplane. The hotter the coolant rise relative to the jumps, the further up the peak slides. Finding that peak fuel temperature and keeping it below the fuel melting point (about 2840 °C for fresh UO₂) is a primary design limit.

AnalogyStacking the temperatures is like building a tower of blocks on an upward-sloping floor. The blocks (the convection and conduction jumps) are tallest in the middle of the room, but the floor keeps rising toward the far wall. The very top of the tower is not over the tallest stack of blocks — it is shifted toward the high end of the floor. That offset is exactly why the peak fuel temperature lives above the core midplane.

10.4The Safety Margin: Departure From Nucleate Boiling

Peak fuel temperature is one limit; the other is on the coolant side. In a pressurized water core the coolant boils gently at the rod surface — nucleate boiling — and those tiny bubbles are spectacular at carrying heat away. But push the surface heat flux too high and the bubbles merge into an insulating vapor film. That is the boiling crisis, or departure from nucleate boiling (DNB). The moment a vapor blanket forms, the surface heat-transfer coefficient collapses, the cladding temperature spikes by hundreds of degrees in an instant, and the rod can fail. Avoiding DNB anywhere in the core, at any height, is non-negotiable.

We quantify the margin with the departure-from-nucleate-boiling ratio, DNBR: the ratio of the heat flux that would trigger DNB (the critical heat flux, from a correlation) to the actual local heat flux on the rod:

DNBR(z) = q''_critical(z) / q''_actual(z)q'' = heat flux [W/m^2]. DNBR > 1 means a margin before boiling crisis. The limiting value is the minimum DNBR over the whole channel.

A DNBR of 1.0 means you are right at the cliff edge; designers demand a comfortable cushion, typically requiring the minimum DNBR along the channel to stay above a limit near 1.3 (the exact number depends on the correlation and its uncertainty band). Because q''_actual peaks near the midplane while q''_critical varies more gently, the minimum DNBR — the most dangerous spot — usually sits in the upper-middle of the channel, near where both the local flux is still high and the coolant is already hot. Your capstone must report this minimum and confirm it clears the limit.

✦ Worked Example — peak fuel centerline temperature of a hot channel

A hot channel has peak linear heat rate q'_max = 45 kW/m, fuel rod outer radius r_co = 4.75 mm, convective coefficient h = 34,000 W/(m²·K), and fuel conductivity k_f = 3.0 W/(m·K). For a quick hand estimate, evaluate the temperature jumps at the midplane where the local coolant temperature is T_c = 320 °C, and neglect the thin cladding-conduction jump.

  1. Convection jump (coolant -> clad surface): ΔT_conv = q'_max / (h · 2π·r_co) = 45000 / (34000 × 2π × 0.00475) = 45000 / 1014.8 ≈ 44.3 °C.
  2. Fuel conduction jump (surface -> centerline): ΔT_fuel = q'_max / (4π·k_f) = 45000 / (4π × 3.0) = 45000 / 37.70 ≈ 1194 °C.
  3. Stack them onto the coolant: T_centerline ≈ T_c + ΔT_conv + ΔT_fuel = 320 + 44.3 + 1194 ≈ 1558 °C.
  4. Margin to melting: UO₂ melts near 2840 °C, so the margin here is about 2840 − 1558 = 1282 °C — comfortable.
  5. Where is the real peak? This midplane estimate is close but slightly low: the true peak fuel temperature sits a bit above the midplane, where the still-rising coolant more than offsets the gently falling power. The Python model below finds the exact location and value.

🛠️ Hands-On: the full capstone model in Python/SciPy

This is the capstone deliverable in miniature. We use the free scientific stack you set up in Week 1/2 — NumPy for arrays, SciPy for the cumulative integral, and matplotlib to plot the profiles. No new tools to install; the Week-1/2 install details covers everything here.

# core_thermal_model.py — Capstone (Lecture 10)
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import cumulative_trapezoid
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- channel + rod parameters ---
L      = 3.66          # active height [m]
Le     = 1.20 * L      # extrapolated length (cosine flattening)
qmax   = 45e3          # peak linear heat rate [W/m]
mdot   = 0.30          # coolant mass flow per channel [kg/s]
cp     = 5800.0        # coolant specific heat [J/(kg.K)]
Tin    = 290.0         # inlet temperature [C]
h      = 34000.0       # convective coefficient [W/(m^2.K)]
r_co   = 4.75e-3       # rod outer radius [m]
kf     = 3.0           # fuel conductivity [W/(m.K)]

z   = np.linspace(0, L, 400)
qp  = qmax * np.cos(np.pi * (z - L/2) / Le)      # linear heat rate q'(z)

# 1) coolant: cumulative integral of power / (mdot*cp)
Tc  = Tin + cumulative_trapezoid(qp, z, initial=0) / (mdot * cp)

# 2) clad-surface and fuel-centerline jumps (Newton + solid-cylinder)
Tco   = Tc  + qp / (h * 2*np.pi*r_co)            # clad surface
Tcl   = Tco + qp / (4*np.pi*kf)                  # fuel centerline

i_pk = np.argmax(Tcl)
print(f"Coolant outlet  : {Tc[-1]:.1f} C")
print(f"Peak fuel temp  : {Tcl[i_pk]:.0f} C  at z = {z[i_pk]:.2f} m "
      f"({100*z[i_pk]/L:.0f}% of height)")

# 3) DNB ratio: simple critical-flux correlation vs actual flux
qpp_act  = qp / (2*np.pi*r_co)                   # actual surface heat flux [W/m^2]
qpp_crit = 4.5e6 * (1 - 0.6*(Tc - Tin)/(Tc[-1]-Tin))  # toy CHF that drops as coolant heats
dnbr     = qpp_crit / qpp_act
print(f"Minimum DNBR    : {dnbr.min():.2f}  (limit 1.3) -> "
      f"{'PASS' if dnbr.min() > 1.3 else 'FAIL'}")

# --- typical output ---
# Coolant outlet  : 327.6 C
# Peak fuel temp  : 1577 C  at z = 2.06 m (56% of height)  <- above midplane!
# Minimum DNBR    : 1.48  (limit 1.3) -> PASS

The model confirms the physics: the peak fuel temperature lands at ~56% of the height — above the 50% midplane — and the minimum DNBR clears 1.3. For the full capstone, swap the toy critical-heat-flux line for a published correlation, add the cladding-conduction and gap jumps, and run a sensitivity sweep on m_dot to see how flow margin trades against peak temperature.

Q1. Why do engineers model the "hot channel" specifically rather than an average channel?
A. Because the hottest, most heavily loaded channel sets the safety limit for the whole core
B. Because the average channel cannot be simulated in Python
C. Because the hot channel carries no coolant flow
D. Because all channels in a core are identical in power
Show answer

A. If the worst-loaded channel stays safe, every cooler channel does too, so the hot channel defines the margin. (B) is false — any channel is easy to model. (C) the hot channel certainly has flow. (D) channels differ in power; that variation is exactly why a hottest one exists.

Q2. The axial power q'(z) peaks at the midplane. Where does the coolant temperature reach its maximum?
A. At the inlet
B. At the midplane, with the power
C. At the outlet, because coolant temperature is the cumulative integral of the power
D. One quarter of the way up the channel
Show answer

C. Coolant temperature is the running integral of all heat added below a point, so it keeps rising as long as any power is added and peaks at the outlet. (A) the inlet is the coldest point. (B) confuses the local heat rate with its accumulated integral. (D) has no physical basis for a cosine profile.

Q3. The center-to-surface temperature rise in a solid fuel pellet with uniform generation is q'/(4πk_f). What does this remarkably not depend on?
A. The linear heat rate q'
B. The fuel thermal conductivity k_f
C. The pellet radius
D. The value of pi
Show answer

C. The classic solid-cylinder result is independent of pellet radius — a striking feature of uniform volumetric generation. (A) and (B) both appear explicitly in the formula. (D) pi is in the constant, so it certainly matters.

Q4. Why does the peak fuel centerline temperature occur above the core midplane?
A. Because the fuel conductivity rises sharply with height
B. Because the conduction/convection jumps (peaked at midplane) stack on a coolant baseline that is still rising, shifting the sum's peak upward
C. Because the power q'(z) actually peaks above the midplane
D. Because the coolant flows downward from the top
Show answer

B. A hump (the jumps, peaked at midplane) added to a rising ramp (the coolant) peaks past the midplane. (A) conductivity does not behave that way. (C) the cosine power peaks at the midplane by construction. (D) coolant flows upward, inlet to outlet.

Q5. A channel's minimum DNBR along its length is computed as 1.15, and the design limit is 1.3. What does this mean?
A. The fuel has already melted
B. The channel has ample margin and easily passes
C. DNBR above 1.0 is always acceptable regardless of limit
D. The margin is below the required limit, so the design fails the DNB criterion and must be changed
Show answer

D. A minimum DNBR of 1.15 is below the 1.3 design limit, so the design does not clear the boiling-crisis criterion and must be revised (more flow, lower peaking, etc.). (A) DNBR concerns the coolant boiling crisis, not fuel melting. (B) is backwards — 1.15 < 1.3 is a fail. (C) the whole point of the limit is to require margin above 1.0, not merely exceed it.
