Boiling Heat Transfer & Critical Heat Flux

Boiling is the most efficient way we know to move heat off a hot surface — right up until the moment it isn't, when a blanket of vapor turns a glowing fuel rod into a melting one in a fraction of a second.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why boiling moves vastly more heat than single-phase convection, in terms of latent heat and bubble agitation.
  • Read the pool-boiling curve and identify the natural-convection, nucleate, transition, and film-boiling regimes.
  • Describe nucleate boiling from first principles: nucleation sites, bubble departure, and the resulting high heat-transfer coefficient.
  • Define departure from nucleate boiling (DNB) and the critical heat flux (CHF), and explain the "burnout" temperature excursion.
  • Compute and interpret a departure-from-nucleate-boiling ratio (DNBR) as a reactor thermal safety margin.

7.1Why Boiling Beats Single-Phase Convection

In Week 6 we cooled a fuel rod with single-phase convection: water flowed past a hot wall, warmed up a little, and carried the heat away. That works, but it is fundamentally limited — the coolant can only absorb sensible heat, raising its temperature by cp ΔT, and the wall has to run hot enough to drive that temperature rise. To push more heat off the same surface you must either run a punishing flow rate or let the wall get dangerously hot.

Boiling changes the game. When the wall is hot enough to vaporize the liquid touching it, two things happen at once. First, evaporation taps the latent heat of vaporization — for water near reactor pressure, turning 1 kg of liquid to vapor absorbs on the order of 1.5–2 MJ, dwarfing the ~4 kJ it takes to warm that kilogram by 1 K. Second, the bubbles that grow and detach violently stir the liquid layer right at the wall, scrubbing away the thermal boundary layer that throttles single-phase convection.

The result is an enormous heat-transfer coefficient h. Where single-phase forced convection of water might give h of a few thousand to tens of thousands of W/m²K, vigorous nucleate boiling reaches tens to hundreds of thousands of W/m²K. We still describe the heat flux with the same Newton's-law-of-cooling form, but now h hides all the bubble physics:

q'' = h ( T_wall - T_sat )q'' = surface heat flux (W/m^2); h = boiling heat-transfer coefficient; (T_wall - T_sat) = wall superheat above the saturation temperature.

The driving temperature difference is measured against the saturation temperature Tsat (the local boiling point at that pressure), not the bulk coolant temperature. The quantity ΔTsat = Twall − Tsat is called the wall superheat, and it is the x-axis of the single most important diagram in two-phase heat transfer.

AnalogySingle-phase cooling is like fanning a hot pan with air — you carry off only as much heat as the air can warm up. Boiling is like flicking droplets of water onto the pan: each droplet that flashes to steam steals a huge gulp of energy as it changes phase, and the hiss and turbulence sweep fresh liquid in behind it. That is why a few millilitres of evaporating coolant outperform a gale of dry air.

7.2The Boiling Curve: Four Regimes on One Graph

Imagine a horizontal wire immersed in a pool of saturated water, and slowly crank up the heat flux while measuring the wall superheat. Plotting heat flux q'' against superheat ΔTsat on log axes traces the famous pool-boiling curve, first mapped quantitatively by Shiro Nukiyama in 1934. It has four distinct regimes.

1. Natural convection (low superheat). Below a few degrees of superheat the wall is hot but no bubbles form; heat leaves by ordinary buoyancy-driven convection. Flux climbs gently with superheat.

2. Nucleate boiling (the workhorse). Past the onset of nucleate boiling, bubbles erupt from microscopic cavities on the surface. This is the steep, high-slope part of the curve: a small increase in superheat buys a large jump in heat flux. Heat transfer is spectacularly efficient. The regime ends at a peak — the critical heat flux (CHF).

3. Transition boiling (unstable). Just past the peak, so much vapor is generated that patches of the wall are intermittently blanketed by an insulating film. Counter-intuitively, increasing the superheat now decreases the heat flux — a negative slope. This regime is unstable and cannot be held in a heat-flux-controlled system like a reactor.

4. Film boiling (the danger zone). Eventually a continuous vapor film coats the entire surface. Vapor is a poor conductor, so heat must now cross an insulating gas blanket, mostly by radiation and conduction. The wall temperature soars — often by hundreds or thousands of degrees — to drive the same flux. The local minimum where the film stabilizes is the Leidenfrost point.

nucleate: q'' ~ (Delta T_sat)^3 (steep, efficient) film: q'' driven by radiation across vapor (very hot wall)The nucleate regime is approximately cubic in superheat; once a vapor film forms, the surface must run far hotter for the same flux.

7.3Nucleate Boiling: Bubbles Doing the Work

Why is nucleate boiling so efficient? The secret is the life cycle of a bubble. Real surfaces are never perfectly smooth; they are pocked with microscopic scratches, cavities, and pits that trap tiny pockets of vapor or gas. These are nucleation sites. When the surrounding liquid is superheated above Tsat, a trapped vapor embryo can grow rather than collapse.

For a spherical vapor bubble of radius r to be stable, the vapor pressure inside must exceed the liquid pressure by enough to balance surface tension. The Young–Laplace relation gives the pressure jump across the interface:

Delta P = 2 sigma / rsigma = liquid surface tension (N/m); r = bubble radius. Smaller bubbles need a larger pressure excess (more superheat) to survive — so larger cavities activate first.

This is why surface texture matters: a cavity of radius r needs a certain superheat to "activate." As superheat rises, more and progressively smaller sites switch on, multiplying the bubble population. Each bubble grows, then departs when buoyancy and drag overcome surface tension holding it to the wall, and a slug of cool liquid rushes in to refill the spot. This relentless pump-and-refill cycle — nucleation, growth, departure, rewetting — is what gives nucleate boiling its huge heat-transfer coefficient. Both the latent heat carried off in each bubble and the micro-convection of the rewetting liquid contribute.

This is exactly the regime a pressurized-water reactor's surface heat transfer is designed to exploit. In subcooled nucleate boiling, bubbles form on the hot cladding even though the bulk coolant is still below saturation; they condense as they drift into the cooler core flow. The wall runs only modestly above Tsat while shedding megawatts per square metre — the ideal operating point.

Common mistakeDo not assume "more superheat always means more cooling." In the nucleate regime that is true — flux rises steeply with superheat. But the boiling curve peaks at CHF and then bends over. Past the peak, raising the wall temperature actually reduces the heat removed because vapor starts to blanket the surface. Treating the q''–ΔT relation as monotonic is precisely the error that hides the burnout cliff.

7.4DNB, Critical Heat Flux & the Burnout Cliff

Push the heat flux higher and higher in the nucleate regime and you approach a tipping point. Bubbles are forming and merging so furiously that vapor cannot escape fast enough; columns and patches of vapor begin to coalesce into a film that blocks fresh liquid from reaching the wall. The moment the surface can no longer be continuously rewetted is called departure from nucleate boiling (DNB), and the heat flux at that point is the critical heat flux (CHF).

Why is CHF a hard limit and not just another point on a curve? Because a reactor is essentially heat-flux-controlled: the fission rate sets q'', and the surface temperature adjusts to dissipate it. If you exceed q''CHF, the surface cannot follow the falling transition branch (that branch is only reachable in a temperature-controlled experiment). Instead the operating point must jump — at constant flux — straight across to the film-boiling branch, where the same flux demands a wall temperature hundreds or thousands of degrees higher. The wall temperature excursion is nearly instantaneous. For a metal fuel cladding this means rapid overheating, loss of strength, and possible melting. That violent jump is why CHF is also called burnout or the boiling crisis.

A widely used correlation for the saturated pool-boiling CHF, derived by Zuber from a hydrodynamic-instability argument, captures the key physics — CHF depends on the latent heat, the vapor density, and the buoyancy that drives vapor away:

q''_CHF = C * h_fg * rho_v^(1/2) * [ sigma * g * (rho_l - rho_v) ]^(1/4)h_fg = latent heat of vaporization; rho_v, rho_l = vapor and liquid densities; sigma = surface tension; g = gravity; C is a constant near 0.13-0.15. Higher pressure raises rho_v and lifts CHF, then it falls again near the critical point.

In a reactor, designers never operate near q''CHF. They keep a margin, quantified by the departure-from-nucleate-boiling ratio (DNBR) — the ratio of the local critical heat flux to the actual local heat flux at the hottest spot in the core:

DNBR = q''_CHF (local) / q''_actual (local)DNBR = 1 means the hot spot is exactly at burnout. Safety analyses require DNBR to stay above a limit (commonly around 1.3) with high statistical confidence in all anticipated transients.

A DNBR of, say, 1.9 at the limiting location means the hottest fuel rod is operating at only about 53% of the flux that would trigger burnout — a comfortable margin. The entire thermal-hydraulic design of the core, which we tackle next week, is in large part a campaign to keep the minimum DNBR safely above its limit during normal operation and credible accidents.

AnalogyThink of CHF like the maximum sweat-cooling rate of a sprinter. Sweat evaporating off skin (nucleate boiling) carries away heat astonishingly well. But push the effort too hard and sweat pools faster than it can evaporate — a film of liquid (then, for a surface, vapor) breaks the efficient evaporative contact, and core temperature spikes. The DNBR is your safety coach insisting you run at, say, 1.5x below that breaking point, never right at the edge.
✦ Worked Example — DNBR margin on the hottest rod

At the limiting axial location of the hottest fuel rod, a core analysis gives a local operating heat flux of q''actual = 1.15 MW/m². A CHF correlation evaluated at the local pressure, flow, and quality predicts q''CHF = 2.30 MW/m². The plant's safety limit is DNBR ≥ 1.30. Find the DNBR and the available margin.

  1. Form the ratio: DNBR = q''CHF / q''actual = 2.30 / 1.15 = 2.00.
  2. Compare to the limit: 2.00 is well above the required 1.30, so this rod is safe at these conditions.
  3. Interpret physically: the rod is operating at 1/2.00 = 50% of the flux that would cause burnout, i.e. flux could double before reaching CHF.
  4. Available margin to the limit: (2.00 − 1.30) / 1.30 ≈ 54% headroom above the safety limit before a violation is even approached.
  5. Sanity check — a transient: suppose a flow reduction cut q''CHF by 35% to 1.50 MW/m² while flux held at 1.15. New DNBR = 1.50 / 1.15 ≈ 1.30 — right at the limit. This shows why CHF margin, not just flux, is the quantity protected by reactor trips.

🛠️ Hands-On: plot the boiling curve and find CHF in Python

We use the same free scientific stack set up in Week 1/2 — NumPy for arithmetic, SciPy for the Zuber CHF correlation constants, and matplotlib for the plot. No new tools are needed here; if you skipped the early setup, the install <details> in Lecture 1 has the one-time conda/pip commands. Below we synthesize a schematic nucleate branch (cubic in superheat) and overlay the Zuber CHF for saturated water near atmospheric pressure.

# boiling_curve.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- Zuber critical heat flux for saturated pool boiling ---
def zuber_chf(h_fg, rho_v, rho_l, sigma, g=9.81, C=0.131):
    # q''_CHF = C * h_fg * rho_v^(1/2) * [ sigma g (rho_l - rho_v) ]^(1/4)
    return C * h_fg * np.sqrt(rho_v) * (sigma * g * (rho_l - rho_v))**0.25

# water at ~1 atm, saturated
h_fg  = 2.257e6      # J/kg, latent heat of vaporization
rho_v = 0.598        # kg/m^3, saturated vapor
rho_l = 958.0        # kg/m^3, saturated liquid
sigma = 0.0589       # N/m, surface tension

q_chf = zuber_chf(h_fg, rho_v, rho_l, sigma)
print(f"Zuber CHF (water, 1 atm) = {q_chf/1e6:.2f} MW/m^2")   # ~1.26 MW/m^2

# --- schematic nucleate branch: q'' ~ (dT_sat)^3 ---
dT = np.linspace(1, 30, 200)         # wall superheat, K
q_nucleate = 700.0 * dT**3           # schematic coefficient, W/m^2
q_nucleate = np.minimum(q_nucleate, q_chf)   # cap at CHF (burnout)

# --- DNBR for an operating point ---
q_actual = 0.65e6                    # W/m^2 operating flux
print(f"DNBR = {q_chf / q_actual:.2f}")       # margin to burnout

plt.semilogy(dT, q_nucleate, 'g', lw=2, label='nucleate (schematic)')
plt.axhline(q_chf, color='orange', ls='--', label='CHF (Zuber)')
plt.xlabel('wall superheat  dT_sat  [K]')
plt.ylabel("heat flux  q''  [W/m^2]")
plt.legend(); plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig('boiling_curve.png', dpi=130)

The Zuber prediction (~1.26 MW/m² for water at 1 atm) lands close to the textbook experimental value of ~1.0–1.2 MW/m² — remarkable for a one-line correlation. Try raising rho_v to mimic higher pressure and watch CHF climb, then fall again as rho_v approaches rho_l near the critical point.

Q1. Why does nucleate boiling remove far more heat per unit area than single-phase forced convection?
A. The coolant pressure is much higher during boiling
B. Latent heat of vaporization plus bubble-driven stirring of the wall layer give a very large heat-transfer coefficient
C. Vapor conducts heat better than liquid water
D. Boiling lowers the saturation temperature, increasing the driving ΔT
Show answer

B. Evaporation taps the huge latent heat and departing bubbles scrub the thermal boundary layer, so h is enormous. (A) Pressure does not jump during boiling. (C) Vapor is a far worse conductor — that is what makes film boiling dangerous. (D) Boiling does not lower Tsat; Tsat is fixed by pressure.

Q2. On the pool-boiling curve, what is the x-axis (the driving variable plotted against heat flux)?
A. Wall superheat, Twall − Tsat
B. Bulk coolant velocity
C. Coolant subcooling, Tsat − Tbulk
D. Absolute system pressure
Show answer

A. The boiling curve plots q'' against wall superheat ΔTsat = Twall − Tsat. (B) Velocity shifts the curve but is not its axis. (C) Subcooling refers to the bulk, not the wall driving potential. (D) Pressure sets Tsat and CHF level but is held fixed while tracing one curve.

Q3. In the transition-boiling regime, what happens to the heat flux as wall superheat increases?
A. It keeps rising steeply, as in nucleate boiling
B. It stays exactly constant
C. It decreases, because intermittent vapor blanketing insulates the surface
D. It oscillates around CHF indefinitely with no net trend
Show answer

C. Past the CHF peak the curve bends over: more superheat means more vapor coverage, which insulates the wall and lowers the flux — a negative slope. (A) The steep rise is the nucleate regime, before the peak. (B) and (D) misdescribe the monotonic decline of the transition branch.

Q4. Why is exceeding the critical heat flux a sudden, dangerous "burnout" rather than a gentle change in a reactor?
A. Because the coolant instantly freezes the cladding
B. Because the reactor is temperature-controlled, so flux drops smoothly
C. Because CHF only matters in fusion, not fission, systems
D. Because the system is heat-flux-controlled, so the operating point leaps to the film-boiling branch at constant flux, spiking the wall temperature
Show answer

D. Fission sets the flux; the wall cannot follow the falling transition branch and must jump to film boiling at the same flux, where the wall runs hundreds of degrees hotter — nearly instantaneous overheating. (A) is nonsense. (B) reverses the control mode. (C) CHF is central to fission thermal limits.

Q5. A hot rod has local q''CHF = 2.4 MW/m² and operates at q''actual = 1.2 MW/m². The plant limit is DNBR ≥ 1.3. Is the rod safe, and what is its DNBR?
A. DNBR = 2.0 — safe, comfortably above the 1.3 limit
B. DNBR = 0.5 — already in burnout
C. DNBR = 3.6 — safe
D. DNBR = 1.2 — below the limit, unsafe
Show answer

A. DNBR = q''CHF / q''actual = 2.4 / 1.2 = 2.0, which exceeds 1.3 — safe. (B) inverts the ratio. (C) adds the fluxes instead of dividing. (D) confuses the actual flux value with the ratio.
