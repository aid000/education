Conduction & the Fuel Temperature Profile

Heat is born uniformly across a fuel pellet but can only escape through its rim — so the center runs blisteringly hot, and how hot decides whether the fuel survives or melts.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State Fourier's law of heat conduction and interpret thermal conductivity k as a material property.
  • Derive the steady-state heat-conduction equation with an internal source from an energy balance.
  • Solve the equation in cylindrical geometry for a fuel pin with uniform volumetric heat generation.
  • Show why the radial temperature profile is parabolic and locate the fuel centerline temperature.
  • Explain why UO₂'s low thermal conductivity, combined with its melting limit, constrains reactor power.

5.1Fourier's Law: How Heat Moves Through a Solid

In Week 4 we found where the heat comes from: fission deposits roughly 200 MeV per event, most of it as the kinetic energy of fragments that stop within microns inside the ceramic fuel. That energy appears as a volumetric heat source — call it q‴ (watts per cubic metre) — smeared almost uniformly through every fuel pellet. The question this lecture answers is: once heat is generated deep inside a solid, how does it get out?

In an opaque solid there is no convection and no radiation to speak of internally — heat moves by conduction, the microscopic hand-off of thermal vibrations (and, in metals, free electrons) from hotter atoms to cooler neighbors. The empirical law that governs it, established by Joseph Fourier two centuries ago, says the heat flux is proportional to the temperature gradient and flows downit:

q'' = -k (dT/dx)q'' = heat flux (W/m^2), k = thermal conductivity (W/m-K), dT/dx = temperature gradient. The minus sign: heat flows from hot to cold.

The constant k is the thermal conductivity, a material property. Copper conducts heat lavishly (k ≈ 400 W/m·K); a ceramic like uranium dioxide is a stubborn insulator (k ≈ 3 W/m·K near operating temperature) — a fact that will dominate everything below. The minus sign encodes the second law: heat spontaneously flows only from hot to cold, so a positive flux requires a negative gradient.

AnalogyThink of a crowded stairwell emptying a building. The "flux" of people is driven by how steeply the crowd thins out as you move toward the exit (the gradient), and by how wide and unobstructed the stairs are (the conductivity k). A narrow, clogged stairwell (low k, like UO₂) forces a steep crowd build-up — a big temperature difference — to push the same number of people out per second.

5.2The Heat-Conduction Equation with a Source

Fourier's law alone is not enough; we need a bookkeeping statement for energy. Take a tiny control volume inside the fuel and write an energy balance over it: rate in by conduction − rate out by conduction + rate generated inside = rate of energy stored. In steady state nothing accumulates, so the storage term is zero. Translating each term into calculus gives the steady-state heat-conduction equation with internal generation (the Poisson form of the heat equation):

div( k grad T ) + q''' = 0q''' = volumetric heat generation (W/m^3). For constant k this is k (Laplacian of T) + q''' = 0.

Read it physically: the divergence term measures how much more heat conduction carries out of a point than it brought in. In steady state that net export must be supplied exactly by local generation q‴. Where heat is made, the temperature field must curve so that conduction can ferry the heat away as fast as it appears.

The full time-dependent version simply restores the storage term, ρcp(∂T/∂t), on the right — that is the equation reactor engineers solve for transients and accident analysis. But for a reactor humming along at constant power, steady state is the right starting picture, and it already reveals the dramatic temperatures inside the fuel.

Common mistakeDo not confuse heat flux q″ (W/m², a surface quantity from Fourier's law) with the volumetric heat generation q‴ (W/m³, an internal source). They have different units and different physical meaning. A common error is to set q″ = q‴ × radius; the correct link between them comes from integrating the source over the volume and dividing by the surface area — which is exactly what the conduction equation does for you.

5.3Steady Radial Conduction in a Cylindrical Fuel Pin

Reactor fuel is stacked as small cylindrical pellets — typically about 8 mm in diameter — inside long metal cladding tubes. Because a fuel pin is long and thin, heat flows almost entirely radially outward toward the coolant; axial conduction is negligible. So we specialize the conduction equation to a cylinder with uniform q‴ and constant k, using the radial Laplacian in cylindrical coordinates:

(1/r) d/dr ( r dT/dr ) = - q''' / kSteady, radial, uniform source. Boundary conditions: dT/dr = 0 at the center (r = 0, by symmetry), T = T_s at the surface r = R.

Integrate once: multiply through by r and integrate, using the symmetry condition that the gradient vanishes at the center (no heat crosses the axis). Integrate a second time and apply the surface temperature Ts at r = R. The result is a clean, exact parabola in radius:

T(r) = T_s + ( q''' / (4k) ) ( R^2 - r^2 )Temperature falls as the square of radius from center to surface. Maximum at r = 0, minimum at r = R.

Three things are worth noticing. First, the profile is highest at the center (r = 0) and lowest at the surface — heat made everywhere must escape through the rim, so the core sits at the top of a thermal hill. Second, the shape is exactly parabolic for uniform generation, regardless of the actual power level. Third, the height of the hill scales with q‴/k: crank up the power, or use a poorer conductor, and the center gets hotter in direct proportion.

5.4UO₂'s Low Conductivity and the Melting Limit

Now the engineering crux. The center-to-surface temperature rise depends only on q‴, R, and k — and uranium dioxide has a notoriously low thermal conductivity. Worse, k for UO₂ actually fallsas the fuel heats up (from about 8 W/m·K when cold to roughly 2.5–3 W/m·K at operating temperature), so the hot center conducts even more poorly than the cool rim — a self-aggravating effect that pushes the centerline still higher.

Because of this, the centerline of a fuel pellet in a power reactor runs extraordinarily hot, often near 1000 °C while the pellet surface sits near 400 °C. The hard ceiling is the melting point of UO₂, about 2840 °C. If the centerline ever reaches it, the fuel can restructure, swell, and release fission gas, threatening the cladding. Regulators therefore impose a fuel-centerline-temperature limit with a large safety margin — the single most important thermal constraint on how much power a fuel pin may produce.

A more useful way to express the limit folds the radius into a single design number. Integrating Fourier's law from center to surface for uniform generation gives a remarkable result: the center-to-surface temperature rise depends on the linear power q′ (watts per metre of pin length, equal to q‴ × πR²) — and not on the pellet radius at all:

T_center - T_surface = q' / (4 pi k)q' = linear heat rate (W/m). The center temperature rise is set by power-per-length and k alone — pellet radius cancels out.

This is why reactor cores are specified by a limit on linear heat rate (typically kept well under ~45 kW/m). It bundles "how hot is the center" into one number an operator can monitor and protect against, independent of pellet size. It is the thermal sibling of the neutronics limits you met earlier — together they bound how hard a core can be pushed.

AnalogyUO₂ is like a thick wool blanket wrapped around a heater: it traps heat so well that even a modest power makes the inside scorching. Engineers cannot make the blanket thinner (the ceramic's properties are fixed by chemistry), so instead they limit how much heat the heater is allowed to put out — that limit is the linear-heat-rate cap, q′/(4πk).
✦ Worked Example — fuel centerline temperature of a pin

A fuel pin operates at a linear heat rate q′ = 25 kW/m. The UO₂ pellet has radius R = 4.1 mm and an effective thermal conductivity k = 3.0 W/m·K. The pellet surface temperature is Ts = 410 °C. Find the volumetric heat generation, the center-to-surface temperature rise, and the centerline temperature. Check it against the UO₂ melting limit.

  1. Volumetric generation: q‴ = q′ / (πR²) = 25 000 / (π × (0.0041)²) = 25 000 / (5.28 × 10⁻⁵) ≈ 4.73 × 10⁸ W/m³.
  2. Center-to-surface rise (radius-free form): ΔT = q′ / (4πk) = 25 000 / (4π × 3.0) = 25 000 / 37.7 ≈ 663 K.
  3. Cross-check with the parabola form: ΔT = q‴R²/(4k) = (4.73 × 10⁸ × 0.0041²) / (4 × 3.0) = (4.73 × 10⁸ × 1.68 × 10⁻⁵)/12 ≈ 7950/12 ≈ 663 K. The two forms agree, as they must.
  4. Centerline temperature: Tcenter = Ts + ΔT = 410 + 663 = 1073 °C.
  5. Margin to melting: UO₂ melts near 2840 °C, so the margin is 2840 − 1073 ≈ 1770 °C — comfortable at this power, but note that doubling the linear heat rate would roughly double ΔT and erase much of that margin. This is precisely why linear heat rate is capped.

🛠️ Hands-On: plot the fuel temperature profile in Python

We use the course's free scientific stack — NumPy for arrays, SciPy for integration, and Matplotlib for plots (Octave works too if you prefer). If you set up the environment in the Week 1–2 lectures (the install details block lives there), you already have everything; just activate it and run the snippet below.

# fuel_profile.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- pin parameters ---
q_lin = 25_000.0     # linear heat rate, W/m
R     = 4.1e-3       # pellet radius, m
k     = 3.0          # UO2 effective conductivity, W/m-K
T_s   = 410.0        # pellet surface temperature, deg C

q3 = q_lin / (np.pi * R**2)        # volumetric source, W/m^3
dT = q_lin / (4 * np.pi * k)       # center-to-surface rise, K (radius-free)

r = np.linspace(0, R, 200)
T = T_s + (q3 / (4 * k)) * (R**2 - r**2)   # parabolic profile

print(f"q''' = {q3:.3e} W/m^3")
print(f"dT (center-surface) = {dT:.1f} K")
print(f"T_center = {T[0]:.1f} deg C   (UO2 melts ~2840 deg C)")

plt.plot(r * 1e3, T, color="#34d399", lw=2)
plt.axhline(T_s, ls="--", color="#60a5fa", label="surface")
plt.xlabel("radius r (mm)"); plt.ylabel("T (deg C)")
plt.title("Parabolic fuel temperature profile"); plt.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("fuel_profile.png", dpi=130)

# q''' = 4.732e+08 W/m^3
# dT (center-surface) = 663.1 K
# T_center = 1073.1 deg C   (UO2 melts ~2840 deg C)

The printed centerline matches the worked example to a fraction of a degree. Try this: make k decrease with temperature — e.g. k = 1.0/(0.04 + 2.3e-4*T) in W/m·K — and re-solve iteratively. You will watch the centerline climb as the hot core conducts even worse, exactly the self-aggravating behavior real fuel shows.

🔗 Where this is heading

You now know how heat fights its way to the pellet rim. But the rim is not the coolant — heat still has to cross a gas-filled gap, the metal cladding, and a thin boundary layer in the flowing water. Week 6 picks up exactly there with convection: Newton's law of cooling, heat-transfer coefficients, and how the coolant carries the heat away. Stack this lecture's conduction resistance with next week's convection resistance and you get the full "thermal circuit" from fuel center to coolant — the backbone of every reactor thermal-hydraulic analysis. Later courses turn that circuit into transient and accident calculations, where the time-dependent heat equation (the storage term we dropped today) decides how fast a core heats up when cooling is lost.

Q1. In Fourier's law q″ = −k(dT/dx), what does the minus sign express?
A. Thermal conductivity is always negative
B. Heat flows down the temperature gradient, from hot to cold
C. The flux is measured in negative units
D. Conduction only occurs when temperature decreases with time
Show answer

B. The minus sign makes a positive flux correspond to a negative gradient, i.e. heat moves from hot toward cold (the second law). (A) k is a positive material property. (C) flux units (W/m²) are not signed by definition. (D) Fourier's law is spatial, not about time dependence.

Q2. In the steady-state conduction equation with a source, div(k·grad T) + q‴ = 0, what does q‴ represent?
A. The surface heat flux leaving the pellet (W/m²)
B. The thermal conductivity of the cladding
C. The volumetric heat generation inside the material (W/m³)
D. The rate of energy stored as the material warms
Show answer

C. q‴ is the internal volumetric source (W/m³), here from fission. (A) is the surface flux q″, a different quantity with different units. (B) k is a separate coefficient. (D) the storage term ρcp∂T/∂t is zero in steady state and is not q‴.

Q3. For a cylindrical fuel pin with uniform heat generation and constant k, the steady radial temperature profile T(r) is…
A. linear in r, decreasing toward the surface
B. constant everywhere inside the pellet
C. logarithmic in r
D. parabolic, T(r) = T_s + (q‴/4k)(R² − r²)
Show answer

D. Integrating the radial equation twice with uniform q‴ gives a parabola peaking at the center. (A) a linear profile would require zero generation and a plane geometry. (B) constant T would mean no heat leaves — impossible with generation. (C) a logarithmic profile arises for a hollow shell with no internal source (e.g. conduction across cladding), not a solid generating pellet.

Q4. The center-to-surface temperature rise in a fuel pin can be written ΔT = q′/(4πk). What does this tell a designer?
A. The centerline rise depends on linear heat rate and k, but not on pellet radius
B. Bigger pellets always run cooler at the center
C. The rise is independent of the fuel's thermal conductivity
D. Increasing power has no effect on centerline temperature
Show answer

A. Radius cancels out; the rise is fixed by linear power q′ and conductivity k — which is why cores are limited by linear heat rate. (B) radius dropped out, so it does not set the rise. (C) k is squarely in the denominator. (D) ΔT is proportional to q′, so more power means a hotter center.

Q5. Why is the low thermal conductivity of UO₂ such a serious design constraint?
A. It makes the fuel chemically react with the coolant
B. Low k forces a large center-to-surface ΔT, pushing the centerline toward the UO₂ melting point and capping allowable power
C. Low k means heat escapes too fast and the fuel cannot stay hot enough to fission
D. Low k increases the neutron multiplication factor dangerously
Show answer

B. Since ΔT = q′/(4πk), a small k makes the center very hot for a given power, approaching the ~2840 °C melt limit; this caps linear heat rate. (A) describes a chemistry issue, not conductivity. (C) fission does not need the fuel to be "hot"; conduction does not throttle the chain reaction. (D) conductivity is a thermal property and does not directly set the neutron multiplication factor.
