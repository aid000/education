Heat Generation in the Reactor Core

A reactor core is a furnace with no flame — billions of fissions per cubic centimetre each second pour their energy directly into solid fuel, and the single hottest speck in that fuel decides how much power the whole machine is allowed to make.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the volumetric heat-generation rate q''' and explain why fission energy appears as a distributed body source inside the fuel.
  • Relate power density to the neutron flux, fission cross-section, and energy released per fission.
  • Convert between q''' and the linear heat rate q' for a cylindrical fuel rod.
  • Describe axial and radial power peaking and compute peaking factors from a flux profile.
  • Explain why the single hot spot — not the average power — sets the safety limit on core power.

4.1The Source Term: Volumetric Heat Generation q'''

In the last three weeks we treated heat as something that flows — across a turbine, through a condenser, around the Rankine loop. Inside a reactor core, heat is something that is born. Each fission of a uranium-235 nucleus deposits about 200 MeV, and roughly 190 MeV of that appears almost instantly as kinetic energy of the fission fragments, which slam to a halt within micrometres and turn that energy into heat right where the fission happened — inside the solid ceramic fuel pellet. There is no flame, no combustion front. The heat is generated throughout the volume of the fuel.

We capture this with the volumetric heat-generation rate, written q''' (read "q triple-prime"), with units of watts per cubic metre (W/m³). It is the local power produced per unit volume of fuel. The triple-prime bookkeeping is worth fixing in your mind now, because it runs through the whole course:

q''' = W/m^3 (volumetric)
q'' = W/m^2 (surface heat flux)
q' = W/m (linear, per unit length)
q = W (total power)Each prime is a "per metre." More primes -> more local. q''' is the most local description of where heat is made.

Why does this matter? Because in a thermodynamics course you often assume heat enters at a surface. In a reactor, heat is a distributed body source — it appears at every point inside the fuel simultaneously, and the temperature anywhere depends on the sum of all that internal generation plus how fast the coolant can carry it away. When we write the conduction equation next week, q''' will be the source term that drives the whole temperature field.

AnalogyThink of an electric blanket versus a hot-water bottle. The hot-water bottle heats you from one surface — that is a boundary heat flux, q''. An electric blanket has heating wire woven through its whole volume; warmth is generated everywhere at once. A reactor pellet is the electric blanket: q''' is the watts generated in every cubic millimetre of the weave, not a flux poured in from outside.

4.2Power Density and the Neutron Flux

Where does q''' come from physically? Fissions happen when neutrons strike fissile nuclei. The fission reaction rate per unit volume is the product of three things: how many fissile nuclei sit in each cubic metre (the number density N), how likely each is to fission when a neutron passes (the macroscopic fission cross-section, or equivalently N times the microscopic cross-section sigma_f), and how many neutrons are streaming through carrying how much speed — the neutron flux phi (neutrons per square metre per second).

fission rate density = Sigma_f * phi [fissions / m^3 / s]Sigma_f = N * sigma_f is the macroscopic fission cross-section (1/m); phi is the scalar neutron flux (n/m^2/s).

Multiply the fission rate by the recoverable energy per fission E_f (about 200 MeV ≈ 3.2 × 10⁻¹¹ J) and you have the volumetric heat-generation rate directly:

q''' = E_f * Sigma_f * phiE_f ≈ 3.2e-11 J per fission; Sigma_f in 1/m; phi in n/m^2/s -> q''' in W/m^3.

This little equation is the bridge between the two halves of nuclear engineering. The reactor-physics side (your neutron-transport course) computes the flux shape phi(r, z) across the core. The thermal-hydraulics side (this course) takes that flux and turns it into a temperature field. Because Sigma_f and E_f are essentially fixed by the fuel, the power density tracks the neutron flux: wherever the flux is high, the fuel is hot. The flux is not uniform — and that non-uniformity is the central drama of the rest of this lecture.

Common mistakeNeutron flux phi is not a flow through a surface like a fluid flux. It is the total path-length travelled by all neutrons per unit volume per unit time — a scalar at every point, equal to neutron density times speed (phi = n·v). Treating phi as a directional "amount crossing an area" leads to sign and geometry errors. The fission rate density Sigma_f·phi uses this scalar flux at the point of interest.

4.3The Linear Heat Rate q'

Designers rarely quote q''' directly. The single most-used number on the thermal side of a reactor is the linear heat rate q' — the power produced per unit length of fuel rod, in watts per metre (often quoted in kW/m or W/cm). Why per length? Because a reactor core is a forest of long, thin, identical rods, and the natural way to describe each one is "how many kilowatts is this rod making for every metre of its height."

q' is just q''' integrated over the fuel cross-sectional area. For a solid cylindrical pellet of radius R_f with uniform generation across the radius:

q' = integral of q''' dA = q''' * (pi * R_f^2)q' (W/m) = q''' (W/m^3) times fuel cross-sectional area (m^2). For a pellet, area = pi * R_f^2.

The total rod power then comes from integrating q' along the active fuel length:

q_rod = integral from 0 to L of q'(z) dzIf q' were constant, q_rod = q' * L. In reality q'(z) varies with height, as the next section shows.

Typical commercial light-water-reactor fuel runs an average linear heat rate around 18–20 kW/m, with a design limit near 45 kW/m at the hottest point to keep the pellet centreline below its melting temperature. That limit — a single number in W/m — is one of the most important safety parameters in the entire plant, and it is the reason we obsess over q' rather than total power. A core can make 3000 MW comfortably; whether it is safe depends on the worst centimetre of the worst rod.

AnalogyThink of a long fluorescent tube. The "total wattage" of the tube tells you the room brightness, but if you want to know whether the glass will crack you ask about the watts per centimetre at the hottest band. q' is exactly that: it ignores how long the rod is and asks how hard each slice is working. Two rods making the same total power can be worlds apart in safety if one concentrates that power into a short hot zone.

4.4Power Peaking and the Hot Spot

The neutron flux in a bare reactor core is not flat. Neutrons leak out at the edges, so the flux sags to near zero at the boundaries and bulges in the middle. For an idealized bare cylindrical core the flux profile is a cosine in the axial direction and a Bessel function (J₀) in the radial direction — but the qualitative truth is simpler: the centre runs hot, the edges run cool. Since q''' tracks the flux, the power density does the same.

Peaking factors

We quantify the non-uniformity with peaking factors — the ratio of the maximum local value to the average. The axial peaking factor compares the hottest height to the height-averaged power; the radial peaking factor compares the hottest rod to the core-average rod. Their product is the total (or "hot-channel") peaking factor:

F_z = q'_max(z) / q'_avg (axial)
F_r = (hottest rod) / (average rod) (radial)
F_q = F_z * F_r = q'''_max / q'''_avg (total)F_q is the total power-peaking factor: hottest local power density divided by core-average power density.

For an unreflected cosine axial profile the axial peaking factor is pi/2 ≈ 1.57; real cores use reflectors (to push leaking neutrons back), enrichment zoning, and burnable poisons to flatten the flux and drive peaking factors down toward 1.4–1.5 overall. Flatter flux means the same total power with a lower, safer hot spot — so flattening the profile literally buys you more allowable power.

Why the hot spot governs the design

Here is the crux of the whole lecture. The fuel does not care about the average. It melts, or its cladding fails, at the single hottest point. Reactor power is therefore limited by the worst spot, not the mean. If the centreline melt limit corresponds to a peak q'_max of 45 kW/m and your peaking factor is 2.5, then your average q' can be at most 45 / 2.5 = 18 kW/m — and the total core power is capped accordingly. Cut the peaking factor and you can raise the average, extracting more power from the same fuel without touching the hot-spot limit. Power peaking is, quite literally, money and safety in a single dimensionless number.

✦ Worked Example — from core power to the hot-spot linear rate

A light-water reactor produces 3000 MW thermal from 50,000 fuel rods, each with an active fuel length of 3.66 m and a pellet radius R_f = 4.1 mm. The total power-peaking factor is F_q = 2.5. Find (a) the average linear heat rate, (b) the peak (hot-spot) linear heat rate, and (c) the peak volumetric generation q'''.

  1. Total active fuel length: 50,000 rods × 3.66 m = 1.83 × 10⁵ m of fuel.
  2. Average linear heat rate: q'_avg = total power / total length = 3.0 × 10⁹ W ÷ 1.83 × 10⁵ m ≈ 16.4 kW/m.
  3. Peak linear heat rate: q'_max = F_q × q'_avg = 2.5 × 16.4 ≈ 41.0 kW/m. This sits just under the ~45 kW/m centreline-melt limit — the margin the design protects.
  4. Peak volumetric generation: q''' = q'_max / (pi R_f²) = 41,000 W/m ÷ [pi × (0.0041 m)²] = 41,000 ÷ (5.28 × 10⁻⁵ m²) ≈ 7.8 × 10⁸ W/m³.
  5. Sanity check: that is ~780 MW packed into every cubic metre of pellet — a furnace power density thousands of times higher than a coal flame, all of it conducted out through a few millimetres of ceramic. That extreme is exactly why the hot spot, not the average, sets the limit.

🛠️ Hands-On: plot the axial power profile and read off the peaking factor

We use the same free Python stack from the Week-1/2 setup — NumPy for arrays, SciPy for integration, and matplotlib for the plot. (If you skipped it, the one-time install details lives in the Week-1 lecture.) Here we build an idealized cosine axial profile, integrate it to get the average, and confirm the peaking factor is pi/2.

# axial_peaking.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import simpson

L  = 3.66                      # active fuel length, m
qp_peak = 41.0e3               # peak linear heat rate, W/m

z  = np.linspace(0, L, 400)
# bare-core cosine, centered, zero-ish at the ends
qp = qp_peak * np.cos(np.pi * (z - L/2) / L)

qp_avg = simpson(qp, z) / L                  # length-averaged q'
F_z    = qp.max() / qp_avg                    # axial peaking factor
P_rod  = simpson(qp, z)                       # total rod power, W

print(f"avg q'      = {qp_avg/1e3:6.2f} kW/m")
print(f"peak q'     = {qp.max()/1e3:6.2f} kW/m")
print(f"F_z (axial) = {F_z:6.3f}   (pi/2 = {np.pi/2:.3f})")
print(f"rod power   = {P_rod/1e3:6.2f} kW")

# avg q'      =  26.10 kW/m
# peak q'     =  41.00 kW/m
# F_z (axial) =  1.571   (pi/2 = 1.571)
# rod power   =  95.54 kW

The code recovers F_z = pi/2 = 1.571 exactly — the analytic peaking factor of a pure cosine. Try multiplying qp by a reflector-flattening factor (e.g. raise the ends) and watch F_z drop toward 1.4: that single number is what lets the designer push more total power through the same rods.

Q1. What does the volumetric heat-generation rate q''' physically represent, and what are its units?
A. The heat flux leaving the rod surface, in W/m²
B. The power generated per unit volume of fuel, in W/m³
C. The total power of the rod, in W
D. The power per unit length of rod, in W/m
Show answer

B. q''' is the local power produced per unit volume (W/m³) — a distributed body source. (A) is the surface flux q'' (W/m²), (D) is the linear rate q' (W/m), and (C) is the total power q (W). Each prime denotes one "per metre," so triple-prime is the most local, per-volume quantity.

Q2. According to q''' = E_f · Sigma_f · phi, if the neutron flux phi doubles at a point while the fuel composition is unchanged, the local power density q'''…
A. doubles
B. stays the same
C. is halved
D. quadruples
Show answer

A. q''' is linearly proportional to phi when E_f and Sigma_f are fixed, so doubling phi doubles q'''. (B) ignores the dependence; (C) inverts it; (D) would require a quadratic dependence, but the relation is linear in flux.

Q3. For a solid cylindrical pellet of radius R_f with uniform generation, the linear heat rate q' relates to q''' by…
A. q' = q''' / (pi R_f²)
B. q' = q''' · 2πR_f
C. q' = q''' · pi R_f²
D. q' = q''' · R_f
Show answer

C. q' is q''' integrated over the cross-sectional area pi R_f², so q' = q''' · pi R_f² (W/m). (A) divides instead of multiplies; (B) uses the circumference (relevant to surface flux, not generation); (D) has the wrong units — area scales as R², not R.

Q4. A bare core has a pure-cosine axial power profile. What is its axial peaking factor F_z?
A. 1.0 (the profile is flat)
B. exactly 2.0
C. pi ≈ 3.14
D. pi/2 ≈ 1.57
Show answer

D. The peak-to-average ratio of a half-cosine over the core height is pi/2 ≈ 1.57. (A) would require a flat profile, which a bare core never has. (B) and (C) are not the cosine peak-to-average ratio; reflectors actually push the real value below pi/2, toward ~1.4.

Q5. Why does the single hot spot — not the core-average power — set the limit on how much power a reactor may produce?
A. Because the average power is impossible to measure
B. Because the coolant only contacts the hottest rod
C. Because fuel melting or cladding failure occurs at the single hottest point, so the peak local rate hits the safety limit first
D. Because total power has no relationship to fuel temperature
Show answer

C. Failure is governed by the maximum local temperature, which tracks the peak q'_max; the average can be far below the limit while the hot spot is already at it. Lowering the peaking factor raises the allowable average. (A) is false — average power is routinely measured. (B) the coolant contacts every rod. (D) total power and average temperature are very much related; the point is the peak matters more.
