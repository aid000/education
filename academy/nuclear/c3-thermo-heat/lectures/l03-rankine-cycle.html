The Rankine Cycle & Power Conversion

The Carnot limit told you the ceiling; the Rankine cycle is the real machine engineers actually build to chase it — four components, one loop of water turning forever between liquid and steam to spin a turbine.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace the four processes of the ideal Rankine cycle — pump, boiler, turbine, condenser — and the state changes of the water at each stage.
  • Plot the cycle on a temperature–entropy (T–s) diagram and read off where heat is added and rejected.
  • Compute the cycle thermal efficiency from enthalpies, η = (w_turbine − w_pump) / q_boiler, using steam-table values.
  • Explain how superheat and reheat raise the average heat-addition temperature and lift efficiency toward the Carnot ceiling.
  • Connect the cycle to a nuclear plant: why a PWR runs a secondary Rankine loop and why its steam stays "wet" compared with a fossil plant.
  • Contrast Rankine with the Brayton (gas-turbine) cycle — compressor, heater, turbine, heat rejection — and compute its ideal efficiency from the pressure ratio, the cycle that matters for Gen-IV gas-cooled reactor concepts.

3.1From the Abstract Engine to a Real Loop

Last week's Carnot engine was a black box: heat in at T_h, work out, waste heat to T_c. It set the efficiency ceiling but said nothing about how to build a machine that approaches it. The Rankine cycle is that machine — the thermodynamic loop behind essentially every steam power plant on Earth, nuclear and fossil alike. Its trick is to use a working fluid (water) that changes phase, because boiling and condensing let you add and reject huge amounts of heat at nearly constant temperature, which is exactly what the Carnot ideal wants.

The cycle is a closed loop of four components, and the same kilogram of water passes through all of them again and again. We track its specific enthalpy h (from Lecture 1) at four state points labelled 1 through 4:

1 → 2 pump · 2 → 3 boiler · 3 → 4 turbine · 4 → 1 condenserState 1: saturated liquid leaving the condenser. State 3: high-pressure steam entering the turbine.

Each component is an open system in steady flow, so the steady-flow energy equation from Lecture 1 applies to every one: with negligible kinetic and potential changes, the heat or work per kilogram is just the enthalpy difference across the device. That single fact — q or w = Δh — is all the bookkeeping the whole cycle needs.

AnalogyThink of the water as a delivery truck on a fixed circular route. The pump is the on-ramp that lifts it to highway pressure; the boiler is the loading dock where it is packed full of energy; the turbine is the customer where it drops most of its cargo (as work); the condenser is the depot where it is emptied and cooled, ready to be sent around again. Nothing is consumed — the same truck (water) circulates endlessly; only energy is loaded and unloaded.

3.2The Four Processes, Component by Component

Walk the loop once, starting where the water is coolest and lowest in energy.

1 → 2 The pump (compression)

Saturated liquid water at low condenser pressure is squeezed up to boiler pressure. Because liquid is nearly incompressible, this takes very little work — the pump work is the small term in the whole cycle. Ideally it is reversible and adiabatic (isentropic), raising enthalpy by w_pump = h₂ − h₁ ≈ v·(p₂ − p₁).

2 → 3 The boiler / steam generator (heat addition)

At constant high pressure the water is heated, brought to a boil, and fully vaporized — and often superheated beyond the saturation point. This is where the reactor's heat enters the power cycle. The heat added per kilogram is q_in = h₃ − h₂, and most of it goes into the latent heat of vaporization at nearly constant temperature.

3 → 4 The turbine (expansion)

High-pressure steam expands through the turbine, spinning the shaft that drives the generator. Ideally this expansion is isentropic; the work extracted is w_turbine = h₃ − h₄. As the steam expands its pressure and temperature fall, and it typically begins to condense into a wet mixture of vapor and droplets by the time it leaves.

4 → 1 The condenser (heat rejection)

The low-pressure wet steam is cooled against the environment (river, sea, or cooling tower) until it is fully condensed back to saturated liquid. This is the mandatory waste-heat dump the second law demands: q_out = h₄ − h₁. The water is now back at state 1, ready for the pump.

3.3Cycle Efficiency & the T–s Diagram

The net work the plant delivers is the turbine work minus the (small) pump work, and the efficiency is that net work divided by the heat the boiler added:

eta = w_net / q_in = (h₃ − h₄) − (h₂ − h₁) / (h₃ − h₂)All four enthalpies come straight from the steam tables once you fix the two pressures and the turbine inlet temperature.

The cleanest way to see a Rankine cycle is the temperature–entropy (T–s) diagram. Heat added shows up as area under the 2→3 path; heat rejected is the area under the 4→1 path; and the enclosed area is exactly the net work. The shape immediately explains why Rankine falls short of Carnot: heat is added over a range of temperatures (warming the liquid up to the boiling point, then boiling it), so the average temperature of heat addition is well below the peak. Carnot would demand heat added at a single high temperature — which is why every efficiency-boosting trick aims to push the heat-addition path higher and flatter.

Common mistakeDo not forget the pump work when you compute net work — but also do not over-weight it. For water the pump term h₂ − h₁ is typically only a percent or two of the turbine work, because compressing a near-incompressible liquid is cheap. The classic error is the reverse: building the cycle around a gas intuition where compression is expensive. In Rankine, liquid pumping is nearly free — that cheapness is a big reason the cycle uses a condensing fluid at all.

3.4Superheat, Reheat & the Nuclear Twist

Three modifications push the real cycle closer to Carnot, all by raising the average temperature at which heat is added or by managing the wet steam in the turbine:

Superheat continues heating the steam past the saturation point at constant pressure. This lifts the average heat-addition temperature and — just as valuable — moves the turbine exit point toward drier steam, protecting the blades from erosion by liquid droplets. Reheat expands the steam partway, sends it back to the boiler to be re-superheated, then finishes the expansion in a second turbine stage; this keeps the steam dry and adds more heat at high temperature. Regeneration bleeds a little steam from the turbine to pre-warm the feedwater, cutting the low-temperature heat addition that drags efficiency down.

Here is the nuclear twist. A typical light-water reactor cannot superheat much: the steam generator is fed by primary coolant capped near 320 °C, so the secondary steam comes off only mildly above saturation — it is relatively wet and runs a modest hot-side temperature. That is the same coolant-temperature ceiling from Lecture 2, now showing up as a limit on the Rankine cycle's T_h. Fossil and advanced high-temperature reactors can superheat aggressively (550 °C and beyond) and so reach higher cycle efficiencies. The reactor's job is simply to be the boiler's heat source; everything downstream is the same Rankine machinery a coal plant uses.

AnalogySuperheating is like topping off a water balloon with extra air after it is already full of water: you push more energy in at the top end without changing the pressure, and the "fuller, drier" steam survives the turbine far better than a soggy mixture that would pit the blades like sandblasting.

3.5The Other Power Cycle: Brayton

Rankine is not the only way to turn heat into shaft work. The Brayton cycle — the cycle behind every jet engine and land-based gas turbine — runs the same four-step idea (compress, heat, expand, reject heat) but keeps the working fluid gaseous the entire time. There is no boiler, no condenser, no saturation dome: just a compressor, a heater (a combustor in a jet engine; a reactor core or an intermediate heat exchanger in a gas-cooled reactor), a turbine, and a heat-rejection step back to the compressor inlet.

1 → 2 compressor (isentropic) · 2 → 3 heater / combustor (constant p) · 3 → 4 turbine (isentropic) · 4 → 1 heat rejection (constant p)Compare directly to Rankine's pump · boiler · turbine · condenser — same four roles, but the fluid never condenses.

Because the fluid stays a gas, the two heat-transfer legs (2→3 and 4→1) are constant-pressure lines that slope upward with entropy instead of the flat, phase-change plateau the Rankine condenser draws — there is no dome to cut across. The two isentropic legs (compressor and turbine) are still vertical, exactly as in Rankine, just now jumping between a low-pressure isobar and a high-pressure isobar that sit one above the other on the T–s plane:

For an ideal gas with constant specific heats, the compressor and turbine work depend only on the temperature change, and the whole cycle's efficiency collapses to a strikingly simple result that depends on nothing but the pressure ratio r_p = p₂/p₁:

eta_Brayton = 1 − (1 / r_p)^((gamma - 1)/gamma)gamma = c_p/c_v (≈ 1.4 for air, ≈ 1.67 for helium). Higher pressure ratio always raises the ideal efficiency — there is no Carnot-style dome limiting it the way Rankine's condenser temperature does.

Plug in a pressure ratio of 10 with air (γ = 1.4): η = 1 − (1/10)^(0.4/1.4) = 1 − 10^−0.286 ≈ 48% — before accounting for real compressor/turbine inefficiencies, which typically bring an actual simple-cycle gas turbine back down into the 35–40% range. Why does this matter for nuclear? Advanced Gen-IV concepts such as the gas-cooled fast reactor (GFR) and the high/very-high-temperature gas reactor (HTGR/VHTR) use helium or CO₂ as the primary coolant and can run a direct, closed Brayton cycle straight off the reactor core — no steam generator, no condenser, no phase change anywhere in the loop. Because the core coolant isn't limited by a boiling point the way PWR water is, these designs can push core outlet temperatures to 750–950 °C, which (per the formula above, since a hotter heater leg at a fixed pressure ratio still raises the average heat-addition temperature) lifts the achievable cycle efficiency well above a PWR's Rankine ~33–37% — one of the main selling points of the gas-cooled Gen-IV line.

AnalogyRankine is like a cycle built around a water balloon that keeps getting filled and emptied (phase change does the heavy lifting). Brayton is like a bicycle pump that's never allowed to condense — you just keep squeezing and releasing the same gas, and all your leverage comes from how hard you squeeze (the pressure ratio), not from any balloon bursting.
✦ Worked Example — efficiency of a simple nuclear Rankine cycle

A reactor's secondary loop runs a simple ideal Rankine cycle. From the steam tables the four state enthalpies are: h₁ = 200 kJ/kg (saturated liquid leaving the condenser), h₂ = 208 kJ/kg (after the pump), h₃ = 2800 kJ/kg (steam entering the turbine), and h₄ = 1900 kJ/kg (wet steam leaving the turbine). Find the turbine work, pump work, heat added, and cycle efficiency.

  1. Turbine work: w_turbine = h₃ − h₄ = 2800 − 1900 = 900 kJ/kg.
  2. Pump work: w_pump = h₂ − h₁ = 208 − 200 = 8 kJ/kg — tiny, as expected for a liquid.
  3. Net work: w_net = 900 − 8 = 892 kJ/kg.
  4. Heat added in boiler: q_in = h₃ − h₂ = 2800 − 208 = 2592 kJ/kg.
  5. Efficiency: η = w_net / q_in = 892 / 2592 ≈ 0.344, i.e. ~34%.
  6. Sanity check: 34% sits right in the band real LWR plants achieve, and comfortably below the Carnot ceiling for these temperatures — exactly what Section 3.3 predicted. Note the pump work is under 1% of the turbine work, so neglecting it would have changed the answer by less than a percentage point.

Let's turn the worked example into reusable code and confirm that raising the turbine-inlet enthalpy (more superheat) lifts the efficiency.

# rankine_efficiency.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

def rankine_efficiency(h1, h2, h3, h4):
    # All enthalpies in kJ/kg. Returns (eta, w_net, q_in).
    w_turbine = h3 - h4          # turbine work out
    w_pump    = h2 - h1          # pump work in (small)
    w_net     = w_turbine - w_pump
    q_in      = h3 - h2          # heat added in boiler
    return w_net / q_in, w_net, q_in

# Worked-example state points (kJ/kg)
eta, w_net, q_in = rankine_efficiency(200.0, 208.0, 2800.0, 1900.0)
print(f"eta = {100*eta:.1f}%   w_net = {w_net:.0f} kJ/kg   q_in = {q_in:.0f} kJ/kg")
# eta = 34.4%   w_net = 892 kJ/kg   q_in = 2592 kJ/kg

# Superheat sweep: raise turbine-inlet enthalpy h3 and watch eta climb
for h3 in (2700.0, 2800.0, 2900.0, 3000.0):
    # a hotter inlet also leaves the turbine a bit hotter (drier): h4 tracks h3
    h4 = 1900.0 + 0.5 * (h3 - 2800.0)
    e, _, _ = rankine_efficiency(200.0, 208.0, h3, h4)
    print(f"h3={h3:6.0f} kJ/kg -> eta = {100*e:4.1f}%")
# h3=  2700 kJ/kg -> eta = 33.8%
# h3=  2800 kJ/kg -> eta = 34.4%
# h3=  2900 kJ/kg -> eta = 35.0%
# h3=  3000 kJ/kg -> eta = 35.5%

Efficiency creeps upward as the turbine-inlet enthalpy rises — the code version of "more superheat means higher T_h means a higher ceiling." Try swapping in real steam-table values (CoolProp is a free library) and plotting the cycle on a T–s diagram with matplotlib.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Rankine cycle is the bridge between the abstract heat engine of Week 2 and the hardware of a real plant — but it took the reactor's heat as given, arriving at the boiler from somewhere. Week 4 onward opens that black box: where the heat is actually born (the volumetric fission source in the fuel), how it claws its way out by conduction through the pellet, convection into the coolant, and eventually boiling — and what caps the coolant temperature that, as you saw here, ultimately limits the whole cycle's efficiency. Every later constraint on T_h traces back to a heat-transfer limit inside the core. The Rankine loop is the payoff; the next weeks are the plumbing that makes it possible without melting the fuel.

Model response

The single biggest gap between a real Rankine plant and the Carnot ceiling is not "friction" — it is structural. Carnot demands heat added at one fixed high temperature, but Section 3.3's T–s diagram shows the boiler path (2→3) sloping upward: the liquid must first be heated to its boiling point before any vaporization even starts, so a chunk of the heat enters at well below the peak steam temperature. That sloped heat-addition path, not any single component's inefficiency, is the biggest conceptual loss relative to Carnot.

On top of that structural gap sit two more losses. First, the coolant-temperature ceiling from Week 2: a PWR's secondary steam is fed by primary coolant capped near 320 °C, so T_h itself is modest and the Carnot ceiling is already only ~46% before any real losses are counted. Second, real turbines and pumps are not isentropic — their irreversibilities (plus condenser subcooling) shave a further few points off efficiency, landing real plants near 33%.

Of these, the mandatory loss is the condenser heat rejection itself — the second law requires some Q_c to be dumped, full stop, and no engineering can "fix" that. What is worth engineering away is everything that widens the gap beyond the mandatory minimum: superheat and reheat raise the average heat-addition temperature and dry the turbine exit (Section 3.4), better turbine/pump design shrinks the non-isentropic losses, and — by far the highest-leverage fix — raising T_h itself, whether through modest superheat or, far more dramatically, through a fundamentally hotter cycle like the Brayton-cycle Gen-IV gas reactors of Section 3.5, which push core outlet temperatures to 750–950 °C and a much higher Carnot ceiling.

Q1. In the ideal Rankine cycle, in which component is the bulk of the heat added to the working fluid?
A. The pump
B. The boiler / steam generator
C. The turbine
D. The condenser
Show answer

B. Heat is added at constant high pressure in the boiler (2→3), q_in = h₃ − h₂. (A) the pump adds work, not heat; (C) the turbine extracts work; (D) the condenser rejects heat, it does not add it.

Q2. Why is the pump work in a Rankine cycle usually only a small fraction of the turbine work?
A. Because the pump runs slower than the turbine
B. Because the pump is more efficient than the turbine
C. Because the pump compresses a nearly incompressible liquid, which costs very little work
D. Because pumps do no thermodynamic work at all
Show answer

C. Compressing liquid water (state 1→2) takes far less work than expanding high-energy steam yields, since w_pump ≈ v·Δp and the liquid's specific volume is tiny. (A) speed is not the reason; (B) relative efficiency is irrelevant to the magnitude; (D) pumps absolutely do work — it is just small.

Q3. Using the steady-flow energy equation with negligible kinetic/potential changes, the turbine work per kilogram equals…
A. h₃ − h₄, the drop in specific enthalpy across the turbine
B. h₃ + h₄, the sum of inlet and outlet enthalpy
C. the heat rejected in the condenser
D. zero, because the turbine is adiabatic
Show answer

A. For an adiabatic steady-flow device the SFEE gives w = h_in − h_out = h₃ − h₄. (B) adding enthalpies is meaningless here; (C) that is q_out, a different quantity; (D) adiabatic means no heat crosses the boundary, but work still does.

Q4. A simple Rankine cycle has h₁ = 190, h₂ = 200, h₃ = 2750, h₄ = 1850 kJ/kg. Its thermal efficiency is closest to…
A. 12%
B. 49%
C. 67%
D. 35%
Show answer

D. w_net = (2750 − 1850) − (200 − 190) = 900 − 10 = 890; q_in = 2750 − 200 = 2550; η = 890/2550 ≈ 0.349 ≈ 35%. (A) and (C) come from mis-pairing the enthalpies; (B) forgets to divide by q_in correctly.

Q5. Why does adding superheat raise a Rankine plant's efficiency and also protect the turbine?
A. It lowers the boiler pressure, reducing stress
B. It raises the average temperature of heat addition and leaves the turbine exit steam drier, reducing blade erosion
C. It eliminates the condenser entirely
D. It converts the cycle into a Carnot cycle exactly
Show answer

B. Superheating pushes heat addition to higher temperature (lifting the ceiling) and shifts the turbine exit toward dry steam, sparing the blades from droplet impact. (A) superheat does not lower boiler pressure; (C) the condenser is still required to reject q_out; (D) a real superheated Rankine cycle still sits below Carnot.
