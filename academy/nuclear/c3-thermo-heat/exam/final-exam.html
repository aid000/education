Thermodynamic foundations · Weeks 1–2
1. A closed system absorbs 120 kJ of heat while doing 50 kJ of work on its surroundings. Using the first law dU = Q − W, the change in internal energy is…
Answer: B. dU = Q − W = 120 − 50 = 70 kJ. (A) adds instead of subtracting; (C) has the wrong sign; (D) just restates the work term.
2. A reactor core produces 3400 MW of thermal power. Coolant flows through at m_dot = 17,000 kg/s with c_p = 5.6 kJ/(kg·K). Treating the core as a steady-flow control volume with no shaft work, the coolant temperature rise across the core is closest to…
Answer: D. ΔT = Q_dot/(m_dot·c_p) = 3.4×10⁹ / (17,000 × 5600) ≈ 35.7 K. (A) is double, (B) is half, (C) is off by a factor of ten — all from slipped factors in the division.
3. A heat engine operates between T_h = 800 K and T_c = 320 K. Its Carnot efficiency is…
Answer: C. η = 1 − T_c/T_h = 1 − 320/800 = 1 − 0.40 = 0.60. (A) is just the ratio T_c/T_h itself, not one minus it. (B) and (D) come from mis-dividing the temperatures.
4. 500 J of heat flows directly from a reservoir at 400 K to one at 250 K. The net entropy change of the two reservoirs combined is…
Answer: A. ΔS = Q/T_c − Q/T_h = 500/250 − 500/400 = 2.0 − 1.25 = +0.75 J/K. (B) flips the sign, which would violate the second law. (C) only counts the cold reservoir's gain, ignoring the hot reservoir's loss. (D) wrongly uses an average temperature instead of the two separate terms.
Power cycles: Rankine & Brayton · Week 3
5. A simple ideal Rankine cycle has state enthalpies h₁ = 180, h₂ = 188, h₃ = 2790, h₄ = 1830 kJ/kg. Its thermal efficiency is closest to…
Answer: B. w_net = (h₃−h₄) − (h₂−h₁) = (2790−1830) − (188−180) = 960 − 8 = 952 kJ/kg; q_in = h₃−h₂ = 2602 kJ/kg; η = 952/2602 ≈ 0.366. (A), (C), and (D) all come from mis-pairing the four enthalpies.
6. An ideal Brayton cycle uses air (γ = 1.4) with a pressure ratio r_p = 8. Its ideal thermal efficiency, η = 1 − (1/r_p)^((γ−1)/γ), is closest to…
Answer: C. η = 1 − (1/8)^(0.4/1.4) = 1 − 8^−0.286 ≈ 1 − 0.552 ≈ 0.448, i.e. about 45%. (A), (B), and (D) come from using the wrong exponent or inverting the pressure ratio.
7. On a temperature–entropy diagram, what distinguishes the ideal Brayton cycle from the ideal Rankine cycle?
Answer: A. Because the Brayton working fluid never condenses, both the heater (2→3) and heat-rejection (4→1) legs are sloped constant-pressure isobars with no phase-change plateau. (B) reverses which cycle has the dome — it is Rankine. (C) the isentropic compressor/turbine legs are still vertical in both cycles. (D) efficiency depends on the pressure ratio and temperatures actually achieved; neither cycle automatically wins.
Core heat generation · Week 4
8. A fuel rod operates at a linear heat rate q′ = 22 kW/m with a pellet radius R = 4.0 mm. Using q‴ = q′/(πR²), the volumetric heat-generation rate q‴ is closest to…
Answer: D. q‴ = 22,000 / (π × 0.004²) = 22,000 / 5.03×10⁻⁵ ≈ 4.38×10⁸ W/m³. (A) drops the π; (B) mistakenly divides by the circumference 2πR instead of the area; (C) divides by R alone instead of R².
9. A core's average linear heat rate is 17 kW/m and its total peaking factor is F_q = 2.4. The hot-spot linear heat rate is closest to…
Answer: B. q'_max = F_q × q'_avg = 2.4 × 17 = 40.8 kW/m, which sits below the ~45 kW/m centerline-melt design limit. (A) forgets to apply F_q at all. (C) divides instead of multiplies. (D) has the right number but the wrong conclusion — 40.8 kW/m is still under 45 kW/m.
Conduction & fuel temperature · Week 5
10. A fuel pin runs at linear heat rate q′ = 30 kW/m with effective fuel conductivity k = 3.2 W/(m·K). Using ΔT = q′/(4πk), the center-to-surface temperature rise is closest to…
Answer: B. ΔT = 30,000 / (4π × 3.2) = 30,000 / 40.2 ≈ 746 K. (A) uses 2πk instead of 4πk (double); (C) uses 8πk instead of 4πk (half); (D) drops the π entirely.
11. For a solid cylindrical fuel pellet with uniform volumetric heat generation and constant thermal conductivity, the steady radial temperature profile T(r) is…
Answer: C. Integrating the radial conduction equation twice with uniform q‴ and the symmetry condition at r = 0 gives an exact parabola, highest at the center. (A) a linear profile would require zero internal generation. (B) exponential decay is not the solution of this Poisson-type equation. (D) a flat profile is impossible with a distributed source — the heat has to escape somehow.
12. If a fuel pin's linear heat rate q′ doubles while its conductivity k stays fixed, the center-to-surface temperature rise ΔT = q′/(4πk)…
Answer: D. ΔT is directly proportional to q′ in this relation, so doubling q′ doubles ΔT. (A) would require a squared dependence, which is not the case here. (B) ignores the dependence entirely. (C) inverts the relationship.
Convection & coolant heat transfer · Week 6
13. Coolant flows through a channel with Re = 3.5×10⁵, Pr = 1.0, hydraulic diameter D = 0.010 m, and fluid conductivity k = 0.55 W/(m·K). Using Nu = 0.023 Re^0.8 Pr^0.4, the convective heat-transfer coefficient h is closest to…
Answer: B. Nu = 0.023 × (3.5×10⁵)^0.8 × 1.0^0.4 ≈ 627; h = Nu·k/D = 627 × 0.55 / 0.010 ≈ 3.45×10⁴ W/(m²·K) — squarely in the forced-liquid range. (A) is off by a factor of ten; (C) and (D) are double and half the correct value, respectively.
14. A fuel-rod surface carries a heat flux of 8.0×10⁵ W/m² with a local convective coefficient h = 25,000 W/(m²·K). The film temperature drop ΔT_film = q″/h is closest to…
Answer: C. ΔT_film = q″/h = 8.0×10⁵ / 25,000 = 32 K. (A) and (B) are off by a factor of ten in opposite directions; (D) is half the correct value.
15. Liquid sodium has a Prandtl number of roughly Pr ≈ 0.01, far below the 0.7–160 range where the Dittus-Boelter correlation (Nu = 0.023 Re^0.8 Pr^0.4) was validated. What does this imply for a sodium-cooled fast reactor?
Answer: D. Pr compares momentum diffusivity to thermal diffusivity; a Pr this far outside the validated range means the correlation's assumptions break down, and liquid-metal-specific correlations are required. (A) ignores the stated validity range. (B) is false — sodium is a real, widely studied fast-reactor coolant. (C) Pr is a perfectly well-defined ratio for any fluid, including liquid metals.
Boiling, critical heat flux & DNBR · Week 7
16. At the hottest location in a core, the local critical heat flux is q″CHF = 1.9 MW/m² and the actual operating flux is q″actual = 1.0 MW/m². The plant's safety limit is DNBR ≥ 1.3. The DNBR here is…
Answer: A. DNBR = q″CHF/q″actual = 1.9/1.0 = 1.9, which is (1.9−1.3)/1.3 ≈ 46% above the required 1.3 — a comfortable margin. (B) inverts the ratio. (C) adds the fluxes instead of dividing. (D) mistakes the safety limit for the computed value.
17. According to the Zuber correlation, q″CHF ∝ hfg·ρv1/2·[σg(ρl − ρv)]1/4, what happens to the critical heat flux as system pressure rises from 1 atm toward the thermodynamic critical point?
Answer: C. Higher pressure raises ρv, initially lifting CHF, but as pressure approaches the critical point ρl − ρv → 0 and CHF falls again — the correlation is not monotonic. (A) ignores the (ρl − ρv) term vanishing near the critical point. (B) and (D) both contradict the explicit pressure dependence through ρv and ρl.
18. In the boiling heat-transfer relation q″ = h(Twall − Tsat), the wall superheat ΔTsat is defined as…
Answer: A. Wall superheat is measured against the local saturation temperature Tsat, not the bulk fluid temperature. (B) describes the single-phase film drop from Week 6, a different quantity entirely. (C) reverses the sign. (D) subcooling describes how far the bulk coolant sits below saturation, not the wall-to-saturation gap.
Thermal-hydraulic core analysis · Week 8
19. A core's average coolant temperature rise is 38 °C. With an enthalpy-rise hot-channel factor FΔH = 1.6, the hot channel's temperature rise is closest to…
Answer: D. ΔT_hot = FΔH × ΔT_avg = 1.6 × 38 = 60.8 °C. (A) ignores the hot-channel factor entirely. (B) divides instead of multiplying. (C) overshoots by using the wrong factor.
20. In single-phase turbulent flow, the frictional pressure drop scales as Δpfriction ∝ v². If the coolant velocity doubles, the frictional pressure drop increases by a factor of…
Answer: A. Since Δp ∝ v², doubling v gives 2² = 4× the pressure drop. (B) would require a linear dependence, which friction loss does not have. (C) would require a cubic dependence. (D) is the square-root factor relevant to natural circulation, not friction loss.
21. A reactor has negative fuel and coolant temperature reactivity coefficients. If a power surge raises the fuel and coolant temperatures, the resulting reactivity feedback…
Answer: C. δρ = αfuel·δTfuel + αcool·δTcool; with both coefficients negative, rising temperatures reduce reactivity, damping the surge — the cornerstone of inherently stable reactor design. (A) describes a dangerous positive-feedback (positive-coefficient) reactor, the opposite of this case. (B) ignores the explicit temperature dependence. (D) the feedback is active continuously during power operation, which is exactly when it matters most.
Natural circulation & decay heat · Week 9
22. In a natural-circulation loop, if the buoyant density difference (ρcold − ρhot) triples while everything else stays fixed, the loop flow velocity (v ∝ √Δρ) increases by a factor of about…
Answer: B. v ∝ √(Δρ), so tripling Δρ multiplies v by √3 ≈ 1.73. (A) assumes a linear relationship. (C) assumes a squared relationship (inverting the square root). (D) ignores the dependence on density difference entirely — the square-root law is why natural circulation is gentle and self-limiting.
23. A 3600 MW-thermal reactor scrams. Using the rule of thumb that decay heat is about 0.4% of full power one day after shutdown, the decay power at that time is closest to…
Answer: C. P_decay = 0.004 × 3600 MW = 14.4 MW. (A) and (B) are off by a factor of ten in opposite directions. (D) uses the ~7% at-scram fraction instead of the one-day fraction — a common mix-up between two different points on the decay-heat curve.
24. Why are passive decay-heat-removal systems specifically designed to work with no AC power and no operator action?
Answer: A. Passive systems exploit gravity and buoyancy precisely because those forces still work when all electrical power is lost, and the modest decay-heat load (a few percent of full power) is well matched to the gentle, square-root-limited flow natural circulation provides. (B) is backwards — active pumps are far more powerful, just power-dependent. (C) operators are not prohibited from acting; passive systems simply don't require them. (D) is false — natural circulation cannot match full-power cooling.
Capstone integration · Week 10
25. In the hot-channel capstone model, the fuel-centerline temperature peaks above the core midplane even though the linear heat rate q′(z) itself peaks exactly at the midplane. What explains this?
Answer: D. T_centerline(z) = T_coolant(z) + (convective jump) + (conduction jump); the jumps peak at the midplane like q′(z), but they ride on a coolant baseline that is still rising, so their sum peaks somewhere above the midplane. (A) the conduction equation holds everywhere along the rod. (B) cladding geometry is uniform along the length. (C) the coolant temperature is monotonically increasing and is hottest at the outlet, not the midplane — that is precisely the subtlety this question tests.
