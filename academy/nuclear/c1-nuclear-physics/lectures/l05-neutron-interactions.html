Week 5 · Lecture 5

Neutron Interactions with Matter

A neutron carries no charge — so it ignores electron clouds entirely and plows straight toward the nucleus, where its whole story unfolds.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Classify the four channels by which a neutron interacts with a nucleus: elastic scattering, inelastic scattering, radiative capture, and fission.
  • Explain why a free neutron, having no charge, sees no Coulomb barrier and interacts only with nuclei.
  • Quantify the maximum energy a neutron loses in one elastic collision and show why light nuclei are efficient moderators.
  • Define lethargy and average logarithmic energy decrement, and use them to count the collisions needed to thermalize a neutron.
  • Distinguish fast, epithermal, and thermal neutrons, and connect each regime to a real reactor or detector application.

5.1Why Neutrons Are Different

A charged particle — an alpha, a proton, a beta — spends its journey through matter feeling the Coulomb force of every electron and nucleus it passes. It loses energy gradually in millions of tiny electromagnetic nudges, leaving a continuous ionization trail. A neutron carries no electric charge, so it feels none of that. It glides through the electron clouds of atoms as if they weren't there, and it travels in a straight line until it makes a direct hit on a nucleus via the short-range nuclear force.

This single fact reshapes everything. Neutrons are not slowed continuously; they travel freely and then interact in discrete, sudden events. A neutron in matter is a random walk of long free flights punctuated by collisions, and the average distance between collisions — the mean free path — is governed by the cross-sections you met in Week 4.

lambda = 1 / (N * sigma_total)Mean free path: N = atom number density (atoms/cm^3), sigma_total = total microscopic cross-section (cm^2). Macroscopic cross-section Sigma = N * sigma carries units of 1/cm.

Because there is no Coulomb barrier to climb, even a neutron with essentially zero kinetic energy can reach the nucleus. That is why slow (thermal) neutrons are so reactive — given more time near the nucleus, the probability of capture or fission can soar. A neutron's fate depends overwhelmingly on its energy, and the four interaction channels each dominate in different energy ranges.

AnalogyThink of a charged particle as a swimmer dragging through thick water — slowed steadily by friction the whole way. A neutron is a diver in a vacuum chamber falling freely until it strikes one specific target. Nothing happens during the flight; everything happens at the impact. That is why neutron transport is modeled as flights-plus-collisions, not as continuous slowing-down.

5.2The Four Interaction Channels

When a neutron does strike a nucleus, one of four things happens. The first two are scattering (the neutron survives and continues, with reduced energy); the last two are absorption (the neutron is swallowed and a new reaction follows).

Elastic scattering (n,n)

The neutron bounces off the nucleus like a billiard ball. Total kinetic energy is conserved between the two; the neutron simply transfers some of its energy to recoil the nucleus. This is the workhorse of moderation — it is how a fast neutron is gradually slowed in a reactor's moderator.

Inelastic scattering (n,n')

The neutron is briefly absorbed, the compound nucleus is left in an excited state, then re-emits a neutron of lower energy. The leftover excitation escapes as a gamma ray. This channel requires the neutron to carry enough energy to reach the first excited level, so it has a threshold — typically around 0.1-1 MeV for heavy nuclei. It is a major energy-loss mechanism for fast neutrons in heavy materials.

Radiative capture (n,γ)

The nucleus absorbs the neutron permanently and de-excites by emitting a gamma ray, forming a heavier isotope of the same element. This is how reactors "burn up" neutrons unproductively, but also how we deliberately breed isotopes — for example, capture in uranium-238 starts the chain that produces plutonium-239.

Fission (n,f)

For certain heavy nuclei the absorbed neutron leaves the compound nucleus so excited it splits into two fragments, releasing roughly 200 MeV and — crucially — two or three fresh neutrons. This is the chain-reaction channel and the subject of Week 6.

sigma_total = sigma_scatter + sigma_absorb = (sigma_el + sigma_inel) + (sigma_gamma + sigma_fission)Every channel adds its own cross-section. The reaction actually observed in a collision is chosen in proportion to these partial cross-sections.
Common mistakeInelastic scattering is not a kind of absorption that destroys the neutron — a neutron still comes out, just at lower energy with a gamma ray emitted. The defining feature of inelastic scattering is that total kinetic energy is not conserved (some goes into nuclear excitation), whereas in elastic scattering kinetic energy is conserved. Do not confuse "inelastic" (energy lost to excitation, neutron survives) with "capture" (neutron permanently absorbed).

5.3Moderation: How Neutrons Slow Down

Fission is born fast — fission neutrons emerge with an average energy near 2 MeV. But many reactors run on thermal neutrons (~0.025 eV), where the fission cross-section of uranium-235 is hundreds of times larger. Bridging that eight-order-of-magnitude gap is the job of the moderator, and the physics is just elastic scattering analyzed with conservation of momentum and energy.

Treat the collision as a neutron of mass 1 (in atomic mass units) striking a stationary nucleus of mass A. Solving the two-body elastic collision, the ratio of the neutron's minimum possible energy after one head-on collision to its energy before is:

alpha = ((A - 1) / (A + 1))^2,   E_min = alpha * E_0alpha is the maximum-loss collision parameter. After one collision the neutron energy lies somewhere between alpha*E_0 (head-on) and E_0 (a grazing miss), uniformly in energy.

Look at what alpha does. For ordinary hydrogen (A = 1), alpha = 0 — a single head-on collision can take the neutron from full energy to zero. For carbon (A = 12), alpha = (11/13)^2 = 0.716, so at most 28% is lost per collision. For uranium (A = 238), alpha = 0.983 — heavy nuclei barely budge, and the neutron rattles off them losing almost nothing. This is why good moderators are light: the closer the target mass is to the neutron's mass, the more energy a single bounce can transfer.

AnalogyRoll a cue ball at another billiard ball of equal mass and a head-on hit stops the cue ball dead — all its energy transfers. Now roll that same cue ball at a bowling ball: it bounces back having lost almost nothing. A neutron hitting hydrogen is the equal-mass case (huge energy loss); a neutron hitting uranium is the bowling-ball case (tiny loss). That is the entire reason water and graphite — not lead — make good moderators.

5.4Lethargy and the Thermal / Fast Divide

Because each collision multiplies the energy by a random factor, energy loss is naturally measured on a logarithmic scale. We define the lethargy u as the logarithm of how far below a reference energy the neutron has fallen:

u = ln(E_ref / E)Lethargy increases as the neutron slows. Each collision adds, on average, a fixed amount of lethargy regardless of the current energy.

That fixed average increment is the average logarithmic energy decrement, written as the Greek letter xi:

xi = 1 + (alpha / (1 - alpha)) * ln(alpha),   xi_H = 1xi is the mean of ln(E_0/E') per collision. It depends only on A, not on energy — which is what makes counting collisions so clean.

Since each collision adds xi of lethargy on average, the number of collisions needed to slow a neutron from E_0 down to a target energy E is simply the total lethargy gap divided by xi:

n_collisions = (1 / xi) * ln(E_0 / E)For hydrogen xi = 1, so it takes about 18 collisions to go from 2 MeV to 0.025 eV; for carbon xi ~ 0.158, it takes about 115.

Once a neutron's energy drops to the thermal range it stops losing energy on average — it has reached equilibrium with the thermal motion of the moderator atoms, and from then on it just as likely gains a little energy in a collision as loses it. We classify neutrons by this final energy:

The three regimes each have a job. Fast neutrons (above ~0.1 MeV) carry the fission birth energy and drive fast reactors and fusion-blanket reactions. Epithermal neutrons (~1 eV to 0.1 MeV) are where sharp resonance capture peaks lurk — designers fear losing neutrons to uranium-238 resonances here. Thermal neutrons (~0.025 eV) are the gold standard for sustaining a chain reaction in uranium-235 and are also the neutrons used in materials science for neutron scattering and in radiography.

✦ Worked Example — how many collisions to thermalize?

A fission neutron born at E_0 = 2.0 MeV must be slowed to thermal energy E = 0.025 eV. Estimate the average number of elastic collisions required in (a) ordinary water (treat as hydrogen, A = 1) and (b) graphite (carbon, A = 12).

  1. Total lethargy gap: ln(E_0/E) = ln(2.0e6 / 0.025) = ln(8.0e7) = 18.2. This is the same for any moderator — it is set only by the start and end energies.
  2. Hydrogen, find alpha: alpha = ((1 - 1)/(1 + 1))^2 = 0. So xi_H = 1 (a head-on hit can remove all the energy).
  3. Hydrogen, collision count: n = ln(E_0/E) / xi = 18.2 / 1 = ~18 collisions.
  4. Carbon, find alpha: alpha = ((12 - 1)/(12 + 1))^2 = (11/13)^2 = 0.716.
  5. Carbon, find xi: xi = 1 + (0.716 / (1 - 0.716)) * ln(0.716) = 1 + (2.521)(-0.334) = 1 - 0.842 = 0.158.
  6. Carbon, collision count: n = 18.2 / 0.158 = ~115 collisions.
  7. Interpretation: hydrogen thermalizes a neutron in about 18 bounces versus 115 for carbon — light nuclei are dramatically more efficient slowing-down agents. (Hydrogen's catch: it also captures neutrons, so heavy water and graphite, with far lower absorption, are often preferred despite needing more collisions.)

🛠️ Hands-On: moderation arithmetic and a Monte Carlo slowing-down

We use the free course stack — Python + Jupyter + NumPy, with OpenMC for full neutron transport later. The one-time install lives in the Week 1/2 setup lectures (the "Install the course stack" details box); reuse that environment here. First, reproduce the worked example, then run a tiny Monte Carlo of repeated elastic collisions to watch a neutron thermalize.

# moderation.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np

def alpha(A):
    return ((A - 1) / (A + 1))**2

def xi(A):
    a = alpha(A)
    if a == 0:                       # hydrogen: limit of the formula is 1
        return 1.0
    return 1 + (a / (1 - a)) * np.log(a)

def n_collisions(A, E0=2.0e6, E=0.025):
    return np.log(E0 / E) / xi(A)

for name, A in [("hydrogen", 1), ("deuterium", 2), ("carbon", 12), ("U-238", 238)]:
    print(f"{name:9s}  alpha={alpha(A):.3f}  xi={xi(A):.3f}  n~{n_collisions(A):.0f}")
# hydrogen   alpha=0.000  xi=1.000  n~18
# carbon     alpha=0.716  xi=0.158  n~115

Now simulate one neutron's energy history. After each collision the post-collision energy is uniform in [alpha*E, E], so we draw a random fraction in that band:

# slowing_down_mc.py — watch a neutron thermalize in graphite
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

A = 12
a = ((A - 1) / (A + 1))**2          # alpha for carbon
E = 2.0e6                            # start at 2 MeV (eV)
E_thermal = 0.025
history = [E]
while E > E_thermal:
    frac = rng.uniform(a, 1.0)       # energy retained this collision
    E *= frac
    history.append(E)

print("collisions to thermal:", len(history) - 1)   # ~ a hundred-ish, scatters around 115
print("first few energies (eV):", [f"{e:.2e}" for e in history[:4]])

Average len(history)-1 over many runs and you will land near the analytic 115. Swap A=1 for hydrogen and watch the count collapse toward ~18 — the simulation reproduces the lethargy formula from first principles.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why does a free neutron pass through an atom's electron cloud almost as if it weren't there?
A. It moves too fast for electrons to react
B. It has no electric charge, so it feels no Coulomb force from electrons
C. Electrons repel each other and clear a path
D. The neutron is much smaller than an electron
Show answer

B. A neutron is electrically neutral, so the electromagnetic interaction that slows charged particles simply doesn't act on it; it interacts only with nuclei via the short-range nuclear force. (A) is false — slow thermal neutrons still ignore electrons. (C) is nonsense, and (D) confuses size with charge.

Q2. In which interaction does a neutron get permanently absorbed and a gamma ray emitted, forming a heavier isotope of the same element?
A. Elastic scattering (n,n)
B. Inelastic scattering (n,n')
C. Radiative capture (n,γ)
D. Fission (n,f)
Show answer

C. Radiative capture swallows the neutron and de-excites by emitting a gamma, yielding a heavier isotope. In elastic (A) and inelastic (B) scattering a neutron re-emerges; in fission (D) the nucleus splits and releases multiple neutrons.

Q3. A neutron loses the most energy per elastic collision when it strikes a nucleus whose mass is…
A. as close as possible to the neutron's own mass (light nuclei like hydrogen)
B. as large as possible (heavy nuclei like uranium)
C. exactly twice the neutron's mass
D. mass has no effect on energy transfer
Show answer

A. Since alpha = ((A-1)/(A+1))^2, alpha shrinks toward 0 as A approaches 1, allowing up to 100% energy loss in one head-on hit. Heavy nuclei (B) give alpha near 1 (tiny loss). (C) is not optimal, and (D) ignores the kinematics entirely.

Q4. The quantity xi (average logarithmic energy decrement) is useful for counting moderation collisions mainly because…
A. it equals the neutron's kinetic energy
B. it is the same for every moderator material
C. it is constant per collision, independent of the neutron's current energy
D. it equals the fission cross-section
Show answer

C. xi depends only on the target mass A, not on energy, so every collision adds the same average lethargy — letting us divide the total lethargy gap by xi to count collisions. (B) is false (xi differs per material: 1 for H, 0.158 for C). (A) and (D) confuse it with unrelated quantities.

Q5. Fission neutrons are born near 2 MeV, yet many power reactors run on thermal (~0.025 eV) neutrons. What is the role of the moderator?
A. It absorbs the fast neutrons to control the reaction
B. It speeds the neutrons up so they reach resonance energies
C. It converts neutrons into gamma rays
D. It slows neutrons by elastic collisions to thermal energy, where the U-235 fission cross-section is far larger
Show answer

D. The moderator's job is to slow fission-born fast neutrons down to thermal energy through many elastic collisions, because U-235's thermal fission cross-section is hundreds of times larger than its fast value. (A) describes a control absorber, not a moderator; (B) is backwards; (C) is not what a moderator does.

✓ What you learned

  • A neutron is neutral, so it feels no Coulomb barrier and interacts only with nuclei — as long free flights punctuated by sudden collisions.
  • The four channels: elastic and inelastic scattering (neutron survives), and radiative capture and fission (neutron absorbed).
  • Maximum fractional energy loss per elastic hit is set by alpha = ((A-1)/(A+1))^2 — small A means big loss, which is why moderators are light.
  • Energy loss is logarithmic: lethargy grows by a constant xi per collision, so collisions to thermalize = ln(E_0/E) / xi.
  • Neutrons split into fast, epithermal, and thermal regimes, each tied to real reactor and detector physics.
