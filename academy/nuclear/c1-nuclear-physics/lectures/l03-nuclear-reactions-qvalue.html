Nuclear Reactions & Q-Values

Smash two nuclei together and a single ledger — mass and energy on the same balance sheet — tells you whether the reaction gives energy back or demands a toll up front.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write and read a nuclear reaction in the compact a(b,c)d notation.
  • Apply the four conservation laws — nucleon number, charge, energy, and momentum — to balance a reaction.
  • Compute the Q-value from rest masses and classify a reaction as exothermic (Q > 0) or endothermic (Q < 0).
  • Derive and use the threshold energy for an endothermic reaction in the lab frame.
  • Take a first look at two-body reaction kinematics and verify a Q-value numerically in Python.
  • Write the semi-empirical mass formula (liquid-drop model) and explain the physical origin of each term.

3.1What Is a Nuclear Reaction?

In Lecture 2 a single unstable nucleus rearranged itself on its own — radioactive decay. A nuclear reaction is the induced cousin: a projectile strikes a target nucleus, the two briefly merge or scatter, and the system comes apart into new products. The classic example is the first artificial transmutation ever observed, an alpha particle striking nitrogen to produce oxygen and a proton:

alpha + N-14 -> O-17 + pA helium-4 nucleus hits nitrogen-14, leaving oxygen-17 and a free proton. This is a reaction, not a decay — something had to come in.

Writing out every nucleus gets clumsy, so physicists use a compact a(b,c)d notation: the target a, the incoming light particle b in parentheses, the outgoing light particle c, and the residual nucleus d. The reaction above becomes:

N-14 ( alpha , p ) O-17Target ( projectile , ejectile ) residual. The two heavy nuclei sit outside the parentheses; the two light particles sit inside.

This shorthand is everywhere in the field. A neutron-capture reaction that makes a medical isotope might be written Mo-98(n, gamma)Mo-99; a fast-neutron reaction used to detect oil and water downhole is O-16(n, p)N-16. The notation packs the whole experiment — what you shoot, at what, and what flies out — into a single line.

AnalogyThink of a(b,c)d like a recipe written as main ingredient ( what you add , what you pour off ) what's left in the pan. The target is the roast already in the oven; b is the splash of stock you add; c is the steam that escapes; d is the finished dish. Read it left-to-right and you know the whole process at a glance.

3.2The Four Conservation Laws

A nuclear reaction is not anything-goes. Four bookkeeping rules must hold in every reaction, and they are the first thing you check before computing anything else.

Nucleon number A (protons + neutrons) is conserved: the total mass number on the left equals the total on the right. Charge Z is conserved: the total proton number balances. These two let you fill in any single unknown product. Energy is conserved — but here "energy" includes rest-mass energy via E = mc², which is the entire point of the Q-value. And momentum is conserved, which constrains the angles and speeds of the products.

sum A : left = right and sum Z : left = rightBalance nucleon number and charge first; they pin down the identities of the nuclei.

Take the reaction Be-9(alpha, n)C-12. On the left: A = 9 + 4 = 13, Z = 4 + 2 = 6. On the right: C-12 plus a neutron gives A = 12 + 1 = 13, Z = 6 + 0 = 6. Both balance, so the reaction is allowed by these two laws — historically this is exactly how the neutron was discovered.

Common mistakeBalancing A and Z does not mean mass is conserved. Mass number (a count of nucleons) is conserved, but the total rest mass is not — the small difference is what becomes kinetic energy. Students routinely assume "A balances, so mass balances," then are baffled where the reaction energy comes from. The missing mass is the energy.

3.3The Q-Value: Exothermic vs Endothermic

The Q-value is the net energy released by a reaction, defined as the rest-mass energy of the reactants minus the rest-mass energy of the products:

Q = ( m_reactants - m_products ) c²Q = (sum of incoming rest masses minus sum of outgoing rest masses) times c-squared.

Equivalently, by energy conservation, Q is the gain in total kinetic energy: Q = KE_products - KE_reactants. The two definitions are the same statement read from opposite sides of the ledger. The sign of Q tells you everything:

Q > 0 exothermic (releases energy) Q < 0 endothermic (absorbs energy)Positive Q: products are lighter, the surplus mass appears as kinetic energy. Negative Q: products are heavier, you must supply the deficit.

An exothermic reaction (Q > 0) converts rest mass into kinetic energy and can in principle proceed from rest. Fission and fusion are both strongly exothermic — that surplus is the energy a reactor or a star lives on. An endothermic reaction (Q < 0) needs kinetic energy poured in; it cannot happen unless the projectile arrives fast enough.

3.4Threshold Energy & a First Look at Kinematics

For an endothermic reaction you might guess you simply need projectile kinetic energy equal to |Q|. You need more. Momentum must be conserved, so the products cannot be left at rest in the lab frame — they have to carry away the momentum the projectile brought in. That leftover kinetic energy is unavailable for the reaction, which raises the bar.

The cleanest way to see this is to switch to the center-of-momentum (CM) frame, where the total momentum is zero. In the CM frame the reaction just barely proceeds when all the available kinetic energy equals |Q|. Transforming back to the lab frame (target at rest, projectile of mass m_b, target of mass m_a), the minimum projectile kinetic energy — the threshold energy — is:

KE_threshold = |Q| * ( m_a + m_b ) / m_aValid when energies are small compared to rest masses (non-relativistic). The factor (m_a + m_b)/m_a is always > 1, so threshold always exceeds |Q|.

For a heavy target struck by a light projectile, m_a + m_b is barely more than m_a and the correction is tiny. But when projectile and target are comparable in mass, the inflation is large — a real effect that accelerator designers must budget for when they choose beam energy. Below threshold, the reaction simply does not occur no matter how many projectiles you fire.

Even for exothermic reactions, kinematics matters: the released energy Q is shared between the two products in inverse proportion to their masses (the light product runs away with most of the speed), and momentum conservation fixes the angle between them. We will make this quantitative in Week 4 when we connect reaction probability to cross-sections.

3.5Where Do the Masses Come From? The Semi-Empirical Mass Formula

Every Q-value above leaned on measured atomic masses read off a table. Lecture 1 promised a model that predicts those masses — and the binding energy per nucleon — from first principles: the liquid-drop picture, formalized as the semi-empirical mass formula (SEMF), also called the Bethe–Weizsäcker formula. It treats the nucleus as an incompressible charged liquid drop and sums five competing physical effects, term by term.

B(A,Z) = aVA − aSA2/3 − aCZ(Z−1)/A1/3 − aA(A−2Z)²/A + δ(A,Z)Volume minus surface minus Coulomb minus asymmetry, plus a pairing correction. One common fit: a_V≈15.8, a_S≈18.3, a_C≈0.714, a_A≈23.2 MeV.

Each term has a direct physical story, several of which you already met in Lecture 1's binding-energy curve:

  • Volume (+aVA): every nucleon binds to its near neighbors with roughly the same strong-force energy, so total binding grows linearly with A — the bulk, saturating attraction from Lecture 1.
  • Surface (−aSA2/3): nucleons sitting at the drop's surface have fewer neighbors than interior nucleons, so less binding — the "surface tension" penalty, scaling with surface area A2/3.
  • Coulomb (−aCZ(Z−1)/A1/3): every pair of protons repels electrostatically; the penalty grows roughly as Z² and hits large, proton-rich nuclei hardest — the long-range force that ultimately drives fission.
  • Asymmetry (−aA(A−2Z)²/A): the Pauli exclusion principle favors roughly equal numbers of protons and neutrons; drifting away from N ≈ Z costs binding energy.
  • Pairing (δ): like-nucleons pair their spins for a small binding bonus. δ is positive for even-even nuclei (extra stable), negative for odd-odd nuclei (extra unstable), and zero for odd-A nuclei.

Plug in Fe-56 (Z=26, A=56, even-even) and the formula gives B/A ≈ 8.76 MeV/nucleon — close to the 8.79 MeV you computed from measured masses in Lecture 1. For U-238 it gives B/A ≈ 7.60 MeV/nucleon, again matching the measured ≈7.6 MeV. The formula is noticeably less reliable for the very lightest nuclei (He-4 comes out well below its true value) — "liquid drop" stops being a good picture once there are only a handful of nucleons. SEMF is a bulk-matter approximation, not an exact quantum calculation, but it is remarkably good from A ≈ 20 upward.

AnalogySEMF is the nuclear equivalent of a scaling law for a water droplet: bulk volume energy, surface tension, and electrostatic repulsion between charged regions, with a small quantum "bonus" for paired spins thrown in. It gets large nuclei right for the same reason continuum scaling laws get large systems right — the law of large numbers smooths out the lumpiness of individual quantum states.

SEMF is also the formula behind the liquid-drop fission barrier you'll meet in Week 6 (Fission: Physics & Energy Release): the competition between the surface term (resisting deformation) and the Coulomb term (favoring a split) is exactly what sets the fissility of heavy nuclei.

✦ Worked Example — Q-value and threshold

Consider the endothermic reaction p + Li-7 -> Be-7 + n, written Li-7(p, n)Be-7. Using atomic masses (in u): m(Li-7) = 7.016004, m(p) = 1.007825, m(Be-7) = 7.016929, m(n) = 1.008665. Take 1 u = 931.494 MeV/c². Find Q and the proton threshold energy.

  1. Mass of reactants: 1.007825 + 7.016004 = 8.023829 u.
  2. Mass of products: 7.016929 + 1.008665 = 8.025594 u.
  3. Mass difference: 8.023829 - 8.025594 = -0.001765 u. Products are heavier, so we expect Q < 0.
  4. Q-value: Q = (-0.001765 u)(931.494 MeV/u) = -1.644 MeV. Endothermic, as expected.
  5. Threshold: projectile is the proton (m_b = 1.0078 u), target is Li-7 (m_a = 7.0160 u). KE_th = |Q|(m_a + m_b)/m_a = 1.644 x (8.0238 / 7.0160) = 1.644 x 1.1437 = 1.880 MeV.
  6. Sanity check: 1.880 MeV > 1.644 MeV = |Q|, as required — the extra 0.236 MeV is the kinetic energy the products must keep to conserve momentum. This reaction is a standard laboratory neutron source, and the threshold is exactly why such sources need a proton beam above ~1.88 MeV.

🛠️ Hands-On: Q-values in Python

Using the same nuclear conda environment from Week 1's install — nothing new to install this week. The snippet below needs only NumPy, so any Python 3 with NumPy works.

Let's compute the Q-value and threshold for the Li-7(p, n)Be-7 reaction from the worked example, then loop over a couple of reactions to classify each.

# qvalue.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

U_TO_MEV = 931.494   # 1 atomic mass unit in MeV/c^2

# atomic masses in u (target, projectile) -> (residual, ejectile)
masses = {
    "Li-7": 7.016004, "p": 1.007825,
    "Be-7": 7.016929, "n": 1.008665,
    "H-2":  2.014102, "H-3": 3.016049,
    "He-4": 4.002602,
}

def q_value(reactants, products):
    m_in  = sum(masses[x] for x in reactants)
    m_out = sum(masses[x] for x in products)
    return (m_in - m_out) * U_TO_MEV          # MeV

def threshold(Q, m_target, m_proj):
    if Q >= 0:
        return 0.0                            # exothermic: no threshold
    return abs(Q) * (m_target + m_proj) / m_target

# 1) endothermic neutron source
Q1 = q_value(["p", "Li-7"], ["Be-7", "n"])
KEth = threshold(Q1, masses["Li-7"], masses["p"])
print(f"Li-7(p,n)Be-7:  Q = {Q1:+.3f} MeV   threshold = {KEth:.3f} MeV")

# 2) the D-T fusion reaction that powers fusion research
Q2 = q_value(["H-2", "H-3"], ["He-4", "n"])
print(f"H-2(t,n)He-4:   Q = {Q2:+.3f} MeV   {'exothermic' if Q2>0 else 'endothermic'}")

You should see Q = -1.644 MeV, threshold = 1.880 MeV for the first line, matching the worked example, and a large positive Q near +17.6 MeV for deuterium-tritium fusion — the reaction at the heart of fusion energy research. Try adding the fission-relevant U-235(n, gamma) capture or your own reaction to the dictionary.

🔗 Where this is heading

A positive Q tells you a reaction can release energy, but not how often it happens — that is the job of the cross-section (Week 4), the effective target area a nucleus presents to an incoming particle. Q-values and thresholds set the energy scale; cross-sections set the rate. Together they drive everything downstream: the neutron transport and criticality calculations of a fission reactor (later in this course), the energy balance of stellar nucleosynthesis in the astrophysics track, and the design of accelerator-driven neutron and isotope sources. Every reactor power level and every fusion break-even target traces back to a Q-value you can now compute by hand.

Model response

The Q-value and the ignition requirement are two completely different barriers, and conflating them is the trap. The Q-value is a rest-mass bookkeeping number: it compares the masses of reactants and products and tells you the net energy released once the reaction completes. For D-T fusion that ledger is firmly positive (+17.6 MeV), so energetically the products are the favorable destination.

But before two nuclei can fuse they must touch, and both are positively charged. The Coulomb barrier — electrostatic repulsion between the two positive nuclei — stands in the way, and it has nothing to do with rest masses. To get the nuclei close enough for the short-range nuclear force to take over, they need enormous relative kinetic energy, supplied as temperature (hence ~10&sup8; K). Once they cross the barrier, the reaction is exothermic and pays back far more than the cost of crossing. So Q is the net energy of the finished reaction; the ignition temperature is the activation cost to even attempt it.

The threshold energy of an endothermic reaction is yet a third thing: it exists because the products are heavier (Q < 0) and momentum must be conserved, so you must pay the mass deficit plus a kinematic surcharge. D-T fusion has no such threshold (Q > 0, it could in principle proceed at zero relative energy if the nuclei could only touch) — its obstacle is purely the Coulomb barrier, a dynamical hurdle, not a rest-mass deficit. The lesson: a favorable Q is necessary for net energy gain but says nothing about whether a barrier blocks the path there.

Self-check rubric

  • Distinguish Q (net rest-mass energy released) from the energy needed to initiate the reaction.
  • Name the Coulomb barrier as the reason fusion needs extreme temperature, and tie it to both nuclei being positively charged.
  • State clearly that the Coulomb barrier is dynamical, not a Q < 0 / mass-deficit threshold — they are different in kind.
  • Note that a positive Q is necessary for net energy gain but does not guarantee the reaction is easy to start.

Q1. In the notation N-14(alpha, p)O-17, which particle is the incoming projectile?
A. N-14
B. the alpha particle
C. the proton p
D. O-17
Show answer

B. In a(b,c)d the first item inside the parentheses (b) is the projectile, here the alpha. N-14 (A) is the target, p (C) is the ejectile, and O-17 (D) is the residual nucleus.

Q2. A reaction has rest-mass reactants heavier than its products. The reaction is…
A. endothermic, with Q < 0
B. impossible because mass must be conserved
C. exothermic, with Q > 0
D. exothermic, but only above a threshold energy
Show answer

C. Q = (m_reactants - m_products)c² > 0, so it is exothermic. (A) has the sign backwards; (B) confuses mass-number with rest-mass conservation; (D) is wrong as a general statement about this reaction's energy ledger — an exothermic reaction has no mass-deficit threshold (Q > 0 means it is favorable at the mass level alone). But don't over-generalize: as Q5 shows, a charged-particle-induced exothermic reaction can still have an effective threshold from the Coulomb barrier, which is a dynamical obstacle, not a Q-value one.

Q3. Which quantity is not conserved in a nuclear reaction?
A. total nucleon number A
B. total charge Z
C. total energy (including rest-mass energy)
D. total rest mass
Show answer

D. Rest mass is not conserved — the difference becomes the Q-value energy. Nucleon number (A), charge (B), and total relativistic energy (C) are all conserved.

Q4. For an endothermic reaction with target mass m_a and projectile mass m_b, the lab threshold energy is…
A. exactly equal to |Q|
B. |Q| × (m_a + m_b) / m_a, which is greater than |Q|
C. |Q| × m_a / (m_a + m_b), which is less than |Q|
D. always exactly twice |Q|
Show answer

B. Momentum conservation forces the products to keep kinetic energy, so the threshold exceeds |Q| by the factor (m_a + m_b)/m_a > 1. (A) ignores that surcharge; (C) is below |Q|, which is impossible; (D) is a fixed factor that has no physical basis.

Q5. Deuterium-tritium fusion has Q ≈ +17.6 MeV yet requires temperatures above 10&sup8; K to proceed. The temperature is needed to…
A. overcome the Coulomb barrier so the nuclei can get close enough to fuse
B. supply the negative Q-value of the reaction
C. make the reaction exothermic instead of endothermic
D. conserve nucleon number
Show answer

A. The barrier is the electrostatic repulsion between the two positive nuclei; high temperature gives them the kinetic energy to cross it. (B) is wrong — Q is positive here, not negative; (C) the reaction is already exothermic; (D) nucleon number is automatically conserved and has nothing to do with temperature.
