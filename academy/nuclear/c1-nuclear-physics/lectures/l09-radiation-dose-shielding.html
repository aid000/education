Week 9 · Lecture 9

Radiation Interaction, Dose & Shielding

A gamma ray crossing a slab of lead, an alpha stopped by a sheet of paper, a worker behind a wall — all of it is one physics: how energy is deposited, and how much of it the body absorbs.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the three dominant photon interactions — photoelectric, Compton, and pair production — and when each one rules.
  • Contrast how photons, charged particles (alpha, beta), and neutrons deposit energy in matter.
  • Derive and apply exponential attenuation I = I0·exp(-mu·x), and use half-value layers.
  • Distinguish absorbed dose (gray) from equivalent dose (sievert) and link them to biological effect.
  • Size a practical gamma shield and estimate the dose behind it.

9.1How Radiation Hands Energy to Matter

Up to now we have treated decay and reactions as events that emit radiation. This week we follow what happens after emission: a particle or photon plows into a wall, a fuel pin, or a person, and gives up its energy through a chain of microscopic collisions. The whole field of radiation protection rests on understanding those collisions from first principles.

Start with the rule of thumb that organizes everything: charged particles interact strongly, photons interact weakly, and neutrons interact strangely. Charged particles (alphas, betas, protons) feel the electric field of every electron they pass, so they lose energy almost continuously and stop in a short, well-defined range. Photons (gammas, X-rays) carry no charge; they travel until a single chance event removes them, so a beam thins out exponentially rather than stopping at a fixed depth. Neutrons carry no charge either, but they ignore electrons entirely and interact only with nuclei, which is why hydrogen-rich materials shield them best.

For a charged particle, the energy lost per unit path length is the stopping power, captured (for heavy charged particles) by the Bethe formula. The key scaling is what matters intuitively:

-dE/dx proportional to z^2 · (Z/A) / v^2Stopping power rises with the particle's charge squared (z) and the medium's electron density (Z/A), and blows up as the particle slows (small v) — producing the Bragg peak near the end of the track.

Because stopping power scales as 1/v^2, a charged particle deposits most of its energy right before it stops — the Bragg peak. That single fact is why proton beams can hit a deep tumor while sparing tissue in front of it, and why an alpha particle, though stopped by skin, is devastating if a source is inhaled and deposits its entire energy inside lung tissue.

AnalogyThink of a sprinter running through a crowd. An alpha particle is a heavyweight shoving through — it slows fast, bumps everyone near it, and collapses after a few meters (a few cm of air, < 0.1 mm of tissue). A gamma photon is a ghost: it usually passes clean through the whole crowd untouched, but when it finally hits someone, the encounter is sudden and total. You cannot predict where a given photon stops — only the probability per unit distance.

9.2The Three Photon Interactions

Photons are the workhorse of shielding problems, so we treat them carefully. A gamma ray removes itself from a beam through one of three competing processes, and which one dominates depends on photon energy E and the absorber's atomic number Z.

Photoelectric effect (low E, high Z): the photon is fully absorbed by a bound atomic electron, which is ejected. The cross-section scales roughly as Z^4 to Z^5 over E^3 — extremely steep — which is exactly why high-Z lead is so effective against low-energy X-rays and why bone (calcium) shows up bright on a radiograph.

Compton scattering (intermediate E, ~0.5 to 5 MeV): the photon scatters off a quasi-free electron, transferring part of its energy and continuing at a lower energy in a new direction. It depends on electron density (so on Z/A, nearly constant across materials), and it dominates in the energy range of most reactor and medical gammas. The scattered photon energy follows the Compton relation:

E' = E / ( 1 + (E/mc^2)(1 - cos theta) )E = incident photon energy, E' = scattered energy, theta = scattering angle, mc^2 = 0.511 MeV (electron rest energy). Backscatter (theta = 180 deg) gives the largest energy loss.

Pair production (high E): if the photon exceeds 1.022 MeV (twice the electron rest energy), it can convert into an electron-positron pair in the field of a nucleus. The cross-section grows with Z^2 and with energy above threshold, taking over above a few MeV. The positron later annihilates, releasing two 0.511 MeV photons — a secondary source the shield designer must remember.

Common mistakeStudents often say "lead always beats water for shielding." It depends on energy. The photoelectric advantage of high-Z lead is huge for keV X-rays, but in the Compton-dominated MeV band lead's edge per gram shrinks to little more than its density advantage. And for fast neutrons, lead is nearly useless — you want hydrogen (water, polyethylene, concrete). Always match the shield to the radiation type and energy.

9.3Exponential Attenuation

Because each photon is removed by a single random event, the fraction removed in a thin slab dx is constant: dI = -mu·I·dx, where mu is the linear attenuation coefficient (units 1/cm), the probability of interaction per unit path. Integrating gives the most important equation in shielding:

I(x) = I0 · exp(-mu · x)I0 = incident beam intensity, I(x) = intensity after thickness x, mu = linear attenuation coefficient (1/cm). The beam never reaches zero — it only halves, and halves again.

Two derived quantities make this practical. The half-value layer (HVL) is the thickness that cuts intensity in half, and the mean free path is the average distance a photon travels before interacting:

HVL = ln(2) / mu = 0.693 / mu   |   mean free path = 1 / muAfter n half-value layers the beam is reduced by a factor 2^n. Tabulated mu values (or mass attenuation mu/rho, in cm^2/g) are looked up by material and energy.

It is often cleaner to use the mass attenuation coefficient mu/rho (cm^2/g), because it is nearly independent of physical state — the same value works for water vapor and ice. Multiply by density rho to recover mu. This exponential law is what every radiographer, reactor shield engineer, and medical physicist uses daily.

9.4From Energy Deposited to Biological Harm

Attenuation tells us how much radiation gets through; dose tells us how much harm it does. The most basic measure is absorbed dose — energy deposited per unit mass of tissue:

D = E_absorbed / m   [ 1 gray (Gy) = 1 joule / kilogram ]The gray is purely physical: it counts deposited energy and says nothing about the type of radiation or which organ absorbed it.

But a gray of alpha particles is far more biologically destructive than a gray of gammas, because alphas dump their energy in dense, hard-to-repair clusters along a short track (high linear energy transfer, LET). We correct for this with a dimensionless radiation weighting factor w_R, giving the equivalent dose:

H = w_R · D   [ sievert (Sv) ]w_R = 1 for photons and betas, ~20 for alphas, and 2 to ~20 for neutrons depending on energy. One sievert of any radiation carries roughly the same stochastic risk.

Biological effects split into two regimes. Deterministic effects (radiation burns, acute radiation syndrome) have a threshold and grow with dose — they appear above a few hundred mGy to grays. Stochastic effects (cancer, heritable mutation) have no firm threshold; their probability rises with dose, the basis of the conservative linear-no-threshold model used in protection limits. For perspective, natural background is around 2 to 3 mSv per year, while a single acute whole-body dose near 4 to 5 Sv is lethal to roughly half of those exposed without treatment.

Radiation protection then reduces to three controllable levers — the practitioner's mantra:

Time · Distance · ShieldingMinimize time near a source; maximize distance (dose falls as 1/r^2 from a point source); interpose shielding (the exponential law of 9.3). All three multiply your protection.
✦ Worked Example — size a lead shield and estimate the dose

A point source emits 1.25 MeV gammas. At a work position the unshielded dose rate is 8.0 mSv/h. Company policy caps it at 0.10 mSv/h. For lead at 1.25 MeV, the mass attenuation coefficient is mu/rho = 0.0589 cm^2/g and the density is rho = 11.35 g/cm^3. How thick a lead slab is needed (ignoring buildup)?

  1. Linear attenuation coefficient: mu = (mu/rho)·rho = 0.0589 × 11.35 = 0.668 cm^-1.
  2. Required attenuation factor: we need I/I0 = 0.10 / 8.0 = 0.0125 (a factor of 80 reduction).
  3. Invert the exponential law: 0.0125 = exp(-mu·x), so mu·x = -ln(0.0125) = ln(80) = 4.382.
  4. Solve for thickness: x = 4.382 / 0.668 = 6.56 cm of lead.
  5. Sanity check via HVL: HVL = 0.693/0.668 = 1.04 cm. A factor of 80 needs log2(80) ≈ 6.3 HVL → 6.3 × 1.04 ≈ 6.6 cm. Consistent. In real design you would multiply by a buildup factor (often 2 to 5×) to account for scattered photons that the simple law ignores — so the engineered shield would be thicker.

🛠️ Hands-On: attenuation and a slab dose in Python + OpenMC

We continue with the free course stack — Python + Jupyter + NumPy, plus OpenMC for transport (the install details lives in the Week 1/2 lectures). First, the textbook exponential law in pure NumPy; then a peek at what a real Monte-Carlo code does for the same slab, including the scattered photons the simple law misses.

# attenuation.py — Lecture 9: analytic exponential law
import numpy as np

mu_over_rho = 0.0589      # cm^2/g, lead at 1.25 MeV
rho         = 11.35       # g/cm^3
mu          = mu_over_rho * rho            # 1/cm

target = 0.10 / 8.0       # required I/I0 (dose-rate ratio)
x = -np.log(target) / mu  # solve I/I0 = exp(-mu x)
hvl = np.log(2) / mu

print(f"mu        = {mu:.3f} 1/cm")
print(f"HVL       = {hvl:.2f} cm")
print(f"thickness = {x:.2f} cm of lead")   # -> 6.56 cm

The analytic law assumes every scattered photon is gone for good. A transport code tracks those scattered photons, so it reports a higher dose behind the slab (the buildup effect). OpenMC sets up the same slab with a photon source and a flux tally:

# slab_openmc.py — sketch of a shielded-slab tally (run in Jupyter)
import openmc

lead = openmc.Material(name="lead")
lead.add_element("Pb", 1.0)
lead.set_density("g/cm3", 11.35)

slab = openmc.model.RectangularParallelepiped(
    -50, 50, -50, 50, 0, 6.56, boundary_type="transmission")
# ... geometry: source side (vacuum) | lead slab | detector cell ...

src = openmc.IndependentSource()
src.particle = "photon"
src.energy   = openmc.stats.Discrete([1.25e6], [1.0])   # 1.25 MeV

tally = openmc.Tally(name="flux_behind")
tally.scores = ["flux"]            # compare to I0*exp(-mu*x)
# settings.particles = 200000; openmc.run() then read statepoint
# the tally/analytic ratio IS the buildup factor

Run both, divide the Monte-Carlo result by the analytic one, and you have measured the buildup factor — the exact correction the worked example warned about. We build the full transport model in the Week 10 capstone.

A facility must shield a mixed field: high-energy gammas and fast neutrons from the same source. A junior engineer proposes "just use a thick lead wall — lead is the densest shield we have." Critique this plan from first principles. What physics does it get right, what does it get dangerously wrong, and what layered design would you propose instead? Show a model response

Model response. Lead is excellent for the gamma component: its high Z gives strong photoelectric absorption at lower energies and a high density that helps in the Compton band, plus it handles pair-production photons well. So the instinct is half-right. But lead is nearly transparent to fast neutrons: neutrons interact only with nuclei, and elastic scattering transfers the most energy when the target mass matches the neutron's — i.e., off light nuclei like hydrogen, not heavy lead. A pure lead wall would let fast neutrons stream through almost unattenuated, and could even make things worse: high-energy neutrons can trigger (n,2n) reactions and activate the lead.

A sound design is layered and ordered by function. First a hydrogen-rich moderator (water, polyethylene, or concrete) to slow fast neutrons down by elastic collisions; then a good thermal-neutron absorber (boron or lithium, which capture slow neutrons with little penetrating gamma); and finally a lead or steel layer to attenuate both the original gammas and the secondary capture gammas produced when neutrons are absorbed. The capture-gamma point is the subtle one: stopping neutrons creates new photons, so the gamma shield must sit downstream of the neutron shield. Match each layer to the physics of the particle it is meant to stop.

Q1. Which photon interaction dominates for a 0.05 MeV X-ray passing through high-Z lead?
A. Photoelectric effect
B. Compton scattering
C. Pair production
D. Elastic neutron scattering
Show answer

A. The photoelectric cross-section scales roughly as Z^4–Z^5 / E^3, so it dominates at low energy and high Z. Compton (B) owns the MeV band; pair production (C) needs E > 1.022 MeV; (D) applies to neutrons, not photons.

Q2. One half-value layer (HVL) of shielding reduces beam intensity to what fraction, and after 3 HVL?
A. 1/3, then 1/9
B. 1/2, then 1/8
C. 1/2, then zero
D. 1/e, then 1/e^3
Show answer

B. Each HVL halves the beam, so 3 HVL give (1/2)^3 = 1/8. (A) confuses halving with thirds; (C) is wrong because the exponential never reaches zero; (D) describes mean-free-path reductions, not the half-value layer.

Q3. The gray (Gy) and the sievert (Sv) differ in that…
A. the gray applies only to neutrons and the sievert only to photons
B. the gray is energy per unit charge; the sievert is energy per unit mass
C. the gray measures absorbed energy per mass; the sievert weights it by radiation type for biological effect
D. they are identical units with different names
Show answer

C. 1 Gy = 1 J/kg of deposited energy (purely physical); the sievert multiplies by w_R (≈1 for photons, ≈20 for alphas) to reflect biological harm. (A) and (B) misstate the definitions; (D) is wrong because they are numerically equal only when w_R = 1.

Q4. A pure lead wall is a poor shield against fast neutrons mainly because…
A. lead has too low a density
B. neutrons are charged and lead repels them
C. lead's high atomic number absorbs all the neutrons instantly
D. neutrons lose little energy scattering off heavy nuclei; light, hydrogen-rich materials moderate them far better
Show answer

D. Elastic scattering transfers maximum energy when target and projectile masses match, so hydrogen moderates neutrons best; heavy lead barely slows them. (A) is false (lead is dense); (B) is false (neutrons are neutral); (C) is the opposite of reality.

Q5. The three core principles of practical radiation protection are…
A. time, distance, and shielding
B. mass, charge, and energy
C. dose, rate, and exposure
D. alpha, beta, and gamma
Show answer

A. Minimize time near a source, maximize distance (1/r^2 falloff), and interpose shielding (exponential attenuation). (B) lists particle properties; (C) lists dose quantities; (D) lists radiation types — none are the protection levers.
