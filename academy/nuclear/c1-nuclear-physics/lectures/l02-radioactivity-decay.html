Radioactivity & Decay Laws

Every unstable nucleus carries a hidden clock — one that ticks not on schedule but by chance, yet still keeps perfect statistical time across trillions of atoms.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Identify the major decay modes — alpha, beta-minus, beta-plus, electron capture, and gamma — and write balanced decay equations for each.
  • Derive the exponential decay law N = N0·e^(−λt) from the single assumption of a constant per-nucleus decay probability.
  • Relate the decay constant λ to half-life T½ and mean life τ, and convert fluently between them.
  • Define activity A = λN and compute it in becquerels and curies.
  • Explain decay chains and the condition for secular equilibrium, and simulate decay in Python with NumPy.

2.1Why Nuclei Decay — and How

In Lecture 1 you saw that a nucleus is bound by the strong force fighting the Coulomb repulsion of its protons, and that the binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron. A nucleus sitting away from that peak — too heavy, or with a lopsided neutron-to-proton ratio — can reach a lower-energy, more tightly bound configuration by radioactive decay: spontaneously emitting particles or photons. Decay happens because it is energetically allowed and quantum-mechanically permitted, not because anything "triggers" a particular atom.

There are a handful of dominant modes, each conserving charge (Z) and nucleon number (A):

  • Alpha (α): emit a helium-4 nucleus. Z drops by 2, A by 4. Favored by heavy nuclei (e.g. uranium, plutonium) where shedding two protons relieves Coulomb strain.
  • Beta-minus (β⁻): a neutron converts to a proton, emitting an electron and an antineutrino. Z rises by 1, A unchanged. Favored by neutron-rich nuclei.
  • Beta-plus (β⁺): a proton converts to a neutron, emitting a positron and a neutrino. Z drops by 1, A unchanged. Favored by proton-rich nuclei.
  • Electron capture (EC): a proton captures an inner-shell electron, becoming a neutron plus a neutrino. Same net effect on Z as β⁺, but no positron — it competes with β⁺.
  • Gamma (γ): an excited nucleus drops to a lower energy level, emitting a high-energy photon. Z and A both unchanged — it usually follows an α or β decay that left the daughter excited.
238/92 U -> 234/90 Th + 4/2 He (alpha)A balanced alpha decay: nucleon number 238 = 234 + 4 and charge 92 = 90 + 2 both balance.

These modes are the basis of nearly every nuclear application: alpha emitters power deep-space probes, beta emitters drive medical tracers and reactor decay heat, and gamma rays from cobalt-60 sterilize medical equipment and treat tumors.

AnalogyThink of an unstable nucleus as a boulder perched on a ledge partway down a hillside. The valley floor (iron, maximum binding) is far below, but a small lip holds the boulder in place. Decay is the boulder finally tipping over that lip — it can only fall down in energy, and once it does it cannot climb back. Different decay modes are simply different paths down the same slope.

2.2The Exponential Decay Law

Here is the deep fact: a single radioactive nucleus has no memory and no age. Whether it was made a second ago or a million years ago, its probability of decaying in the next instant is the same constant. Call that probability per unit time λ (the decay constant, units of 1/time). This one assumption is enough to derive everything.

If you have N identical nuclei, the expected number decaying in a short interval dt is λN·dt. So the population shrinks at a rate proportional to its own size:

dN/dt = −λ NThe rate of loss is proportional to how many nuclei remain — the signature of exponential behavior.

This is the same differential equation that governs cooling, capacitor discharge, and drug clearance. Separating variables and integrating from N0 at t = 0 gives the radioactive decay law:

N(t) = N0 · e^(−λ t)N0 is the initial count; after a time t the surviving fraction is e^(−λt).

Notice what this does not say: it does not predict which atom decays or when a given atom decays — that is pure chance. It predicts the statistical behavior of the ensemble with extraordinary precision, which is exactly what you need for radiometric dating and reactor physics.

2.3Half-Life, Mean Life & Activity

The decay constant λ is fundamental, but two more intuitive timescales come straight out of the same exponential. The half-life T½ is the time for the population to fall to half. Set N = N0/2:

N0/2 = N0 e^(−λ T½) -> T½ = ln 2 / λ ≈ 0.693 / λHalf-life and decay constant are inverse partners; a large λ (fast decay) means a short half-life.

The mean life τ is the average lifetime of a nucleus — the time at which the population falls to 1/e of its start. Integrating, τ = 1/λ, so:

τ = 1/λ = T½ / ln 2 ≈ 1.443 · T½The mean life is always a bit longer than the half-life because the slow-to-decay survivors pull the average up.

What a detector actually measures is not N but the activity A — the number of decays per second. Since each decay removes one nucleus, A is just the magnitude of dN/dt:

A = λ N = A0 e^(−λ t)Activity decays with the same exponential and the same half-life as the population itself.

Activity has its own SI unit, the becquerel (Bq) = 1 decay per second. The older unit is the curie (Ci), defined as exactly 3.7×10¹⁰ Bq — roughly the activity of one gram of radium-226. A medical tracer dose is a few hundred megabecquerels; a reactor core, many terabecquerels.

Common mistakeActivity is not the number of radioactive atoms — it is the rate at which they decay. A sample with a huge number of very long-lived atoms (tiny λ) can have a lower activity than a tiny sample of short-lived atoms (large λ). Always use A = λN: a long half-life means small λ, hence small activity per atom. Confusing "how much is there" with "how fast it's decaying" is the classic error.

2.4Decay Chains & Secular Equilibrium

Most heavy radionuclides do not decay to a stable nucleus in one step. They form a decay chain: a parent decays to a radioactive daughter, which decays again, and so on down to a stable end. Uranium-238, for instance, runs through fourteen steps — including radium-226 and the gas radon-222 — before finally resting as lead-206.

For a parent (constant λ₁) feeding a daughter (λ₂), the daughter's population obeys a balance: it is created by the parent's decays and removed by its own:

dN2/dt = λ1 N1 − λ2 N2Daughter gain (parent decays) minus daughter loss (its own decays).

A special, very useful case arises when the parent is far longer-lived than the daughter (λ₁ ≪ λ₂, i.e. T½ of the parent ≫ T½ of the daughter). After a few daughter half-lives the daughter's population stops changing — production equals decay — and the system reaches secular equilibrium:

λ1 N1 = λ2 N2 -> A1 = A2In secular equilibrium every link in the chain has the same activity as the parent.

Secular equilibrium is why an old uranium ore sample shows nearly equal activities of uranium-238 and its radon daughter, and it underpins radon monitoring, radium dating, and the calibration of long-lived reference sources.

✦ Worked Example — activity of a cobalt-60 source

A sealed cobalt-60 source contains 1.0×10¹⁸ atoms. Co-60 (a β⁻/γ emitter used in radiotherapy and sterilization) has a half-life of 5.27 years. Find (a) its decay constant, (b) its current activity in Bq and Ci, and (c) the activity remaining after 10.5 years.

  1. Convert the half-life to seconds: T½ = 5.27 yr × 3.156×10⁷ s/yr ≈ 1.663×10⁸ s.
  2. Decay constant: λ = ln 2 / T½ = 0.693 / 1.663×10⁸ s ≈ 4.17×10⁻⁹ s⁻¹.
  3. Activity now: A = λN = (4.17×10⁻⁹)(1.0×10¹⁸) ≈ 4.17×10⁹ Bq.
  4. In curies: A = 4.17×10⁹ / 3.7×10¹⁰ ≈ 0.11 Ci — a modest research-grade source.
  5. After 10.5 yr: that is almost exactly two half-lives (10.5 / 5.27 ≈ 1.99), so A ≈ A0 / 2² = 4.17×10⁹ / 4 ≈ 1.04×10⁹ Bq. The source has lost three-quarters of its punch — which is why irradiation facilities must periodically reload their sources.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate decay in Python

This course runs entirely on a free stack: Python + Jupyter + NumPy for analysis and OpenMC (an open-source Monte Carlo transport code) for full neutron/photon simulations in later weeks. Week 2 setup: install once below, then reuse it the whole course.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy + Jupyter + OpenMC (one-time setup)

The cleanest path uses conda, which pulls in OpenMC's compiled dependencies for you:

conda create -n nuclear python=3.11
conda activate nuclear
conda install -c conda-forge openmc numpy matplotlib jupyter

Prefer pip? NumPy/Jupyter install directly; OpenMC's Python API is on PyPI:

python3 -m venv nuclear-env
source nuclear-env/bin/activate     # macOS / Linux
pip install numpy matplotlib jupyter openmc

Verify: run python -c "import numpy, openmc; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

Now compare the smooth analytic decay law against a stochastic, atom-by-atom Monte Carlo decay — the same statistical idea OpenMC uses at scale. Watch how the random simulation tracks the exponential ever more tightly as N0 grows.

# decay_law.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

T_half = 5.27 * 3.156e7        # Co-60 half-life in seconds
lam    = np.log(2) / T_half    # decay constant lambda (1/s)
N0     = 100_000               # initial number of atoms

# --- analytic decay law ---
t  = np.linspace(0, 3 * T_half, 50)
N_analytic = N0 * np.exp(-lam * t)

# --- stochastic Monte Carlo (each atom flips a biased coin per step) ---
rng   = np.random.default_rng(0)
dt    = t[1] - t[0]
p_dec = 1 - np.exp(-lam * dt)  # P(decay in one step)
N     = N0
counts = [N0]
for _ in t[1:]:
    N -= rng.binomial(N, p_dec) # remove the decayers
    counts.append(N)

print(f"lambda    = {lam:.3e} 1/s")
print(f"activity0 = {lam * N0:.3e} Bq")
print(f"after 1 T_half: analytic={N0*0.5:.0f}, simulated={counts[len(t)//3]}")

The simulated curve wobbles around the analytic line by roughly √N each step — the statistical noise that real detectors see as count-rate fluctuation. Try N0 = 100 versus 10 million and watch the relative scatter shrink.

🔗 Where this is heading

The decay law you just derived is the backbone of the whole specialization. In Week 3 we attach energy to these decays via Q-values and reaction kinematics. In reactor physics it reappears as the Bateman equations governing fission-product inventories and the decay heat that must be cooled long after shutdown. In radiometric dating, λ turns a measured isotope ratio into an absolute age. And the Monte Carlo sampling you wrote by hand is exactly the engine inside OpenMC, which we will use later to track neutron populations through real geometries. One constant, λ, threads through dating, medicine, reactor safety, and astrophysical nucleosynthesis.

Q1. In beta-minus (β⁻) decay, what happens to the atomic number Z and mass number A?
A. Z increases by 1, A unchanged
B. Z decreases by 1, A unchanged
C. Z decreases by 2, A decreases by 4
D. Z and A both unchanged
Show answer

A. A neutron becomes a proton (emitting an electron and antineutrino), so Z rises by 1 while A is unchanged. B describes β⁺/electron capture; C describes alpha decay; D describes gamma emission.

Q2. A radionuclide has a half-life of 8 days. After 24 days, what fraction of the original atoms remains?
A. 1/2
B. 1/3
C. 1/8
D. 0 (all decayed)
Show answer

C. 24 days = 3 half-lives, so the fraction is (1/2)³ = 1/8. A is one half-life; B confuses elapsed half-lives with a fraction; D is wrong because exponential decay never reaches exactly zero.

Q3. Which relationship correctly connects the decay constant λ, half-life T½, and mean life τ?
A. τ = T½ and both equal 1/λ
B. T½ = λ·ln 2 and τ = λ
C. T½ = ln 2 / λ and τ = 1/λ, so τ ≈ 1.44·T½
D. T½ = 1/λ and τ = ln 2 / λ
Show answer

C. Setting N = N0/2 gives T½ = ln 2/λ, and the average lifetime is τ = 1/λ, so τ = T½/ln 2 ≈ 1.44·T½. A wrongly equates τ and T½; B and D invert or scramble the relations.

Q4. Sample X has 10¹⁵ atoms of a nuclide with half-life 1 hour. Sample Y has 10¹⁵ atoms of a nuclide with half-life 1000 years. Which has the greater activity right now?
A. Sample Y, because long-lived nuclei are more energetic
B. Sample X, because a shorter half-life means a larger λ
C. They are equal, since both have the same number of atoms
D. Cannot be determined without the decay energy
Show answer

B. Activity A = λN, and λ = ln 2/T½, so the shorter half-life (X) gives a far larger λ and hence far larger activity for the same N. C ignores λ; A and D invoke energy, which does not enter A = λN.

Q5. A long-lived parent feeds a much shorter-lived daughter in a decay chain. After many daughter half-lives the system reaches secular equilibrium. What is true of the activities?
A. The daughter's activity is zero
B. The parent's activity is twice the daughter's
C. The activities oscillate forever
D. The daughter's activity equals the parent's (A1 = A2)
Show answer

D. In secular equilibrium production equals decay: λ1N1 = λ2N2, i.e. A1 = A2. A and B contradict that balance; C is wrong because the system settles to a steady state, it does not oscillate.
