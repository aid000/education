The Fission Chain Reaction & Criticality

One neutron splits a nucleus and out come two or three more — whether that cascade fizzles, holds steady, or runs away is decided by a single number near 1.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain how a self-sustaining fission chain reaction works in terms of neutron birth and loss.
  • Define the multiplication factor k and classify a system as subcritical, critical, or supercritical.
  • Read the four- and six-factor formula as a budget of where neutrons go between generations.
  • Reason about critical mass and why geometry, density, and reflectors change it.
  • Explain why delayed neutrons are the reason a power reactor can be controlled at all.

7.1What Makes a Chain Reaction Self-Sustaining

In Lecture 6 you saw that when a slow neutron is absorbed by a U-235 nucleus, the nucleus splits, releasing ~200 MeV and — crucially — emitting on average about 2.4 fresh neutrons. That last fact is the whole game. If even one of those new neutrons goes on to cause another fission, the reaction perpetuates itself without any further outside push. This is a chain reaction.

But emitting neutrons is not the same as using them. Every neutron born in one fission faces a competition before it can trigger the next one. It can:

  • Leak out of the material entirely (escape through the surface).
  • Be absorbed without fissioning — captured by U-238, structural metal, coolant, or control material.
  • Cause a new fission — the only outcome that keeps the chain alive.

So the chain reaction is fundamentally a bookkeeping problem about neutrons: of all the neutrons born in one "generation," how many survive the gauntlet to be born again in the next? That ratio is the single most important quantity in all of reactor physics.

AnalogyPicture a room full of mousetraps, each loaded with two ping-pong balls. Set off one trap and its two balls fly out. If on average more than one ball lands on and triggers another trap, the whole room erupts; if fewer than one does, the popping dies away; if exactly one does, the popping continues at a steady rate. The chain reaction is identical — neutrons are the ping-pong balls, fissions are the traps.

7.2The Multiplication Factor k

We make the mousetrap idea quantitative with the effective multiplication factor, written k (or keff):

k = (neutrons produced in one generation) / (neutrons in the previous generation)The ratio of one fission generation's neutron population to the one before it.

Equivalently, k = (rate of neutron production) / (rate of neutron loss to absorption + leakage). The value of k relative to 1 defines three regimes:

  • k < 1 — subcritical: each generation is smaller than the last. The chain dies out. (A shut-down reactor, or a fresh fuel pellet sitting on a bench.)
  • k = 1 — critical: each generation exactly replaces itself. The neutron population — and the power — holds steady. This is the normal operating state of a reactor.
  • k > 1 — supercritical: each generation is larger. The population grows exponentially. A controlled reactor goes briefly supercritical to raise power, then returns to k = 1.

Because each generation multiplies the population by k, after n generations starting from N0 neutrons you have:

N(n) = N0 * k^nThe population grows or shrinks geometrically. Even tiny departures of k from 1 compound fast.

This exponential sensitivity is why "near 1" is not good enough — control means holding k to within a fraction of a percent of 1. We also define reactivity rho = (k - 1)/k, a handy measure of how far from critical the system sits; rho = 0 is exactly critical.

7.3The Four- and Six-Factor Formula

Where does k actually come from? We can break the journey of one generation of neutrons into a chain of independent "survival probabilities" and "multipliers." For an idealized infinite medium (no leakage), the multiplication factor is the product of four factors:

k_inf = eta * f * p * epsilonThe four-factor formula: each factor is a yield or a survival fraction for one neutron generation.

Reading them in the order a neutron lives its life (born fast, slowing down, then absorbed):

  • epsilon — fast fission factor: a small bonus of extra fissions caused by fast neutrons in U-238 before they slow down (epsilon slightly > 1).
  • p — resonance escape probability: the fraction of neutrons that slow down past U-238's hungry capture resonances without being swallowed.
  • f — thermal utilization factor: of the neutrons that reach thermal energy and get absorbed, the fraction absorbed in fuel rather than in coolant, cladding, or moderator.
  • eta — reproduction factor: the average number of new fission neutrons produced per neutron absorbed in the fuel.

Real reactors are finite, so neutrons also leak out the edges. The six-factor formula adds two leakage survival probabilities:

k_eff = eta * f * p * epsilon * P_FNL * P_TNLP_FNL = fast non-leakage probability, P_TNL = thermal non-leakage probability. Both are < 1.

The power of this formula is that it turns one abstract number into a design checklist: enrich the fuel to raise eta and f; choose a good moderator to raise p; make the core large and compact (low surface-to-volume) to push the non-leakage factors toward 1. Every knob a reactor engineer turns shows up in one of these six factors.

7.4Critical Mass & Delayed Neutrons

Two of the six factors — the leakage probabilities — depend on size and shape. A tiny fuel sample loses almost every neutron through its surface before it can cause a fission, so k < 1 no matter how pure the fuel. Grow the sample and the volume (where fissions happen) rises faster than the surface (where neutrons leak). At some size leakage falls enough that k reaches exactly 1.

critical mass = the minimum mass for which k = 1Below it the assembly is subcritical; at it, a steady chain reaction is just barely self-sustaining.

Critical mass is not a fixed property of an element — it depends on everything in the six-factor budget. It drops if you: increase enrichment, increase density (compression), use a more compact shape (a sphere minimizes surface area), or surround the fuel with a neutron reflector that bounces escaping neutrons back in. These are exactly the levers that distinguish a reactor core from a fizzle.

Common mistakeCritical mass is not a single number for "uranium." Students quote one figure as if it were a constant of nature. In reality the critical mass of the same material can change by large factors with geometry, density, and a reflector — bare versus reflected metal can differ by roughly a factor of two. Always ask "critical mass under what conditions?"

Why a reactor isn't a runaway

Here is the puzzle. A fission generation lasts only about 10-4 to 10-3 seconds (the time a neutron takes to be born, slow down, and cause the next fission). If k were even slightly above 1 and that were the whole story, the power would double thousands of times per second — far too fast for any mechanical control rod to respond. No reactor could ever be steered.

The rescue is delayed neutrons. About 0.65% of U-235's fission neutrons are not emitted instantly. They come out seconds to minutes later from the radioactive decay of certain fission fragments (the "delayed neutron precursors"). This fraction is called beta.

if (k - 1) < beta -> reactor is only "delayed critical"The chain cannot sustain itself on prompt neutrons alone; it must wait on the slow delayed ones, which sets the timescale.

When a reactor is critical with the help of these delayed neutrons (delayed critical), the effective generation time becomes seconds, not milliseconds. That sluggishness is exactly what gives control rods and operators time to act. The danger boundary to never cross is prompt critical — when k - 1 reaches beta and the chain can sustain itself on prompt neutrons alone, the slow delayed neutrons no longer set the pace, and the power excursion becomes essentially instantaneous.

AnalogyImagine pushing a heavy cart that responds to your shove only after a 10-second delay. Annoying, but you can steer it — nudge, wait, observe, correct. Now imagine it responds in a millisecond: the slightest twitch sends it into a wall. Delayed neutrons add that built-in lag to the reactor, turning an impossibly twitchy system into one a human (and a control system) can comfortably drive.
✦ Worked Example — power growth and the role of delayed neutrons

A reactor is taken slightly supercritical to k = 1.001. Take the prompt-only generation time as ℓ = 10-4 s, and the delayed-neutron fraction beta = 0.0065. (a) If only prompt neutrons mattered, how fast would the population grow? (b) Why does the delayed fraction make this safe?

  1. Excess multiplication per generation: k - 1 = 0.001, so each generation grows the population by 0.1%.
  2. Generations per second (prompt only): with ℓ = 10-4 s there are 1/ℓ = 104 generations every second.
  3. Prompt-only growth: N(t) = N0 * k^(t/ℓ) = N0 * (1.001)^(10000 t). After just 1 s the factor is 1.001^10000 ≈ e^(10) ≈ 2.2 × 104. A 22,000-fold jump per second — uncontrollable.
  4. The delayed rescue: because k - 1 = 0.001 is well below beta = 0.0065, the reactor is only delayed critical. The chain cannot run on prompt neutrons alone; it must wait on precursor decays (effective generation time of order seconds).
  5. Realistic growth: the effective response is set by the much longer delayed timescale, so the power rises gently over many seconds — slow enough for control rods to track. This is the entire reason a power reactor can be operated at all.

Lesson: never let k - 1 approach beta. Staying below "prompt critical" is the bright line of reactor safety.

🛠️ Hands-On: a point-kinetics chain in Python

We continue with the free course stack — Python + Jupyter + NumPy, plus OpenMC for full Monte Carlo criticality later. The full install lives in the Week 1–2 setup notes; nothing new is needed here. Below we simulate a neutron population with a prompt-only model versus one including a delayed group, to see why delayed neutrons tame the response.

# chain_kinetics.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np

k        = 1.001        # multiplication factor (slightly supercritical)
ell      = 1e-4         # prompt generation time [s]
beta     = 0.0065       # delayed neutron fraction
lam      = 0.08         # effective precursor decay const [1/s]

# ---- prompt-only model: N grows by k each generation ----
gens = np.arange(0, 10000)          # 1 second of prompt generations
N_prompt = k ** gens
print("prompt-only growth after 1 s:", f"{N_prompt[-1]:.3e}")   # ~2e4 x

# ---- point kinetics with one delayed group (Euler integration) ----
rho = (k - 1) / k                   # reactivity
N, C = 1.0, beta / (lam * ell)      # neutron pop, precursor pop
dt, T = 1e-4, 5.0
hist = []
for _ in range(int(T / dt)):
    dN = ((rho - beta) / ell) * N + lam * C
    dC = (beta / ell) * N - lam * C
    N += dN * dt;  C += dC * dt
    hist.append(N)

print("delayed model growth after 5 s:", f"{hist[-1]:.3e}")     # gentle, controllable

Run it: the prompt-only model explodes by ~104 in one second, while the point-kinetics model with delayed neutrons rises slowly over seconds. Now sweep k upward toward 1 + beta and watch the delayed model's response sharpen dramatically as you approach prompt critical.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A reactor operating at steady, constant power has a multiplication factor of approximately…
A. k = 0
B. k = 1
C. k = 2.4
D. k much greater than 1
Show answer

B. Steady power means each generation exactly replaces the last — k = 1 (critical). k = 2.4 (C) is the raw neutrons emitted per fission, not the survival ratio. k > 1 (D) means rising power; k = 0 (A) would mean no neutrons at all.

Q2. Which outcome for a fission neutron keeps the chain reaction alive?
A. leaking out through the core surface
B. being captured by U-238 without fissioning
C. being absorbed in a control rod
D. being absorbed in fuel and causing another fission
Show answer

D. Only a neutron that induces a new fission produces the next generation. Leakage (A), parasitic capture in U-238 (B), and absorption in control material (C) all remove the neutron from the chain.

Q3. In the four-factor formula k_inf = eta·f·p·epsilon, the factor p (resonance escape probability) represents…
A. the fraction of neutrons that slow past U-238's capture resonances without being absorbed
B. the number of neutrons produced per fission
C. the probability a neutron leaks out of the core
D. the fraction of thermal absorptions that occur in the fuel
Show answer

A. p is the chance a slowing-down neutron escapes U-238's resonance capture. Neutrons per fission relates to eta/nu (B); leakage is the non-leakage factors of the six-factor form (C); thermal-in-fuel is the utilization factor f (D).

Q4. Which change would decrease the critical mass of a fuel assembly?
A. spreading the fuel into a thin flat sheet
B. surrounding it with a neutron-reflecting shell
C. lowering its density
D. lowering the enrichment
Show answer

B. A reflector bounces escaping neutrons back in, cutting leakage and lowering the critical mass. A thin sheet (A) raises surface-to-volume and leakage; lower density (C) and lower enrichment (D) both make the chain harder to sustain, raising critical mass.

Q5. Delayed neutrons are essential to reactor control because they…
A. carry away most of the fission energy
B. increase the total number of neutrons per fission tenfold
C. stretch the effective neutron generation time from milliseconds to seconds
D. make the reactor prompt critical
Show answer

C. Emitted seconds-to-minutes after fission, delayed neutrons lengthen the effective generation time so power changes slowly enough to control. They are a tiny ~0.65% of neutrons, not a tenfold boost (B), carry negligible energy (A), and being below prompt critical — not at it (D) — is precisely the safe regime they enable.

✓ What you learned

  • A chain reaction sustains itself when at least one neutron from each fission goes on to cause the next — a neutron bookkeeping problem.
  • The multiplication factor k sorts systems into subcritical (k<1), critical (k=1), and supercritical (k>1), with population growing as N0·kn.
  • The four/six-factor formula decomposes k into yields and survival fractions — a design checklist for enrichment, moderation, and leakage.
  • Critical mass is the minimum mass for k=1, and it shrinks with higher density, compact shape, enrichment, and reflectors.
  • Delayed neutrons (~0.65%) stretch the generation time to seconds, which is the only reason a reactor is controllable; crossing into prompt critical is the danger line.
