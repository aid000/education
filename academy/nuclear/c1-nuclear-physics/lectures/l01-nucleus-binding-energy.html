Week 1 · Lecture 1

The Atomic Nucleus & Binding Energy

A teaspoon of atomic nuclei would weigh a billion tonnes — and the missing mass when they assemble is the energy that lights the stars and powers reactors.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the nucleus in terms of protons and neutrons, and read nuclide notation (A, Z, N).
  • Explain how the strong nuclear force holds the nucleus together against electrostatic repulsion.
  • Compute mass defect and convert it to binding energy with E = mc².
  • Interpret the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve and explain why iron-56 sits at its peak.
  • Use the curve to argue why fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei both release energy.

1.1Inside the Nucleus: Protons, Neutrons & Nuclide Notation

Nearly all of an atom's mass — but only about a hundred-thousandth of its diameter — lives in the nucleus, a dense knot of protons (charge +1) and neutrons (charge 0). Together protons and neutrons are called nucleons. The electrons that fill the rest of the atom barely register on the scale: a proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron.

We label a specific nuclear species — a nuclide — by three integers. The atomic number Z counts the protons (and fixes which chemical element it is). The neutron number N counts the neutrons. Their sum is the mass number A:

A = Z + NA = nucleons (protons + neutrons), Z = proton count (the element), N = neutron count.

The standard shorthand places A as a superscript and Z as a subscript before the element symbol — for example carbon-12 is written as ¹²₆C (A = 12, Z = 6, so N = 6). Nuclei with the same Z but different N are isotopes: carbon-12, carbon-13, and carbon-14 are all carbon (Z = 6) but carry 6, 7, and 8 neutrons respectively. Isotopes are chemically near-identical yet can behave completely differently in a reactor — uranium-235 fissions readily while uranium-238 mostly does not, even though both are "uranium."

AnalogyThink of nucleons as tiny, sticky marbles packed into a drop of liquid. The drop's size grows with the number of marbles, but its density stays almost constant — exactly what we see for nuclei, whose radius follows R ≈ 1.2 fm × A1/3. This "liquid-drop" picture, due to George Gamow and refined by others, is the first model we will quantify in Week 3.

1.2The Strong Nuclear Force: What Holds It Together

Here is a paradox. Protons all carry positive charge, and the electrostatic (Coulomb) repulsion between two protons just 2 fm apart is enormous — tens of newtons between two specks you cannot see. By that logic every nucleus should instantly blow itself apart. It does not. Something stronger must be pulling the nucleons together.

That something is the strong nuclear force. It has two defining features that explain the entire architecture of the nucleus:

It is short-ranged. Unlike the Coulomb force, which reaches across the whole atom, the strong force essentially vanishes beyond about 2–3 fm — roughly the width of a nucleon or two. A nucleon only feels its immediate neighbors, not the whole nucleus.

It is charge-blind and attractive. Within its short range it pulls proton-proton, proton-neutron, and neutron-neutron together with nearly equal strength, far overpowering Coulomb repulsion at those distances.

strong force: attractive, ~ 100x Coulomb, range ~ 2-3 fmDominant at nucleon separations; negligible at atomic distances.

These two facts immediately explain why heavy nuclei need extra neutrons. Every proton repels every other proton across the whole nucleus (long-range Coulomb), but the strong "glue" only acts between neighbors. As Z grows, repulsion piles up faster than gluing, so nature adds neutrons — which feel the strong attraction but add no charge — to dilute the repulsion. That is why light stable nuclei have N ≈ Z, while heavy stable nuclei like lead-208 carry far more neutrons (N = 126) than protons (Z = 82).

1.3Mass Defect & Binding Energy: E = mc²

Now the central, almost unbelievable fact. Weigh a helium-4 nucleus, then weigh its 2 protons and 2 neutrons separately and add them up. The assembled nucleus is lighter than its parts. Mass went missing. That missing mass is the mass defect:

Delta m = [ Z * m_p + N * m_n ] - m_nucleusSum of free-nucleon masses minus the mass of the bound nucleus. Always positive for a bound nucleus.

The missing mass did not disappear — it was converted to energy and radiated away when the nucleus formed. To pull the nucleus back apart into free nucleons, you must put that energy back in. That energy is the binding energy, and Einstein's mass-energy relation tells us exactly how much:

E_b = (Delta m) c^2c = 3.00e8 m/s. A handy unit conversion: 1 atomic mass unit (u) of mass equals 931.5 MeV of energy.

The binding energy is the energy "owed" to the nucleus — the deep potential well the nucleons have fallen into. A larger binding energy means a more tightly bound, more stable nucleus. Because c² is a colossal number, even a sliver of mass defect translates into a staggering energy. This single equation is why nuclear processes release roughly a million times more energy per reaction than chemical burning, which only rearranges electrons.

Common mistakeDo not compare the binding energy of one nucleus to another directly — a uranium nucleus has a larger total binding energy than a helium nucleus simply because it has far more nucleons. Stability is governed by binding energy per nucleon (E_b / A), not total binding energy. Forgetting to divide by A is the number-one error students make when reasoning about which reactions release energy.

1.4The Binding-Energy-per-Nucleon Curve

If we compute E_b / A for every nuclide and plot it against mass number A, we get one of the most important graphs in all of physics. It rises steeply for the lightest nuclei, climbs to a broad peak around iron-56 at about 8.8 MeV per nucleon, then declines gently for the heaviest nuclei down toward roughly 7.6 MeV per nucleon at uranium.

The peak at iron has a clean physical reason. Bulk attraction from the strong force grows with the number of neighbors, favoring bigger nuclei — but only neighbors count, so this benefit saturates. Meanwhile surface nucleons have fewer neighbors (less binding), and Coulomb repulsion between protons grows with Z² and reaches across the entire nucleus, penalizing big nuclei more and more. The tug-of-war between short-range gluing and long-range repulsion is optimized near A ≈ 56. Iron-56 (and its neighbors nickel-62 and iron-58) sit at the bottom of the energy valley — the most tightly bound, most stable matter there is.

Why the curve makes BOTH fission and fusion release energy

This is the punchline of the whole lecture. Energy is released whenever a reaction produces nuclei that are more tightly bound — higher on the E_b/A curve — than the starting material. The released energy equals the gain in total binding energy.

Fusion (light nuclei): two light nuclei on the steep left slope (e.g. hydrogen isotopes) merge into a heavier nucleus higher up the curve. The product is more tightly bound per nucleon, so energy pours out. This is how the Sun shines and what fusion reactors aim to harness.

Fission (heavy nuclei): a heavy nucleus past the peak (e.g. uranium-235) splits into two mid-mass fragments that lie higher on the curve. Again binding energy per nucleon increases, and energy is released. This powers every fission reactor on Earth.

AnalogyPicture the curve as a valley with iron-56 at the bottom. Every nucleus is a ball on the slope. Light nuclei sit on the high left wall; heavy nuclei sit partway up the gentler right wall. Any process that rolls a nucleus downhill toward iron — fusing up the left side or splitting down the right side — releases the energy difference, exactly like a ball giving up gravitational potential energy as it rolls down. You cannot extract energy past iron: that is the bottom of the valley.
✦ Worked Example — binding energy per nucleon of iron-56

Iron-56 has Z = 26 protons and N = 30 neutrons. Using m_p = 1.007825 u (hydrogen atom, which conveniently bundles the proton + electron mass), m_n = 1.008665 u, and the measured atomic mass of Fe-56 = 55.934936 u, find the binding energy per nucleon.

  1. Add the free constituents: 26 × 1.007825 + 30 × 1.008665 = 26.20345 + 30.25995 = 56.46340 u. (Using the hydrogen-atom mass for protons automatically includes the 26 electrons, matching the atomic mass of Fe-56.)
  2. Mass defect: Delta m = 56.46340 − 55.934936 = 0.528464 u.
  3. Convert to energy: E_b = 0.528464 u × 931.5 MeV/u ≈ 492.3 MeV total.
  4. Per nucleon: E_b / A = 492.3 MeV ÷ 56 ≈ 8.79 MeV per nucleon.
  5. Sanity check: 8.79 MeV/nucleon sits right at the top of the curve — exactly where iron-56 should be. Compare helium-4 at 7.07 MeV/nucleon: iron is more tightly bound, which is why fusion toward iron releases energy while nothing past iron does.

Let's turn the physics of this lecture into code: compute binding energy per nucleon from masses and reproduce the iron-56 peak.

# binding_energy.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

U_TO_MEV = 931.5            # 1 atomic mass unit -> MeV
M_H = 1.007825              # hydrogen atom (proton + electron), u
M_N = 1.008665              # neutron, u

def binding_per_nucleon(Z, N, atomic_mass_u):
    A = Z + N
    mass_defect = (Z * M_H + N * M_N) - atomic_mass_u   # in u
    Eb = mass_defect * U_TO_MEV                          # total, MeV
    return Eb / A                                        # MeV per nucleon

# (Z, N, atomic mass in u)
nuclides = {
    "He-4":  (2, 2,   4.002602),
    "Fe-56": (26, 30, 55.934936),
    "U-238": (92, 146, 238.050788),
}
for name, (Z, N, m) in nuclides.items():
    print(f"{name}: {binding_per_nucleon(Z, N, m):.2f} MeV/nucleon")

# He-4: 7.07   Fe-56: 8.79   U-238: 7.57
# Fe-56 is the most tightly bound -> the peak of the curve.

Notice U-238 (7.57) and He-4 (7.07) both fall below Fe-56 (8.79) — the very gap that fission and fusion exploit. Try adding deuterium (Z=1, N=1, 2.014102 u) to see how low the lightest nuclei start.

Q1. A nuclide is written as ⁵⁶₂₆Fe. How many neutrons does it contain?
A. 26
B. 56
C. 30
D. 82
Show answer

C. N = A − Z = 56 − 26 = 30. (A) is the proton count Z, (B) is the mass number A, and (D) = A + Z, which has no physical meaning here.

Q2. Why does the nucleus not fly apart despite the Coulomb repulsion between its protons?
A. Neutrons are negatively charged and cancel the protons
B. The short-range strong nuclear force attracts nucleons more strongly than Coulomb repulsion at nuclear distances
C. Gravity between nucleons holds it together
D. Electrons orbiting outside pull the protons inward
Show answer

B. The strong force is attractive, charge-blind, and far stronger than Coulomb within ~2–3 fm. (A) is false — neutrons are neutral. (C) Gravity between nucleons is ~10³⁶ times too weak. (D) Electron attraction is irrelevant at nuclear scale.

Q3. The mass of a bound nucleus compared with the summed mass of its free protons and neutrons is…
A. exactly equal
B. unrelated to binding energy
C. greater, because binding adds energy and therefore mass
D. less, by an amount equal to the binding energy divided by c²
Show answer

D. The bound nucleus is lighter by the mass defect Delta m = E_b / c². Energy was released when it formed. (C) reverses the sign; (A) and (B) ignore the mass defect entirely.

Q4. On the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve, which nucleus sits closest to the peak (most tightly bound)?
A. Iron-56
B. Hydrogen-1
C. Uranium-238
D. Helium-4
Show answer

A. Iron-56 sits at the peak near 8.8 MeV/nucleon — the optimum balance of strong-force gluing and Coulomb repulsion. (B) Hydrogen-1 has no binding (one nucleon). (C) U-238 (~7.6) and (D) He-4 (~7.1) both lie below the peak.

Q5. Why do BOTH fission of heavy nuclei and fusion of light nuclei release energy?
A. Both increase the total number of nucleons
B. Both move products to a higher binding energy per nucleon, closer to the iron-56 peak
C. Both destroy protons, converting them entirely to energy
D. Both require an external energy source and release none
Show answer

B. Energy is released when products are more tightly bound per nucleon (higher on the curve, toward iron). Fusion climbs the left slope; fission slides down the right slope — both toward the peak. (A) nucleon number is conserved; (C) protons are not annihilated; (D) is backwards — these reactions are net energy-releasing.
