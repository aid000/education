Week 4 · Lecture 4

Cross-Sections & Reaction Rates

A nucleus is a target almost too small to imagine — yet the "barn" it presents to a passing neutron decides whether a reactor runs or stalls.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the microscopic cross-section σ as an effective target area, and use the barn unit.
  • Build the macroscopic cross-section Σ = Nσ from atom density and explain its units (1/cm).
  • Compute a reaction rate R = Σφ from flux and relate it to power in a reactor.
  • Derive the mean free path λ = 1/Σ and interpret it as the average distance between interactions.
  • Explain the energy dependence of cross-sections — the 1/v law and resonance peaks — and why it shapes reactor design.

4.1The Microscopic Cross-Section

In Lecture 3 you balanced reaction energetics with Q-values. But knowing a reaction can happen says nothing about how likely it is. That likelihood is captured by the cross-section σ — the single most important number in all of reactor physics.

Imagine firing a beam of neutrons at a thin foil. Each nucleus behaves as if it presents a tiny effective area to the incoming neutron: hit that area and the reaction occurs; miss it and the neutron sails through untouched. The cross-section σ is that effective area. It is not the geometric size of the nucleus — it can be far larger or far smaller — but it has the units of an area.

σ = (reactions per nucleus per second) / (incident flux)An effective target area per nucleus, measured in cm². A bigger σ means a more probable interaction.

Nuclear areas are absurdly small, so physicists invented a friendlier unit, the barn:

1 barn = 10^-24 cm²Roughly the geometric cross-section of a heavy nucleus. The name is a wartime joke: to a neutron, such a target was "as big as a barn."

Each reaction channel has its own cross-section: σa for absorption, σs for scattering, σf for fission, and the total σt = σs + σa for any interaction at all. A uranium-235 nucleus, for example, has a thermal-neutron fission cross-section near 585 barns — enormous, which is exactly why it sustains a chain reaction.

AnalogyPicture throwing darts blindfolded at a wall studded with coins. The chance of hitting a coin depends on the coin's area, not on how hard you throw. The cross-section is each nucleus's "coin area" — but a quantum coin whose size depends on the neutron's energy, sometimes ballooning to hundreds of times the nucleus's physical face.

4.2The Macroscopic Cross-Section & Mean Free Path

A single nucleus is one target; real matter packs in a staggering number of them. Multiply the microscopic cross-section by the atom number density N (nuclei per cm³) and you get the macroscopic cross-section Σ:

Σ = N σUnits: (nuclei/cm³) x (cm²) = 1/cm. It is the total target area packed into one cm³ of material, i.e. the probability of interaction per unit path length.

Read Σ as a probability of interaction per centimeter of travel. If Σt = 0.5 /cm, a neutron has on average half a chance of interacting in each centimeter it crosses. The number density comes from the material's mass density ρ and atomic mass M via Avogadro's number:

N = ρ N_A / MN_A = 6.022 x 10^23 atoms/mol. So denser, lighter-atom materials pack more targets per cm³.

Now follow a single neutron. The probability it travels a distance x without interacting falls off exponentially, just like light through an absorbing medium — this is the attenuation law:

I(x) = I_0 e^(-Σ_t x)The surviving (uncollided) beam decays exponentially with the macroscopic cross-section as the decay constant.

The average distance a neutron travels between interactions is the mean free path λ, which is simply the reciprocal of Σ:

λ = 1 / ΣIf Σ = 0.5 /cm then λ = 2 cm: the neutron travels 2 cm on average before its next collision.
Common mistakeDo not confuse σ (microscopic, units cm², per nucleus) with Σ (macroscopic, units 1/cm, per cm of material). They differ by the atom density N, which can span many orders of magnitude. Plugging a barn value straight into λ = 1/Σ without first multiplying by N gives a mean free path off by a factor of ~1023.

4.3Neutron Flux & the Reaction Rate

So far we tracked one neutron. A reactor core holds a swarm of them moving in all directions. We describe that swarm with the neutron flux φ:

φ = n vn = neutron density (neutrons/cm³), v = their speed (cm/s). Units: neutrons/(cm² s) — the total track length neutrons sweep per cm³ per second.

Flux is best understood not as a directed current but as total path length traveled per unit volume per unit time. The more track length the neutrons lay down inside a material, the more chances they have to find a nucleus. Combining flux with the per-length interaction probability Σ gives the reaction rate density R — reactions per cm³ per second:

R = Σ φ(1/cm) x (neutrons/cm²/s) = reactions/(cm³ s). Use Σ_f for fission rate, Σ_a for absorption rate, etc.

This deceptively simple product is the engine of reactor physics. The fission rate Rf = Σfφ sets the reactor's power: each fission releases about 200 MeV ≈ 3.2 x 10-11 J of recoverable energy, so the power density is just Rf times that energy per fission. Raise the flux and you raise the power, linearly.

AnalogyThink of mowing a lawn. The flux φ is the total distance your mower travels each second; Σ is how densely the lawn is seeded with dandelions per meter mowed. The dandelions cut per second = (path length per second) x (dandelions per length) = Σφ. More mowing or more dandelions both raise the harvest rate.

4.4Energy Dependence: 1/v & Resonances

The cross-section is not a fixed number — it depends strongly on the neutron's energy (or equivalently its speed v). Three regimes dominate the absorption cross-section of most heavy nuclei:

1. The 1/v region (low energy). At thermal and lower energies, many absorption cross-sections rise as the neutron slows down:

σ_a(v) ∝ 1/vA slower neutron spends more time near a nucleus, so the capture probability grows. This is why "thermal" reactors moderate neutrons down to ~0.025 eV.

2. The resonance region (intermediate energy). At specific energies the neutron-plus-nucleus system matches a quantum energy level of the compound nucleus. There the cross-section spikes — sometimes by thousands of barns — in sharp resonance peaks, described by the Breit–Wigner formula. Uranium-238, for example, has towering capture resonances in the few-eV to keV range.

3. The fast region (high energy). Above the resonances the cross-section settles down to a smooth, roughly geometric value of a few barns.

This energy structure drives reactor design. Thermal reactors deliberately slow neutrons into the high-σf region of U-235 — but they must avoid parking neutrons in U-238's capture resonances along the way, a balance quantified by the resonance escape probability. Get this physics wrong and the chain reaction dies.

Common mistakeThe 1/v rule applies to many absorption cross-sections, not to everything. Elastic scattering off light moderators (like hydrogen) is roughly constant over a wide energy range, and resonances violate 1/v entirely. Never assume a single σ value for a problem that spans many decades of neutron energy — always ask "at what energy?"
✦ Worked Example — reaction rate in natural uranium

A block of pure U-235 (density ρ = 18.7 g/cm³, M = 235 g/mol) sits in a thermal flux of φ = 1.0 x 1013 neutrons/(cm² s). The thermal fission cross-section is σf = 585 barns. Find the atom density, Σf, the fission rate density, and the power density.

  1. Atom density: N = ρNA/M = (18.7)(6.022 x 1023)/235 = 4.79 x 1022 atoms/cm³.
  2. Convert σf to cm²: 585 barns x 10-24 cm²/barn = 5.85 x 10-22 cm².
  3. Macroscopic cross-section: Σf = Nσf = (4.79 x 1022)(5.85 x 10-22) = 28.0 /cm.
  4. Fission rate density: Rf = Σfφ = (28.0)(1.0 x 1013) = 2.8 x 1014 fissions/(cm³ s).
  5. Power density: each fission yields ~3.2 x 10-11 J, so P = Rf x 3.2 x 10-11 = (2.8 x 1014)(3.2 x 10-11) ≈ 9.0 x 103 W/cm³ = 9 kW/cm³. (Pure U-235 at this flux is intensely power-dense — real cores dilute the fuel and limit flux for exactly this reason.)

🛠️ Hands-On: cross-sections in Python

We continue with the free Python + Jupyter + NumPy stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the one-time install lives in the early lectures). This week we add OpenMC, the open-source Monte Carlo transport code, to pull real evaluated cross-section data.

⬇ Install OpenMC (new this week)

OpenMC ships on conda-forge; install it into your course environment:

conda install -c conda-forge openmc

You also need nuclear data. Download an open cross-section library (e.g. an ENDF/B-based HDF5 set) and point OpenMC at it:

export OPENMC_CROSS_SECTIONS=/path/to/cross_sections.xml

Verify: run python -c "import openmc; print(openmc.__version__)" — a version string means you're set.

First, reproduce the worked example by hand with NumPy, then plot the 1/v trend and overlay a real evaluated cross-section from OpenMC's data API.

# cross_sections.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np

# --- macroscopic cross-section and reaction rate (worked example) ---
N_A   = 6.022e23            # atoms/mol
rho   = 18.7                # g/cm^3 (U-235)
M     = 235.0               # g/mol
sig_f = 585e-24             # cm^2  (585 barns)
phi   = 1.0e13              # neutrons/(cm^2 s)

N      = rho * N_A / M       # atom density, 1/cm^3
Sig_f  = N * sig_f           # macroscopic, 1/cm
R_f    = Sig_f * phi         # fissions/(cm^3 s)
P      = R_f * 3.2e-11       # W/cm^3  (~200 MeV per fission)
print(f"N={N:.3e}/cm3  Sig_f={Sig_f:.2f}/cm  R_f={R_f:.3e}  P={P:.3e} W/cm3")

# --- mean free path of a total Sigma_t = 0.5 /cm ---
Sig_t = 0.5
print("mean free path =", 1.0 / Sig_t, "cm")

# --- pull a real evaluated cross-section with OpenMC ---
import openmc.data
u235 = openmc.data.IncidentNeutron.from_hdf5("U235.h5")
xs   = u235[18].xs["294K"]          # MT=18 is total fission
E    = np.logspace(-3, 7, 500)      # 1 meV to 10 MeV
print("fission xs at 0.025 eV =", xs(0.025), "barn")   # ~585 barn

Run it: the hand calculation should print Σf ≈ 28 /cm and P ≈ 9 kW/cm³, and OpenMC's evaluated fission cross-section at thermal energy should land near 585 barns — your formula and real data agree.

❓ Lecture 4 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The microscopic cross-section σ has units of…
A. area (cm²), and 1 barn = 10-24 cm²
B. inverse length (1/cm)
C. neutrons per cm² per second
D. energy (MeV)
Show answer

A. σ is an effective area per nucleus; the barn is 10-24 cm². Inverse length (B) is the macroscopic Σ; (C) is flux; (D) is the wrong dimension entirely.

Q2. Given atom density N and microscopic cross-section σ, the macroscopic cross-section is…
A. Σ = σ / N
B. Σ = Nσ, with units 1/cm
C. Σ = N / σ
D. Σ = Nσφ
Show answer

B. Σ = Nσ multiplies (nuclei/cm³)(cm²) = 1/cm. Dividing (A, C) gives wrong units; including flux (D) gives the reaction rate R, not Σ.

Q3. The mean free path of a neutron in a material with total macroscopic cross-section Σt = 0.25 /cm is…
A. 0.25 cm
B. 0.0625 cm
C. 4 cm
D. 2.5 cm
Show answer

C. λ = 1/Σt = 1/0.25 = 4 cm. (A) just repeats Σ; (B) squares it; (D) is unrelated. The neutron travels 4 cm on average between interactions.

Q4. The reaction rate density in a flux φ is R = Σφ. If you double both the flux and the macroscopic cross-section, the reaction rate…
A. stays the same
B. doubles
C. increases by a factor of 4
D. halves
Show answer

C. R = Σφ is linear in each factor, so doubling both multiplies R by 2 x 2 = 4. (B) would hold if only one doubled; (A) and (D) ignore the product structure.

Q5. In the "1/v" region, why does a slower (lower-energy) neutron have a larger absorption cross-section?
A. Slower neutrons carry more kinetic energy to break the nucleus apart
B. Slower neutrons are physically larger objects
C. The nucleus expands to meet slow neutrons
D. A slower neutron spends more time in the vicinity of a nucleus, raising the capture probability
Show answer

D. Lower speed means longer interaction time, so capture probability (and thus σa) scales as 1/v. (A) is backwards — slower means less kinetic energy; (B) and (C) are not real mechanisms.

✓ What you learned

  • The microscopic cross-section σ is an effective target area per nucleus, measured in barns (10-24 cm²).
  • The macroscopic cross-section Σ = Nσ (units 1/cm) is the interaction probability per unit path length, and the beam attenuates as e−Σx.
  • The mean free path λ = 1/Σ is the average distance between interactions.
  • The reaction rate R = Σφ ties flux to fission and hence to reactor power.
  • Cross-sections depend on energy: a 1/v absorption rise, sharp resonance peaks, and a flat fast region — the physics that shapes reactor design, and the backbone of your Week-4 project checkpoint.
