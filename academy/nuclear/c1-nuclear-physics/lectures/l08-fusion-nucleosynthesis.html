Fusion & Nucleosynthesis

Every atom heavier than hydrogen in your body was forged by fusion inside a star — here is the physics of how, and why we struggle to copy it on Earth.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain nuclear fusion as the mirror image of fission on the binding-energy curve, and compute its energy release from mass defect.
  • Describe the Coulomb barrier and why classical physics forbids fusion at stellar temperatures — yet quantum tunneling makes it happen.
  • Write the deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction and explain why it is the easiest fusion fuel to ignite.
  • Trace stellar nucleosynthesis from the proton-proton chain up to iron, and explain why fusion stops at iron-56.
  • State the Lawson criterion and explain the triple product that confines and heats a terrestrial fusion plasma.

8.1Fusion: Climbing the Binding-Energy Curve the Other Way

In Lecture 6 we plotted binding energy per nucleon against mass number and found a peak near iron-56 (about 8.8 MeV/nucleon). Fission released energy by splitting heavy nuclei that sit to the right of the peak. Fusion does the opposite: it merges two light nuclei from the far left of the curve into a heavier one closer to the peak. Both processes climb toward iron, and both release the difference as kinetic energy.

The energy released, as always in nuclear physics, comes from the mass defect — the fused product weighs slightly less than its ingredients, and that missing mass becomes energy via Einstein's relation:

Q = (m_in - m_out) c^2 = Delta-m * 931.49 MeV/uQ is the energy released. Mass differences in atomic mass units (u) convert to energy at 931.49 MeV per u.

Because the left edge of the binding-energy curve is so steep, light nuclei have a lot to gain by fusing. Per kilogram of fuel, D-T fusion releases roughly 4 times the energy of uranium fission and about ten million times the energy of burning the same mass of coal. This is why fusion powers stars and why it is the most energy-dense reaction known to physics.

8.2The Coulomb Barrier & Quantum Tunneling

If fusion is so favorable, why doesn't all hydrogen instantly fuse? Because two nuclei are both positively charged, and they repel ferociously as they approach. The electrostatic potential energy climbs as the inverse of separation:

U(r) = k * Z1 * Z2 * e^2 / rk is Coulomb's constant, Z1 and Z2 are the charges, r is the separation. The repulsion peaks just before the short-range strong force can grab hold.

The strong nuclear force only acts over ~1-2 femtometers. To reach that range the nuclei must overcome a repulsive peak — the Coulomb barrier — of order several hundred keV for hydrogen isotopes. Yet the center of the Sun sits at only ~1.3 keV of thermal energy (about 15 million kelvin). Classically, almost no proton has enough energy to clear the barrier, and the Sun should not shine.

The resolution is quantum tunneling: a nucleus has a small but nonzero probability of appearing on the far side of a barrier it could never classically surmount. The tunneling probability falls off exponentially through the Gamow factor, so fusion is rare per collision — but stars have astronomical numbers of collisions per second, and that is enough.

AnalogyThink of rolling a ball up a steep hill toward a pit on the other side. You throw it far too gently to reach the top — classically it just rolls back. But quantum mechanics says the ball is also a wave that leaks through the hill: occasionally it materializes in the pit without ever having gone over the top. Stellar fusion runs entirely on this "leaking through the hill."

8.3The Deuterium-Tritium Reaction & Fusion in Stars

Not all fusion fuels are equally easy. The reaction with the lowest barrier and the largest reaction cross-section at achievable temperatures is the deuterium-tritium (D-T) reaction — the fuel of choice for every terrestrial reactor design:

D + T -> He-4 (3.5 MeV) + n (14.1 MeV)Deuterium (1p+1n) fuses with tritium (1p+2n) into helium-4 plus a fast neutron. Total Q = 17.6 MeV.

The energy splits inversely with mass: the light neutron carries 14.1 MeV (which a reactor blanket captures as heat), and the helium-4 "alpha" keeps 3.5 MeV — energy that, if confined, reheats the plasma to sustain the reaction. We will see in 8.4 why keeping that alpha heating is the whole game.

How stars actually burn

Stars cannot use tritium (it is radioactive and not abundant). Instead, in stars like our Sun, four protons fuse — in several steps — into one helium-4 nucleus through the proton-proton (p-p) chain. The slowest, rate-limiting step requires one proton to convert to a neutron via the weak force during the collision, which is staggeringly improbable. That is exactly why the Sun burns slowly and steadily for ~10 billion years rather than exploding.

4 p -> He-4 + 2 e+ + 2 nu + ~26.7 MeVThe net proton-proton chain: four protons become one helium-4, releasing positrons, neutrinos, and ~26.7 MeV of energy.

In heavier, hotter stars the CNO cycle dominates, using carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen as catalysts to fuse hydrogen faster. As a star ages and its core contracts and heats, it ignites successive fuels: helium burns to carbon and oxygen (the triple-alpha process), then carbon, neon, oxygen, and silicon burning build elements up toward iron. This is stellar nucleosynthesis — the origin of nearly every element in the periodic table up to iron.

8.4Why It Stops at Iron — and Why Earth Fusion Is Hard

Fusion releases energy only while it moves nuclei up the binding-energy curve toward the iron-56 peak. Once a star's core is iron, fusing iron into anything heavier would cost energy rather than release it — there is no further fuel. The core can no longer support itself, and for massive stars it collapses, triggering a supernova. The titanic neutron flux of that explosion (and of neutron-star mergers) forges the elements heavier than iron, like gold and uranium, by rapid neutron capture. Iron is therefore the great divide: fusion below it, fission above it, both releasing energy by approaching it.

The Lawson criterion: a first look

To get net energy from fusion on Earth we must do three things at once: heat the fuel hot enough to tunnel (high temperature T), pack it densely enough to collide often (high density n), and hold it together long enough to fuse before it cools (long energy confinement time tau). The Lawson criterion packages these into a single triple product that must exceed a threshold for ignition:

n * T * tau >= ~3 x 10^21 keV s / m^3For D-T fusion: the product of density, temperature, and confinement time must clear this threshold for the plasma to be self-sustaining.

This is brutally hard. The plasma must reach ~150 million kelvin — ten times hotter than the Sun's core, because Earth reactors cannot use the Sun's crushing gravitational pressure to compensate with density and time. No solid container survives that, so we confine the plasma with powerful magnetic fields (the tokamak's doughnut) or by inertial compression (laser-imploded fuel pellets). Achieving the triple product, and recovering more energy than we put in, is the central engineering challenge of fusion power.

Common mistakeDo not confuse scientific breakeven (the fusion energy released exceeds the energy delivered to the fuel) with engineering/commercial breakeven (the plant outputs more electricity than its entire facility consumes, including the inefficient lasers, magnets, and cooling). A "net energy gain" headline almost always means the former. Reactors can pass the fuel-level Lawson threshold while the whole facility still runs at a large net loss.
✦ Worked Example — energy from D-T fusion

Verify the energy released in D + T -> He-4 + n from atomic masses, and find the energy per kilogram of D-T fuel. Use: m(D) = 2.014102 u, m(T) = 3.016049 u, m(He-4) = 4.002602 u, m(n) = 1.008665 u, and 1 u = 931.49 MeV/c^2.

  1. Mass in: m(D) + m(T) = 2.014102 + 3.016049 = 5.030151 u.
  2. Mass out: m(He-4) + m(n) = 4.002602 + 1.008665 = 5.011267 u.
  3. Mass defect: Delta-m = 5.030151 - 5.011267 = 0.018884 u.
  4. Energy released: Q = 0.018884 u x 931.49 MeV/u = 17.59 MeV per reaction — matching the quoted 17.6 MeV. (Using atomic masses, the electron masses cancel since both sides have one proton's worth of electrons.)
  5. Per kilogram: one D-T pair has mass ~5.03 u = 8.35 x 10^-27 kg, so 1 kg holds ~1.2 x 10^26 reactions. Energy = 1.2 x 10^26 x 17.59 MeV x 1.602 x 10^-13 J/MeV ≈ 3.4 x 10^14 J/kg — about 340 TJ, roughly 4x the yield of fissioning a kilogram of U-235.

🛠️ Hands-On: tunneling, Q-values & the Lawson threshold

We reuse the free Python + Jupyter + NumPy stack from the Week 1/2 setup (the install <details> lives in those lectures). No new tools are needed here — OpenMC returns in the reactor weeks; today is pure NumPy. The script computes the D-T Q-value from masses, estimates the Gamow tunneling suppression, and checks a sample plasma against the Lawson triple product.

# fusion_basics.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np

# --- 1. D-T Q-value from atomic masses (u) ---
u_to_MeV = 931.49
m = {"D": 2.014102, "T": 3.016049, "He4": 4.002602, "n": 1.008665}
dm = (m["D"] + m["T"]) - (m["He4"] + m["n"])
Q = dm * u_to_MeV
print(f"D-T Q-value = {Q:.2f} MeV")          # ~17.59 MeV

# --- 2. Gamow factor: relative tunneling probability vs energy ---
# P ~ exp(-sqrt(E_G / E)); E_G is the Gamow energy for D-T (~1180 keV)
E_G = 1180.0                                  # keV, approximate for D-T
for E in [1.3, 10, 100]:                      # keV: Sun core, lab, hot plasma
    P = np.exp(-np.sqrt(E_G / E))
    print(f"E = {E:6.1f} keV  ->  tunneling factor ~ {P:.2e}")

# --- 3. Lawson triple product check (D-T) ---
n   = 1.0e20      # particle density, 1/m^3
T   = 15.0        # temperature, keV (~150 million K)
tau = 3.0         # energy confinement time, s
triple = n * T * tau
threshold = 3.0e21
print(f"triple product = {triple:.2e} keV s/m^3 "
      f"({'IGNITION' if triple >= threshold else 'sub-ignition'})")

Notice how the tunneling factor explodes upward as you raise E from the Sun's 1.3 keV to a lab plasma's 10-100 keV — that is the entire reason terrestrial reactors must run far hotter than the Sun. Try sweeping n, T, and tau to find combinations that clear the Lawson threshold; you will see there are many trade-offs (magnetic confinement favors long tau; inertial confinement favors huge n).

Q1. Fusion releases energy when it combines light nuclei because the products…
A. have more total mass than the reactants
B. have a higher binding energy per nucleon, lying closer to the iron-56 peak
C. are radioactive and decay
D. have fewer total nucleons than the reactants
Show answer

B. Moving up the steep left side of the binding-energy curve toward iron-56 means more tightly bound products, and the freed binding energy appears as kinetic energy. (A) is backwards — products are lighter. (C) is irrelevant to the energy source. (D) violates nucleon conservation; nucleons are conserved, only the binding changes.

Q2. Why does fusion occur in the Sun's core despite its temperature being far too low to classically clear the Coulomb barrier?
A. The strong force is long-range and reaches across the barrier
B. Gravity physically pushes the nuclei together past the barrier
C. Quantum tunneling lets nuclei pass through the barrier with small probability
D. The Coulomb barrier disappears at high density
Show answer

C. Tunneling gives a nonzero probability of crossing a classically forbidden barrier; with enormous collision rates, that suffices. (A) is false — the strong force is short-range (~1-2 fm). (B) gravity sets temperature/density but cannot itself shove nuclei through. (D) the barrier height depends on charge, not density.

Q3. In the reaction D + T -> He-4 + n with Q = 17.6 MeV, which statement is correct?
A. The neutron carries about 14.1 MeV and the helium-4 about 3.5 MeV
B. The helium-4 carries about 14.1 MeV and the neutron about 3.5 MeV
C. The energy splits equally, 8.8 MeV each
D. All 17.6 MeV goes to gamma rays
Show answer

A. Momentum conservation gives the lighter neutron the larger share: ~14.1 MeV to the neutron, ~3.5 MeV to the alpha. (B) reverses the masses. (C) ignores that energy splits inversely with mass, not equally. (D) the products are massive particles, not photons.

Q4. Why does fusion in stars stop producing energy at iron-56?
A. Iron is too dense to fuse
B. Stars run out of hydrogen before reaching iron
C. Iron-56 sits at the binding-energy peak, so fusing it further would absorb rather than release energy
D. Iron is not produced by stars at all
Show answer

C. At the binding-energy maximum, no rearrangement of nucleons can release further energy — fusing iron costs energy. (A) density is not the reason. (B) the core has already fused through to iron when this happens. (D) is false — silicon burning produces iron-peak nuclei.

Q5. The Lawson criterion for a D-T reactor requires that…
A. the plasma be cooled below 1 keV to slow the reaction
B. only the temperature exceed a threshold, regardless of density or time
C. the magnetic field exceed a fixed strength
D. the product of density, temperature, and confinement time exceed a threshold
Show answer

D. The triple product n·T·tau must clear a threshold (~3×10^21 keV·s/m^3 for D-T) for self-sustaining fusion. (A) is the opposite — you must heat to ~150 million K. (B) ignores the equally essential density and confinement factors. (C) magnetic field is one means of confinement, not the criterion itself.

✓ What you learned

  • Fusion releases energy by climbing the binding-energy curve from light nuclei toward the iron-56 peak — the mirror image of fission.
  • The Coulomb barrier blocks fusion classically, but quantum tunneling (the Gamow factor) lets stars fuse even at "too cold" core temperatures.
  • The D-T reaction releases 17.6 MeV (14.1 MeV neutron + 3.5 MeV alpha) and is the easiest fuel to ignite on Earth.
  • Stars build elements up to iron via the p-p chain, CNO cycle, and successive burning stages — stellar nucleosynthesis.
  • The Lawson criterion ties net fusion to a density × temperature × confinement-time triple product, and your project checkpoint reproduces the D-T Q-value and a Lawson check in code.
