Fission: Physics & Energy Release

Split one heavy nucleus and you unleash, atom for atom, tens of millions of times the energy of any chemical fire — here is exactly why, and where every megaelectronvolt goes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain nuclear fission and use the liquid-drop model to argue why heavy nuclei are unstable against splitting.
  • Distinguish fissile nuclei from merely fissionable ones and connect this to neutron energy.
  • Account for the ~200 MeV released per fission and break down where that energy is deposited.
  • Describe fission products, the fragment mass distribution, and the number of neutrons released per fission.
  • Contrast prompt and delayed neutrons and explain why delayed neutrons make a reactor controllable.

6.1What Fission Is — and Why Heavy Nuclei Want to Split

In Lecture 5 we tracked how a free neutron scatters and is absorbed. Nuclear fission is the most dramatic absorption outcome: a heavy nucleus, having swallowed a neutron, deforms and tears into two middleweight fragments, spitting out a few extra neutrons and a burst of energy. The reason this releases energy traces straight back to the binding-energy-per-nucleon curve from Week 2.

Recall that binding energy per nucleon peaks near iron (A ~ 56) at about 8.8 MeV and sags to roughly 7.6 MeV out at uranium (A ~ 235). Nucleons are more tightly bound in the mid-mass region. So if you take 235 weakly bound nucleons and repackage them as two tightly bound fragments near A ~ 95 and A ~ 140, the surplus binding energy is liberated:

E_release = [ B(fragments) ] - [ B(parent) ] ~ (8.5 - 7.6) MeV/nucleon x ~235 nucleons ~ 200 MeVThe energy comes from the gain in binding energy per nucleon when heavy nuclei reshape into mid-mass fragments.

To see why a heavy nucleus is poised to do this, we use the liquid-drop model: treat the nucleus as a charged droplet of incompressible nuclear fluid. Two forces compete. The short-range strong (surface) force behaves like surface tension — it pulls the drop into a sphere and resists deformation. The long-range Coulomb repulsion between protons pushes the drop apart, and because every proton repels every other, it grows like Z2. As A and Z climb, the disruptive Coulomb term gains on the cohesive surface term.

6.2The Fission Barrier and the Fissility Parameter

A heavy nucleus does not fall apart on its own merely because splitting is energetically favorable. To split, the drop must first stretch into a dumbbell shape, and stretching temporarily raises the surface area (costing energy) before the Coulomb energy of the separated halves drops. The net effect is an energy hump — the fission barrier — that the nucleus must climb over before it slides apart.

The barrier height is governed by the dimensionless fissility parameter, the ratio of the disruptive Coulomb energy to the cohesive surface energy:

x = E_Coulomb / (2 E_surface) is proportional to Z^2 / AWhen Z^2/A exceeds ~ 48 the barrier vanishes and the nucleus is unstable against immediate (spontaneous) splitting.

For uranium-235, Z2/A ~ 36, so a real barrier remains — but it is low enough that a modest energy injection clears it. That injection is exactly what an incoming neutron provides.

6.3Fissile vs. Fissionable — and the Energy Ledger

When a nucleus captures a neutron, the neutron's binding energy alone deposits several MeV of excitation, even before adding any kinetic energy. Whether that is enough to clear the barrier depends on a subtle pairing effect.

A fissile nucleus can be split by neutrons of any energy, including slow (thermal) neutrons. The classic example is uranium-235: capturing a neutron forms uranium-236, and because U-236 has an even number of neutrons, the pairing bonus pushes the excitation energy above the barrier with energy to spare. A fissionable but not fissile nucleus (e.g. uranium-238) can only be split by fast neutrons, because forming the odd-neutron compound nucleus gives less binding-energy bonus, leaving a threshold of about 1 MeV that the neutron's kinetic energy must supply.

AnalogyThink of the barrier as a hill and the captured neutron as a push. For U-235, simply catching the neutron (its binding energy) shoves the nucleus over the crest — no running start needed, so even a slow neutron works. For U-238, catching the neutron only gets you partway up; you also need the neutron to arrive already running fast (kinetic energy) to make it over. "Fissile" means the catch alone suffices; "fissionable" means you also need speed.

Either way, once over the barrier, ~200 MeV is released. That energy does not all appear as one thing — and where it goes matters enormously for reactor engineering, because only some of it is recoverable as usable heat. The approximate ledger for U-235:

The headline is that over 80% of the energy is the kinetic energy of the two charged fragments, which stop within microns and become heat almost instantly. This is the heat a power reactor turns into steam.

6.4Fission Products and the Neutrons That Sustain the Chain

The two fragments are rarely equal halves. The fragment mass distribution for thermal fission of U-235 is famously double-humped: one peak near A ~ 95 and another near A ~ 138, with symmetric splitting (A ~ 117 each) suppressed by roughly a factor of several hundred. These fragments are extremely neutron-rich (they inherited the parent's high N/Z ratio), so they decay by a chain of beta-minus emissions toward stability — the source of fission-product radioactivity and decay heat.

Crucially, each fission also ejects free neutrons. On average U-235 thermal fission releases ν ≈ 2.4 neutrons per fission. These neutrons are what allow the reaction to chain: if at least one survives to trigger another fission, the reaction sustains itself (Week 7). Of these, the vast majority are prompt neutrons, emitted within ~10-14 s of the split, directly from the highly excited fragments.

Prompt vs. delayed neutrons

A small but decisive fraction — about 0.65% for U-235, called the delayed neutron fraction β — are delayed neutrons. These are not emitted at the moment of fission. Instead, certain neutron-rich fission products (precursors such as bromine-87 and iodine-137) first beta-decay, leaving a daughter so excited it promptly boils off a neutron. Because the rate-limiting step is the precursor's beta decay, these neutrons appear seconds to minutes later.

l_prompt ~ 10^-4 s vs effective lifetime with delayed neutrons ~ 0.1 sThe handful of delayed neutrons stretch the mean neutron generation time by ~1000x, slowing the chain's response to a human-controllable pace.
Common mistakeDo not assume the ~200 MeV per fission is all instantly recoverable, and do not ignore the delayed neutrons because they are "only 0.65%." Both errors are dangerous. The antineutrinos (~12 MeV) carry energy clean out of the plant, so usable energy is ~190 MeV, not 200. And it is precisely that tiny β ≈ 0.0065 that makes a reactor controllable — design a reactor to depend on prompt neutrons alone and its power would double on a millisecond timescale, far faster than any mechanical control could respond.
✦ Worked Example — energy from a gram of U-235

Estimate the thermal energy released by completely fissioning 1.0 gram of uranium-235, and compare it to burning coal (~24 MJ/kg). Use 200 MeV per fission and 1 MeV = 1.602 x 10-13 J.

  1. Number of nuclei. 1.0 g of U-235 is 1.0/235 = 4.26 x 10-3 mol. Times Avogadro's number: N = 4.26 x 10-3 x 6.022 x 1023 = 2.56 x 1021 nuclei.
  2. Energy per fission in joules. 200 MeV x 1.602 x 10-13 J/MeV = 3.20 x 10-11 J.
  3. Total energy. E = (2.56 x 1021) x (3.20 x 10-11 J) ≈ 8.2 x 1010 J = 82 GJ.
  4. Coal comparison. 82 x 109 J / (24 x 106 J/kg) ≈ 3.4 x 103 kg of coal, i.e. about 3.4 tonnes of coal per gram of U-235 — a factor of ~3 million by mass.
  5. Sanity check. Recoverable thermal energy is closer to 190 MeV/fission, so the answer drops only ~5% to ~78 GJ — the millionfold advantage over chemical fuel is unchanged. This energy density is the entire reason fission powers submarines and grid-scale plants.

🛠️ Hands-On: energy ledger and a neutron multiplicity sanity check

We continue with the free course stack from Weeks 1–2 (Python + Jupyter + NumPy, with OpenMC for transport). If you have not yet set up the environment, the one-time install details lives in the Week 1 and Week 2 lectures. Today's snippet is pure NumPy — no new tools needed — and reproduces the energy ledger and the per-gram estimate from the worked example.

# fission_energy.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np

MEV_J = 1.602e-13            # joules per MeV
NA    = 6.022e23             # Avogadro

# approximate energy ledger for U-235 thermal fission (MeV)
ledger = {
    "fragment KE":     168.0,
    "prompt neutrons":   5.0,
    "prompt gammas":     7.0,
    "beta particles":    8.0,
    "delayed gammas":    7.0,
    "antineutrinos":    12.0,   # escapes the plant
}
total      = sum(ledger.values())
recoverable = total - ledger["antineutrinos"]
print(f"total = {total:.0f} MeV, recoverable = {recoverable:.0f} MeV")

# energy from 1.0 g of U-235 fully fissioned
mass_g = 1.0
n_nuclei = (mass_g / 235.0) * NA
E_joule  = n_nuclei * recoverable * MEV_J
print(f"nuclei      = {n_nuclei:.3e}")
print(f"thermal E   = {E_joule/1e9:.1f} GJ")
print(f"coal equiv  = {E_joule/24e6:.0f} kg of coal")

# delayed-neutron fraction: prompt vs delayed split of nu = 2.4
nu, beta = 2.4, 0.0065
print(f"delayed neutrons/fission = {nu*beta:.4f}")
print(f"prompt  neutrons/fission = {nu*(1-beta):.4f}")

Expected output: total = 207 MeV, recoverable = 195 MeV, ~78 GJ, ~3200 kg coal, and only ~0.016 delayed neutrons per fission. Try editing beta to 0 and reflect on why that tiny number is what makes a reactor steerable — a question we make quantitative in Week 7.

💬 Discussion

The delayed neutron fraction for U-235 is only β ≈ 0.0065 — yet engineers often say it is "the most important 0.65% in nuclear engineering." Suppose a reactor were designed so that it became critical on prompt neutrons alone (ignoring the delayed ones). Explain physically what would change about how the reactor responds to a small increase in reactivity, and argue why the delayed neutrons — despite being a tiny minority — are what make the difference between a controllable machine and an uncontrollable one. Could you compensate for missing delayed neutrons with faster control rods?

Show a model response

The chain's response speed is governed by the mean neutron generation time — how long, on average, between one fission and the next it triggers. Prompt neutrons are reborn in ~10-4 s, so a chain running on prompt neutrons alone reacts on a sub-millisecond timescale. A small reactivity bump would then make the power multiply almost instantly (a "prompt critical" excursion), doubling in milliseconds — far faster than any mechanical control rod, which moves on a timescale of seconds, could ever catch.

Delayed neutrons change this completely. Because ~0.65% of neutrons wait seconds to minutes for a precursor to beta-decay, the effective generation time jumps by a factor of ~1000, to roughly 0.1 s. Reactors are deliberately operated so that they are critical only when the delayed neutrons are included (and subcritical on prompt neutrons alone). Then power changes are paced by the slow delayed-neutron clock, giving operators and automatic systems ample time to respond. You generally cannot "make up" for missing delayed neutrons with faster rods: the prompt timescale is set by neutron physics (microseconds), and no mechanical actuator is that fast. The delayed neutrons buy time that hardware simply cannot.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer should:

  • Tie controllability to the mean neutron generation time, not just neutron count.
  • Give the order-of-magnitude contrast (~10-4 s prompt vs ~10-1 s with delayed).
  • Define/allude to prompt critical as the dangerous regime to stay below.
  • Conclude that mechanical control cannot match the prompt timescale, so delayed neutrons are essential.

Q1. Fission of a heavy nucleus releases energy fundamentally because…
A. the fragments have higher binding energy per nucleon than the parent
B. mass is created out of the released neutrons
C. the Coulomb force is attractive at nuclear distances
D. the fragments are lighter elements with more protons total
Show answer

A. Repackaging weakly bound heavy nucleons into more tightly bound mid-mass fragments liberates the surplus binding energy. B violates mass-energy conservation (mass is lost, not created); C is false (Coulomb is repulsive); D misstates conservation of protons.

Q2. A nucleus that can be fissioned by neutrons of any energy, including thermal neutrons, is called…
A. fertile
B. fissionable but not fissile
C. fissile
D. a moderator
Show answer

C. Fissile (e.g. U-235) means thermal neutrons suffice. "Fissionable but not fissile" (B, e.g. U-238) requires fast neutrons above a threshold. Fertile (A) describes nuclei that breed into fissile ones; a moderator (D) slows neutrons, not a nuclear classification.

Q3. Of the ~200 MeV released per fission, the single largest share is carried by…
A. the antineutrinos
B. the kinetic energy of the two fission fragments
C. the prompt gamma rays
D. the prompt neutrons
Show answer

B. Fragment kinetic energy is ~168 MeV, over 80% of the total, deposited locally as heat. Antineutrinos (A, ~12 MeV) escape; prompt gammas (C, ~7 MeV) and prompt neutrons (D, ~5 MeV) are minor by comparison.

Q4. The fission barrier exists because, as the nucleus deforms toward splitting…
A. the strong force suddenly becomes long-range
B. the surface (tension-like) energy first rises before the Coulomb energy falls
C. neutrons must be created to carry away spin
D. the nucleus must first absorb a gamma ray
Show answer

B. Stretching the drop increases surface area (an energy cost) before the separated charges lower the Coulomb energy, producing the energy hump. A is false (the strong force stays short-range); C and D are not what creates the barrier.

Q5. Delayed neutrons are critical to reactor control because they…
A. carry most of the fission energy
B. are emitted instantly from the excited fragments
C. eliminate the need for control rods entirely
D. dramatically lengthen the effective neutron generation time, slowing the chain's response to a controllable pace
Show answer

D. Though only ~0.65% of neutrons, their seconds-to-minutes delay stretches the effective generation time from ~10-4 s to ~10-1 s, letting controls keep up. A is false (they carry little energy); B describes prompt neutrons; C is false (control rods are still required).

✓ What you learned

  • Fission releases ~200 MeV because mid-mass fragments are more tightly bound than the heavy parent.
  • The liquid-drop model pits Coulomb repulsion (~Z2) against surface tension, creating a fission barrier set by the fissility parameter Z2/A.
  • Fissile nuclei (U-235) split with thermal neutrons; fissionable ones (U-238) need fast neutrons above a threshold.
  • Most energy (~168 MeV) is fragment kinetic energy; antineutrinos (~12 MeV) escape, so ~190 MeV is recoverable; each fission yields ~2.4 neutrons and a double-humped product distribution.
  • A tiny delayed-neutron fraction (β ≈ 0.0065) lengthens the effective generation time ~1000x and makes a reactor controllable.
