Capstone: Monte-Carlo Particle Transport

Nine weeks of cross sections, fission, and shielding converge here — we throw a million random neutrons at a reactor and let chance reveal its flux and its criticality.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why neutron transport is solved by simulating random histories instead of by closed-form integration.
  • Trace one neutron history: sample a flight distance, choose a reaction, and tally what it deposits.
  • Estimate flux and the multiplication factor k-eff from many histories, with a Monte-Carlo error bar that shrinks like 1/sqrt(N).
  • Set up a minimal geometry and material in OpenMC and run a fixed-source or eigenvalue calculation.
  • Frame and deliver the C1 capstone: a self-contained criticality study with quantified uncertainty.

10.1Why Simulate Random Neutrons?

A neutron born in a reactor does not travel in a tidy straight line. It flies a random distance, scatters off a nucleus into a random new direction with a random new energy, flies again, and eventually is absorbed or leaks out. Over its life it visits dozens of materials and samples cross sections that swing by orders of magnitude with energy (recall the resonance forest from Week 4). The quantity we want — the neutron flux φ(r, E, Ω) everywhere, in every energy group, in every direction — is the solution of the Boltzmann transport equation, a seven-dimensional integro-differential beast.

flux phi = (neutrons crossing area) / (area x time) = n(r,E) vn is the neutron number density; v is the speed. Reaction rate density = Sigma(E) x phi, the master quantity every tally is built from.

For all but the simplest slab geometries that equation has no closed-form answer. We have two honest options. One is to discretize everything — chop space into a mesh, energy into groups, angle into directions — and solve a giant linear system (the deterministic approach). The other is the Monte-Carlo method: stop trying to integrate, and instead play out the physics. Simulate one neutron's random walk faithfully using the actual cross sections, then do it again for millions of neutrons, and let the law of large numbers turn those random histories into accurate averages.

AnalogyYou want the average path length of water draining through a complicated maze of gravel. You could write the fluid equations for every pore — hopeless. Or you could drop a thousand tracer dye droplets in, time each one, and average. Monte-Carlo transport is the dye-droplet method: each neutron is a tracer, the gravel is the cross-section data, and the average over many droplets is the answer.

10.2The Life of One Neutron History

The engine of Monte-Carlo transport is a loop that follows a single neutron from birth to death. Every step is a random draw from a distribution that the cross sections define. The key physics fact: in a uniform material with total macroscopic cross section Σt, the distance a neutron flies before its next collision is exponentially distributed, with mean free path 1/Σt.

s = -ln(xi) / Sigma_txi is a uniform random number in (0,1). This inverts the exponential attenuation law exp(-Sigma_t x s) to sample a flight distance s.

At the collision site we draw a second random number to pick which reaction happened, weighting each channel by its share of the total cross section: scatter with probability Σs/Σt, capture with Σγ/Σt, fission with Σf/Σt, and so on. A scatter sends the neutron off in a new sampled direction and energy; a capture ends the history; a fission ends this history but banks ν new neutrons for the next generation. Along every flight segment we add to running tallies — the path length contributes to the flux estimate, and reaction events contribute to reaction-rate estimates.

10.3From Histories to k-eff and Error Bars

A reactor is a chain reaction, so the central question is whether each generation of neutrons reproduces itself. The effective multiplication factor k-eff is exactly that ratio:

k_eff = (neutrons produced in generation g+1) / (neutrons produced in generation g)k < 1 subcritical (chain dies out); k = 1 critical (steady); k > 1 supercritical (chain grows). This is the eigenvalue of the transport operator.

In a Monte-Carlo eigenvalue ("k-code") run we follow a batch of, say, 10,000 neutrons, count how many fission neutrons they bank, and form the ratio to the previous batch. We discard the first several inactive batches while the spatial fission distribution settles into its fundamental mode, then average k over the active batches. Because each batch is a random sample, the answer comes with a statistical uncertainty — and this is the defining feature of Monte-Carlo: the standard error of the mean shrinks as you run more histories.

standard error ~ s / sqrt(N)s is the sample standard deviation across batches; N is the number of independent samples. To halve the error bar you must run 4x as many neutrons.

That 1/sqrt(N) law is both the promise and the cost. It guarantees convergence to the true answer with no mesh or group approximation — but cutting the uncertainty by 10x demands 100x the compute. Knowing when "good enough" is good enough is a core skill of the practicing analyst.

Common mistakeNever report a Monte-Carlo k-eff without its uncertainty, and never trust an eigenvalue run whose inactive batches were too few for the source to converge. A tight-looking k = 1.00021 ± 0.00008 is meaningless if the fission source had not yet settled — the result is biased even though the error bar is small. A small error bar measures precision, not correctness; always check the source-convergence (Shannon entropy) diagnostic too.

10.4The Simulation Workflow & the Capstone

Every Monte-Carlo transport study, whether a homework slab or a full power-reactor core, follows the same five-stage pipeline. OpenMC — a free, open-source Monte-Carlo code with a Python API — organizes its inputs around exactly these stages.

The C1 capstone, due this week, is the payoff for the whole course. You will build a small but complete criticality study: a bare or reflected sphere/cylinder of fissile material, defined material by material, with geometry, an initial fission source, and a tally that reports k-eff and the radial flux profile. The deliverable is a single notebook that (1) defines the model, (2) runs an eigenvalue calculation with enough active batches to reach a stated precision, (3) reports k-eff ± its uncertainty, and (4) interprets the result physically — is it subcritical, critical, or supercritical, and what would push it toward k = 1?

AnalogyThink of the workflow like staging a play: Materials are the actors, Geometry is the set they stand on, Settings is the script and cast size, Tallies are the critics taking notes, and the run is opening night. Skip any stage and the show falls apart — OpenMC will refuse to run a geometry with an undefined material, just as a play can't open without a set.
✦ Worked Example — k-eff and its error bar from batch data

An eigenvalue run uses 5 inactive and then 5 active batches. The per-batch k estimates from the active batches are: 1.012, 0.997, 1.005, 1.001, 0.995. Report the best estimate of k-eff with its 1-sigma standard error.

  1. Discard inactive batches. Only the 5 active values count; the inactive ones merely let the source converge.
  2. Mean. k̄ = (1.012 + 0.997 + 1.005 + 1.001 + 0.995) / 5 = 5.010 / 5 = 1.0020.
  3. Deviations. +0.0100, -0.0050, +0.0030, -0.0010, -0.0070. Squares: 1.00e-4, 2.5e-5, 9e-6, 1e-6, 4.9e-5; sum = 1.84e-4.
  4. Sample standard deviation. s = sqrt(1.84e-4 / (5-1)) = sqrt(4.6e-5) = 0.00678 (divide by N-1, Bessel's correction).
  5. Standard error of the mean. SE = s / sqrt(N) = 0.00678 / sqrt(5) = 0.00678 / 2.236 = 0.0030.
  6. Report. k-eff = 1.0020 ± 0.0030. Since 1.0000 lies within one sigma, this configuration is statistically consistent with critical — but to claim super- vs sub-critical you'd need a tighter error bar, i.e. more active batches (the 1/sqrt(N) law).

🛠️ Hands-On: a criticality run in OpenMC

We use the free Python stack from Week 1 (NumPy + Jupyter) plus OpenMC, the open-source Monte-Carlo transport code introduced here for the capstone. The full Python/Jupyter setup lives in the Week-1 install note; OpenMC is the one new tool, so install just it below.

⬇ Install OpenMC + nuclear data (one-time, for the capstone)

OpenMC installs cleanly from conda-forge (it ships the compiled transport solver):

conda create -n omc -c conda-forge openmc python=3.11 numpy matplotlib jupyter
conda activate omc

Then download a free cross-section data library and point OpenMC at it:

python -c "import openmc; print(openmc.__version__)"
# set OPENMC_CROSS_SECTIONS to your cross_sections.xml path
export OPENMC_CROSS_SECTIONS=/path/to/endfb-vii.1-hdf5/cross_sections.xml

Printing a version number and a valid data path means you're ready to run criticality calculations.

A minimal eigenvalue model: a bare sphere of highly enriched metal. The vacuum boundary lets escaping neutrons leak away (no reflection).

# bare_sphere_keff.py — Lecture 10 capstone starter
import openmc

# 1. MATERIAL: enriched uranium metal
fuel = openmc.Material(name="HEU metal")
fuel.add_nuclide("U235", 0.93, "wo")   # 93 wt% U-235
fuel.add_nuclide("U238", 0.07, "wo")
fuel.set_density("g/cm3", 18.7)
mats = openmc.Materials([fuel])
mats.export_to_xml()

# 2. GEOMETRY: sphere with a vacuum (leakage) boundary
sph = openmc.Sphere(r=8.7, boundary_type="vacuum")
core = openmc.Cell(fill=fuel, region=-sph)   # inside the sphere
openmc.Geometry([core]).export_to_xml()

# 3. SETTINGS: eigenvalue (k) run, point source at center
s = openmc.Settings()
s.run_mode = "eigenvalue"
s.batches = 130           # total batches
s.inactive = 30           # discard while source converges
s.particles = 10000       # neutrons per batch
s.source = openmc.IndependentSource(
    space=openmc.stats.Point((0, 0, 0)))
s.export_to_xml()

# 4. RUN -> k-eff +/- sigma printed and saved to statepoint.h5
openmc.run()

OpenMC prints the combined k-effective with its uncertainty at the end. For the capstone, grow the radius until k crosses 1.0 to find the bare critical radius, add a openmc.Tally with a spatial mesh filter for the radial flux, and write up what each result means. Reuse the 1/sqrt(N) reasoning from §10.3 to justify how many active batches you chose.

🔗 Where this is heading

The same neutron random walk you just simulated scales straight up to professional practice. Add burnup coupling and you track how fuel composition evolves over a reactor cycle; add thermal-hydraulic feedback and you model how temperature changes cross sections in real time. Swap neutrons for photons and the identical Monte-Carlo machinery becomes the engine of medical-physics dose calculation for radiotherapy and the radiation-shielding design you met in Week 9. Beyond this course, these methods feed reactor licensing, fusion blanket design, and detector modeling for particle physics — every one of them a chain of random histories, averaged until the noise drops below the engineering tolerance.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why do reactor analysts use the Monte-Carlo method for neutron transport instead of solving the Boltzmann equation directly?
A. Monte-Carlo is always faster than any deterministic method
B. The full transport equation has no closed-form solution for realistic geometries, so simulating random histories estimates it without mesh/group approximations
C. Neutrons genuinely move according to random-number generators
D. Monte-Carlo gives exact answers with zero uncertainty
Show answer

B. The 7-D transport equation is intractable in closed form for real geometries; Monte-Carlo samples physical histories to estimate it. (A) is false — MC is often slower but more faithful. (C) confuses the model's random sampling with real physics. (D) is false — MC always carries statistical uncertainty.

Q2. The flight distance to a neutron's next collision is sampled as s = -ln(ξ)/Σt. This reflects that flight distances are…
A. uniformly distributed between 0 and the cell size
B. always equal to the mean free path
C. exponentially distributed with mean free path 1/Σt
D. normally distributed about the cell center
Show answer

C. Attenuation exp(-Σts) makes path lengths exponential with mean 1/Σt; inverting it gives the formula. (A,D) are the wrong distributions; (B) is just the mean, not the per-flight random value.

Q3. A criticality run reports k-eff = 0.94 ± 0.001. The configuration is…
A. subcritical — the chain reaction dies out
B. critical and self-sustaining
C. supercritical — the chain reaction grows
D. impossible, since k must equal 1
Show answer

A. k < 1 means each generation produces fewer neutrons than the last, so the chain dies — subcritical. (B) needs k = 1, (C) needs k > 1, and (D) is wrong because k can take any value depending on the design.

Q4. You ran 1,000,000 neutrons and want to halve the statistical error bar on k-eff. Roughly how many should you run?
A. 2,000,000
B. 1,500,000
C. 500,000
D. 4,000,000
Show answer

D. Error ~ 1/sqrt(N), so halving it needs 4x the histories: 4,000,000. (A) and (B) under-shoot; (C) would increase the error.

Q5. Why does an eigenvalue run discard its first several "inactive" batches?
A. To warm up the computer's cache
B. To let the spatial fission source converge to its fundamental mode before tallying k
C. Because those batches use a different cross-section library
D. They are the only batches that count toward the final answer
Show answer

B. The initial guessed source is wrong; inactive batches let it settle so the active-batch average is unbiased. (A) is irrelevant, (C) is false (same data throughout), and (D) is backwards — it's the active batches that count.
