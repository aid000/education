Binding energy & nuclear structure · Week 1
1. An alpha particle (He-4, Z=2, N=2) has mp = 1.007825 u, mn = 1.008665 u, and m(He-4) = 4.002602 u. Its binding energy per nucleon is closest to…
Answer: A. Free mass = 2(1.007825) + 2(1.008665) = 4.03298 u. Mass defect = 4.03298 − 4.002602 = 0.030378 u. Total Eb = 0.030378 × 931.494 ≈ 28.30 MeV. Per nucleon: 28.30 / 4 ≈ 7.07 MeV (D is the total, not per-nucleon; B is iron-56's value, not helium's).
2. The binding-energy-per-nucleon curve reaches its maximum — the most tightly bound nuclei — in which mass region?
Answer: B. The curve peaks near A ≈ 56–62 at about 8.8 MeV/nucleon; nickel-62 is very slightly higher than iron-56, but both sit at the bottom of the "binding-energy valley." Hydrogen (A) and helium (D) sit on the steep low end; uranium (C) sits on the gentle declining tail.
3. In the semi-empirical mass formula B(A,Z) = aVA − aSA2/3 − aCZ(Z−1)/A1/3 − aA(A−2Z)²/A + δ, which term is chiefly responsible for driving very heavy nuclei toward fission?
Answer: C. The Coulomb term grows roughly as Z² and penalizes large, proton-rich nuclei; as the fissility parameter Z²/A grows, it increasingly overwhelms the cohesive surface term, favoring deformation and split. The volume (A) and pairing (B) terms don't scale this way, and the asymmetry term (D) penalizes N≠Z, not size.
Radioactivity & decay laws · Week 2
4. A radionuclide has a half-life of 10 days. Its decay constant λ is closest to…
Answer: D. λ = ln(2) / T½ = 0.6931 / 10 ≈ 0.0693 /day. The others misplace the decimal or invert the ratio.
5. A sample contains N = 1.0×1015 undecayed atoms of a nuclide with λ = 2.0×10-6 s-1. Its activity is…
Answer: A. A = λN = (2.0×10-6)(1.0×1015) = 2.0×109 Bq (decays/second). (C) divides instead of multiplies; (B) and (D) are off by huge factors.
6. A long-lived parent feeds a much shorter-lived daughter. After many daughter half-lives, secular equilibrium is reached. Which statement is true?
Answer: B. Secular equilibrium means production equals decay for the daughter, so the parent and daughter activities match (A1=A2) even though their populations N1, N2 are very different (A) — the parent has far more atoms but a far smaller λ. A truly stable end-of-chain nuclide, by contrast, has λ=0 and simply accumulates rather than reaching equal activity.
Nuclear reactions, Q-values & SEMF · Week 3
7. For n + Li-6 → He-4 + T, using m(n)=1.008665, m(Li-6)=6.015123, m(He-4)=4.002602, m(T)=3.016049 u (1 u = 931.494 MeV), the Q-value and classification are…
Answer: C. min = 1.008665+6.015123 = 7.023788 u; mout = 4.002602+3.016049 = 7.018651 u. Δm = 0.005137 u × 931.494 ≈ +4.79 MeV. Positive Q means exothermic — this is the reaction used in tritium-breeding blankets. (A) has the sign backwards; (D) doubles it in error.
8. An endothermic reaction has Q = −2.0 MeV, target mass ma = 12 u, projectile mass mb = 1 u. The lab-frame threshold kinetic energy is closest to…
Answer: D. KEth = |Q|(ma+mb)/ma = 2.0 × (13/12) ≈ 2.17 MeV. (A) ignores the momentum surcharge; the threshold must always exceed |Q|, ruling out (B).
Cross-sections & reaction rates · Week 4
9. A material has a macroscopic cross-section Σt = 0.1 cm-1. The mean free path of a neutron in this material is…
Answer: A. Mean free path = 1/Σt = 1/0.1 = 10 cm.
10. A material has an atom density N = 3.0×1022 atoms/cm³ and a microscopic cross-section σ = 2.0 barns. Its macroscopic cross-section Σ is…
Answer: B. σ = 2.0 barns = 2.0×10-24 cm². Σ = Nσ = (3.0×1022)(2.0×10-24) = 0.06 /cm.
11. One barn equals…
Answer: C. 1 barn = 10-24 cm² = 10-28 m² — roughly the cross-sectional area of a uranium nucleus, which is why it was named "big as a barn" by nuclear-weapons-era physicists.
Neutron interactions with matter · Week 5
12. Which property makes light nuclei like hydrogen especially effective neutron moderators?
Answer: D. In an elastic collision, the maximum fractional energy loss occurs when the target mass equals the projectile mass — hydrogen (A≈1) is nearly a perfect match for a neutron, so each collision removes a large fraction of its kinetic energy. Heavy nuclei (A) barely slow a neutron down; high absorption (B) wastes neutrons rather than moderating them.
Fission: physics & energy release · Week 6
13. In the liquid-drop model, a nucleus becomes increasingly susceptible to fission as the fissility parameter Z²/A grows, because…
Answer: A. The disruptive Coulomb term grows roughly as Z², while the cohesive surface term only grows as A2/3; once Z²/A is large enough, the Coulomb term wins and the fission barrier shrinks toward zero.
14. Complete fission of 1 gram of U-235 (≈2.56×1021 atoms), releasing ~200 MeV per fission, liberates a total energy closest to…
Answer: B. Total energy = 2.56×1021 fissions × 200 MeV × 1.602×10-13 J/MeV ≈ 8.2×1010 J — about 20 tons of TNT equivalent from a single gram, the signature of nuclear energy density.
15. Thermal-neutron-induced fission of U-235 releases, on average, about how many prompt neutrons per fission event?
Answer: C. About 2.4 neutrons are released per thermal fission of U-235 (slightly more for U-238 or Pu-239 fast fission) — enough above 1.0 to sustain a chain reaction once losses are accounted for.
Chain reaction & criticality · Week 7
16. A reactor has η=2.0, f=0.71, p=0.87, ε=1.02, and leakage factors Lf=0.97, Lt=0.99. The multiplication factor k is closest to… and the reactor is…
Answer: D. k = η·f·p·ε·Lf·Lt = 2.0 × 0.71 × 0.87 × 1.02 × 0.97 × 0.99 ≈ 1.21. Since k > 1, the neutron population grows each generation — supercritical.
17. A reactor configuration with keff exactly equal to 1 is…
Answer: A. keff=1 defines criticality: each generation produces exactly as many neutrons as the last, so the chain reaction self-sustains at steady power (which can be any nonzero level, ruling out D).
18. Delayed neutrons make up only about β≈0.65% of all fission neutrons in U-235, yet they are critical for reactor control because…
Answer: B. Prompt neutrons alone would make the reactor respond in microseconds (uncontrollable); the small delayed fraction, released seconds after fission from fission-product decay, slows the effective population dynamics enough for control rods and feedback to act in time.
Fusion & nucleosynthesis · Week 8
19. D + T → He-4 + n, with m(D)=2.014102, m(T)=3.016049, m(He-4)=4.002602, m(n)=1.008665 u (1 u = 931.49 MeV). The Q-value is closest to…
Answer: C. Δm = (2.014102+3.016049) − (4.002602+1.008665) = 0.018884 u × 931.49 ≈ 17.6 MeV total. (A) and (B) are the individual kinetic energies carried by the He-4 and neutron, not the total Q; (D) is the proton-proton chain's net energy, a different reaction entirely.
20. D-T fusion has Q≈+17.6 MeV (strongly exothermic) yet still requires temperatures around 108 K to proceed, because…
Answer: D. The Coulomb barrier is a dynamical obstacle, unrelated to the rest-mass bookkeeping that sets Q. High temperature supplies the kinetic energy (and quantum tunneling does the rest) needed to overcome electrostatic repulsion before the short-range strong force can bind the nuclei.
21. A D-T plasma has n=5×1019 m-3, T=20 keV, τ=2 s. Its Lawson triple product nTτ, compared to the ignition threshold (~3×1021 keV·s/m³), is…
Answer: A. nTτ = (5×1019)(20)(2) = 2×1021 keV·s/m³, which is below the ~3×1021 threshold — this plasma has not yet reached ignition.
Radiation interaction, dose & shielding · Week 9
22. Gamma rays pass through a shield with linear attenuation coefficient μ=0.15 cm-1 and thickness x=10 cm. The fraction of the original intensity transmitted is closest to…
Answer: B. I/I0 = e−μx = e−(0.15)(10) = e−1.5 ≈ 0.223, i.e. about 22% transmitted.
23. 1 Gy of alpha-particle absorbed dose and 1 Gy of gamma-ray absorbed dose can cause very different biological harm, because…
Answer: C. Equivalent dose H = D × wR; alphas get wR ≈ 20 (dense ionization tracks, high biological damage per unit energy) versus wR = 1 for gammas, so the same absorbed energy (Gy) produces ~20× the equivalent dose (Sv) for alphas.
Capstone: Monte-Carlo particle transport · Week 10
24. In OpenMC (and most Monte-Carlo transport codes), a model is assembled from which ordered sequence of components?
Answer: D. You define Materials (what things are made of), then Geometry (the set built from those materials), then Settings (the source and batch counts), then Tallies (what to record), and finally Run. OpenMC enforces this ordering — it refuses to build a geometry with an undefined material, for instance.
25. In a Monte-Carlo transport calculation, the statistical (relative) uncertainty on a tally scales as 1/√N, where N is the number of particle histories simulated. To cut the uncertainty by a factor of 10, you must increase N by a factor of…
Answer: A. Uncertainty ∝ 1/√N, so to shrink it by 10× you need √N to grow by 10×, i.e. N must grow by 10² = 100. This is why Monte-Carlo convergence is expensive: each extra decimal place of precision costs a 100× increase in simulated histories.
