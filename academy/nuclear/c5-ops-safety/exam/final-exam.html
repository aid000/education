Plant Operations · Week 1
1. During approach to criticality, operators withdraw control rods in small steps and plot 1/M (inverse count-rate ratio) after each step. If 1/M keeps trending toward zero in a controlled, predictable way with each step, that indicates the core is…
Answer: A. A smooth, predictable 1/M trend toward zero is the expected signature of a subcritical core approaching k = 1 in a controlled way — the whole point of the 1/M method is to extrapolate safely without ever actually reaching criticality uncontrolled.
2. A reactor is to "load follow" — reduce power smoothly as grid demand drops overnight. The self-regulating feedback that lets fuel and coolant temperature settle at a new steady state without the operator constantly chasing power is…
Answer: B. A negative temperature coefficient means a power rise heats the core, which removes reactivity, damping the rise — the self-correcting feedback behind load-following. A positive coefficient would do the opposite and is not tolerated in the normal operating range.
Reactivity & Operational Limits · Week 2
3. A power-level automatic reactor trip (the LSSS) is set at 118% of rated power. The TRUE Safety Limit for this same parameter (e.g. minimum DNBR or fuel-centerline melt) is located…
Answer: C. The LSSS is the automatic trip setpoint, deliberately set with margin below the true Safety Limit so the protection system scrams the reactor well before DNBR or fuel-centerline melt could ever be reached. (A) and (B) confuse the two layers; (D) ignores the whole point of the layered hierarchy.
4. As a reactor is brought from cold, zero-power conditions up to full power, the net reactivity change from fuel and moderator heating (the power/temperature defect) in a well-designed LWR is…
Answer: D. Doppler broadening in the fuel and moderator expansion/density reduction both remove reactivity as temperature rises — the negative power coefficient that makes a reactor inherently self-stabilizing. A positive defect would create runaway feedback and is not permitted in the operating range.
5. The "iodine pit" — a transient rise in xenon-135 concentration ABOVE its prior equilibrium value — occurs because…
Answer: A. After a trip or power cut, fission (and new iodine production) drops toward zero, but the large existing iodine-135 inventory keeps decaying into xenon-135 for hours, pushing xenon temporarily above its pre-trip equilibrium before it falls back. (D) describes an unrelated, genuine startup transient — xenon building from zero, not overshooting equilibrium.
Defense-in-Depth · Week 3
6. A 3000 MW-thermal reactor scrams. Using P/P0 ≈ 0.066·t(-0.2) (valid once the reactor has run a long time), the decay heat approximately ONE HOUR (3600 s) after shutdown is closest to…
Answer: B. 0.066 × 3600-0.2 ≈ 1.28%, so Pdecay ≈ 0.0128 × 3000 MW ≈ 38 MW. (A) is the value right at scram (t ≈ 1 s). (C) is actually closer to the 1-DAY value, a common mix-up. (D) underestimates by roughly a factor of 6.
7. The defense-in-depth philosophy places multiple independent layers between radioactivity and the public because it assumes…
Answer: C. Defense-in-depth is built on the premise that no single barrier is trusted alone — each layer is assumed capable of failing, so independent layers are stacked so a failure in one is caught by the next.
Design-Basis LOCA & 10 CFR 50.46 · Week 4
8. Also under 10 CFR 50.46, the companion limit on local cladding oxidation is…
Answer: A. 10 CFR 50.46(b)(2) caps local cladding oxidation at 17% of the original thickness, alongside the 1204 °C PCT limit and the core-wide hydrogen-generation limit of 1% of the hypothetical maximum — together keeping the cladding ductile enough to stay coolable.
9. In a large-break LOCA, peak cladding temperature is typically reached during which phase?
Answer: B. During refill the core is poorly cooled (steam-blanketed) while decay heat keeps adding energy, so temperature peaks just before the reflood quench front arrives. (C) recirculation only handles the slowly-decaying long-term tail, well after the peak.
10. Under the NRC's 10 CFR 50.46, the peak cladding temperature acceptance limit for a design-basis LOCA is…
Answer: D. 10 CFR 50.46(b)(1) sets the PCT acceptance limit at 1204 °C (2200 °F) — well below where the zirconium-steam reaction and embrittlement would make the geometry uncoolable, with margin built in for licensing analysis.
Emergency Core Cooling Systems · Week 5
11. Compared with low-pressure injection (LPI), high-pressure injection (HPI) is designed to deliver…
Answer: C. HPI trades flow for head — it can push water against a still-pressurized system but only at modest flow. LPI does the opposite: low head, very high flow, which is actually what refloods a large break.
12. In a BWR, the Automatic Depressurization System (ADS) exists primarily to…
Answer: D. A BWR has no passive accumulators, so for a small break that would never blow the vessel down by itself, the ADS deliberately vents steam to force depressurization, letting LPCS/LPCI take over. (B) describes a PWR accumulator, which BWRs do not have.
13. The NRC's single-failure criterion, codified in 10 CFR 50 Appendix A (General Design Criteria, e.g. GDC 35 for ECCS), requires that a safety system…
Answer: A. The single-failure criterion is a design margin, not a claim of perfection: the plant must survive the design-basis accident plus the worst single active failure, which is why ECCS is built from redundant, independent trains.
Decay Heat & Loss-of-Flow · Week 6
14. Decay heat after shutdown follows a smooth power-law decline rather than a single clean exponential because…
Answer: B. Short-lived fission products burn off in seconds to minutes while long-lived ones linger for years; summing all those different decay constants produces a smooth power-law curve, not one clean exponential.
15. Modern passive-safety plant designs advertise that they can remove decay heat with no AC power and no operator action, using gravity-fed water and natural circulation, for roughly…
Answer: C. The commonly advertised passive-plant grace period is about 72 hours — long enough to buy time to restore active cooling, but still finite, since passive capacity is fixed.
16. A loss-of-flow accident (LOFA) is best described as…
Answer: D. A LOFA is about losing forced circulation (pump trip, bus failure) rather than losing coolant inventory (that's a LOCA) — the reactor trips on low flow, but decay heat still has to be carried off by coast-down flow or natural circulation.
Probabilistic Risk Assessment · Week 7
17. Core-damage frequency (CDF) is expressed in units of per reactor-year. The NRC's Safety Goal Policy Statement sets a benchmark around…
Answer: A. "Fewer than one core-damage event expected per 10,000 reactor-years" is the NRC's roughly 1e-4/ry CDF benchmark; LERF carries a similar idea forward to offsite release, with its own benchmark around 1e-5/ry.
18. Two redundant pumps are each 2.5% likely to fail; naive multiplication gives a combined failure probability of 0.0625%. This simple multiplication is undermined by…
Answer: B. The AND-gate multiplication only holds if failures are truly independent. Common-cause failure defeats that assumption and is the dominant contributor to risk in most real PRAs precisely because it undermines redundancy.
19. Stanley Kaplan framed probabilistic risk assessment around three questions. They are…
Answer: C. PRA enumerates accident sequences (what can go wrong), attaches a frequency to each (how likely), and multiplies by consequence to get risk — the Kaplan risk triplet.
Severe Accidents & Lessons Learned · Week 8
20. At Three Mile Island, the component that stuck open and drained coolant while a misleading instrument led operators to throttle back emergency injection was…
Answer: D. A stuck-open PORV on top of the pressurizer drained coolant while ambiguous instrumentation led operators to throttle back ECCS, believing the system was too full — a human-machine-interface failure, not a containment failure (containment held).
21. A key reactor-physics factor that made the Chernobyl accident possible, and that modern operating licenses forbid in the normal operating range, was…
Answer: A. The RBMK's positive void coefficient meant boiling added reactivity rather than removing it — combined with a flawed control-rod design and no robust Western-style containment, this drove the prompt-critical excursion and the uncontained release.
Radiation, Waste & Decommissioning · Week 9
22. Under the NRC's 10 CFR 20, the occupational effective-dose limit for a radiation worker is…
Answer: B. 10 CFR 20.1201 sets the occupational whole-body effective-dose limit at 50 mSv/yr (5 rem/yr); the public dose limit under 10 CFR 20.1301 is far lower, at 1 mSv/yr (100 mrem/yr) — option (A). ALARA keeps actual doses far below either limit.
23. Under the NRC's 10 CFR 61, low-level waste (LLW) is sorted into Classes A, B, and C, all of which may go to near-surface disposal. Waste exceeding the Class C limits — Greater-Than-Class-C (GTCC) — is handled differently because…
Answer: C. GTCC waste exceeds the Class C concentration limits entirely, so near-surface disposal (the pathway for Classes A/B/C) is not permitted — it requires a deep geologic facility, and by statute is the federal government's responsibility.
Capstone: Transient Analysis · Week 10
24. The Way–Wigner decay-heat correlation used consistently throughout this course takes the form P(t)/P0 = (prefactor) × [t(-0.2) − (t+T0)(-0.2)]. The prefactor used consistently across this course is…
Answer: D. Every lecture in this course uses 0.066 as the Way–Wigner prefactor — giving ~6.6% of pre-trip power at 1 s, ~1.3% at 1 hour, and ~0.68% at 1 day. Using a different prefactor in one place and 0.066 elsewhere would make a course's own numbers contradict each other.
25. A capstone transient analysis predicts a peak cladding temperature Tpeak below the acceptance limit Tlimit. Professional licensing practice treats this positive margin as trustworthy chiefly because…
Answer: A. Real licensing analyses deliberately bias every input against themselves (worst single failure, lost offsite power, high peaking factors, high decay heat, low conductance) so that if even the pessimistic model shows positive margin, the actual plant is known to be safer still — that is what makes the margin defensible.
