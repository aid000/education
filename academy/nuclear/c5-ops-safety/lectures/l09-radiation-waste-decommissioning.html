Radiation Protection, Waste & Decommissioning

A reactor's job ends, but its radioactivity does not — this week we follow the radiation out of the core: protecting the workers near it, controlling what leaves the fence, classifying the waste it becomes, and finally dismantling the plant itself.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the ALARA principle and the three levers — time, distance, shielding — that hold occupational dose down.
  • Compute dose from the point-source / inverse-square and shielding (attenuation) relations and explain why the goal is "low," not zero.
  • Describe how a plant controls gaseous and liquid effluents and why a tiny routine release is monitored against a dose limit.
  • Classify radioactive waste by activity and half-life under the NRC's 10 CFR 61 scheme (LLW Classes A/B/C, Greater-Than-Class-C, HLW / spent fuel) and match each to its disposal pathway.
  • Compare the main decommissioning strategies — immediate dismantling (DECON) versus deferred dismantling (SAFSTOR) — and the role of radioactive decay in the choice.

9.1ALARA & Occupational Dose

Every barrier in this course has been about keeping radioactivity away from people. When workers must operate near it — refueling, maintenance, inspection — the governing rule is ALARA: keep dose As Low As Reasonably Achievable, economic and social factors taken into account. Notice the word reasonably. ALARA is deliberately not "as low as possible," because driving the last microsievert to zero can cost more dose elsewhere (extra workers, longer jobs) than it saves. The goal is an optimized, justified low — not an impossible perfect.

Dose is measured as effective dose in sieverts (Sv); a millisievert (mSv) is a thousandth of that. In the United States, the controlling regulatory limits come from the NRC's 10 CFR 20 ("Standards for Protection Against Radiation"): the occupational limit is 50 mSv/yr (5 rem/yr) whole-body effective dose, while the public dose limit is far lower at 1 mSv/yr (100 mrem/yr). (Internationally, the ICRP recommends a lower occupational benchmark of 20 mSv/yr averaged over five years; US-licensed plants are governed by the NRC's 50 mSv/yr annual limit, with ALARA driving actual doses far below it in practice.) Background dose from nature is already a few mSv/yr, which is part of why "zero" is neither meaningful nor the target.

The radiation-protection toolkit reduces to three physical levers, drilled into every worker:

Dose ~ (dose rate) * time rate ~ 1 / distance^2 rate_shielded = rate_0 * exp(-mu * x)TIME: halve the time, halve the dose. DISTANCE: inverse-square — double the distance, quarter the rate. SHIELDING: exponential attenuation through thickness x with linear attenuation coefficient mu.

The most powerful of the three is usually distance, because the inverse-square law is so steep: a remote handling tool that doubles a worker's standoff cuts the rate to a quarter for free. Time is managed by planning and rehearsing jobs so people spend the fewest seconds in the field. Shielding — lead, concrete, water, or steel — attenuates the rate exponentially with thickness. Good radiation-protection design uses all three together.

AnalogyThink of standing near a campfire on a cold night. You can leave sooner (time), step back from the flames (distance), or hold up a shield like a log or a tarp (shielding). Each independently cuts how much heat you absorb, and the smart camper uses all three. Radiation dose works the same way — except the "warmth" is invisible and you trust your dosimeter, not your skin.

9.2Effluent Control: What Leaves the Fence

A normally operating plant does release small, deliberate, monitored amounts of radioactivity to the air and water — these are effluents. The philosophy is not "release nothing" (impossible) but "release so little, so well-characterized, that the dose to the most-exposed member of the public stays a small fraction of the limit." Every release is measured, logged, and reported.

Gaseous effluents — mainly noble gases (krypton, xenon) and trace iodine and tritium — pass through delay beds (letting short-lived isotopes decay), HEPA and charcoal filters, and continuous radiation monitors before going up the stack. Liquid effluents are collected, sampled, treated (filtration, demineralization, evaporation), held in tanks, and only released — diluted into a large water body — after the activity is verified below limits.

The link between a release and a dose is the dose factor: how many sieverts a member of the public receives per becquerel released, accounting for dispersion, the pathway (breathing, drinking, the food chain), and biology:

Public dose = (activity released, Bq) * (dose conversion factor, Sv/Bq)The plant works backward from the dose limit to a release limit. Monitoring keeps the actual release a small fraction of that — ALARA applied to effluents.

This is ALARA again, now pointed outward. The plant could in principle hold everything forever, but that creates its own hazards (storage volume, worker dose handling tanks), so the optimized answer is a tiny, filtered, monitored release whose public dose is far below natural background. The monitoring — not the absence of any release — is what makes it safe and accountable.

Common mistakeDo not equate "detectable" with "dangerous." Modern instruments can detect radioactivity at levels millions of times below anything biologically meaningful, so a routine effluent being measurable at the fence does not mean it delivers a meaningful dose. The right question is always "what dose, compared with the limit and with natural background?" — never merely "is anything detectable?"

9.3Waste Classes & Spent Fuel

Radioactive waste is not one thing. It is sorted by two properties that decide how it must be handled: how active it is (how fast it emits, hence how much shielding and cooling it needs) and how long-lived it is (how many half-lives until it decays to background). The US regulatory framework, set by the NRC in 10 CFR 61 ("Licensing Requirements for Land Disposal of Radioactive Waste"), sorts low-level waste (LLW) — contaminated gloves, tools, resins, filters, and most activated or contaminated hardware — into three classes of rising activity and rising disposal rigor:

LLW Class A -> Class B -> Class C -> Greater-Than-Class-C (GTCC) least restrictive most restrictive LLW exceeds Class C limits all near-surface disposal (10 CFR 61) not near-surface disposableClass A/B/C are all still near-surface-disposable LLW, just with increasing concentration limits, waste-form, and stability requirements. GTCC waste exceeds the Class C limits entirely and, like high-level waste, needs a deep geologic disposal facility instead.

Class A is the least hazardous and least restrictive LLW; Class C is the most active LLW still suitable for near-surface disposal, and carries the strictest packaging, stability, and intruder-protection requirements. Waste exceeding the Class C limits — Greater-Than-Class-C (GTCC) waste, mostly from reactor internals and sealed sources — is not permitted in near-surface disposal at all; by statute it is the federal government's responsibility, and (as of this writing) no permanent GTCC disposal facility yet exists in the US, so it remains in storage at reactor sites or other licensed facilities. High-level waste (HLW) and spent nuclear fuel sit outside the 10 CFR 61 LLW scheme entirely — they are regulated separately (interim dry-cask storage under 10 CFR 72, with an eventual deep geologic repository required by law) because they hold nearly all the radioactivity and generate decay heat for decades, needing active cooling first and then permanent geologic isolation. (Internationally, the IAEA instead describes a three-tier LLW / intermediate-level waste (ILW) / HLW scheme; the US NRC framework above — Classes A/B/C/GTCC plus a separate HLW/spent-fuel track — is what actually governs US-licensed plants.)

Spent fuel itself follows a now-familiar two-stage path driven by decay heat. Fresh out of the core it is intensely hot and radioactive, so it sits in a spent-fuel pool for years — water both cools it and shields it. Once the decay heat has fallen enough (the same power-law decline from Lecture 8), it can be moved to passively-cooled dry-cask storage on a concrete pad, awaiting a long-term repository. The reason for that wait is precisely the decay-heat curve you have modeled all course.

9.4Decommissioning: Taking the Plant Apart

When a plant reaches end of life, it must be decommissioned — defueled, decontaminated, dismantled, and the site released for other use. The core engineering insight is that radioactivity is your ally if you can wait: many of the activation products in the reactor structure (for example cobalt-60, with a ~5.3-year half-life) decay substantially over a few decades, so a component that is highly active at shutdown can be far easier and lower-dose to cut up later.

That tradeoff defines the two principal strategies. Immediate dismantling (DECON) begins teardown soon after shutdown — the site is released fastest, but workers face higher dose and the work is harder. Deferred dismantling (SAFSTOR) puts the plant into a monitored, safe-storage state for decades, letting short- and medium-lived isotopes decay before dismantling — lower worker dose, but the site is tied up longer and the institution must remain to steward it. (A third path, entombment, permanently encasing the activity in place, is rare for power reactors.)

The decay that makes SAFSTOR attractive follows the same exponential law as everything radioactive:

A(t) = A_0 * (1/2)^(t / T_half) equivalently A(t) = A_0 * exp(-lambda * t), lambda = ln 2 / T_halfA_0 = activity at shutdown, T_half = half-life of the dominant isotope. After n half-lives the activity is A_0 / 2^n — the quantitative reason waiting lowers dose.

So the choice between DECON and SAFSTOR is really an optimization — the same ALARA spirit as §9.1, now across decades: spend money and accept higher dose now to free the site, or wait, let decay do the shielding for free, and accept a long stewardship commitment. There is no universally "right" answer; it depends on the dominant isotopes, funding, site reuse plans, and workforce — exactly the kind of justified tradeoff the discussion this week asks you to weigh.

AnalogyDecommissioning timing is like deciding when to handle a hot pan. You can grab it now with thick gloves and extra care (DECON — fast, but more effort and risk), or set it aside and come back when it has cooled on its own (SAFSTOR — easier and safer to handle, but you wait and must keep the kitchen tied up). Radioactive decay is the pan cooling; the half-life tells you how long the wait buys you.
✦ Worked Example — distance, shielding, and the SAFSTOR wait

A maintenance task is done 1 m from a source where the dose rate is 8 mSv/h. (a) What is the rate if the worker uses a tool to stand 2 m away instead? (b) Adding a lead sheet with mu·x = 1.6 instead — by what factor does it cut the rate? (c) Activation product Co-60 (T½ = 5.3 yr) dominates a component; how much of its activity remains after a 50-year SAFSTOR wait?

  1. (a) Distance — inverse square. rate ~ 1/d^2, so going from 1 m to 2 m multiplies the rate by (1/2)^2 = 1/4. New rate = 8 × 1/4 = 2 mSv/h. Distance alone removed three-quarters of the dose rate, for free.
  2. (b) Shielding — exponential attenuation. Transmission = exp(−mu·x) = exp(−1.6) = 0.202. So the lead cuts the rate to about 20% (a factor of ~5 reduction).
  3. (c) Combine for the job. If the worker uses both the 2 m standoff and the shield: 8 × 1/4 × 0.202 ≈ 0.40 mSv/h — a 20× improvement from two levers stacked.
  4. (d) The SAFSTOR wait. Number of half-lives in 50 yr: n = 50 / 5.3 ≈ 9.43. Remaining fraction = (1/2)^9.43 = 2^(−9.43) ≈ 0.0014, i.e. ~0.14% of the shutdown activity.
  5. Interpret. Waiting ~50 years lets Co-60 decay by a factor of ~700 essentially for free — that is the quantitative case for SAFSTOR. The DECON case must justify accepting today's far-higher dose against the value of releasing the site sooner.

🛠️ Hands-On: model dose levers and SAFSTOR decay in Python

We reuse the same free stack from the Week-1 setup — NumPy for the math, matplotlib for plots, Jupyter if you like notebooks. (Full health-physics codes exist but are license-gated; an open Python model captures the same inverse-square, attenuation, and decay physics.) The install <details> in the early lectures covers the one-time environment; nothing new to install here.

Below we combine the three ALARA levers into a single dose estimate and then plot how a Co-60-dominated component's activity decays over a SAFSTOR wait.

# radprot.py — Lecture 9: ALARA levers and SAFSTOR decay
import numpy as np

# --- (1) dose from the three levers: time, distance, shielding ---
rate0   = 8.0      # dose rate at reference distance, mSv/h
d0, d1  = 1.0, 2.0 # reference and working distance, m
mu_x    = 1.6      # shield optical thickness (mu * x), dimensionless
t_job   = 0.5      # time in the field, h

rate_dist   = rate0 * (d0 / d1)**2          # inverse-square
rate_shield = rate_dist * np.exp(-mu_x)     # exponential attenuation
dose_job    = rate_shield * t_job           # dose = rate * time
print(f"rate after distance : {rate_dist:.2f} mSv/h")
print(f"rate after shielding: {rate_shield:.3f} mSv/h")
print(f"job dose            : {dose_job:.3f} mSv")

# --- (2) SAFSTOR: Co-60 activity over a deferred-dismantling wait ---
T_half = 5.3                       # Co-60 half-life, years
lam    = np.log(2) / T_half
years  = np.array([0, 5, 10, 20, 50])
frac   = 0.5 ** (years / T_half)   # = exp(-lam * years)
for y, f in zip(years, frac):
    print(f"t = {y:2d} yr  ->  activity = {f*100:6.2f}% of shutdown value")

# rate after distance : 2.00 mSv/h
# rate after shielding: 0.404 mSv/h
# job dose            : 0.202 mSv
# t =  0 yr  ->  activity = 100.00%
# t = 50 yr  ->  activity =   0.14%

The three levers stack multiplicatively — that is why radiation-protection planning attacks all of them at once. Experiment: sweep the SAFSTOR wait from 0 to 80 yr and plot activity vs time on a log axis to see the straight-line exponential, then add a second isotope with a longer half-life and watch it dominate the tail — the reason SAFSTOR helps most when short-lived activation products govern the dose.

Model response

A strong answer recognizes that ALARA is an optimization principle, not a zero-tolerance rule. Driving dose to literally zero would demand costs — more workers sharing a job to shorten each person's time, longer and more complex procedures, extra shielding and handling that itself generates waste and risk — that can increase total risk and dose elsewhere even as the original task's dose drops. Meanwhile, natural background already delivers a few mSv/yr that no plant-side effort can eliminate, so "zero" from the plant was never the true floor anyway. The right question is never "can we reach zero" but "is the next increment of dose reduction worth what it costs, in money, in other workers' dose, and in schedule."

That same logic cascades directly into this week's other topics. Effluent control: holding every gaseous and liquid release forever to achieve "zero" would mean ever-growing storage tanks with their own leak and worker-handling risks; the optimized answer is a tiny, filtered, continuously monitored release kept far below the public dose limit (1 mSv/yr under the NRC's 10 CFR 20) — monitored and justified, not merely absent. Waste classification: sorting waste into LLW Classes A/B/C (near-surface disposal) versus GTCC and HLW/spent fuel (deep geologic isolation) is itself an ALARA judgment — spending maximum disposal rigor on genuinely high-hazard material while not over-engineering disposal for low-activity waste. Decommissioning: DECON versus SAFSTOR is an explicit decades-long ALARA tradeoff — dismantle now at higher worker dose to free the site faster, or wait for isotopes like Co-60 to decay and cut worker dose at the cost of a long stewardship commitment. There is no universally "zero-risk" choice in any of these — only a justified, reasoned optimum given funding, site-reuse plans, and workforce.

Q1. The ALARA principle states that occupational dose should be kept:
A. exactly at the regulatory limit
B. as low as reasonably achievable, accounting for economic and social factors
C. at zero, with no exceptions
D. equal to the natural background dose
Show answer

B. ALARA optimizes dose downward to a justified low, weighing cost and effort. (A) The limit is a ceiling, not a target — you stay well below it. (C) Zero is neither achievable nor the goal; chasing it can cost more dose than it saves. (D) Background is a reference point, not the ALARA target.

Q2. A worker stands 1 m from a source reading 9 mSv/h. Using a tool to work from 3 m instead, the dose rate becomes approximately:
A. 3 mSv/h
B. 27 mSv/h
C. 1 mSv/h
D. 9 mSv/h (distance does not matter)
Show answer

C. Inverse-square: rate scales as 1/d², so tripling the distance divides the rate by 9: 9 ÷ 9 = 1 mSv/h. (A) is a factor of 3, not 9. (B) multiplies instead of divides. (D) is false — distance is usually the strongest lever.

Q3. Why does a normally operating plant deliberately release small, monitored effluents rather than retaining everything?
A. Because any release, however large, is harmless
B. Because regulators require the maximum possible release
C. Because detectable always means dangerous, so it is hidden
D. Because retaining everything is impossible and creates its own hazards, so an optimized, monitored release whose public dose is far below the limit is the ALARA answer
Show answer

D. ALARA pointed outward: tiny, filtered, monitored releases keep public dose far below limits, while holding everything forever would create storage and worker-dose hazards. (A) is false — magnitude and monitoring matter. (B) regulators set ceilings, not minimum releases. (C) detectable is not the same as dangerous; the question is the dose.

Q4. Which best describes high-level waste / spent fuel relative to low-level waste?
A. Large volume, low activity, near-surface disposal
B. Small volume but nearly all the activity and heat, requiring cooling then deep geologic isolation
C. No radioactivity, disposed as ordinary trash
D. Identical to low-level waste in every respect
Show answer

B. HLW/spent fuel is a small volume holding nearly all the activity and decay heat, so it needs active cooling (pools, then dry casks) and ultimately deep geologic disposal. (A) describes LLW. (C) is false — it is the most radioactive waste. (D) inverts the whole classification.

Q5. The main reason deferred dismantling (SAFSTOR) can lower worker dose compared with immediate dismantling (DECON) is that:
A. waiting lets short- and medium-lived activation products radioactively decay before the work begins
B. the radioactivity is permanently sealed in concrete and never handled
C. newer tools eliminate all radiation
D. the fuel becomes more radioactive over time, which is safer
Show answer

A. During the storage period, isotopes like Co-60 (T½ ~5.3 yr) decay by large factors, so later dismantling delivers far less dose. (B) describes entombment, a different strategy. (C) no tool removes radiation, only attenuates or distances it. (D) is false — activity falls with time, and more activity would not be safer.
