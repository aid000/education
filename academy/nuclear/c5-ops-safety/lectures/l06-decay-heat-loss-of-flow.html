Week 6 · Lecture 6

Decay Heat & Loss-of-Flow Transients

Press SCRAM and the chain reaction dies in a heartbeat — but the core keeps pouring out megawatts of heat for days, and removing it becomes the single hardest problem in reactor safety.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a scrammed reactor still produces substantial power, and where decay heat comes from physically.
  • Estimate decay-heat fraction over time using the standard ~7% initial / power-law decline rule of thumb.
  • Trace the timeline of a loss-of-flow accident (LOFA) and a station blackout (SBO) from trip to potential core damage.
  • Describe how natural circulation and passive backup cooling remove decay heat when forced flow is lost.
  • Articulate why decay-heat removal — not reactivity control — is the central safety challenge after shutdown.

6.1Why Shutting Down Is Not Enough

When an operator presses SCRAM, control rods drop into the core in seconds and the prompt fission chain reaction collapses. Fission power falls by more than 90% almost instantly. It is tempting to conclude that the safety problem is solved — the reactor is "off." It is not. The fuel that has been running for months is now loaded with hundreds of radioactive fission products, and those nuclei do not care that the chain reaction stopped. They keep decaying, and every decay deposits energy in the fuel as decay heat.

Decay heat has two main sources. First, the radioactive decay of fission products — beta particles and gamma rays from the unstable fragments left behind by fission, which thermalize right in the fuel pin. Second, a smaller contribution from delayed fissions driven by neutrons and the decay of heavy actinides bred during operation. The first dominates after shutdown.

The crucial number: immediately after a trip from full power, decay heat is roughly 6–7% of the previous operating power. For a large 3000 MW-thermal reactor, that is over 200 MW of heat with the chain reaction completely dead — enough to boil away the entire coolant inventory in minutes if you do nothing.

P_decay(t=0+) ~ 0.07 * P_0P_0 = power level before shutdown; the fission-product inventory is proportional to how long and how hard the fuel ran.
AnalogyThink of a cast-iron skillet pulled off a hot burner. Turn the stove off (that is the SCRAM) and the flame is gone instantly — but the skillet stays dangerously hot for a long time because of the thermal energy already stored in it. Decay heat is the reactor's "hot skillet": the source that produced it is gone, yet the heat keeps coming and must still be carried away.

6.2How Decay Heat Decays: The Power Law

Decay heat does not vanish on a single clean half-life because it is the sum of thousands of fission-product chains, each with its own decay constant. Short-lived isotopes burn off in seconds to minutes; long-lived ones linger for years. The net result is a smooth power-law decline rather than a single exponential. A widely used engineering approximation (a simplified form of the Way–Wigner correlation) captures it:

P_decay(t) / P_0 ~ 0.066 * t^(-0.2)t in seconds after shutdown, valid from a few seconds to ~10^6 s; assumes long prior operation at steady power P_0.

Read what this curve tells you. At 1 second after trip, decay heat is about 6.6% of full power. At 1 hour (3600 s) it has fallen to roughly 1.3%. At 1 day (~86400 s) it is near 0.7%, and after a week it is around 0.4%. The heat drops fast at first, then crawls — which is exactly why the first minutes after a trip are the most demanding for the cooling system, and why spent fuel must still be actively cooled for years.

The total energy you must remove is the integral of this curve. Because the exponent is small (0.2), the tail is "heavy": even though the instantaneous power is small after a day, the cumulative energy over the following weeks is substantial. This is the physics behind spent-fuel pools and long-term passive cooling requirements.

Common mistakeDo not assume "reactor tripped" means "no more heat to remove." The chain reaction stops, but decay heat is an independent, unstoppable source set by the fission-product inventory. You cannot SCRAM decay heat away — you can only carry it off with coolant. Treating a tripped core as thermally safe is the conceptual error behind several real-world severe accidents.

6.3Loss-of-Flow and Station-Blackout Transients

During normal operation, large reactor coolant pumps force coolant through the core fast enough to carry away full power. A loss-of-flow accident (LOFA) happens when that forced circulation is degraded or lost — a pump trips, a power bus fails, or a pipe breaks. The reactor will trip on low flow, killing fission power, but decay heat keeps arriving while the coolant slows. Now the question is whether the remaining flow (coasting pumps, then natural circulation) can carry off that decay heat before fuel temperatures climb past their limits.

The most demanding version is a station blackout (SBO): loss of all off-site and on-site AC power. The pumps stop, instrument air may bleed down, and the only heat sinks left are whatever can run without AC power — battery-backed valves, steam-driven pumps, and ultimately passive natural circulation. SBO is a defining scenario in reactor safety analysis precisely because it strips away the active systems and forces the plant to survive on passive physics.

The core energy balance during any of these transients is simple to state:

m * c_p * dT/dt = P_decay(t) - Q_removed(t)m c_p = thermal mass of fuel + coolant; if heat removed lags heat generated, dT/dt > 0 and temperature rises.

If Q_removed > P_decay, temperatures fall and the core is safe. If Q_removed < P_decay, temperatures rise — and if they rise far enough, the coolant boils off, fuel cladding overheats and can react with steam, and the path toward core damage opens. The entire art of post-shutdown safety is keeping that inequality on the right side, with margin, using systems that do not depend on the power you just lost.

6.4Natural Circulation & Passive Backup Cooling

When the pumps stop, physics offers a free backup: natural circulation. Heat from the core warms the coolant, making it less dense; cooler, denser coolant sits in the heat exchanger or steam generator above. Gravity does the rest — the warm fluid rises through the core and the cool fluid sinks, setting up a self-sustaining loop with no moving parts. The driving force is buoyancy from the density difference over the height between the heat source (core) and the heat sink:

Delta P_driving = (rho_cold - rho_hot) * g * HH = elevation difference between core and heat sink; larger H and larger temperature difference give stronger natural-circulation flow.

Natural circulation is weak compared with full pumped flow — it carries perhaps a few percent of rated flow — but that is often exactly the right scale, because decay heat is also only a few percent of rated power. This matching is why modern plant designs deliberately place heat sinks high above the core: the taller the loop, the stronger the natural-circulation driving head when you need it most.

Passive safety systems push this idea further. Gravity-driven water tanks, condensers that dump heat to a large elevated pool, and air-cooled containment shells are all designed to remove decay heat for hours to days with no AC power, no pumps, and no operator action. They trade the high capacity of active systems for the priceless property of working when everything else has failed — directly answering the station-blackout problem.

AnalogyNatural circulation is a hot-air balloon for liquids. Warm the air (or coolant) and it becomes buoyant and rises on its own; cool it and it sinks. No fan, no pump — just the density difference and gravity. A reactor designed for natural circulation is one where the core can keep cooling itself the way a balloon keeps rising, as long as there is a hot bottom and a cold top.
✦ Worked Example — how fast does the coolant heat up in a station blackout?

A 3000 MW-thermal reactor trips into a complete station blackout: no pumps, no active heat removal at all (Q_removed = 0). The reactor coolant system holds about m = 2.5×10⁵ kg of water with c_p ≈ 5000 J/(kg·K) at operating conditions. Estimate the coolant heat-up rate at 10 seconds and at 1 hour after trip.

  1. Decay heat at 10 s: P/P_0 = 0.066 × 10^(−0.2) = 0.066 × 0.631 = 0.0417, so P_decay = 0.0417 × 3000 MW ≈ 125 MW.
  2. Heat-up rate at 10 s: dT/dt = P_decay / (m c_p) = 125×10⁶ W / (2.5×10⁵ × 5000) = 1.25×10⁸ / 1.25×10⁹ = 0.10 K/s — about 6 K per minute.
  3. Decay heat at 1 hr: P/P_0 = 0.066 × 3600^(−0.2) = 0.066 × 0.193 = 0.0127, so P_decay ≈ 0.0127 × 3000 MW ≈ 38 MW.
  4. Heat-up rate at 1 hr: dT/dt = 38×10⁶ / 1.25×10⁹ ≈ 0.030 K/s — about 1.8 K per minute.
  5. Interpretation: with zero heat removal, the coolant would reach boiling within minutes and the inventory would start to boil away — which is why some passive or backup heat sink must engage long before then. The early minutes dominate the challenge: the heat-up rate at 10 s is more than 3× that at 1 hr.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate a loss-of-flow heat-up in Python

For the thermal-hydraulics weeks we use the free Python scientific stack — NumPy for arrays and SciPy for ODE integration (the industry code RELAP5-3D is license-gated, so we build a transparent lumped-parameter model instead). If you already set up the course environment in Week 1/2, you have everything you need; SciPy ships in that same conda/pip environment.

We integrate the lumped energy balance dT/dt = (P_decay(t) − Q_removed) / (m·c_p), using the power-law decay-heat model and a fixed passive heat-removal capacity.

# lofa_heatup.py — Lecture 6: decay-heat-driven coolant heat-up
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

P0   = 3000e6        # pre-trip thermal power, W
m    = 2.5e5         # coolant mass, kg
cp   = 5000.0        # specific heat, J/(kg.K)
Q_passive = 30e6     # passive heat sink capacity, W (constant)
T0   = 560.0         # initial coolant temp, K

def decay_heat(t):
    # Way-Wigner style power law; clamp t to avoid t=0 singularity
    t = np.maximum(t, 1.0)
    return 0.066 * P0 * t**(-0.2)

def dTdt(t, T):
    return (decay_heat(t) - Q_passive) / (m * cp)

sol = solve_ivp(dTdt, [1, 7200], [T0], dense_output=True,
                t_eval=[1, 10, 60, 600, 3600, 7200])

for t, T in zip(sol.t, sol.y[0]):
    print(f"t = {t:7.0f} s   P_decay = {decay_heat(t)/1e6:6.1f} MW   T = {T:6.1f} K")

# Early on decay heat >> Q_passive, so T climbs fast;
# once decay heat falls below the 30 MW sink, T peaks and turns over.

Try sweeping Q_passive from 20 MW to 60 MW: you will find a threshold capacity below which the coolant reaches boiling before decay heat drops enough — the quantitative version of "is the passive sink big enough?" that safety analysts compute for real plants.

💬 Discussion

Modern passive plants advertise that they can remove decay heat for roughly 72 hours with no AC power and no operator action, relying on gravity-fed water and natural circulation. Active plants instead rely on diesel generators and pumps that can run essentially indefinitely as long as they keep working and have fuel. If you were arguing for one design philosophy over the other for decay-heat removal, which would you defend, and what is the key trade-off you would have to confront? Consider what each approach assumes about the worst-case scenario.

Show a model response

A strong answer recognizes that the two approaches optimize for different failure modes, so the "right" choice depends on which scenario you fear most.

Defending passive: The decisive advantage is that passive systems remove the failure mode that has caused the most severe real accidents — prolonged loss of all power. Natural circulation and gravity feed depend only on physics that cannot be switched off, so they survive a station blackout that would defeat diesels and pumps. The 72-hour grace period buys time to restore active cooling without any operator action or power. The trade-off you must confront: passive capacity is fixed and finite. After the grace period, you still need an active path (refilling tanks, restoring power), and natural circulation carries only a few percent of rated flow, so it works precisely because decay heat is small — it could not cool a core at full power.

Defending active: Diesels and pumps can run indefinitely and remove far more heat, giving margin and flexibility a fixed passive tank cannot. The trade-off: this depends on equipment continuing to function and on a fuel/maintenance supply chain — a common-cause event (flood, earthquake, sustained grid loss) can disable all of it at once, which is exactly the scenario passive systems are built to survive.

✓ Self-check rubric: A complete answer (1) correctly states that decay heat, not reactivity, is the post-shutdown challenge; (2) ties passive cooling to the station-blackout failure mode; (3) names a concrete trade-off (finite grace period / low flow capacity vs. dependence on power and equipment); and (4) avoids claiming either approach is risk-free.

Q1. Immediately after a reactor trips from full power, roughly what fraction of its previous thermal power does it still produce as decay heat?
A. About 6–7%
B. Essentially 0% — the chain reaction has stopped
C. About 50%
D. About 100% — unchanged from operation
Show answer

A. Decay heat starts at roughly 6–7% of pre-trip power. (B) ignores fission-product decay, the whole point of the lecture. (C) and (D) vastly overstate it — fission power itself collapses by >90% at SCRAM; only the decay-heat remainder persists.

Q2. What is the dominant physical source of decay heat in the seconds-to-hours after shutdown?
A. Residual prompt fission from neutrons still in the core
B. Friction from the coasting coolant pumps
C. Radioactive decay (beta and gamma) of fission products accumulated in the fuel
D. Chemical reaction between the cladding and coolant
Show answer

C. Beta and gamma decay of fission products thermalizing in the fuel dominates. (A) Prompt fission essentially ends at SCRAM. (B) Pump friction is negligible and stops anyway. (D) Cladding-steam reaction only occurs if the core has already overheated badly — it is a consequence, not the source.

Q3. Using P/P_0 ≈ 0.066·t^(−0.2), decay heat one hour (3600 s) after trip is closest to which fraction of full power?
A. About 6.6%
B. About 1.3%
C. About 0.07%
D. About 5%
Show answer

B. 0.066 × 3600^(−0.2) = 0.066 × 0.193 ≈ 0.0127, i.e. ~1.3%. (A) is the value near t = 1 s, not 1 hr. (C) is off by a factor of ~20. (D) ignores the power-law decline entirely.

Q4. What primarily drives natural circulation cooling when the coolant pumps have stopped?
A. Magnetic forces on the coolant
B. Residual momentum from the stopped pumps
C. Electrostatic repulsion in the fluid
D. Buoyancy from the density difference between hot and cold coolant over the loop height
Show answer

D. Warm, less-dense coolant rises and cool, denser coolant sinks; the driving head is (rho_cold − rho_hot)·g·H. (B) Residual momentum lasts only seconds during coastdown, not the sustained loop. (A) and (C) are not real mechanisms in this context.

Q5. Why is a station blackout (loss of all AC power) considered a defining reactor-safety scenario?
A. It causes an immediate prompt-critical power excursion
B. It strips away the active pumps and systems, leaving decay-heat removal to passive physics and limited backups
C. It instantly stops decay heat, so no cooling is needed
D. It only matters for reactors that are shut down for refueling
Show answer

B. SBO removes the AC-powered cooling systems, so the plant must remove decay heat with battery/steam-driven backups and passive natural circulation. (A) confuses it with a reactivity accident; SCRAM still works. (C) is backwards — decay heat continues regardless of power. (D) is false — SBO is most demanding for an operating core at full decay-heat load.
