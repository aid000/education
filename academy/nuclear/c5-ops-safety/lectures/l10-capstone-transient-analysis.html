Capstone: A Transient / Safety Analysis

When the pumps trip and the core still glows with decay heat, a few lines of Python — not a press release — decide whether you sleep at night: this week you build that model and defend its safety margin.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Model decay heat with the Wigner–Way correlation and balance it against a simple convective cooling term.
  • Integrate the energy balance with SciPy's solve_ivp to find the peak fuel/coolant temperature after the trip.

10.1The Transient: What Happens When the Pumps Stop

Everything in this course has been building toward one moment of judgment: a plant is running steadily, then something fails, and you must predict whether the fuel stays cool enough. This capstone takes the simplest non-trivial version of that question — a loss of forced flow (the reactor trips and the main coolant pumps coast down) — and turns it into a model you can run on a laptop.

The key physical fact is that shutting a reactor down does not stop the heat. The fission chain reaction ceases within a fraction of a second once control rods insert, but the fuel is packed with radioactive fission products that keep decaying. Their beta and gamma emissions deposit energy in the fuel for hours and days. This is decay heat, and it is the reason a reactor that "scrammed" perfectly can still melt if cooling is lost — the lesson written into every modern safety system.

Right at shutdown, decay heat is roughly 6–7% of the pre-trip thermal power. For a 3000 MW-thermal core that is over 200 MW — the output of a small power station — appearing in a core that suddenly has no forced cooling. The transient is a race: decay heat falls with time, but if cooling falls faster, temperature climbs. Our job is to model both curves and see who wins.

AnalogyThink of a kettle you just unplugged. The heating element is off (the chain reaction stopped), but the element is still glowing hot and keeps dumping heat into the water for a while (decay heat). If you also cut off the air cooling the kettle, the water can still come to a boil after you pulled the plug. A reactor is the same idea, except the "glow" comes from radioactive decay and lasts for days, not seconds.

10.2Modeling Decay Heat: The Wigner–Way Curve

To predict temperature we first need the heat source as a function of time. A widely used engineering correlation for decay power after shutdown is the Wigner–Way form, which captures how the mix of short- and long-lived fission products decays:

P_decay(t) = P_0 * 0.0622 * [ t^(-0.2) - (t + T0)^(-0.2) ]P_0 = pre-trip thermal power; t = time since shutdown (s); T0 = reactor operating time before shutdown (s). Valid for t roughly 1 s to 1e8 s.

The bracket has two pieces. The t^(-0.2) term is the heat that would be released if the reactor had run forever; the (t + T0)^(-0.2) term subtracts off the contribution that has not yet built up, because the reactor only ran for a finite time T0. For a core that has operated many months, T0 is huge and the second term is tiny early on, so near t = 1 s the fraction is close to the familiar 6–7%.

The crucial qualitative feature is the power-law decay: heat falls off slowly, not exponentially. After 1 second you have ~6.5%, after 1 hour ~1.4%, after a day ~0.6%. That long tail is exactly why spent fuel needs cooling for years, and why the first minutes after a trip are the most dangerous — power is highest and the heat sink may not yet be established.

Common mistakeDo not evaluate the Wigner–Way correlation at t = 0. The t^(-0.2) term diverges to infinity at t = 0 — it is a fit valid only for t > ~1 s. Students who naively start their time array at zero get an inf or a wild first point that corrupts the whole integration. Always start the clock at a small positive time (e.g. t = 1 s) or clip the array.

10.3The Energy Balance: Heat In, Heat Out, Temperature Up

Now we conserve energy. Lump the fuel-plus-coolant inventory into a single thermal mass with heat capacity m*c_p (joules per kelvin). Its temperature rises when the decay heat deposited exceeds the heat carried away by cooling. That is a first-order ordinary differential equation:

m * c_p * dT/dt = P_decay(t) - h * A * (T - T_sink)m*c_p = thermal capacity (J/K); P_decay = decay heat source (W); h*A = effective heat-transfer conductance (W/K); T_sink = ultimate heat-sink temperature.

The cooling term h*A*(T - T_sink) is Newton's law of cooling: the hotter the core relative to its heat sink, the faster heat leaves. After forced flow is lost, the conductance h*A collapses to whatever natural circulation or passive cooling provides — often a small fraction of the pumped value. That collapse is the whole danger.

This single equation already tells the story. Early on, P_decay is large and (T − T_sink) is small, so dT/dt > 0 and temperature climbs. As T rises, the cooling term grows; as t grows, P_decay shrinks. The peak temperature occurs exactly when the two balance — when dT/dt = 0:

at the peak: P_decay(t_peak) = h * A * (T_peak - T_sink)Setting dT/dt = 0. Solve for T_peak once you know the time t_peak at which heat in equals heat out.

Because P_decay falls monotonically and the cooling term grows with T, there is a single peak. Find it, compare it to the limit, and you have your answer. In practice we rarely solve this by hand — the source term is a power law and the equation is stiff early on — so we integrate it numerically, which is what Section 10.4 builds.

10.4Interpreting the Safety Margin & Framing the Deliverable

A peak temperature on its own means nothing. Engineering safety is always stated as a margin: the distance between your predicted peak and a regulator- or design-defined acceptance limit. For PWR fuel cladding, a common limit is the point where rapid oxidation runs away (around 1200 °C / 1473 K for zirconium cladding). The margin is simply:

margin = T_limit - T_peakPositive margin = the limit is not reached. Often expressed as a fraction: (T_limit - T_peak) / (T_limit - T_initial).

A positive margin means your design survives the transient with room to spare; a negative margin means the limit is exceeded and the design (or the cooling assumptions) must change. The number alone is not enough — a good analysis states the margin and the conditions under which it holds: which conductance you assumed, whether you took the conservative (worst-case) decay-heat fraction, and how sensitive the margin is to each input.

Conservatism is the professional habit that makes a margin trustworthy. Real licensing analyses deliberately stack assumptions against themselves — highest plausible decay heat, lowest plausible cooling, hottest starting point — so that the true plant is always safer than the model. If even your pessimistic model shows positive margin, you have a defensible result.

That is your capstone deliverable: a short report that (1) states the scenario and assumptions, (2) presents the model equations, (3) shows the integrated temperature history and the peak, (4) computes the margin against a stated limit, and (5) honestly discusses the limitations — a lumped single-node model is not RELAP5-3D, and you should say so.

AnalogyA safety margin is like the clearance under a bridge. The sign does not say "trucks are 4.0 m tall and the bridge is 4.1 m" — it gives you the margin: "low clearance, 0.1 m." What matters for the driver is the gap, computed with the tallest truck and the lowest point of the arch. Quote the peak without the limit, and you have told the driver the truck height but hidden the bridge.
✦ Worked Example — peak temperature after a loss of forced flow

A test loop trips from P_0 = 20 MW after a long run (treat T0 as effectively infinite, so use the leading t^(-0.2) term). Lumped thermal capacity m*c_p = 4.0e7 J/K. After flow is lost, natural-circulation conductance is h*A = 1.5e5 W/K to a sink at T_sink = 320 K. Estimate the peak temperature and the margin to a 1473 K cladding limit.

  1. Decay heat at the danger window. Take t = 100 s as a representative early time. Fraction = 0.0622 × 100^(-0.2) = 0.0622 × 0.398 = 0.0247, so P_decay ≈ 0.0247 × 20 MW = 0.495 MW = 4.95e5 W.
  2. Quasi-steady temperature if held at that power. Setting dT/dt = 0: T − T_sink = P_decay / (h*A) = 4.95e5 / 1.5e5 = 3.3 K rise. That is small — at 100 s cooling easily keeps up.
  3. Check the worst early instant. At t = 1 s, fraction = 0.0622 × 1 = 0.0622, so P_decay = 1.24e6 W. Steady rise = 1.24e6 / 1.5e5 = 8.3 K. Still modest because conductance is healthy here.
  4. Account for thermal lag. The core cannot heat instantly: the time constant is τ = (m*c_p)/(h*A) = 4.0e7 / 1.5e5 ≈ 267 s. Over that window decay heat has already dropped, so the true peak rise lands between the two estimates — roughly 6–8 K above sink, i.e. T_peak ≈ 326–328 K.
  5. Margin. margin = T_limit − T_peak = 1473 − 328 = ~1145 K, hugely positive. Interpretation: with this (generous) conductance the loop is nowhere near the limit. The real design question is: how far can h*A degrade before the margin vanishes? Solving 1473 − 320 = P_decay,1s / (h*A) gives h*A ≈ 1.24e6 / 1153 ≈ 1.1e3 W/K — cooling would have to fall ~140× before the limit is threatened. That sensitivity is the headline result.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate the transient in Python

We use the same free stack from Week 1/2 (NumPy + Jupyter, install details in the early lectures) and add SciPy for ODE integration. Industry codes like RELAP5-3D are license-gated, so this open Python model is our stand-in — same physics, fewer nodes. SciPy's solve_ivp handles the stiff early transient with an implicit method.

⬇ Add SciPy to your Week-1 environment (one extra package)

If you set up the conda or venv environment in Week 1, just add SciPy:

conda activate nuclear
conda install -c conda-forge scipy        # or: pip install scipy

Verify: run python -c "import scipy; print(scipy.__version__)" — a version number means you're ready.

# transient.py — Lecture 10 capstone: loss-of-forced-flow peak temperature
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

# --- plant / scenario parameters (state your assumptions!) ---
P0      = 20e6        # pre-trip thermal power, W
mcp     = 4.0e7       # lumped thermal capacity, J/K
hA      = 1.5e5       # natural-circulation conductance after trip, W/K
T_sink  = 320.0       # ultimate heat-sink temperature, K
T0_run  = 1e8         # prior operating time, s (long run)
T_limit = 1473.0      # cladding acceptance limit, K

def decay_heat(t):
    # Wigner-Way correlation; clip t to >=1 s so t^-0.2 stays finite
    t = np.maximum(t, 1.0)
    frac = 0.0622 * (t**-0.2 - (t + T0_run)**-0.2)
    return P0 * frac

def dTdt(t, T):
    # energy balance: heat in minus Newtonian cooling out
    return (decay_heat(t) - hA * (T - T_sink)) / mcp

sol = solve_ivp(dTdt, [1.0, 1e5], [T_sink],
                method="BDF", dense_output=True,    # BDF = stiff-stable
                rtol=1e-8, atol=1e-6)

t = np.logspace(0, 5, 4000)
T = sol.sol(t)[0]
T_peak = T.max()
t_peak = t[np.argmax(T)]
margin = T_limit - T_peak

print(f"Peak temperature : {T_peak:7.2f} K at t = {t_peak:.1f} s")
print(f"Acceptance limit : {T_limit:7.2f} K")
print(f"Safety margin    : {margin:7.2f} K  ({'PASS' if margin > 0 else 'FAIL'})")

# Peak temperature :  327.9x K at t ~ a few hundred s
# Safety margin    : ~1145 K  (PASS)  -- generous conductance assumed

Now make it a real study: loop hA from 1e5 down to 1e3 W/K and plot margin vs conductance to find where it crosses zero. That sensitivity curve — not the single peak — is what a reviewer wants to see, and it is the heart of your capstone report.

Q1. Immediately after a reactor scrams (control rods fully inserted), why does the fuel keep producing significant heat?
A. The fission chain reaction continues at full power for several minutes
B. Radioactive fission products in the fuel continue to decay, releasing decay heat
C. The control rods themselves become a heat source
D. Coolant friction heats the fuel
Show answer

B. Decay of accumulated fission products supplies ~6–7% of pre-trip power right after shutdown and decays slowly. (A) is false — the chain reaction stops within a fraction of a second once rods insert. (C) Control rods absorb neutrons; they are not a meaningful heat source. (D) With pumps tripped there is little forced flow, and friction is negligible compared to decay heat.

Q2. The Wigner–Way correlation gives decay power as P_0 × 0.0622 × [t^(-0.2) − (t+T0)^(-0.2)]. What goes wrong if you evaluate it at t = 0?
A. It returns exactly zero
B. It returns the full pre-trip power P_0
C. The t^(-0.2) term diverges to infinity; the correlation is only valid for t greater than about 1 s
D. Nothing — t = 0 is the most accurate point
Show answer

C. t^(-0.2) blows up as t -> 0, so the fit is restricted to t > ~1 s; you must start the clock at a small positive time. (A) and (B) misstate the limit — the term grows without bound, it does not go to 0 or to P_0. (D) is the opposite of the truth; t = 0 is exactly where the formula fails.

Q3. In the energy balance m·c_p·dT/dt = P_decay(t) − h·A·(T − T_sink), the peak core temperature occurs at the instant when…
A. the decay heat equals the convective heat removal, so dT/dt = 0
B. the decay heat is at its maximum (t = 1 s)
C. the coolant reaches the sink temperature
D. the thermal capacity m·c_p reaches zero
Show answer

A. Temperature peaks when heat-in balances heat-out, i.e. dT/dt = 0. (B) is wrong because of thermal lag — the core has not yet heated up at t = 1 s; the peak comes later. (C) The core is hotter than the sink throughout. (D) m·c_p is a fixed property, not something that goes to zero.

Q4. A loss-of-flow analysis predicts T_peak = 1300 K against a cladding limit of 1473 K. What is the correct safety statement?
A. The design fails because 1300 K is dangerously hot
B. Margin is irrelevant; only the peak value matters
C. There is a positive margin of about 173 K, but it must be qualified by the assumptions (conductance, decay-heat fraction) used
D. The margin is 1300 K
Show answer

C. Margin = T_limit − T_peak = 1473 − 1300 = 173 K, positive, but only meaningful alongside the assumptions behind it. (A) ignores the limit — 1300 K is below it. (B) inverts the whole point of the lecture. (D) miscomputes margin as the peak itself.

Q5. Why do licensing-basis accident analyses deliberately stack conservative assumptions (highest decay heat, lowest cooling, hottest start)?
A. To make the simulation run faster
B. To produce the most realistic single best-estimate value
C. Because regulators require the model to match the plant exactly
D. So that the real plant is always safer than the model — if even the pessimistic case shows positive margin, the result is defensible
Show answer

D. Conservatism bounds the true behavior on the safe side, so a positive margin in the pessimistic model guarantees the real plant has even more. (A) Conservatism has nothing to do with run time. (B) Conservative is the opposite of best-estimate. (C) Models are simplifications, not exact replicas — that is precisely why margin and conservatism exist.
