Plant Operations: Startup, Power & Shutdown

A reactor spends most of its life sitting at one steady power level — but the riskiest, most disciplined hours are the slow, deliberate climb to criticality and the careful walk back down.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the standard reactor operating modes and explain what physically distinguishes one from the next.
  • Describe the approach to criticality and how the operator stays ahead of an exponentially growing power.
  • Construct and read a 1/M (inverse-count-rate) plot and use it to predict the critical rod position safely.
  • Explain steady power operation, the role of reactivity feedback, and how a plant load-follows.
  • Walk through a normal shutdown and cooldown, and state why decay heat keeps the core hot long after shutdown.

1.1Operating Modes: The Reactor's Gears

A power reactor is not a switch you flip on and off. It is a large thermal-hydraulic machine that must be moved deliberately through a sequence of well-defined operating modes, each with its own temperature, pressure, and reactivity rules. Plant procedures and technical specifications define these modes precisely so that every operator, on every shift, means the same thing by "the plant is in Mode 3."

While the exact names and numbers vary between plant designs, a typical pressurized-water plant uses six modes ordered by how close the core is to producing power:

Mode 1: Power Operation (k ~ 1, > 5% power) Mode 2: Startup (approaching / at criticality, < 5% power) Mode 3: Hot Standby (subcritical, hot & pressurized) Mode 4: Hot Shutdown (cooling down, intermediate temperature) Mode 5: Cold Shutdown (cold, depressurized, subcritical) Mode 6: Refueling (vessel head off, fuel being moved)Modes are defined by reactivity condition (keff), average coolant temperature, and percent power. Moving between modes follows written procedures.

The two quantities that pin down a mode are the effective multiplication factor keff (how the neutron population changes generation to generation) and the average coolant temperature. A reactor is subcritical when keff < 1 (the chain reaction dies out), critical when keff = 1 (the population is exactly self-sustaining), and supercritical when keff > 1 (power is rising). The whole craft of operation is managing the transition between these states without ever surprising the operator.

AnalogyThink of operating modes like the gears of a car driven in heavy fog. You never jump from park straight to highway speed. You move up one gear at a time — engaging the clutch (heating up), idling (hot standby), accelerating (startup), then cruising (power). Each gear has a safe speed band, and the written procedure is your route map through fog you cannot see through: the neutron flux.

1.2Approach to Criticality & the 1/M Plot

Startup is where physics gets dangerous in slow motion. A shutdown core is deeply subcritical, but it is never truly dark: a neutron source (a startup source plus spontaneous fission and prior fission products) keeps a small, steady trickle of neutrons present. When the operator withdraws control rods or dilutes boron, keff creeps upward, and that source trickle gets multiplied.

For a subcritical core driven by a source of strength S, the steady-state neutron population — and therefore the count rate C on the startup instruments — is amplified by the subcritical multiplication factor M:

M = 1 / (1 - k_eff) C = S * M = S / (1 - k_eff)As k_eff -> 1, M -> infinity. The count rate blows up smoothly as the core approaches critical.

Here is the trap: count rate C rises slowly at first, then faster and faster, finally rocketing toward infinity right at criticality. If an operator watched only C, the late surge could arrive faster than they could react. The fix is one of the most elegant tricks in operations — plot the reciprocal:

1 / M = 1 - k_eff = S / C1/M is proportional to (initial count rate)/(current count rate). It falls LINEARLY toward zero as k_eff -> 1.

Because 1/M = 1 − keff, and keff rises roughly linearly with rod withdrawal, a plot of 1/M against rod position (or boron dilution, or fuel additions during initial loading) is approximately a straight line that heads for zero exactly at criticality. Operators take a count, withdraw rods a step, wait for the count to stabilize, take another count, and extrapolate the line. The x-intercept predicts the critical condition before they reach it — turning a runaway exponential into a tame, forecastable straight line.

Common mistakeDo not withdraw the next rod step until the count rate has fully stabilized after the previous step. The subcritical population takes time to settle to its new steady value (the closer to critical, the longer it takes). Pulling rods while the count is still climbing makes 1/M look artificially high, hides how close you really are, and can carry the core past critical unexpectedly. Patience between steps is the rule, not the exception.

1.3Power Operation & Load Following

Once critical, the operator raises power by holding keff a hair above 1 just long enough to climb, then trims it back to exactly 1 at the target power. At steady power the reactor is critical — keff = 1 — and the neutron population, and therefore the thermal power, holds constant generation after generation. This is the plant's natural home; it may stay here for an entire 18-to-24-month fuel cycle.

What makes steady operation stable is reactivity feedback. Define reactivity, the fractional departure from criticality:

rho = (k_eff - 1) / k_effrho = 0 at exactly critical; rho > 0 means power rising, rho < 0 means power falling. Often quoted in pcm (1 pcm = 1e-5).

In a well-designed reactor, a rise in power heats the fuel and coolant, and that heating removes reactivity (negative temperature feedback — the fuel Doppler effect and coolant expansion). The reactor pushes back against its own power excursions, settling at a new equilibrium without the operator chasing it. This self-regulation is what lets a plant load follow: when the grid needs less power at night, the operator inserts a little negative reactivity, power and temperature drop, feedback finds a new steady state, and the turbine throttles back.

The balance the operator manages is simple in spirit — the heat generated by fission must equal the heat carried away by the coolant to the steam system:

P_fission = m_dot * cp * (T_hot - T_cold)At steady state, thermal power produced = power removed by coolant flow m_dot at specific heat cp across the core temperature rise.

Load following is routine in grids with a high nuclear share, but it stresses fuel and is limited by a slow poison we meet next week: xenon-135, a fission product that builds in and burns out over hours, fighting the operator's every power maneuver.

1.4Normal Shutdown, Cooldown & Decay Heat

A normal shutdown is the climb-down in reverse, done deliberately over hours — not to be confused with a reactor trip (scram), which slams all rods in within seconds for safety. In a normal shutdown the operator slowly inserts negative reactivity (rods in, boron up), reduces turbine load, and walks the plant down through hot standby and into cold shutdown, controlling the cooldown rate to protect the thick reactor vessel from thermal stress.

Here is the fact that defines nuclear safety: turning off the chain reaction does not turn off the heat. The radioactive fission products accumulated during operation keep decaying, releasing decay heat long after fission stops. Immediately after shutdown decay heat is roughly 6–7% of the pre-shutdown thermal power — for a 3000 MWth core that is over 200 MW of heat with the reactor "off." It falls off as a rough power law:

P_decay(t) ~ 0.066 * P0 * [ t^(-0.2) - (t + t0)^(-0.2) ]Wigner-Way type estimate. P0 = power before shutdown, t = seconds after shutdown, t0 = seconds of prior operation. Decay heat is a few percent immediately, ~1% after an hour, but never zero.

This is why a reactor can never simply be abandoned after shutdown: cooling must continue for days. The residual-heat-removal system and, ultimately, natural circulation must carry decay heat away, or the fuel will overheat even with the chain reaction long dead. Every major reactor accident in history was fundamentally a failure to remove decay heat, not a failure to stop fission. Managing decay heat is the single most important reason the operator never truly walks away.

✦ Worked Example — predicting criticality from a 1/M plot

During startup, an operator records source-range count rates as control rods are withdrawn. With rods at 0 steps the baseline count rate is C₀ = 20 counts/s. After withdrawing to 40 steps the count rate stabilizes at 50 c/s; at 80 steps it is 100 c/s. Estimate the critical rod position.

  1. Form 1/M at each step using 1/M = C₀ / C (normalized to the baseline count): at 0 steps 1/M = 20/20 = 1.00.
  2. At 40 steps: 1/M = 20/50 = 0.40.
  3. At 80 steps: 1/M = 20/100 = 0.20.
  4. Find the slope of the (near-linear) 1/M line over the last interval: from 40 to 80 steps, 1/M fell from 0.40 to 0.20, a change of −0.20 over 40 steps = −0.005 per step.
  5. Extrapolate to 1/M = 0 (criticality): starting from 0.20 at 80 steps, we need an additional 0.20 / 0.005 = 40 more steps. Predicted critical position ≈ 80 + 40 = 120 steps withdrawn.
  6. Operational takeaway: the operator now knows criticality is still ~40 steps away and approaches the last interval in smaller steps, re-plotting after each — never trusting a single extrapolation blindly as the line can curve near critical.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate subcritical multiplication & a 1/M plot in Python

Let's model a startup: as rods withdraw, k_eff rises, the source is multiplied, and we build the 1/M line the operator would extrapolate.

# startup_1overM.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

S = 20.0                      # source-driven baseline counts/s (k_eff ~ 0)
steps = np.array([0, 40, 80, 120])      # rod steps withdrawn

# model: k_eff rises linearly with withdrawal, reaching 1.0 at 120 steps
k_eff = 1.0 * steps / 120.0

def count_rate(S, k):
    # subcritical multiplication: C = S / (1 - k_eff)
    return S / (1.0 - k)

# avoid division by zero exactly at criticality
C = np.array([count_rate(S, k) if k < 1 else np.inf for k in k_eff])
inv_M = S / C                 # 1/M = C0 / C  (with C0 = S here)

for s, k, c, im in zip(steps, k_eff, C, inv_M):
    print(f"steps={s:3d}  k_eff={k:.3f}  C={c:7.1f} c/s  1/M={im:.3f}")

# extrapolate the last two finite points to 1/M = 0
slope = (inv_M[2] - inv_M[1]) / (steps[2] - steps[1])
crit = steps[1] - inv_M[1] / slope
print(f"predicted critical position ~ {crit:.0f} steps")

# steps=  0  k_eff=0.000  C=   20.0 c/s  1/M=1.000
# steps= 40  k_eff=0.333  C=   30.0 c/s  1/M=0.667
# steps= 80  k_eff=0.667  C=   60.0 c/s  1/M=0.333
# predicted critical position ~ 120 steps

The 1/M values fall toward zero and the extrapolation lands on 120 steps — exactly where we set k_eff = 1. Try making k_eff non-linear in steps (real cores curve near critical) and watch how the early extrapolation over- or under-predicts, which is why operators re-plot every step.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A reactor at steady, constant power is best described as:
A. supercritical, k_eff > 1
B. critical, k_eff = 1
C. subcritical, k_eff < 1
D. source-driven only, k_eff = 0
Show answer

B. Constant power means the neutron population is exactly self-sustaining, so k_eff = 1. (A) supercritical means rising power; (C) subcritical means power dying away; (D) k_eff = 0 describes no multiplication at all, not steady power.

Q2. Why do operators plot 1/M instead of the raw count rate during an approach to criticality?
A. 1/M increases exponentially, giving an early warning
B. The raw count rate is impossible to measure below critical
C. 1/M falls along a near-straight line to zero at criticality, so the critical condition can be extrapolated before reaching it
D. 1/M removes the need to wait for the count rate to stabilize
Show answer

C. Since 1/M = 1 − k_eff falls linearly toward zero at critical, its x-intercept predicts the critical rod position. (A) is backwards — 1/M falls, and the raw count rate is the one that blows up. (B) the count rate is measurable and is the source data. (D) you still must let counts stabilize at each step — that requirement does not go away.

Q3. Immediately after a normal shutdown, the core still produces heat primarily because:
A. the chain reaction continues at low level for hours
B. control rods generate heat as they insert
C. radioactive fission products keep decaying, releasing decay heat
D. the coolant pumps add frictional heat equal to full power
Show answer

C. Decay of accumulated fission products yields ~6–7% of full power right after shutdown and falls slowly. (A) the chain reaction is stopped, not lingering; (B) rods absorb neutrons, they do not generate core heat; (D) pump heat is tiny compared with decay heat just after shutdown.

Q4. A plant lowers its electrical output at night to match grid demand, then raises it in the morning. This routine maneuver is called:
A. load following
B. a reactor scram
C. a cold shutdown
D. refueling
Show answer

A. Adjusting power to track grid demand is load following, enabled by negative reactivity feedback. (B) a scram is a rapid emergency rod insertion, not a routine power change; (C) cold shutdown takes the plant subcritical and cold, far below power; (D) refueling happens with the reactor shut down and the vessel open.

Q5. While the count rate is still climbing after a rod withdrawal step, the safest action is to:
A. immediately withdraw the next rod step to save time
B. insert rods to force the count back down before continuing
C. ignore the count rate and rely on the turbine output instead
D. wait for the count rate to stabilize before taking the next reading and step
Show answer

D. The subcritical population needs time to settle; reading or stepping early distorts the 1/M plot and can hide how near critical you are. (A) stepping early is exactly the dangerous mistake; (B) needlessly reversing wastes time and adds no safety once you simply wait; (C) at startup the turbine carries no load and gives no useful signal.
