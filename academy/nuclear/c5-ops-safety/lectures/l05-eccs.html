Emergency Core Cooling Systems (ECCS)

When a pipe bursts and the coolant flashes to steam, the core does not care that the reactor is shut down — it keeps making heat, and a layered web of pumps, tanks, and pressurized bottles must flood the fuel before it melts.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a shut-down core still needs aggressive cooling, and what the ECCS is designed to prevent.
  • Distinguish high-pressure injection, low-pressure injection, and passive accumulators by the conditions in which each acts.
  • Describe how decay (residual) heat removal carries the core through hours and days after shutdown.
  • Trace the sequence of ECCS actuation during a large-break loss-of-coolant accident (LOCA).
  • State the single-failure criterion and explain why ECCS is built from redundant, independent trains.

5.1Why a Shut-Down Core Still Needs Cooling

The first instinct of a newcomer is that once the control rods drop and fission stops, the danger is over. It is not. A reactor that has been running at full power is loaded with intensely radioactive fission products, and those nuclei keep decaying for a long time after the chain reaction is dead. Each decay deposits energy in the fuel as decay heat (also called residual heat). The instant after a SCRAM, decay heat is roughly 6–7% of the previous full-power level — for a 3000 MW-thermal core that is well over 150 MW of heat with no fission at all.

That number falls fast at first, then very slowly. A widely used engineering correlation (the Way-Wigner form) approximates the decay-heat fraction at time t after a long irradiation as:

P(t) / P_0 = 0.066 * [ t^(-0.2) - (t + T)^(-0.2) ]t = seconds after shutdown, T = seconds of prior operation, P_0 = full thermal power. A rule of thumb: ~6.5% at 1 s, ~1.5% at 1 hour, ~0.5% at 1 day.

The takeaway: decay heat cannot be switched off. It must be carried away continuously, or the fuel temperature climbs until the cladding balloons, ruptures, and eventually melts. Under normal shutdown the main coolant loop and a dedicated residual-heat-removal system handle this comfortably. The crisis arises when the coolant that carries the heat is suddenly leaving the system — a loss-of-coolant accident. That is the scenario the Emergency Core Cooling System (ECCS) exists to defeat.

AnalogyThink of a cast-iron skillet you have just taken off a hot burner. The flame (fission) is gone, but the skillet stays dangerously hot for many minutes because of stored and slowly-released heat. Now imagine the skillet keeps generating its own heat from within. You cannot just walk away — you must keep pouring water on it, and the moment your water supply springs a leak, you need a backup hose ready instantly. ECCS is that stack of backup hoses, sized for a skillet that never fully cools.

5.2The Layered Toolkit: High-Pressure, Low-Pressure, and Passive Injection

No single pump can cover every accident, because the conditions inside the reactor coolant system (RCS) change drastically depending on how big the leak is. A pump that can push water against a still-pressurized system (~155 bar in a pressurized-water reactor) cannot move much flow; a pump that delivers a torrent of water can only do so once the pressure has fallen. So ECCS is built as a layered toolkit, each layer matched to a pressure band.

High-Pressure Injection (HPI / HPSI)

For small leaks the RCS stays at high pressure. The high-pressure injection pumps are designed to overcome that pressure — they develop very high discharge head but modest flow. Their job is to make up inventory faster than a small break loses it, keeping the core covered while the operators bring pressure down. Many designs also draw on a charging path for the smallest leaks.

Passive Accumulators

The cleverest layer needs no power at all. Accumulators are large tanks half-filled with borated water and pressurized with nitrogen to around 40–50 bar, connected to the RCS through a check valve that is held shut by the higher RCS pressure. The instant a large break drops RCS pressure below the nitrogen pressure, the check valve swings open and the nitrogen blanket shoves the water into the core — automatically, instantly, with no operator action and no electricity.

accumulator injects when: P_RCS < P_N2 (~40-50 bar)A passive check valve opens on its own when system pressure falls below the stored gas pressure. No pump, no signal, no power.

Low-Pressure Injection (LPI / LPSI)

Once a large break has emptied the system down to near containment pressure, the low-pressure injection pumps take over. They deliver enormous flow at low head — exactly what is needed to keep a depressurized core flooded for the long haul. The borated water comes first from a large tank (the refueling water storage tank), and when that runs low the suction switches over to the containment sump, recirculating the water that has collected on the floor so cooling can continue indefinitely.

Common mistakeStudents often assume the high-pressure pumps are the "powerful" ones because they fight the highest pressure. The opposite is true for flow. HPI trades flow for head (high pressure, low flow); LPI trades head for flow (low pressure, high flow). Confusing the two leads to the wrong conclusion about which system actually refloods a large break — it is the high-flow, low-pressure pumps (plus accumulators) that do the heavy lifting after a big LOCA, not HPI.

5.3Decay-Heat Removal and the Energy Balance

ECCS does not just add water; it must remove energy. To see why the layers are sized the way they are, write an energy balance on the core. The water carries heat away by warming up (sensible heat) and by boiling (latent heat). For a coolant mass flow rate ṁ, the heat it can remove while staying below boiling is:

Q_removed = m_dot * c_p * (T_out - T_in)m_dot = coolant mass flow (kg/s), c_p ~ 4.2 kJ/(kg.K) for water, (T_out - T_in) = temperature rise across the core.

If the injected flow cannot match the decay-heat generation, the difference goes into boiling off coolant and heating the fuel. The minimum flow that just balances the decay heat by boiling alone is set by the latent heat of vaporization:

m_dot_min = P_decay / h_fgh_fg ~ 2257 kJ/kg (latent heat of vaporization of water at ~1 bar). Below this flow, water boils away faster than it is replaced and the core uncovers.

This is why the residual-heat-removal (RHR) system — which after a LOCA shares pumps and heat exchangers with low-pressure injection — is the long-duration workhorse. It pulls hot water from the core or containment sump, passes it through a heat exchanger cooled by an outside water loop, and returns it. The decay-heat curve falls slowly, so the RHR function must run for days to weeks, long after the dramatic first minutes are over. Sizing ECCS means covering both the violent early transient and this long tail.

AnalogyBailing a leaking boat has two phases. First the swamping wave: you need buckets fast (accumulators and high-flow LPI). Then the slow seep that never stops: you need a small bilge pump you can run for days (the RHR function). A boat that survives the wave but has no bilge pump still sinks overnight — which is exactly why decay-heat removal, not just initial injection, is a safety requirement.

5.4LOCA Response, Redundant Trains & the Single-Failure Criterion

Now we put the pieces in motion. Consider a large-break LOCA: a major pipe in the RCS shears open. Coolant at 155 bar and over 300 °C flashes explosively to steam, and the system blows down to near containment pressure in tens of seconds. The reactor trips automatically, but decay heat is still pouring out. The ECCS responds in a choreographed sequence:

1. Blowdown & trip (0–20 s): pressure plummets; the reactor SCRAMs; high-pressure injection starts but its flow is limited as long as any pressure remains.

2. Accumulator dump (when P falls below ~45 bar): check valves open and the nitrogen-driven accumulators slam borated water into the downcomer, refilling the lower plenum and beginning to reflood the core — entirely passively.

3. Low-pressure injection (P near containment pressure): high-flow LPI pumps reflood and then keep the core quenched, drawing from the refueling water storage tank.

4. Long-term recirculation (sump switchover): when the storage tank empties, suction shifts to the containment sump and the RHR heat exchangers carry decay heat to the ultimate heat sink for as long as needed.

Redundancy and the single-failure criterion

A safety system that works only when every component works is not a safety system. Regulatory practice enforces the single-failure criterion: the ECCS must perform its safety function even if any one active component fails — a pump that will not start, a valve stuck shut, or a diesel generator that trips — on top of the accident itself. The engineering answer is redundant, independent trains. ECCS is typically built as two (or more) complete trains, each with its own pumps, valves, power supply, and emergency diesel generator, physically and electrically separated so that no single fault, fire, or flood can disable both.

design rule: (accident) + (worst single active failure) -> core still cooledThe plant must meet its safety limits assuming the initiating event AND the most damaging single component failure occur together.

The accumulators reinforce this philosophy beautifully: being passive, they have no pump to fail and no power to lose. Defense-in-depth means combining diverse mechanisms (active pumps + passive tanks + gravity in some newer designs) so that the failure modes do not overlap.

Common mistakeThe single-failure criterion is about one failure on top of the accident, not "the system never fails." Students sometimes read "single-failure proof" as a guarantee of perfection. It is not — it is a design margin: the plant is required to survive the design-basis accident plus the worst single active failure, which forces genuine redundancy rather than a single shiny pump.
✦ Worked Example — minimum injection flow to keep the core covered

A 3000 MW-thermal core has just tripped. Ten minutes (600 s) after shutdown, estimate the decay heat, then find the minimum injection flow that just removes it by boiling, using h_fg = 2257 kJ/kg.

  1. Decay-heat fraction at 600 s: a 1.5%-at-one-hour rule of thumb scaled toward the early curve gives roughly 2% at 10 minutes. Using the Way-Wigner estimate P/P_0 ≈ 0.066 × t-0.2 at t = 600 s: 0.066 × 600-0.2 = 0.066 × 0.277 ≈ 0.0183, i.e. ~1.8%.
  2. Decay power: P_decay = 0.0183 × 3000 MW = 54.9 MW = 5.49 × 10⁷ W.
  3. Minimum boiling flow: ṁ_min = P_decay / h_fg = 5.49 × 10⁷ W ÷ 2.257 × 10⁶ J/kg ≈ 24.3 kg/s.
  4. Interpret: about 24 kg/s (~24 L/s, or ~390 gpm) of water boiled away just balances the heat. Real LPI pumps deliver many times this — hundreds of kg/s — to keep the core fully submerged with margin, not merely on the edge of boiling dry.
  5. Sanity check: at full power the same core boils thousands of kg/s; needing only ~24 kg/s ten minutes after trip shows how steeply decay heat has already dropped — yet 55 MW with no cooling would still destroy the core in minutes.

🛠️ Hands-On: model the decay-heat race against injection in Python

We use the course's free thermal-hydraulics stack — NumPy for arrays and SciPy for integrating the energy balance over time. (Production codes like RELAP5-3D are license-gated, so throughout this course we build transparent Python models instead — same physics, fully open.) Setup: if you already built the nuclear environment in the Week 1/2 install step, you are ready; SciPy ships in that same conda/pip set.

⬇ One new package this lecture: SciPy (skip if Week 1/2 env already has it)

SciPy adds the ODE integrator used below. It installs alongside the existing stack:

conda activate nuclear
conda install -c conda-forge scipy      # or: pip install scipy

Verify: run python -c "import scipy; print(scipy.__version__)" — a version string means you are set.

Here we integrate the coolant inventory after a LOCA: decay heat boils off water, ECCS injects make-up. If injection beats boil-off, inventory stays positive and the core stays covered.

# eccs_inventory.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

P0     = 3000e6        # full thermal power, W
H_FG   = 2257e3        # latent heat of vaporization, J/kg
M_INIT = 2.0e5         # initial coolant mass in core+RCS, kg

def decay_power(t):
    # Way-Wigner-style fraction (t in seconds after trip)
    return 0.066 * P0 * (t**-0.2)

def injection(t):
    # HPI early, accumulators burst, LPI takes over (kg/s)
    if t < 20:      return 30.0        # HPI: high head, low flow
    if t < 60:      return 400.0       # accumulator dump
    return 250.0                        # LPI long-term flow

def dmdt(t, m):
    boiloff = decay_power(t) / H_FG     # kg/s lost to boiling
    return injection(t) - boiloff       # net inventory change

sol = solve_ivp(dmdt, [1, 3600], [M_INIT], max_step=1.0, dense_output=True)
m_min = sol.y[0].min()
print(f"minimum inventory over 1 hour: {m_min:,.0f} kg")
print("core stays covered" if m_min > 0 else "CORE UNCOVERS")

# Try cutting injection to 15 kg/s (a failed train) to watch inventory crash.

Lower the LPI flow toward the ~24 kg/s minimum we computed by hand and the margin vanishes; drop one train entirely and the model shows the core uncovering — a quantitative picture of why the single-failure criterion forces redundancy.

Q1. Immediately after a reactor SCRAMs from full power, roughly what fraction of full thermal power is still being generated as decay heat?
A. 0% — fission has stopped, so heat generation is zero
B. About 6–7%
C. About 50%
D. About 100% — unchanged from full power
Show answer

B. Decay of accumulated fission products yields ~6–7% of full power at the moment of trip, falling over time. (A) ignores decay heat entirely — the central error this lecture corrects. (C) and (D) wildly overstate it; only fission, which has stopped, could sustain such levels.

Q2. Which ECCS component injects coolant with NO pump and NO electrical power?
A. High-pressure injection pumps
B. Low-pressure injection pumps
C. Passive accumulators (nitrogen-pressurized tanks behind a check valve)
D. The residual-heat-removal heat exchanger
Show answer

C. Accumulators store borated water under nitrogen pressure; when RCS pressure drops below the gas pressure, a check valve opens on its own — no pump, no power, no operator. (A) and (B) are powered pumps. (D) is a heat exchanger, not an injection source, and relies on pumped flow.

Q3. During a small-break LOCA the RCS stays at high pressure. Which system is best suited to make up inventory under these conditions?
A. High-pressure injection (high head, low flow)
B. Low-pressure injection (low head, high flow)
C. Accumulators, which dump only once pressure is well below ~45 bar
D. The containment spray system
Show answer

A. HPI develops enough head to push water against a still-pressurized system, trading flow for pressure. (B) LPI cannot overcome high RCS pressure. (C) accumulators only act once pressure has fallen below their gas pressure, which a small break may never reach. (D) containment spray cools containment atmosphere, it does not inject into the core.

Q4. The single-failure criterion requires that the ECCS perform its function assuming…
A. nothing ever fails
B. all trains fail simultaneously
C. only the reactor scrams, with no other event
D. the design-basis accident occurs together with the worst single active component failure
Show answer

D. The criterion stacks one worst-case active failure (a stuck valve, a dead pump, a tripped diesel) on top of the accident, forcing redundant independent trains. (A) is the misconception "single-failure proof = perfect." (B) is far harsher than the criterion requires and would be impractical. (C) ignores the accident the system must mitigate.

Q5. After the refueling water storage tank empties during a large LOCA, how does ECCS keep cooling the core for hours and days?
A. It refills the tank from the city water main
B. It switches pump suction to the containment sump and recirculates the collected water through heat exchangers (long-term recirculation)
C. It stops, because decay heat is negligible by then
D. The accumulators refill themselves and inject again repeatedly
Show answer

B. On sump switchover, LPI/RHR pumps draw the water pooled on the containment floor, pass it through heat exchangers to the ultimate heat sink, and return it — sustaining cooling indefinitely. (A) safety cooling cannot depend on an off-site water main. (C) decay heat is still tens of MW for hours. (D) accumulators are one-shot; their check valves do not refill them.

✓ What you learned

  • A shut-down core still makes decay heat (~6–7% at trip), so it must be cooled continuously or it melts.
  • ECCS is a layered toolkit: high-pressure injection (high head), passive accumulators (mid-pressure, no power), and low-pressure injection (high flow).
  • The energy balance Q = ṁ·c_p·ΔT and the boiling floor ṁ_min = P/h_fg set the minimum injection flow to keep the core covered.
  • During a LOCA, the layers hand off in sequence — HPI, accumulator dump, LPI reflood, then sump recirculation for the long decay-heat tail.
  • The single-failure criterion forces redundant, independent trains so the core stays cooled even with the worst single failure.
