Probabilistic Risk Assessment (PRA)

A reactor accident is not one bad valve — it is a rare unlucky chain of dozens of independent failures, and PRA is the disciplined arithmetic that traces every chain and tells us which links matter most.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what probabilistic risk assessment (PRA) is and why it complements deterministic, defense-in-depth safety analysis.
  • Identify initiating events and explain how they seed an accident sequence.
  • Build and read event trees and fault trees, and combine their probabilities.
  • Define core-damage frequency (CDF) as a quantitative risk metric and interpret its units.
  • Account for common-cause failure and explain how PRA informs risk-informed regulation and design.

7.1What PRA Is — and Why "Defense in Depth" Alone Is Not Enough

Classical reactor safety is deterministic: engineers pick a small set of worst-case "design-basis accidents" — a guillotine break of the largest pipe, a station blackout — and prove the plant survives each one with margin to spare. This defense-in-depth philosophy stacks independent barriers (fuel cladding, the reactor coolant boundary, containment) so that no single failure can release radioactivity. It is robust, but it has a blind spot: it never asks how likely each accident is, nor what happens when several modest failures line up at once.

Probabilistic risk assessment fills that gap. PRA asks three questions, first framed by the safety analyst Stanley Kaplan: What can go wrong? How likely is it? What are the consequences? Instead of one bounding scenario, PRA enumerates thousands of accident sequences, attaches a frequency to each, and multiplies frequency by consequence to get risk. The first full reactor PRA, the 1975 Reactor Safety Study, showed that the dominant risks were not the big design-basis pipe breaks regulators had obsessed over, but humble "transients" — losing feedwater, losing offsite power — compounded by equipment and operator failures. That insight reshaped the field.

Risk = SUM over sequences i of [ frequency_i x consequence_i ]Risk is a set of (scenario, frequency, consequence) triplets — not a single number. A rare event with huge consequence can outweigh a common event with mild consequence.

PRA does not replace defense in depth — it audits it. By quantifying how much each barrier and each system actually buys you, PRA tells operators where to spend maintenance dollars, which equipment to keep in service before a risky evolution, and which proposed design change genuinely lowers risk versus merely looking prudent.

AnalogyThink of a medieval castle with three walls (defense in depth). The deterministic engineer proves each wall can stop a battering ram and stops there. The PRA analyst instead asks: how often does an army actually show up, what is the chance the gate is left open during a supply run, and what is the probability all three gates fail on the same night? PRA does not knock down the walls — it tells the castellan which gate hinge, left un-oiled, secretly dominates the chance of the whole castle falling.

7.2Initiating Events: the Spark That Starts a Sequence

Every accident sequence begins with an initiating event — a disturbance that knocks the plant out of stable, full-power operation and challenges the safety systems. Initiating events fall into broad families: transients (loss of feedwater, turbine trip, loss of offsite power), loss-of-coolant accidents or LOCAs (a pipe break draining the primary coolant), and external/internal hazards (earthquakes, floods, internal fires). Each family is assigned an initiating-event frequency, usually expressed in events per reactor-year, drawn from plant operating history and industry-wide databases.

For example, a small-break LOCA might occur with frequency on the order of 1e-3 per reactor-year (once in a thousand years of operation), while a simple turbine trip might happen roughly once per year. The art of PRA begins with a complete, mutually-exclusive list of initiators — miss one and its entire risk contribution vanishes from the model.

f_init = (number of occurrences) / (total reactor-years of experience)Units: events per reactor-year (/ry). One reactor running 40 years contributes 40 reactor-years; a fleet of 100 reactors for 1 year also contributes 100 reactor-years.

Crucially, the initiating event is only the start. Whether it becomes a non-event or a catastrophe depends on how the plant's safety systems respond — and that response is what the event tree maps out next.

Common mistakeDo not treat the initiating-event frequency as the accident frequency. A small-break LOCA at 1e-3/ry does not mean core damage every thousand years — emergency cooling almost always succeeds. The core-damage frequency is the initiating frequency multiplied by the probability that the mitigating systems also fail, which is typically far smaller. Confusing "challenge frequency" with "damage frequency" overstates risk by orders of magnitude.

7.3Event Trees and Fault Trees: the Two-Tool Toolkit

PRA combines two complementary logic models. The event tree works forward in time from the initiating event. At each safety function it forks into success and failure, so the tree fans out into a complete set of mutually-exclusive accident sequences ending in defined states — “OK” or “core damage.” Because the branches at each node are exclusive and exhaustive, the probability of any one sequence is simply the product of the branch probabilities along its path.

P(sequence) = f_init x p1 x p2 x ... x pnEach p is the conditional probability of that branch given everything upstream. The event tree assumes the branch events are independent unless told otherwise.

But what is the failure probability of a whole safety system — say, “high-pressure injection fails”? That number is not measured directly; it is built up from component failures by a fault tree. A fault tree reasons backward: it starts with an undesired “top event” (system fails) and decomposes it through logic gates into the basic component faults that could cause it. The two gates that do almost all the work:

OR gate — the output fails if any input fails (redundancy is absent; a series chain). For independent, rare events the probabilities approximately add:

P(A OR B) ~= P(A) + P(B) (rare-event approximation)Exact: P(A or B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A)P(B). When P<<1 the product term is negligible, so adding is accurate to better than 1%.

AND gate — the output fails only if all inputs fail (true redundancy). For independent events the probabilities multiply:

P(A AND B) = P(A) x P(B)Two redundant pumps each with failure probability 1e-2 give a combined 1e-4 — redundancy buys orders of magnitude, IF the failures are truly independent.

The fault tree's top-event probability becomes the branch probability in the event tree. Forward-looking event trees set the skeleton of accident sequences; backward-looking fault trees fill in each branch's number. Together they let an analyst compute the frequency of every path from a pipe break to a damaged core.

7.4Core-Damage Frequency & Common-Cause Failure

The headline output of a Level-1 PRA is the core-damage frequency (CDF): the expected number of accidents that uncover or melt the core, per reactor-year. You obtain it by summing the frequencies of every event-tree sequence that ends in core damage, across every initiating event:

CDF = SUM over initiators j [ f_init,j x P(mitigation fails | initiator j) ]Units: per reactor-year (/ry). A modern plant target is roughly 1e-4 /ry or lower; advanced designs aim for 1e-5 to 1e-6 /ry.

CDF is the field's common currency. A regulatory safety goal of 1e-4 per reactor-year means “fewer than one core-damage event expected per 10,000 reactor-years.” A second metric, the large early release frequency (LERF), carries this forward to containment failure and offsite release — the consequence side of the risk triplet. CDF measures how often the core is hurt; LERF measures how often the public is.

The redundancy-killer: common-cause failure

The AND gate's beautiful multiplication — two 2.5% trains giving 0.06% — rests entirely on the assumption that the trains fail independently. Reality fights back. A single shared cause can knock out supposedly redundant equipment simultaneously: a miscalibration applied to all pumps by the same technician, a clogged common suction strainer, a flood that submerges both trains. This is common-cause failure (CCF), and it is the dominant contributor to risk in most real PRAs precisely because it defeats redundancy.

PRA models CCF with a correction term. The simplest is the beta-factor model: a fraction beta of a component's failures are common-cause, hitting all redundant copies at once. For two redundant components each with failure probability Q:

P(both fail) = (1 - beta) Q^2 + beta x QThe first term is the independent double failure (tiny); the second is the common-cause hit (linear in Q, so much larger). Typical beta ~ 0.05 to 0.1.

Plug in Q = 2.5e-2 and beta = 0.1: the independent term is about 5.6e-4, but the common-cause term is 2.5e-3 — over four times larger. The CCF term dominates and the “6e-4” independent estimate was optimistic by a factor of several. This is why analysts physically separate redundant trains, stagger their maintenance, and diversify component types: every measure attacks beta, not Q.

AnalogyTwo independent locks on a door multiply your security — unless you use the same key for both. Common-cause failure is that single key: it does not matter how many redundant locks you install if one shared weakness (the key, the locksmith, a power cut to all of them) opens them together. PRA's job is to hunt down the shared key hiding behind apparent redundancy.

Because PRA produces a defensible number for how much each system, component, and human action contributes to CDF, regulators use it to make decisions risk-informed rather than purely prescriptive: relaxing an over-conservative inspection that barely moves CDF while tightening attention on a component whose failure dominates it. Designers use the same machinery in reverse — testing whether a proposed passive cooling system or extra diverse pump actually lowers CDF enough to justify its cost. PRA turns “is it safe enough?” from an argument into an arithmetic.

✦ Worked Example — CDF contribution from a small-break LOCA

A small-break LOCA occurs with initiating frequency f_init = 1e-3 /ry. Mitigation requires (1) high-pressure injection, which fails with probability 6.25e-4 (from the fault tree above), then (2) recirculation cooling, which fails with probability 1e-2. If injection fails outright, the core is damaged regardless of recirculation. Compute the CDF contribution from this initiator, then redo step 2 with common-cause failure (beta = 0.1) on the injection trains.

  1. Sequence 1 — injection fails: frequency = f_init × P(inj fails) = 1e-3 × 6.25e-4 = 6.25e-7 /ry. This path goes straight to core damage.
  2. Sequence 2 — injection succeeds, recirculation fails: frequency = 1e-3 × (1 − 6.25e-4) × 1e-2 ≈ 1e-3 × 1 × 1e-2 = 1.0e-5 /ry.
  3. Total CDF from SB-LOCA (independent model): 6.25e-7 + 1.0e-5 = 1.06e-5 /ry. Recirculation failure dominates this initiator.
  4. Now add common-cause to injection: with Q = 2.5e-2 per train and beta = 0.1, P(inj fails) = (1−0.1)(2.5e-2)² + 0.1(2.5e-2) = 5.6e-4 + 2.5e-3 = 3.06e-3 — about 5× the independent 6.25e-4.
  5. Revised Sequence 1: 1e-3 × 3.06e-3 = 3.06e-6 /ry. New SB-LOCA total = 3.06e-6 + 1.0e-5 ≈ 1.3e-5 /ry. Common-cause raised the injection-failure contribution nearly fivefold — a vivid reminder that ignoring CCF understates risk.

🛠️ Hands-On: a minimal PRA engine in Python

All course we use the free Python scientific stack — NumPy and SciPy for the math, Jupyter for notebooks. The full one-time install lives in the Week 1/2 setup (the install <details> in the early lectures); no new tools are needed here — PRA logic is just arithmetic on probabilities, so plain Python plus NumPy is enough.

Below we encode fault-tree gates, a beta-factor common-cause model, and an event-tree walk to reproduce the worked example's CDF.

# pra_min.py — Lecture 7: event tree + fault tree + common-cause
import numpy as np

# --- fault-tree gates (rare-event approximation for OR) ---
def OR(*ps):                       # series: any input fails the output
    return 1.0 - np.prod([1.0 - p for p in ps])   # exact complement

def AND(*ps):                      # redundancy: all must fail
    return float(np.prod(ps))

def ccf_pair(Q, beta):             # two redundant items, beta-factor model
    return (1 - beta) * Q**2 + beta * Q

# --- high-pressure injection fault tree ---
train = OR(2e-2, 5e-3)             # pump OR valve per train -> 2.5e-2
inj_indep = AND(train, train)      # both trains, independent -> 6.25e-4
inj_ccf   = ccf_pair(train, beta=0.10)            # with common cause
print(f"P(inj fail) indep = {inj_indep:.2e}")     # 6.25e-04
print(f"P(inj fail) +CCF  = {inj_ccf:.2e}")        # 3.06e-03

# --- event tree for SB-LOCA: f_init, then injection, then recirc ---
f_init  = 1e-3
p_recirc_fail = 1e-2

def cdf_from_sbloca(p_inj_fail):
    seq1 = f_init * p_inj_fail                      # injection fails -> CD
    seq2 = f_init * (1 - p_inj_fail) * p_recirc_fail # inj ok, recirc fails -> CD
    return seq1 + seq2

print(f"CDF (indep) = {cdf_from_sbloca(inj_indep):.2e} /ry")  # 1.06e-05
print(f"CDF (+CCF)  = {cdf_from_sbloca(inj_ccf):.2e} /ry")    # 1.31e-05

# sensitivity: sweep beta to see how common-cause drives CDF
for b in (0.0, 0.05, 0.10, 0.20):
    cdf = cdf_from_sbloca(ccf_pair(train, b))
    print(f"beta={b:.2f} -> CDF={cdf:.2e} /ry")

Run it and watch the beta sweep: as common-cause fraction climbs, CDF rises even though every component's individual reliability is unchanged. That single curve is the quantitative argument for separating and diversifying redundant trains.

Q1. What distinguishes probabilistic risk assessment from classical deterministic safety analysis?
A. PRA ignores consequences and looks only at failure rates
B. PRA assigns frequencies to many accident sequences instead of bounding a few worst-case scenarios
C. PRA replaces defense in depth with a single barrier
D. PRA only applies to external hazards like earthquakes
Show answer

B. PRA enumerates thousands of sequences and attaches a frequency and consequence to each. (A) is wrong — risk explicitly multiplies frequency by consequence. (C) PRA audits defense in depth, it does not remove barriers. (D) PRA covers transients, LOCAs, internal and external hazards alike.

Q2. A turbine trip occurs about once per reactor-year, but emergency systems almost always cope. What does this tell you about the initiating-event frequency versus the core-damage contribution?
A. They are equal, since any initiator that occurs causes core damage
B. The core-damage contribution is larger than the initiating frequency
C. The core-damage contribution is the initiating frequency times the (small) probability that mitigation also fails
D. Initiating frequency is irrelevant to core-damage frequency
Show answer

C. CDF contribution = f_init × P(mitigation fails), and mitigation usually succeeds, so the contribution is far below the initiating frequency. (A) confuses challenge frequency with damage frequency. (B) is impossible — a conditional probability cannot exceed 1. (D) is wrong: f_init is a direct multiplier.

Q3. A safety system has two redundant trains, each failing with probability 2.5e-2. Assuming independent failures, what is the AND-gate (system) failure probability?
A. 6.25e-4
B. 2.5e-2
C. 5.0e-2
D. 1.0e-4
Show answer

A. An AND gate multiplies independent failure probabilities: 2.5e-2 × 2.5e-2 = 6.25e-4. (C) adds them (that would be an OR gate). (B) is one train alone. (D) would come from a different per-train value (1e-2 each).

Q4. What are the units of core-damage frequency (CDF), and what is a typical regulatory safety goal?
A. Dimensionless probability; goal around 0.5
B. Per reactor-year; goal around 1e-4 /ry or lower
C. Sieverts per year; goal around 20 mSv
D. Events per second; goal around 1e-4 /s
Show answer

B. CDF is a frequency in events per reactor-year; a common goal is about 1e-4 /ry (fewer than one core-damage event per 10,000 reactor-years). (A) confuses frequency with probability. (C) sieverts measure dose, not core-damage frequency. (D) the relevant timescale is reactor-years, not seconds.

Q5. Why is common-cause failure (CCF) so important in PRA, and how do plants combat it?
A. CCF makes redundant components more independent, so it can be ignored
B. CCF only affects the initiating-event frequency, not mitigation
C. CCF lowers risk by adding more trains, so plants add identical pumps
D. CCF defeats redundancy by failing multiple trains from one shared cause, so plants separate, stagger maintenance, and diversify equipment
Show answer

D. A shared cause (miscalibration, flood, clogged common strainer) fails redundant trains together, breaking the AND-gate multiplication, so it often dominates CDF; defenses attack the common cause via separation, staggered maintenance, and diversity. (A) is backwards — CCF destroys independence. (B) CCF chiefly attacks mitigation systems. (C) adding identical pumps does nothing against a common cause and can even share the weakness.
