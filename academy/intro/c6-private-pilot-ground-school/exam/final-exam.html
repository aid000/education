Regulations, Airspace & Human Factors · Weeks 2–3, 10
1. A student pilot is building a checklist of the aeronautical experience and recent-flight-time she still needs before her practical test. Which part of 14 CFR should she open?
Answer: A. Part 61 holds certification, ratings, aeronautical experience and currency. Part 91 is operating rules once airborne, Part 830 is NTSB accident/incident reporting, Part 43 is maintenance.
2. To carry passengers during the day, a pilot must have made how many takeoffs and landings in the preceding 90 days?
Answer: B. Day currency requires 3 takeoffs and landings in the preceding 90 days; night currency requires 3 full-stop takeoffs/landings at night, separately.
3. Night passenger-carrying currency requires the 3 required landings to be…
Answer: C. Night currency is a separate requirement: 3 takeoffs and full-stop landings at night within the preceding 90 days.
4. Descending through 8,500 ft MSL in Class E airspace with a strong tailwind, your groundspeed is climbing and you are tempted to leave the power in. What indicated airspeed may you not exceed?
Answer: D. §91.117(a): 250 KIAS below 10,000 ft MSL. It is an indicated limit — a high groundspeed from tailwind does not violate it. The 200 KIAS figure applies under a Class B shelf and in Class C/D surface areas.
5. In the right-of-way category order, which aircraft always has priority over all others?
Answer: A. An aircraft in distress outranks every other category, always.
6. Two airplanes are converging at the same altitude. Which one has the right-of-way?
Answer: B. The aircraft to the right has right-of-way; the other yields, turns right, and passes behind it.
7. Cruising at 4,500 ft you see an aircraft at your 12 o'clock, same altitude, growing rapidly in the windscreen. Under §91.113 you must…
Answer: C. Head-on or nearly so, each pilot alters course to the right. Both pilots applying the same rule is what keeps the two avoidance maneuvers from creating a new conflict.
8. You are overtaking a slower aircraft of the same category from directly behind on the same course, and want to pass. §91.113(f) requires that you…
Answer: D. The aircraft being overtaken has the right-of-way. The overtaking aircraft gives way by altering course to the right and passing well clear — regardless of which aircraft is faster.
9. Two aircraft are converging to land at the same airport. Which generally has priority?
Answer: A. The lower aircraft on final approach has priority; it must not be cut off or overtaken by a higher aircraft.
10. Your engine begins running rough over rough terrain and you elect to declare an emergency, but the frequency is saturated with other traffic. Before you get a word in, what should you set in the transponder?
Answer: B. 7700 is the emergency code — it alerts every controller watching that scope without a word being spoken. (7600 = lost communications, 7500 = hijack/unlawful interference, 1200 = standard VFR.)
11. The correct radio call to declare a life-threatening emergency is…
Answer: C. "Mayday" said three times signals a life-threatening emergency; "Pan-Pan" is for an urgent, non-life-threatening situation.
12. Under NTSB Part 830, wreckage from a reportable accident should be…
Answer: D. Wreckage and records must be preserved until the NTSB releases them, with narrow exceptions for further protection or helping injured persons.
13. Per 14 CFR §91.17, a pilot may not act as crew within how many hours of consuming alcohol, or with a blood alcohol level at or above what limit?
Answer: A. The "8 hours, bottle to throttle" rule with a 0.04 blood alcohol limit — a floor, since effects can last longer.
14. A pilot reads an NTSB report on a fuel-exhaustion accident and remarks, "that guy was careless — nothing like that is ever going to happen to me." Which hazardous attitude is on display, and what is its antidote?
Answer: B. Invulnerability is the belief that accidents happen to other people. The antidote is the deliberate counter-thought "it could happen to me," which is what makes the pilot actually run the fuel numbers.
Aircraft Systems & Instruments · Week 4
15. During preflight you find the pitot mast plugged with an insect nest. Which instrument's indication is directly affected?
Answer: C. Only the ASI uses ram (pitot) pressure — it measures the difference between ram and static. The altimeter and VSI run on static pressure alone; the HI is gyroscopic.
16. In cruise, freezing drizzle seals over the static port while the pitot tube stays clear and the alternate static source is inoperative. For the remainder of the flight, the altimeter and VSI will…
Answer: D. With no new static pressure reaching them, both instruments hold the last valid reading — the altimeter appears to show level flight even in a climb or descent. This is why the alternate static source exists.
17. Established on a heading of 090° in smooth air, you add full power to accelerate. Before it settles, the magnetic compass will momentarily indicate a turn toward…
Answer: A. ANDS: Accelerate North, Decelerate South. The error is caused by magnetic dip acting on the compass card and is strongest on easterly and westerly headings, absent on north/south.
18. Your heading indicator has failed and you are turning onto 350° using the magnetic compass alone. To roll out on the correct heading, you should…
Answer: B. UNOS: Undershoot North, Overshoot South. Turning onto a northerly heading the compass leads — it shows 350° before you are actually there — so you must roll out early.
19. The vacuum pump fails in flight and the attitude indicator slowly topples over the next few minutes. The two physical properties that made that instrument work in the first place are…
Answer: C. A spinning gyro resists tilting of its axis (rigidity in space) and responds to an applied force 90° later in the direction of rotation (precession). Losing rotor speed is what lets the gyro topple.
20. Carburetor icing can occur…
Answer: D. The rapid pressure drop and fuel vaporization can chill the carburetor below freezing even on a mild, humid day.
21. You rent a G1000-equipped 172 and expect traffic targets on the MFD, but no traffic ever appears even near a busy field. The most likely explanation is that the traffic display…
Answer: A. The PFD/MFD is a display, not a sensor — it can only draw traffic that a separate ADS-B In receiver or TCAS-type unit feeds it. ADS-B Out alone transmits your position and shows you nothing.
Weather · Weeks 5–6
22. A front is best defined as…
Answer: B. A front is the boundary between differing air masses — where most significant weather occurs.
23. A fast-moving cold front will cross your route about two hours before your ETA; yesterday's flight went through a warm front instead. Brief this one for…
Answer: C. A cold front's steep leading edge shoves warm air up abruptly, concentrating the weather into a narrow, violent band — often thunderstorms — that clears quickly. A warm front's shallow slope spreads it out into hours of low ceilings and steady rain.
24. Taxiing out on a summer afternoon you see puffy, towering cumulus building over the departure end of the runway. What does that tell you about the air mass, and what should you expect en route?
Answer: D. Cumulus development is the visible signature of instability and rising air; expect a bumpy ride and possible convective build-ups. Flat, layered stratus is the stable-air counterpart — smooth, but with poorer visibility.
25. Descending just below the freezing level through large supercooled droplets, you pick up a hard, heavy, glassy coating that spreads back over the leading edges. This is…
Answer: A. Large supercooled droplets flow back before freezing, producing clear ice — dense, tenacious, and the hardest to shed. Rime forms from small droplets that freeze on contact and is milky, brittle and lighter.
26. Structural icing is generally most hazardous between the freezing level and roughly…
Answer: B. Supercooled liquid water is most abundant from the freezing level up to roughly 20,000 ft.
27. A humid day is followed by a clear, calm night. At 0600 the field is obscured; by 0900 it has burned off completely. This is characteristic of…
Answer: C. Radiation fog needs exactly those ingredients — clear skies and calm wind letting the ground radiate heat away until the surface layer reaches its dew point. Solar heating after sunrise usually dissipates it. Advection fog, by contrast, is blown in and can persist all day.
28. Fog that forms when warm, moist air moves horizontally over a colder surface is called…
Answer: D. Advection fog forms from warm, moist air over a cold surface and can persist regardless of time of day.
29. The turbulence intensity scale used in weather reports runs from Light to…
Answer: A. Light → Moderate → Severe → Extreme, based on aircraft response.
30. The commonly cited minimum distance to avoid a thunderstorm cell showing strong radar returns is…
Answer: B. At least 20 miles — thunderstorms combine severe turbulence, hail, wind shear, and icing at once.
31. A METAR showing a BKN (broken) or OVC (overcast) layer is the lowest such layer reported. This defines the…
Answer: C. The lowest BKN or OVC layer defines the ceiling; FEW and SCT layers do not count.
Aerodynamics & Flight Principles · Week 7
32. You trim the airplane for cruise at 110 KIAS and it holds both altitude and airspeed hands-off. At that moment…
Answer: D. "Unaccelerated" means zero net force in every axis: the opposing pairs balance exactly. Lift exceeding weight would produce a climb; thrust exceeding drag would produce an acceleration.
33. A wing generates lift through the combined effect of…
Answer: A. Both Bernoulli (pressure) and Newton (downward-deflected air, reaction force) describe real, complementary parts of lift.
34. Midway through a steep turn a pilot pulls harder, the airplane buffets and the wing stalls — even though the airspeed indicator reads well above the published 1G stall speed. This happened because…
Answer: B. A stall is an angle-of-attack event, not an airspeed event. Increased load factor in the turn raises the AoA required for level flight, so the critical AoA is reached at a much higher indicated airspeed.
35. Two identical trainers fly the same approach, one loaded to 1,900 lb and the other to 2,400 lb. Comparing the angle of attack at which each wing stalls…
Answer: C. Critical AoA is a property of the airfoil and configuration, essentially fixed. Weight, load factor and configuration change the airspeed at which that AoA is reached — which is why the heavier airplane's stall speed is higher.
36. With a 1G stall speed of 50 KIAS, you hold altitude through a steep turn at 60° of bank — roughly doubling the wing's loading. The airplane will now stall at about…
Answer: D. Stall speed scales with the square root of load factor: 50 × √2 ≈ 50 × 1.41 ≈ 71 KIAS. Note it is not doubled — but 21 kt of margin has quietly vanished.
37. Cruising at 130 KIAS you hit unexpected moderate turbulence; the POH lists Va as 105 KIAS at your current weight. Slowing to or below Va protects the airplane because at or below that speed…
Answer: A. At or below Va the wing stalls first and sheds the load, acting as a mechanical fuse. Above Va, an abrupt full input can exceed structural limits before the wing lets go. Va decreases as weight decreases — a lighter airplane must slow down more.
38. On a slow, high-angle-of-attack short-field approach you find you need substantially more power than in cruise at the same altitude. The form of drag dominating in this regime is…
Answer: B. Induced drag is the price of producing lift — it is generated by wingtip vortices and rises steeply as speed drops and AoA increases. Parasite drag does the opposite, growing with the square of airspeed; the sum of the two is what gives the drag curve its U shape.
39. On short final you extend the flaps from 10° to 30°. The immediate aerodynamic effect is that the flaps…
Answer: C. Flaps increase camber (and on Fowler types, area), which raises the lift coefficient — and raises drag with it. The extra drag is useful: it lets you fly a steeper approach without accelerating.
40. A heavily loaded airplane lifts off, accelerates poorly, then sinks back toward the runway during initial climb. The cushion that let it fly early comes from a phenomenon that, close to the ground…
Answer: D. The surface interferes with wingtip vortex formation, cutting induced drag and letting the wing fly at a lower speed than it truly can sustain. Climbing out of ground effect, that help disappears — the classic density-altitude accident sequence.
Navigation · Week 8
41. Forty miles out, your GPS quits and both VOR receivers go dark; you still have a nav log, a watch, and a compass. Falling back on dead reckoning means you will compute heading and position from…
Answer: A. Dead reckoning is arithmetic navigation: known course and distance, plus airspeed, elapsed time and the wind triangle. It is the reason a paper nav log and a watch remain a genuine backup when the avionics quit.
42. Your planned true course is 090°, but with the forecast wind you must fly a true heading of 097° to stay on the line. Those 7° are…
Answer: B. The wind correction angle is the offset between true course (the path over the ground you want) and true heading (where the nose points), sized so wind drift and heading combine into the desired track. Variation and deviation are separate corrections applied later in the TC→CH chain.
43. Your VOR indicator centers with a FROM flag while the OBS reads 270°. Relative to the station, you are…
Answer: C. Radials are magnetic bearings measured outward from the station, so being on the 270° radial places you west of it. The FROM flag confirms the outbound sense; a TO flag with 270° set would put you east.
44. ATC asks you to "report 10 DME from the VOR." Complying with that request requires equipment providing…
Answer: D. DME interrogates a ground transponder and times the reply, yielding slant-range distance alongside the radial — the second coordinate that turns a line of position into a fix. A plain VOR gives bearing only.
45. Halfway through a cross-country your panel GPS annunciates a RAIM alert. The correct interpretation and immediate action are that…
Answer: A. Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring uses redundant satellites to police its own solution; a warning means it can no longer guarantee the position is trustworthy. Treat it as "verify by other means," not as a hardware failure.
46. You become uncertain of your position over unfamiliar terrain with about 90 minutes of fuel remaining. The standard private-pilot lost procedure directs you to…
Answer: B. Climb for a wider view and better radio/navaid reception, Communicate with ATC or Flight Service, and Confess the situation plainly so they can help. Doing it early — while fuel is still plentiful — is what keeps it an inconvenience rather than an accident.
47. Deteriorating ceilings prompt you to divert to an airport roughly 18 NM off your left wing. The recommended technique is to…
Answer: C. Turn first, refine second. An approximate heading flown now, corrected as you go, beats a perfect plot that costs you ten minutes of fuel, daylight, or deteriorating weather.
48. On a sectional chart, an obstruction symbol is typically labeled with its…
Answer: D. Obstruction symbols show elevation and/or height, critical for terrain/obstacle clearance planning.
49. Converting true course to a final compass heading requires correcting, in order, for…
Answer: A. True course → (wind correction) → true heading → (magnetic variation) → magnetic heading → (compass deviation) → compass heading.
50. The wind triangle used in dead reckoning has three vectors: true course, wind, and…
Answer: B. True course, wind, and the resulting true heading/groundspeed form the closed triangle.
51. On a VOR receiver, a centered CDI needle with a "TO" flag while flying the selected course means…
Answer: C. A centered CDI with a "TO" flag confirms the aircraft is on the selected radial/course, flying toward the station.
Performance & Weight-and-Balance · Week 9
52. Field elevation is 5,000 ft, your altimeter setting yields a pressure altitude of 5,200 ft, and the OAT is 30 °C — so the POH has you enter the takeoff chart at roughly 8,250 ft. That 8,250 ft figure is…
Answer: D. That is density altitude. ISA at 5,200 ft is about +4.6 °C, so 30 °C is roughly 25 °C hot; at about 120 ft per degree that adds ~3,050 ft. The airplane performs as though the runway were at 8,250 ft.
53. Noon departure from a 9,900-ft mountain strip, OAT 30 °C, at maximum gross weight. Measured against the book numbers for a standard day, expect performance that is…
Answer: A. High density altitude cuts air density, which simultaneously reduces engine power, propeller thrust, and lift at a given speed. All three losses push the same direction: a much longer ground roll and a badly degraded climb gradient over rising terrain.
54. Two runways are usable: one gives a 10-kt headwind, the other a 10-kt tailwind. Comparing the POH's two wind corrections to takeoff distance…
Answer: B. The corrections are deliberately asymmetric — a tailwind costs far more distance than the same headwind saves. That is why POH charts often cap the tailwind correction outright and why taking the downwind runway "just this once" is a recurring accident factor.
55. A 170-lb passenger occupies a seat with an arm of 85 in aft of datum, and you enter 14,450 lb-in for that seat in the loading table. That number was obtained by…
Answer: C. Moment = weight × arm, so 170 × 85 = 14,450 lb-in. Summing every station's moment and dividing by total weight yields the loaded CG.
56. The center of gravity (CG) is computed as…
Answer: D. CG = ΣMoments ÷ ΣWeights.
57. You load two adults up front, a third in back, and 60 lb of baggage in the aft compartment; the computed CG lands 1.2 in behind the aft limit. In flight the airplane will tend to be…
Answer: A. An aft CG shortens the tail's stabilizing moment arm, weakening the natural nose-down tendency that makes stall recovery self-correcting. Spin recovery in particular can become unreliable — which is why the aft limit is a hard limit, not a guideline.
58. Looking at the CG envelope in your POH, the shaded region is noticeably wider at the bottom than at the top. The allowable CG range is narrowest as weight approaches…
Answer: B. The envelope pinches in near max gross weight: at maximum load the structure and control authority have the least margin left, so the permissible CG band shrinks exactly when a loading error is most likely.
59. You total the station moments, divide by a gross weight of 2,340 lb, and get a CG of 39.1 in aft of datum. Before you fly, you must…
Answer: C. The arithmetic alone proves nothing about legality. Both the weight and the CG must fall inside the POH envelope for this airplane, and the envelope's limits vary with weight — a CG that is legal light can be illegal heavy.
60. Increasing an aircraft's weight, all else equal, will…
Answer: D. More weight requires more lift at the same AoA, raising stall speed and required distance for both takeoff and landing.
