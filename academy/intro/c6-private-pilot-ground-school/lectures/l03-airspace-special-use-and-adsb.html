Week 3 · Lecture 3

Airspace, Special-Use & ADS-B

The sky is not one uniform space — it's tiered like a wedding cake, and the rules get stricter the closer you get to busy, fast traffic.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization for the FAA knowledge test only. Airspace dimensions and requirements are depicted precisely on current sectional charts and the AIM — always fly from up-to-date charts, never from memory of a study guide.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe Class A through G airspace and what distinguishes each from the others.
  • Recall VFR weather minimums (visibility and cloud clearance) by airspace class.
  • Identify special-use airspace types and what each restricts.
  • State when a transponder and ADS-B Out are required.
  • Read basic airspace symbology on a sectional chart.
  • Apply that symbology to a real, cluttered chart excerpt (San Francisco Bay Area case study).

3.1The Wedding Cake: Class A Through G

U.S. airspace is organized by how much air traffic control service you get and how tightly separated traffic is — not by geography. The classic mental picture is a wedding cake: wide, unrestricted airspace at the bottom and edges, narrowing into more controlled, more demanding airspace around busy airports and at high altitude.

A18,000 ft MSL (FL180) and above, up to FL600IFR flight only — no VFR
BAround the busiest airports (inverted wedding cake)ATC clearance to enter + Mode C transponder; student-pilot restrictions apply
CAround medium-activity airports (two tiers)Two-way radio contact established + Mode C transponder
DAround towered airports without approach controlTwo-way radio contact established (no clearance needed)
EMost other controlled airspace (fills the gaps)No equipment requirement by class alone; VFR minimums apply
GUncontrolled airspace, typically near the groundNo ATC service; least restrictive VFR minimums
Memory aid

The classes also split into two groups, and that split matters more than the individual letters: A, B, C, D, and E are all controlled airspace (ATC service exists there in some form), while G is the only uncontrolled one. Every equipment and clearance requirement in this lecture lives on the controlled side; Class G's defining feature is the absence of all of it.

(There's no Class F in the U.S. — it exists in the ICAO system but the FAA doesn't use it, which is why the sequence jumps E → G.)

3.2VFR Weather Minimums — Visibility & Cloud Clearance

Every airspace class except A (where VFR isn't allowed at all) sets a minimum flight visibility and a minimum distance from clouds. The broad pattern: visibility minimums relax as airspace gets less controlled and lower — in busy controlled airspace you need to see traffic from farther away, and near the ground in uncontrolled airspace there's little IFR traffic to hide in the clouds. Two honest exceptions to that slogan, both commonly tested:

  • Class B is "clear of clouds" — a looser cloud clearance than C/D/E, not a stricter one. The logic: inside Class B, ATC is separating everybody, so you don't need a self-protection buffer the way you do elsewhere.
  • Crossing 10,000 ft MSL tightens everything — visibility jumps to 5 SM and clearances to 1,000/1,000/1 mile, because aircraft above 10,000 may be doing more than 250 knots and closing speeds get much higher.

B3 statute milesClear of clouds
C, D, E (below 10,000 ft MSL)3 statute miles500 ft below · 1,000 ft above · 2,000 ft horizontal

G — above 1,200 ft AGL but below 10,000 ft MSL, day1 statute mile500 ft below · 1,000 ft above · 2,000 ft horizontal

Full §91.155 table for airplanes. Two things worth noticing: Class G is the only class whose minimums change between day and night, and the only place you may legally fly VFR in 1 SM "clear of clouds" is Class G at or below 1,200 ft AGL in the daytime. (Helicopters get lower numbers, and §91.155(b) allows 1 SM clear of clouds at night in an airport traffic pattern within ½ mile of the runway — both beyond what the private-pilot written normally asks.)

AnalogyThink of cloud clearance like following distance in traffic — the busier and faster the "road" (airspace class), the bigger the buffer you need, because an IFR aircraft could pop out of a cloud with very little warning.

3.3Special-Use Airspace

Beyond the lettered classes, certain areas are marked for a specific hazard or mission:

  • Prohibited Area (P-): flight is forbidden entirely (e.g., over sensitive national-security sites).
  • Restricted Area (R-): flight may be hazardous (artillery, aerial gunnery) — entry requires permission from the controlling agency when active.
  • Warning Area: similar hazards, but over international/offshore waters where the U.S. can't restrict entry outright.
  • Military Operations Area (MOA): military training (e.g., aerobatics, low-altitude tactics) — VFR flight is legal but requires caution; check if the MOA is "hot" (active) before transiting.
  • Alert Area: unusually high volume of pilot training or unusual aerial activity — no entry restriction, just extra vigilance.
  • Temporary Flight Restriction (TFR): not a fixed chart symbol — a short-term restriction (VIP movement, wildfires, major sports events) published via NOTAM. Always check current NOTAMs before flying.

"Other Airspace Areas" — the Leftover Category

Beyond the lettered classes and the special-use areas above, the FAA groups a handful of remaining designations under "other airspace areas." None of them prohibit VFR flight, but each carries something you're expected to know:

AreaWhat it isWhat it means for you
MTR
Military Training Route		Routes for military proficiency flying, usually below 10,000 ft MSL at speeds above 250 knotsLegal to cross, but closing speeds are enormous. Read the identifier: IR = IFR, VR = VFR, and the digit count tells you the altitude — four digits (IR1206, VR1207) means no segment above 1,500 ft AGL; three digits (IR206, VR207) means one or more segments above 1,500 ft AGL
TRSA
Terminal Radar Service Area		Areas where radar sequencing and separation service is available to VFR aircraftParticipation is voluntary for VFR — a distinctive feature, since it's not a lettered class with mandatory rules. The primary airport inside is Class D. Depicted with solid black lines
NSA
National Security Area		Airspace around ground facilities needing increased securityPilots are requested to avoid it voluntarily. Flight can be temporarily prohibited by regulation when needed — and that prohibition arrives by NOTAM
CFA
Controlled Firing Area		Activities hazardous to non-participating aircraftThe one you'll never see on a chart — and for a good reason: activity stops whenever a spotter, radar, or ground lookout detects an approaching aircraft. Since it never requires you to change course, there's nothing to depict
Published VFR routesVFR flyways, corridors, and transition routes near busy Class B airspacePre-planned paths for transiting congested areas. A VFR corridor is a "hole" through Class B needing no clearance; a transition route does require an ATC clearance

The one-line distinctions worth memorizingCFA is the only one not charted (activity suspends when you approach). TRSA participation is voluntary for VFR aircraft. NSA compliance is requested, but can become mandatory by NOTAM. And for MTRs, fewer digits means higher altitude — the counterintuitive one, so remember it as 3 digits = 3,000-ish and up, 4 digits = down low.

3.4Transponder & ADS-B Requirements

A transponder (with Mode C altitude reporting) and ADS-B Out are required in roughly the places where controllers most need automated traffic and altitude data:

  • In Class B and Class C airspace.
  • Within the 30-nautical-mile "Mode C veil" around a Class B primary airport, from the surface up to 10,000 ft MSL.
  • Above the ceiling of a Class B or Class C airspace area, up to 10,000 ft MSL.
  • In all airspace above 10,000 ft MSL over the 48 contiguous states, excluding airspace at or below 2,500 ft AGL.

Because most airspace above 10,000 ft MSL happens to be Class E, this is often summarized loosely as "Class E above 10,000 ft" — but the actual rule (14 CFR §91.215 / §91.225 for ADS-B) is based on altitude and proximity to Class B/C, not the airspace letter itself.

Note · not a traffic displayA transponder or ADS-B Out transmits your position — it doesn't show you other traffic. Seeing traffic on a cockpit display requires ADS-B In or a separate TCAS-type system; a glass panel like a G1000 is not automatically a traffic system.

3.5Reading a Sectional Chart

Sectional charts depict airspace with specific line styles and colors — recognizing these is a direct, practice-focus item on the knowledge test.

3.6Case Study — Reading the San Francisco Bay Area Sectional

A real sectional is far busier than the clean two-symbol excerpt above — dozens of airspace boundaries, frequencies, and obstructions all overlap. Here's the same skill applied to a genuinely cluttered area: the airspace around San Francisco Intl (SFO), with Oakland (OAK), San Carlos (SQL), and Hayward (HWD) all packed nearby. Open the live version yourself at skyvector.com — search "SFO" and zoom in to follow along.

  • SFO vs. KSFO — same airport. The figure uses both: "SFO" is the plain 3-letter identifier, and "KSFO" is the 4-letter ICAO identifier used in chart notes, METARs, and ATC. Any airport in the contiguous 48 states gets a "K" prefixed onto its identifier for the ICAO form — SFO → KSFO, OAK → KOAK, SQL → KSQL, HWD → KHWD — same airport, same identifier, just with or without the leading K.
  • Same shape, different scale. SFO's Class B is the "inverted wedding cake" from 3.1 in real form — the numbers on each ring (e.g. 100/SFC) are the ceiling and floor in hundreds of feet MSL for that tier. The innermost ring reaching the surface is exactly why Class B needs an explicit ATC clearance rather than just radio contact.
  • Three towered fields, three rules. Oakland's solid magenta rings (Class C) need two-way radio contact plus Mode C; San Carlos and Hayward's dashed blue circles (Class D) need radio contact only — same distinction as the table in 3.1, just crammed into a few nautical miles of each other.
  • Airspace isn't the only hazard. The cluster of towers, cranes, and piers near the waterfront stadium is a reminder from 3.5: obstruction heights are printed independently of airspace boundaries. A tall crane can matter even in airspace that itself imposes no special requirement.
  • "NO SVFR" is a real chart note. Special VFR (§91.157) lets ATC clear an aircraft into a Class B/C/D/E surface area below normal VFR minimums (as low as 1 SM, clear of clouds, by day) when the tower and pilot agree. Some very busy fields — SFO among them — are marked NO SVFR specifically because traffic density makes that reduced margin unsafe.
  • Marginal notes carry real information. The "CAUTION 124.3 at/below 2,000′" box is the chart telling you a frequency gets crowded at low altitude in this area — the kind of local knowledge that only shows up by reading the actual chart, not a generic table.

✦ Worked Example — can you enter this airspace?

Scenario: You're VFR at 4,500 ft MSL, 3 statute miles visibility, approaching a Class C airport with your Mode C transponder squawking. Can you enter, and what minimums apply?

  1. Check equipment. Class C requires a two-way radio and a Mode C transponder — you have both.
  2. Establish contact. You need two-way radio communication established with ATC (not necessarily a "cleared" readback like Class B — just contact) before entering.
  3. Check weather minimums. Class C requires 3 SM visibility and 500/1,000/2,000 cloud clearance below 10,000 ft MSL — your 3 SM meets the visibility minimum exactly.
  4. Decision: if cloud clearance also meets 500 below/1,000 above/2,000 horizontal, you may enter once radio contact is established. If visibility were 2 SM, you could not legally enter Class C VFR.

💬 Discussion 1 — Week 3

Prompt: Airspace rules get stricter the busier and faster the traffic gets (G → E → D → C → B → A). Why does it make sense to tie the rules to traffic density and speed rather than just altitude, and where does ADS-B fit into that logic?

✓ Saved

Model answer: Traffic density and speed are the actual risk drivers, not altitude alone — two aircraft need more separation and reaction time the faster they close and the more traffic is nearby. Tying equipment and clearance requirements to that risk (rather than a flat altitude rule) concentrates the FAA's most demanding requirements exactly where a mid-air is most likely and most consequential — a major hub — while leaving remote Class G airspace unencumbered. ADS-B extends this logic: it gives ATC (and, with ADS-B In, other pilots) precise position/velocity data automatically, which is most valuable exactly where traffic is densest — hence its requirement mirrors the transponder/Mode C footprint (Class B/C, the Mode C veil, and above 10,000 ft MSL).

✓ Rubric: mentions risk/closing-speed reasoning (not just "rules are rules"); connects equipment requirements to where controllers most need traffic data; correctly ties ADS-B to the same high-traffic footprint as the transponder rule.

B. 5 statute miles

Q4. A Military Operations Area (MOA) that is "hot" (active) means…

D. MOAs don't legally bar VFR traffic even when active, but flying through one during military training (aerobatics, low-level tactics) carries real collision risk — checking activation status first is standard practice.

A. TFRs are not permanent chart symbols — they're issued as needed via NOTAM, so checking current NOTAMs before every flight is essential.

✓ What you learned

  • Airspace is a "wedding cake": A (18,000+ IFR only), B (major, clearance), C (medium, radio+Mode C), D (towered, radio), E (fills gaps), G (uncontrolled).
  • VFR visibility minimums relax as airspace gets less controlled and lower — with two exceptions to know: Class B is clear of clouds (looser, because ATC separates everyone), and crossing 10,000 ft MSL tightens everything to 5 SM and 1,000/1,000/1 mile. Class G is the only class with different day and night numbers.
  • Special-use airspace — Prohibited, Restricted, Warning, MOA, Alert — each flags a different kind of hazard; TFRs are temporary and NOTAM-published.
  • Transponder/ADS-B requirements track Class B/C, the Mode C veil, and altitude above 10,000 ft MSL — not a single airspace letter.
  • Sectional charts use specific line styles/colors (dashed blue = Class D, solid magenta = Class C) to depict all of this.
  • Applied to a real excerpt (SFO/OAK/SQL/HWD): the same symbols hold up under clutter, and charts also carry frequencies, obstruction heights, and marginal notes like NO SVFR that a plain rules table won't tell you.
  • Other airspace areas: CFA is never charted (activity stops when you approach) · TRSA participation is voluntary for VFR · NSA avoidance is requested (can become mandatory by NOTAM) · MTR digit count is inverted — 4 digits = below 1,500 AGL, 3 digits = some segment above.
