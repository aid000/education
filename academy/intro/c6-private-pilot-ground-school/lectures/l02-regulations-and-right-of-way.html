Week 2 · Lecture 2

Regulations — Part 61/91 & Right-of-Way

Who gets to fly, under what rules, and who yields to whom when two aircraft meet in the sky.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization for the FAA knowledge test only — not a substitute for a CFI or the current FAR/AIM. Regulations are amended periodically; always verify the current rule before relying on it operationally.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Distinguish what Part 61 (certification/currency) governs from what Part 91 (operating rules) governs.
  • State the day/night passenger-carrying currency requirements.
  • Recite the right-of-way hierarchy among aircraft categories, and the rules for converging, head-on, overtaking, and landing traffic.
  • Explain why each right-of-way rule is built the way it is — maneuverability, closing speed, and visibility.
  • Describe how to declare and signal an in-flight emergency.
  • Name the five traffic-pattern legs, fly the standard 45° entry, and use correct CTAF self-announce phraseology.
  • List the documents required onboard (ARROW) and the inspection intervals that keep an aircraft airworthy.
  • Interpret runway/taxiway markings and signs, VASI/PAPI indications, and light gun signals.

2.1Part 61 vs. Part 91: Two Different Jobs

The regulations pilots live under are spread across many parts of 14 CFR, but two carry almost all the private-pilot-relevant weight, and the test consistently distinguishes them:

  • 14 CFR Part 61 — Certification of Pilots. Who may hold a certificate, what ratings/endorsements mean, and how you stay current to exercise the privileges of that certificate.
  • 14 CFR Part 91 — General Operating and Flight Rules. What you may and may not do once airborne — right-of-way, VFR minimums, speed limits, equipment requirements.
AnalogyThink of Part 61 as your driver's license rules (who may drive, how you keep the license valid) and Part 91 as the rules of the road (speed limits, right-of-way, when headlights are required). One is about the pilot; the other is about the flight.

Currency (Part 61.57) — the recurring test favorite

To carry passengers, a pilot must have made, within the preceding 90 days, at least 3 takeoffs and 3 landings in an aircraft of the same category/class (and type, if required). For night currency (carrying passengers between one hour after sunset and one hour before sunrise), those 3 takeoffs and landings must have been done at night, and — for landings — to a full stop.

Day currency: 3 T/L in 90 days
Night currency: 3 full-stop T/L at night in 90 daysTwo separate clocks — day currency does not satisfy night currency, or vice versa.

2.2Part 91 Operating Rules — the Headlines

A few Part 91 rules show up on almost every test form:

  • Speed limit: 250 knots indicated airspeed (KIAS) below 10,000 ft MSL (§91.117) — a see-and-avoid safety margin, not a fuel-saving rule.
  • VFR weather minimums vary by airspace class — covered in depth next lecture, since they're really an airspace topic.
  • Careless or reckless operation (§91.13) is a catch-all: even if no specific rule is broken, endangering life or property is itself a violation.
  • Preflight action (§91.103): before any flight, a pilot must become familiar with all available information concerning that flight — weather, runway lengths, fuel requirements, alternatives if the flight cannot be completed as planned.
Note · reporting after an accidentNot all incidents are "accidents" in the regulatory sense. NTSB Part 830 defines what must be reported (an accident involves death, serious injury, or substantial aircraft damage) and requires wreckage/records to be preserved until the NTSB releases them — don't move wreckage prematurely except to protect it from further damage or to help injured people.

Documents Onboard: ARROW

Part 61 covers what you must carry; a separate set of rules covers what the aircraft must carry. The standard mnemonic is ARROW:

Inspections: What Must Be Current

Under Part 91 the owner/operator is responsible for keeping the aircraft airworthy — but as PIC you're responsible for confirming it is, before every flight. The intervals worth memorizing:

InspectionIntervalApplies to
AnnualEvery 12 calendar monthsAll aircraft. Must be signed off by an A&P with Inspection Authorization (IA)
100-hourEvery 100 hours in serviceOnly if carrying persons for hire, or giving flight instruction for hire — so it applies to most rental/trainer aircraft but not private use. An annual can substitute for a 100-hour; the reverse is not true
TransponderEvery 24 calendar monthsRequired regardless of airspace, before transponder use (§91.413)
Altimeter/pitot-staticEvery 24 calendar monthsRequired for IFR in controlled airspace (§91.411)
ELTEvery 12 calendar monthsInspection of installation, battery corrosion, controls, and signal (§91.207). Battery replaced after 1 hour of cumulative use or at 50% of useful life
VOR checkEvery 30 daysOnly for IFR flight using VOR navigation

The 100-hour limit may be exceeded by up to 10 hours, but only to reposition the aircraft to where the inspection will be done — and the overage counts against the next 100-hour interval.

Airworthiness Directives & inoperative equipmentAirworthiness Directives (ADs) are mandatory — issued by the FAA when an unsafe condition is found in a type of aircraft, engine, or part. Compliance isn't optional and an aircraft with an overdue AD is not airworthy.

For inoperative equipment, the default under Part 91 is that everything installed must work before departure. The common path for small aircraft without a formal Minimum Equipment List is the §91.213(d) deferral: if the item isn't required by the type certificate, the equipment list, an AD, or the VFR-day instrument requirements, it may be deactivated or removed, placarded "INOPERATIVE," and the flight made — after a pilot or mechanic determines it isn't a hazard. Deferrals are a preflight decision, never an in-flight one.

2.3Right-of-Way: Why the Order Exists

§91.113 sets a strict pecking order for which aircraft category must give way when two converge at about the same altitude. The order runs from least maneuverable / hardest to see to most maneuverable — the aircraft that can least easily get out of the way has the right-of-way:

As a private-pilot-airplane candidate, you'll be flying near the bottom of that order almost every time — meaning you typically give way to balloons, gliders, airships, and powered parachutes/weight-shift aircraft.

2.4Converging, Head-On, Overtaking, and Landing

Within the same category (e.g., two airplanes), four specific geometries each have their own rule — and each rule reduces to the same underlying logic: turn right, pass behind, don't cut anyone off.

AnalogyIt's the aviation version of a four-way-stop rule: "yield to the right" removes ambiguity. If both pilots independently apply the same rule (turn right, give way to the right), their solutions can't collide with each other — which is exactly why "both turn left" or "whoever is faster goes first" would be dangerous alternatives.

2.5Declaring an Emergency

An aircraft in distress outranks every other rule in this lecture. To declare an emergency:

  • Radio: say "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday" (life-threatening emergency) or "Pan-Pan" (urgent, not immediately life-threatening) on the frequency you're using, or 121.5 MHz if no other contact is available.
  • Transponder: squawk 7700 — this lights up your aircraft on every ATC radar scope in range as an emergency, even with no radio contact.
  • Once declared, §91.3 gives the pilot-in-command final authority to deviate from any rule in Part 91 to the extent required to meet the emergency.
7700 = emergency · 7600 = lost communications · 7500 = hijackingThe three "special purpose" transponder codes — 7700 is the one that matters most for this lecture.

2.6Airport Traffic Patterns

Every airport, towered or not, funnels arriving and departing traffic through the same predictable rectangle in the sky — the traffic pattern. Knowing its five named legs, the standard turn direction, how to join it, and what to say (or not say) on the radio is what actually keeps that shared airspace safe, and it's tested directly.

The Rectangular Pattern — Five Legs

Starting from takeoff, a full circuit has five legs, each with its own name and job:

  • Departure (upwind) leg — runway heading, straight ahead after takeoff, climbing.
  • Crosswind leg — a 90° turn away from the runway, flown beyond the departure end at (or approaching) pattern altitude.
  • Downwind leg — parallel to the runway, opposite direction of landing, at pattern altitude — this is where most position reports happen and where you run the pre-landing checklist.
  • Base leg — a 90° turn toward the runway's approach end, usually the point you start descending.
  • Final approach leg — aligned with the runway centerline, descending to land.

Note the terminology trap: "upwind leg" strictly means a leg parallel to the runway, flown in the landing direction — the departure leg technically becomes the upwind leg only if you remain in the pattern (e.g. a touch-and-go) and re-fly that same ground track. Most training materials use the two terms almost interchangeably for the initial climb-out; the ACS/AIM distinction rarely matters for the written test, but knowing it exists avoids confusion reading FAA diagrams.

Joining the Pattern: the 45° Entry

At a towered field, ATC tells you exactly how to join. At a non-towered field, there's no one to ask — so pilots follow a standard, predictable procedure precisely so everyone's expectations match: arrive at pattern altitude and join the downwind leg at a 45° angle, intercepting it at or before the point abeam the runway's midpoint.

✦ Worked Example — self-announce calls at a non-towered field

Scenario: Inbound to Podunk Airport (non-towered), landing runway 36, standard left traffic. The format is always: [Airport] traffic, [callsign], [position/leg], [runway], [airport].

  1. 10 miles out: "Podunk traffic, Skyhawk One-Two-Three-Four-Five, ten miles south, inbound, will enter left downwind runway three-six, Podunk."
  2. Entering downwind: "Podunk traffic, Skyhawk One-Two-Three-Four-Five, entering left downwind runway three-six, Podunk."
  3. Turning base: "Podunk traffic, Skyhawk One-Two-Three-Four-Five, turning left base runway three-six, Podunk."
  4. Turning final: "Podunk traffic, Skyhawk One-Two-Three-Four-Five, final runway three-six, Podunk."
  5. Clear of the runway: "Podunk traffic, Skyhawk One-Two-Three-Four-Five, clear of runway three-six, Podunk."

Why Takeoff and Landing Go Into the Wind

A headwind reduces the groundspeed needed to reach the same critical airspeed the wing needs to fly (§7.3) — so a headwind takeoff needs less ground roll to reach rotation speed, climbs out at a steeper angle over the ground, and a headwind landing touches down at a lower groundspeed with a shorter rollout. A tailwind is the exact opposite: more ground roll to reach the same airspeed, and a faster, longer landing rollout — which is exactly why most POHs cap tailwind takeoffs/landings at a small limit (often around 10 kt) rather than prohibiting them outright.

So is the airport "set up" for the wind?Partly, yes — runways are laid out according to the local prevailing winds, which is why a single-runway field points the way it does. But prevailing isn't permanent: airports with more variable winds (and most larger fields) add a second or third runway orientation so there's always a reasonable headwind option. Either way the "active" runway isn't fixed — it's whichever available runway is best aligned into the wind right now, taken fresh from ATIS/AWOS or the windsock every time you fly. At a single-runway field in a tailwind, that may mean landing the other direction on the same strip.

2.7Airport Markings, Signs & Lighting

Once the pattern puts you on the ground, a second language takes over — paint on the pavement, signs beside it, and lights around it. It's standardized nationwide precisely so an unfamiliar airport still reads the same, and it's one of the most directly tested topics on the written.

Runway Markings

Runway markings are always white; taxiway markings are always yellow. That one color rule resolves most "which surface am I on?" questions instantly.

Hold-Short Markings & Signs

The single most important marking on the airport surface is the runway holding position marking: four yellow lines — two solid, two dashed. The rule that prevents most runway incursions is simply which side you're on.

LAHSO & runway incursionsLand and Hold Short Operations (LAHSO) ask you to land and stop before an intersecting runway. A LAHSO clearance requires your acceptance — and as PIC you may always decline it, with no explanation needed, if the available landing distance or conditions make you uncomfortable. Student pilots should not accept LAHSO.

A runway incursion is any unauthorized presence on a runway. The defenses are mundane and effective: read back all hold-short instructions verbatim, have an airport diagram out during taxi, never cross a solid double-line without an explicit clearance, and keep the cockpit sterile (no non-essential talk) below the hold line.

Visual Glideslope Indicators: VASI & PAPI

These give you a glidepath reference by eye, using red-vs-white light. Both provide obstacle clearance within 10° of the extended centerline out to 4 NM, and both are normally set to a 3° glidepath.

Runway Lighting & Pilot Control

  • Runway edge lights are white, except the last 2,000 ft (or half the runway, whichever is less) of an instrument runway, where they turn yellow as a caution zone.
  • Threshold lights are bi-directional: green seen from the approach end, red seen from the rollout end — green to land toward, red to stop before.
  • REIL (Runway End Identifier Lights) are a pair of synchronized flashing white lights at the threshold — for identifying a runway that's hard to pick out from surrounding lighting.
  • Taxiway edge lights are blue; taxiway centerline lights are green. Blue = taxiway is the fastest night cue there is.
  • Pilot-controlled lighting: at many non-towered fields you key the mic on the CTAF to turn lights on — 7 clicks = highest intensity, 5 = medium, 3 = lowest, within 5 seconds. Keying 7 times first always activates everything, which is why the standard advice is 7 clicks then dial down.

Light Gun Signals

If your radio fails, a towered airport can still clear you using a directional light gun. This table is a perennial test item — and the underlying logic makes it easier than memorizing twelve cells: green = go/permitted, red = stop/don't, flashing = a modified or conditional version of the steady meaning.

Acknowledging a light signalYou can't reply by radio, so you reply by moving something visible: in flight, rock your wings by day or blink your landing/navigation lights at night; on the ground, move the ailerons or rudder by day, or blink the lights at night. And remember the lost-comms squawk from §2.5 — 7600.
✦ Worked Example — resolving a converging scenario

Scenario: You're flying a Cessna 172 straight and level. A Piper Cherokee, also straight and level at your altitude, is approaching from your 2 o'clock position on a converging course. Who yields, and what do you do?

  1. Identify the category. Both are airplanes — same category, so the category-order pyramid doesn't apply; use the converging-traffic rule instead.
  2. Find the relative bearing. The other aircraft is at your 2 o'clock — off to your right.
  3. Apply the rule. The aircraft to the right has right-of-way. Since the Cherokee is on your right, you must yield.
  4. Execute. Alter course to the right so you pass behind the Cherokee — never dive under or climb over blindly, and never turn left across its path.
  5. Confirm. Maintain visual contact through the maneuver; don't resume your original course until well clear.

13 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Which part of 14 CFR governs pilot certification and currency requirements?
A. Part 61
B. Part 91
C. Part 830
D. Part 43
Show answer

A. Part 61 covers certification of pilots, including currency; Part 91 covers operating rules in flight.

Q2. To carry passengers at night, a pilot's 3 required takeoffs and landings in the preceding 90 days must be…
A. Made during daylight hours only
B. Made at night, with landings to a full stop
C. Any 3 landings, full-stop or not
D. Not required if day currency is met
Show answer

B. Night currency requires 3 takeoffs and full-stop landings at night within the preceding 90 days — a separate requirement from day currency.

Q3. The maximum indicated airspeed below 10,000 ft MSL (outside of special airspace) is…
A. 200 KIAS
B. 230 KIAS
C. 250 KIAS
D. 300 KIAS
Show answer

C. 250 KIAS below 10,000 ft MSL — a see-and-avoid margin, since closing speeds and reaction time matter most near busy, lower-altitude traffic.

Q4. In the right-of-way category order, which aircraft yields to a glider?
A. A balloon
B. Another glider of equal category
C. An aircraft in distress
D. An airplane
Show answer

D. Airplanes are at the bottom of the order and yield to balloons, gliders, airships, and powered parachutes/weight-shift aircraft. Only an aircraft in distress outranks a glider.

Q5. Two airplanes are approaching head-on at the same altitude. What should each pilot do?
A. Both turn right
B. Both climb
C. The faster aircraft turns left
D. Both hold course and altitude
Show answer

A. Head-on traffic: each pilot alters course to the right. Both applying the same rule prevents the avoidance maneuvers from creating a new conflict.

Q6. An aircraft is overtaking another aircraft of the same category from directly behind. Who has the right-of-way?
A. The overtaking (faster) aircraft
B. The aircraft being overtaken
C. Whichever aircraft is higher
D. Right-of-way does not apply to overtaking
Show answer

B. The aircraft being overtaken has the right-of-way; the overtaking aircraft must alter course to the right and pass well clear.

Q7. What transponder code signals an in-flight emergency?
A. 7500
B. 7600
C. 7700
D. 1200
Show answer

C. 7700 = emergency. (7600 = lost comms, 7500 = hijacking, 1200 = standard VFR code in the U.S.)

Q8. In a standard traffic pattern, all four turns (crosswind, downwind, base, final) are made in which direction?
A. Right, always
B. Left, unless the airport indicates right traffic
C. Alternating left and right
D. Whichever direction avoids the sun
Show answer

B. Left traffic is the default; some airports use right traffic instead (marked on the sectional and via the airport's segmented circle), but nothing alternates — a given airport/runway is consistently one or the other.

Q9. At a non-towered airport, the recommended way to join the traffic pattern is to…
A. Cross directly over midfield at pattern altitude, then descend on the far side
B. Always enter straight in on final, regardless of other traffic
C. Enter the downwind leg at a 45° angle, at pattern altitude
D. Circle overhead above pattern altitude until the pattern is empty
Show answer

C. The 45°-to-downwind entry, at pattern altitude, is the standard community practice (AC 90-66) — predictable to everyone else in the pattern, which is the whole point when there's no tower to sequence traffic.

Q10. While taxiing, you approach four yellow lines — two solid on your side, two dashed beyond them. Without a clearance, you may…
A. Cross, since dashed lines always mean crossing is permitted
B. Not cross — you must hold short until ATC clears you onto the runway
C. Cross only if you cannot see any landing traffic
D. Cross, because solid lines apply only to aircraft exiting the runway
Show answer

B. Solid lines on your side means you're approaching the runway and must hold short. You cross from the dashed side only when exiting the runway — and you're not clear of the runway until the entire aircraft is past the solid lines.

Q11. On approach you see two red lights over two white lights on the PAPI. This means you are…
A. Too high
B. On the glidepath
C. Too low
D. Aligned left of centerline
Show answer

B. Two red + two white = on glidepath. More red means lower, more white means higher — "red over white, you're all right; all red, you're dead; all white, you're high as a kite."

Q12. Your radio fails inbound to a towered airport and the tower aims a steady green light at you. This means…
A. Return for landing
B. Cleared to land
C. Airport unsafe, do not land
D. Give way to other aircraft and continue circling
Show answer

B. Steady green in flight = cleared to land (on the ground it means cleared for takeoff). Flashing green in flight is "return for landing"; steady red is "give way and keep circling"; flashing red is "airport unsafe, do not land."

Q13. Which document required onboard is needed only for international flight?
A. Airworthiness certificate
B. Registration certificate
C. Radio station license
D. Weight and balance data
Show answer

C. The radio station license (an FCC document, not FAA) is in the ARROW mnemonic but is only required when flying internationally — a common trap. The other four are required for all flights.

✓ What you learned

  • Part 61 governs the pilot (certification, currency); Part 91 governs the flight (operating rules).
  • Day currency = 3 T/L in 90 days; night currency = 3 full-stop T/L at night in 90 days — separate clocks.
  • The right-of-way category order runs from least to most maneuverable: distress → balloon → glider → airship → powered parachute/weight-shift → airplane/rotorcraft.
  • Converging: right has priority. Head-on: both turn right. Overtaking: overtaking aircraft yields right. Landing: lower aircraft on final has priority.
  • Declare an emergency by radio (Mayday) and/or transponder (squawk 7700) — the PIC then has authority to deviate from any rule as needed.
  • Onboard docs = ARROW (radio license only for international). Key intervals: annual 12 months, 100-hour only if for hire, transponder & altimeter 24 months, ELT 12 months. ADs are mandatory; inoperative gear is deferred and placarded under §91.213(d).
  • Runway markings are white, taxiway yellow; solid hold-short lines face the side you must hold on. Sign colors: red = mandatory, yellow-on-black = location, black-on-yellow = direction. VASI/PAPI: more red = lower. Light gun: steady green = cleared to land/takeoff.
  • The traffic pattern has five legs — departure/upwind, crosswind, downwind, base, final — flown as four left turns by default. Join via the 45° entry to downwind at pattern altitude; self-announce on CTAF at non-towered fields since there's no tower to sequence you. Takeoffs/landings favor a headwind runway because it shortens ground roll for the same airspeed.
