Aircraft Systems & Instruments

Two invisible systems — a plumbing system and a spinning-wheel system — are what let a "six-pack" of gauges tell you the truth about an airplane you can't feel from the inside.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization for the FAA knowledge test only. Specific aircraft systems vary by make/model — always fly from your aircraft's POH/AFM, not a general study guide.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the six primary flight instruments and which system (pitot-static or gyroscopic) drives each.
  • Trace how the pitot-static system produces airspeed, altitude, and vertical speed — and predict the effect of a blockage.
  • Explain the two magnetic compass errors — ANDS and UNOS — and when each matters most.
  • Describe basic electrical, fuel, and engine systems at a private-pilot level.
  • Explain what a glass cockpit (e.g. G1000-family) does and doesn't provide by default.
  • Read a turn coordinator's miniature airplane and ball, and explain why rudder is needed to keep a turn coordinated.
  • Read a three-pointer altimeter and distinguish indicated, pressure, density, true, and absolute altitude.
  • Explain the reasoning behind standard responses to engine failure, in-flight fire, and system malfunctions.

4.1The "Six-Pack": Two Systems, Six Gauges

Nearly every traditional analog panel arranges six instruments in two rows of three — the "six-pack." Despite looking like six independent gadgets, they're driven by just two physical systems: the pitot-static system (air pressure) and gyroscopic instruments (spinning wheels).

4.2The Pitot-Static System

A forward-facing pitot tube captures ram air pressure (moving air hitting it); a flush static port on the fuselage senses undisturbed ambient air pressure. Comparing and using these two pressures drives three instruments:

BlockageEffect
Pitot tube blocked (static port open)ASI acts like an altimeter: reads inaccurately, rising in a climb and falling in a descent, regardless of actual airspeed
Static port blocked (pitot open)Altimeter freezes at the last correct altitude; VSI freezes at zero; ASI reads incorrectly, erring low above and high below the blockage altitude
Note · alternate static sourceMost training aircraft include an alternate static source valve inside the cabin for exactly this failure — opening it restores static pressure (usually reading slightly differently than the blocked primary source, per the POH).

Reading the Airspeed Indicator: the Color-Coded Arcs

Any FAA-certificated aircraft weighing 12,500 lb or less and built after 1945 must have its ASI marked with a standard color-coded system — so the arcs mean the same thing in every airplane you'll fly, and the boundary between two colors is always a named V-speed. The whole point is that you can check a limit at a glance without remembering a number.

MarkingV-speedMeaning
Bottom of whiteVSOStall speed in landing configuration (gear + full flaps down), power off, max landing weight
Top of whiteVFEMaximum flaps-extended speed — don't extend flaps above this
Bottom of greenVS1Stall speed clean (gear/flaps up), power off, max takeoff weight
Top of greenVNOMaximum structural cruising speed — exceed only in smooth air

Not on the dialSome limits are never marked on the ASI face and live only on placards or in the POH — most importantly VA (design maneuvering speed, from §7.4), because it changes with weight and so can't be printed as a fixed mark. VX (best angle of climb) and VY (best rate of climb) aren't marked either. If a question asks for a speed you can't find a color for, that's the tell — it's a POH number, not an arc.

Reading a Three-Pointer Altimeter

The classic round altimeter puts three hands on one dial, each completing a revolution at a different rate: the short, solid hand reads ten-thousands of feet (one revolution = 100,000 ft), the medium hand with a hollow arrowhead reads thousands of feet (one revolution = 10,000 ft), and the long, thin hand reads hundreds of feet (one revolution = 1,000 ft). Read them shortest to longest and add the three numbers together.

✦ Worked Example — reading the altimeter above
  1. Short hand (ten-thousands): just past "0," well short of "1" → reads 0 (0 × 10,000 = 0 ft). This hand barely moves in normal GA flying — don't mistake it approaching a number for having reached it.
  2. Medium hand (thousands): exactly halfway between "9" and "0" → reads 9 (9 × 1,000 = 9,000 ft). This is the hand most often misread: if it looks close to "0," you have to judge whether it has completed the loop back to 0, or is still approaching 0 from the "9" side.
  3. Long hand (hundreds): pointing straight down at "5" → reads 5 (5 × 100 = 500 ft).
  4. Add them: 00,000 + 9,000 + 500 = 9,500 ft.

The trap gets sharper the closer you are to a "rollover," where several hands approach their next number at once. At 19,900 ft, all three hands sit just before a mark:

The rule that avoids both trapsWhen a hand sits close to a numeral, decide whether it has completed the trip to that mark or is still approaching it — cross-check against the hand one size down (the thousands hand near "9,900" confirms the hundreds hand should also be near its top, not freshly past zero). When genuinely unsure, treat it as not yet arrived: misjudging just the ten-thousands hand turns 19,900 ft into 29,900 ft — a 10,000 ft error from one hand, which is exactly the kind of mistake a real altimeter misread produces.

What you read off the dial depends entirely on what's set in the Kollsman window — so "altitude" isn't one number, it's five different ones, each answering a different question.

Altimeter Errors Caused by Pressure

An altimeter only tells you the truth if the Kollsman window matches the air you're actually in right now. Fly a long way without updating it and the atmosphere itself, not the instrument, is what drifts out from under you: pressure surfaces slope — the "29.0 inch" layer of air, for instance, sits at a different true altitude depending on where you are. If you hold a constant indicated altitude while that slope drops away beneath you, your true altitude drops with it, silently.

The paired memory aid "High to low, watch below" — flying from high pressure into low pressure (or warm into cold) without resetting the altimeter, indicated altitude reads higher than true altitude: you're lower than the instrument says, and terrain/obstacle clearance is the real danger.

"Low to high, clear the sky" — the reverse trip (low pressure into high, or cold into warm) makes indicated altitude read lower than true altitude: you're actually higher than the instrument says. Not risk-free (separation from other traffic/airspace floors can still be off), but not the terrain trap the other direction is — hence "clear the sky" rather than a ground warning.

This is also why density altitude matters for performance: high density altitude (hot, high, humid) means the wing and engine perform as if you were much higher than pressure altitude alone suggests — longer takeoff roll, reduced climb rate.

Cold Weather Altimeter Errors

Pressure isn't the only thing the altimeter can't see — it also has no way to sense outside air temperature. Cold air is denser than standard air, so it "compresses" the atmosphere: a given pressure level physically sits lower than it would on a standard-temperature day. The altimeter, which only ever measures pressure, has no idea any of that happened — it keeps reporting the same indicated altitude regardless of temperature. The result is the same direction of error as the high-to-low pressure case, just with a different cause: on a cold day, holding a constant indicated altitude means your true altitude is lower than what the instrument shows — and the colder it is, or the higher the terrain, the bigger that gap gets. This is exactly why cold-weather/mountain airports publish (and some approaches require) a manual cold-temperature altitude correction.

One combined rulePressure and temperature errors stack in the same direction, which is why the full classic memory aid combines both: "From high to low, or hot to cold, look out below." A flight that's both heading into lower pressure and colder air (a cold front passage, for example) gets the worst of both — true altitude sags well below what the altimeter reports, exactly when you're least likely to notice.

4.3Gyroscopic Instruments

The attitude indicator, heading indicator, and turn coordinator all rely on a spinning gyroscope's two core properties: rigidity in space (a spinning mass resists having its axis tilted) and precession (a force applied to a spinning gyro is felt 90° later, in the direction of rotation). Older aircraft spin these gyros with engine-driven vacuum pumps; many glass panels replace them with solid-state electronic sensors that emulate the same display.

The heading indicator specifically is not magnetic — it just holds whatever heading it's set to via rigidity in space, and has no idea which way is actually north. Small amounts of internal friction and the Earth's own rotation underneath the gyro's fixed axis cause it to slowly drift off true heading over time even in smooth air. The fix is procedural: reset the heading indicator against the magnetic compass about every 15 minutes, and only during straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight (see §4.4) — the one condition where the compass itself is trustworthy.

Turn Coordinator — Roll Rate, Then Yaw Rate

The turn coordinator (TC) is the electric gyro that replaced the older turn-and-slip indicator. Its miniature airplane symbol does something the attitude indicator can't: during the roll into a turn, its deflection is proportional to roll rate; once the bank stabilizes, that same needle instead reflects rate of turn (yaw rate) — the AI shows actual bank angle, the TC shows how fast you're rolling or turning. The wingtip lining up with the fixed "doghouse" index mark on either side means the airplane is turning at the standard rate — 3°/second, a full 360° circle in 2 minutes (hence the "2 MIN" printed on the face) — regardless of what the actual bank angle happens to be at that airspeed.

Why "D.C. ELEC." mattersUnlike the vacuum-driven attitude and heading indicators, the turn coordinator is electrically powered — that's exactly what the "D.C. ELEC." label on its face is telling you. A vacuum-pump failure takes out the AI and HI together but leaves the TC alive; an electrical failure (alternator/battery) does the opposite. Knowing which instruments share a power source is a direct, testable partial-panel scenario.

Coordinated Flight: Why the Rudder Matters

Ailerons alone don't turn an airplane cleanly. Rolling into a right turn deflects the left aileron down (increasing that wing's camber, lift, and — critically — induced drag) and the right aileron up (decreasing lift and drag on that wing). The extra drag on the rising, left wing yaws the nose away from the turn at first — this is adverse yaw, and it's why an aileron-only roll makes the nose momentarily swing the wrong way. Coordinated rudder pressure in the direction of the turn (right rudder for a right turn) cancels that yaw so the nose tracks smoothly into the bank instead of skidding sideways through it.

The inclinometer — the ball built into the bottom of the turn coordinator — is how you check that balance all the way through the turn, not just at roll-in. A slip (not enough rudder, or too much bank for the turn rate) throws the ball toward the low wing; a skid (too much rudder, or too little bank for the turn rate) throws it toward the high wing. Either way, the fix is the same one-line rule: step on the ball — apply rudder pressure toward whichever side the ball has moved.

4.4Magnetic Compass Errors: ANDS & UNOS

A magnetic compass is simple, reliable, and always works — but it lags and leads in predictable, memorizable ways whenever the airplane accelerates, decelerates, or turns. These errors are two of the highest-yield items on the entire knowledge test.

ANDS — acceleration/deceleration error (matters most on East/West headings)

UNOS — turning error (strongest near North/South, worst at high latitude)

AnalogyPicture the compass card as a weight hanging from a pivot inside a bowl of fluid — it's not flat, it's tilted to align with the dipping magnetic field lines. Any acceleration or banked turn briefly swings that tilted weight, and the swing directions are exactly what ANDS and UNOS describe. The fix is procedural, not magical: use the gyroscopic heading indicator to fly precise headings and turns, and cross-check/reset it against the compass about every 15 minutes (§4.3 — gyro drift from precession), doing so only in straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight — the one condition where the compass reads true.

4.5Electrical, Fuel & Engine Basics; Glass Cockpits

A typical training airplane's electrical system runs off an engine-driven alternator charging a battery, feeding radios, lights, and electric instruments through a bus and circuit breakers. The fuel system gravity-feeds or pumps fuel from tanks through a selector valve, strainer, and mixture control to the engine; carbureted engines are also susceptible to carburetor icing even in warm, humid conditions, countered with carb heat. The engine itself (typically a four-stroke, air-cooled piston engine) is monitored via oil pressure/temperature and, on many aircraft, an exhaust/cylinder head temperature gauge.

Ignition: Why Two Magnetos

A magneto generates its own spark using a permanent magnet — completely independent of the aircraft's electrical system. That's the key fact: the engine keeps running even if the entire electrical system fails, and it will keep running as long as the crankshaft turns, which is exactly why a hot propeller is dangerous even with the master off.

Certificated aircraft use a dual ignition system — two magnetos, two sets of wires, and two spark plugs per cylinder. This buys redundancy and better performance: two flame fronts burn the mixture more completely than one.

Why you check L and R during runupThe ignition switch reads OFF – R – L – BOTH – START. Moving from BOTH to a single magneto drops rpm slightly, because one spark plug per cylinder burns the mixture less efficiently — a small drop is normal and is what you want to see. Two failures to worry about: no drop at all can mean a magneto isn't grounding (the engine could fire with the switch off), and a drop exceeding the POH limit, or the engine quitting entirely, means something is wrong — often fouled plugs. Either way, don't fly it.

Detonation & Preignition

Two abnormal-combustion failures that the test likes to contrast. They often occur together and each can cause the other, but the definitions are distinct:

DetonationPreignition
What it isThe mixture explodes instead of burning smoothly — uncontrolled, after normal ignitionThe mixture ignites too early, before the spark plug fires at all
CauseFuel grade too low, very high manifold pressure with low rpm, or high power with an over-lean mixtureA residual hot spot in the cylinder — carbon deposit, cracked plug insulator, or other damage glowing hot enough to light the charge
SymptomsHigh cylinder head temperature, roughness, overheating, power loss — most likely at high power settingsPower loss and high operating temperature
DamageBoth produce excessive pressure and temperature that can destroy pistons, cylinders, or valves — quickly

The prevention for both is the same and is worth stating as one line: use the recommended fuel grade and keep the engine inside its temperature, pressure, and rpm limits. If you suspect detonation in flight, the standard actions are to enrich the mixture, reduce power, and open cowl flaps if fitted — all of which lower cylinder temperature.

Fuel: Grades, Color & Contamination

AVGAS grade is an antiknock rating — how much pressure the fuel takes before detonating. Higher-compression engines need higher grades. Fuels are dyed so you can verify by eye what went in the tank:

FuelColorNote
80 AVGASRedLow-compression engines
100 AVGASGreenHigh-compression engines
100LL AVGASBlue"Low lead" — the common GA fuel today
Jet AColorless or strawTurbine fuel — misfueling a piston engine with Jet A is catastrophic
The substitution rule, and sumpingIf the specified grade isn't available, use the next higher grade — never a lower one. Running too low a grade drives cylinder head and oil temperatures past their limits and invites detonation. Automotive gas is prohibited unless the aircraft holds an STC for it.

For contamination, drain the fuel strainer and each tank sump into a clear container and check for water and dirt. Water is denser, so it collects at the bottom as distinct globules. One subtlety: when draining the strainer, water in the tank may not show up until all the fuel in the lines has passed — so draw enough to be sure you're sampling the tank. Storing the aircraft with partially filled tanks invites condensation, which is why full tanks overnight is standard practice.

Propellers: Fixed-Pitch vs. Constant-Speed

  • Fixed-pitch — the blade angle is built in and cannot change, so a single lever (throttle) controls everything and the tachometer alone tells you engine speed. Simple, light, cheap; efficient at only one combination of airspeed and rpm.
  • Constant-speed (adjustable-pitch) — a governor varies blade angle to hold a selected rpm. You get two controls: the propeller lever sets rpm (read on the tachometer) and the throttle sets manifold pressure (read on the manifold pressure gauge). The blades take a small bite (low pitch, high rpm) for takeoff and a bigger bite (high pitch, low rpm) for efficient cruise — like shifting gears.
  • Power-setting order matters: when increasing power, propeller (rpm) first, then throttle; when reducing, throttle first, then propeller. This keeps you from producing high manifold pressure at low rpm — one of the listed causes of detonation above.

Glass cockpits (e.g. the Garmin G1000 family) replace the six-pack with a Primary Flight Display (PFD) — attitude, airspeed, altitude, heading all in one synthetic display — and a Multi-Function Display (MFD) for engine data, moving maps, and weather. It's important for the test to know what a glass panel does not automatically include: traffic display requires a separate ADS-B In or TCAS-type input — the PFD/MFD itself is not a traffic-avoidance system.

4.6When Systems Fail: In-Flight Emergencies

⚠ Knowledge only — these are not procedures to fly fromEvery airplane has its own emergency checklist in the AFM/POH, and that checklist always wins over anything written here. What follows is the reasoning behind the standard actions, at the level the knowledge test asks about — actual emergency handling is trained with a CFI in the aircraft.

§2.5 covered how to declare an emergency (Mayday, squawk 7700). This section covers the ones the test actually asks you to reason about — and nearly all of them reduce to one hierarchy: aviate, navigate, communicate, in that order.

Engine Failure & the Forced Landing

  • Pitch for best glide first. Best glide speed (L/D-max, §7.5) buys the most distance per foot of altitude — it's a specific airspeed in the POH, and holding it is what gives you options. Trim for it so you don't have to fight the airplane while you work the problem.
  • Pick a landing site early, then plan the approach to it. Prefer into the wind, and favor a suitable field you can reach comfortably over an ideal one you can barely reach.
  • Then troubleshoot — fuel selector, mixture, carb heat, primer, magnetos, fuel pump — from the checklist flow, not from memory improvisation.
  • Engine failure right after takeoff is the special case: at low altitude the instinct to turn back to the runway (the "impossible turn") has killed many pilots, because the turn costs far more altitude than it appears to and ends in a stall/spin from a steep, low, uncoordinated turn. The standard guidance is to land more or less straight ahead, within a shallow arc, unless you are demonstrably high enough — a decision altitude worth briefing before every takeoff, not improvising at 300 ft.

In-Flight Fire

TypeRecognizing itThe logic of the response
Engine fireSmoke/flames from the cowling; sometimes only discoloration or bubbling of the cowl skinStarve it of fuel: mixture to idle cut-off, fuel selector OFF — but leave the ignition ON to burn off fuel still in the lines. Unless the fire is electrical in nature or a crash landing is imminent, the AFH's guidance is to think before reaching for the master.

Emergency Descent

Used when you need to lose altitude fast — an uncontrollable fire being the classic reason. The objective is maximum rate of descent without exceeding any structural limit:

  • Power to idle; gear and flaps extended as the POH allows, to add drag.
  • Establish roughly a 30–45° bank. This does two things: it keeps positive load factor on the airframe, and the turn lets you clear the area below and look for a landing site.
  • Respect the speed limits from §4.2's airspeed arcs — stay below VNE, and below VLE/VFE for whatever you've extended. In turbulence, respect VA as well.

Other System Malfunctions

  • Electrical failure (alternator out): you're on battery, which is finite. Shed non-essential loads immediately and land before it's exhausted. Note which instruments this costs you — from §4.3, the turn coordinator is electric, while the vacuum-driven attitude and heading indicators keep running.
  • Vacuum/pump failure: the mirror image — the AI and HI degrade (often slowly and deceptively), while the electric TC survives. Partial-panel flying means cross-checking the survivors, not trusting a lazily-drifting AI.
  • Pitot-static blockage: already covered in §4.2 — the failure signature tells you which port is blocked, and the alternate static source is the fix.
  • Flap malfunction: an asymmetric (split) flap produces a strong roll toward the retracted side. Slower flap movement, higher approach speed, and being ready with opposite aileron is the theme; the POH gives the airplane-specific numbers.
  • Door opening in flight: startling and loud, but rarely a control problem. Fly the airplane first — this is a classic case where the danger is the distraction, not the failure.
✦ Worked Example — diagnosing a blocked pitot tube

Scenario: In cruise flight, your airspeed indicator begins climbing steadily even though you haven't changed pitch or power, while the altimeter and VSI behave normally. What's happening?

  1. Note which instruments are affected. Only the ASI is misbehaving — altimeter and VSI are fine, so the static port is clear.
  2. Recall the plumbing. ASI needs both ram (pitot) and static pressure; ALT/VSI need only static. A problem isolated to the ASI points to the pitot side.
  3. Predict the failure mode. With the pitot blocked (commonly by icing, drain hole included) but the static port open, the pressure trapped in the pitot line stays constant — so as you climb and ambient static pressure falls, the difference between the two grows and the ASI reads an increasing, false airspeed (and the reverse in a descent). That growing-with-altitude behaviour is exactly why a blocked pitot makes the ASI act like an altimeter.
  4. Conclusion: a blocked pitot tube, most likely icing — apply pitot heat if installed and cross-check groundspeed/attitude rather than trusting the ASI.

B. Vertical Speed Indicator
C. Altimeter
D. Heading Indicator
Show answer

A. The ASI compares ram (pitot) pressure to static pressure; the altimeter and VSI use static pressure alone, and the heading indicator is gyroscopic, not pitot-static.

Q2. If the static port is blocked but the pitot tube remains clear, the altimeter will…
A. Read correctly
B. Freeze at the altitude where the blockage occurred
C. Immediately read zero
D. Spin rapidly and erratically
Show answer

B. With no new static pressure reaching it, the altimeter (and VSI) freeze at the last correct reading — the aircraft's true altitude can change without the instrument showing it.

Q3. Under the ANDS rule, accelerating while flying an easterly heading will make the magnetic compass momentarily indicate a turn toward…
A. East (no change)
B. South
C. North
D. West
Show answer

C. ANDS: Accelerate → North, Decelerate → South. This error is strongest on east/west headings and negligible on north/south headings.

Q4. Per UNOS, when rolling out of a turn onto a northerly heading, a pilot should…
A. Roll out exactly on the desired heading
B. Ignore the compass entirely and use the ASI
C. Roll out well past the desired heading
D. Roll out before reaching the desired heading (undershoot)
Show answer

D. UNOS: Undershoot North — the compass reads the northerly heading before you've actually reached it, so you roll out early to land on the correct heading. (Overshoot South is the mirror case.)

Q5. Which two gyroscopic properties do the attitude indicator, heading indicator, and turn coordinator rely on?
A. Rigidity in space and precession
B. Ram air pressure and static pressure
C. Magnetic dip and variation
D. Bernoulli's principle and Newton's third law
Show answer

A. A spinning gyro resists having its axis tilted (rigidity in space) and reacts to an applied force 90° later in the direction of rotation (precession) — the basis of all three gyroscopic instruments.

Q6. Even in warm, humid conditions, a carbureted engine can experience…
A. Vapor lock only above 10,000 ft
B. Carburetor icing
C. Static port blockage
D. Alternator overcharge
Show answer

B. The rapid pressure drop and fuel vaporization in a carburetor can chill it below freezing even on a mild, humid day — carb heat is the standard countermeasure.

Q7. A Garmin G1000-family glass cockpit will show nearby traffic on the display…
A. Automatically, with no additional equipment
B. Only when the aircraft is above 10,000 ft MSL

C. The PFD/MFD itself doesn't sense other traffic — a traffic display requires ADS-B In (or a TCAS-type system) feeding it separately.

🧩 Challenging Questions

Real test-bank questions logged while practicing — kept here for spaced review, separate from the lecture quiz above. More get added as they come up.

Q# 3275ACS PA.I.G.K1h — pitot-static, vacuum/pressure, and associated flight instruments
(Refer to Figure 5 — Turn Coordinator.) A turn coordinator provides an indication of the
A. movement of the aircraft about the yaw and roll axes.
B. angle of bank up to but not exceeding 30°.
C. attitude of the aircraft with reference to the longitudinal axis.
Show answer

A. This is exactly the §4.3 point above: as the aircraft banks to start a turn, the miniature airplane's initial movement is proportional to roll rate (roll axis); once the bank stabilizes and the aircraft is turning, its deflection is proportional to yaw rate (yaw axis) instead. B is wrong because the TC doesn't cap or measure a specific bank angle — the doghouse marks indicate a rate of turn (3°/sec standard rate), not "30° and no more." C is wrong because that's the job of the attitude indicator (pitch/bank vs. the horizon), and the TC face explicitly says "NO PITCH INFORMATION."

Q# 3262ACS PA.I.G.K1h — pitot-static system (§4.2)
The pitot system provides impact pressure for which instrument?
A. Altimeter.
B. Vertical-speed indicator.
C. Airspeed indicator.
Show answer

C. Direct restatement of the §4.2 figure: the ASI is the only instrument that needs ram (impact/pitot) pressure at all — it compares that to static pressure to derive airspeed. A and B are wrong because the altimeter and VSI are wired to the static port only, which is exactly why a blocked pitot tube (static still open) leaves the ALT and VSI reading normally while only the ASI misbehaves.

Q# 3260ACS PA.I.G.K1h — altitude types (§4.2, "Five Kinds of Altitude")
Under what condition is indicated altitude the same as true altitude?
A. If the altimeter has no mechanical error.
B. When at sea level under standard conditions.
C. When at 18,000 feet MSL with the altimeter set at 29.92.
Show answer

B. This is the §4.2 altitude-types table and figure, pushed to their logical edge case. Indicated altitude only equals true altitude when the local altimeter setting exactly matches standard sea-level pressure (29.92" Hg) and the airplane is actually sitting at sea level — the one point where every reference plane (indicated, pressure, true, and absolute, since the ground is also at sea level there) collapses onto the same value. A is a trap: a mechanically perfect altimeter still won't read true altitude if the actual pressure or temperature is non-standard — "no mechanical error" only rules out instrument error, not the pressure/temperature differences the figure above is about. C is a trap for a different reason: setting 29.92 at 18,000 ft gives you pressure altitude (that's literally the definition, and why 18,000 ft is the base of Class A / the flight-level system) — not true altitude, unless conditions happen to be standard at that level too, which the question doesn't state.

Q# 3261ACS PA.I.G.K1h — Kollsman window / altimeter setting (§4.2, "Altimeter Errors Caused by Pressure")
If it is necessary to set the altimeter from 29.15 to 29.85, what change occurs?
A. 70-foot increase in indicated altitude.
B. 70-foot increase in density altitude.
C. 700-foot increase in indicated altitude.
Show answer

C. This introduces the one conversion rule the H/L pressure-slope diagram above assumes but doesn't spell out: a 1" Hg change in the Kollsman setting ≈ a 1,000 ft change in indicated altitude, in the same direction. Here the setting goes up by 29.85 − 29.15 = 0.70" Hg, so indicated altitude jumps up by 0.70 × 1,000 = 700 ft — the needle moves even though the airplane hasn't. A is a trap that gets the rule's scale wrong by 10× (70 ft instead of 700 ft — easy to do if you treat 0.70" as "0.70 units" instead of running it through the ×1,000 conversion). B is a trap on category: this is purely a Kollsman-window/indicated-altitude change, nothing here says temperature changed, so density altitude isn't what's being asked about at all.

Q# 3282ACS PA.VI.A.K2 — magnetic compass errors (§4.4, ANDS)
In the Northern Hemisphere, a magnetic compass will normally indicate a turn toward the north if
A. a left turn is entered from a west heading.
B. an aircraft is decelerated while on an east or west heading.
C. an aircraft is accelerated while on an east or west heading.
Show answer

C. Straight off the §4.4 mnemonic: ANDS — Accelerate → North, Decelerate → South — and it only shows up on east/west headings, which is exactly what C states. B is wrong because it names the right headings but the wrong half of the mnemonic (decelerate gives a false south indication, not north). A is wrong because it's not an acceleration/deceleration error at all — turning itself is UNOS territory (undershoot north/overshoot south), a different error with a different trigger (banking through a turn, not speeding up or slowing down on a steady heading).

Q# 3653ACS PA.I.G.K1c — powerplant and propeller (§4.6, constant-speed prop)
How is engine operation controlled on an engine equipped with a constant-speed propeller?
A. The throttle controls power output as registered on the manifold pressure gauge and the propeller control regulates engine RPM.
B. The throttle controls power output as registered on the manifold pressure gauge and the propeller control regulates a constant blade angle.
C. The throttle controls engine RPM as registered on the tachometer and the mixture control regulates the power output.
Show answer

A. Direct match to the §4.6 two-control split: throttle → power output, read on the manifold pressure gauge; propeller lever → RPM, held constant by the governor, read on the tachometer. B has the right gauge for the throttle half but gets the propeller half backwards — the whole point of a governor is that it varies blade angle as needed (low pitch for takeoff, high pitch for cruise) specifically so RPM stays constant, not the other way around; a "constant blade angle" describes a fixed-pitch prop, not this one. C swaps which control does which job entirely — the mixture control sets fuel/air ratio for altitude/economy, not power output, and the throttle (not RPM) is what directly sets power.

Q# 3655ACS PA.I.G.K1c — powerplant and propeller (§4.6, avoiding high MP / low RPM)
A precaution for the operation of an engine equipped with a constant-speed propeller is to
A. avoid high RPM settings with high manifold pressure.
B. avoid high manifold pressure settings with low RPM.
C. always use a rich mixture with high RPM settings.
Show answer

B. The combination to avoid is high manifold pressure paired with low RPM: high MP is pushing a lot of fuel/air into the cylinders on every stroke, and low RPM means fewer, slower strokes to spread that pressure over — so each combustion event peaks harder, driving up cylinder pressure and risking detonation/engine damage. A is actually backwards from a hazard standpoint: high RPM with high manifold pressure is the normal takeoff/climb power setting, not something to avoid — more strokes per second means each one is handling a smaller, safer share of the load. C is wrong because mixture leaning is an altitude/economy question, not a blanket "always rich" rule — a lean mixture is often correct at altitude even with high RPM. Same underlying idea as §4.6's fixed-vs-constant-speed material: match manifold pressure and RPM together (the "shifting gears" analogy), never max one while minimizing the other.

✓ What you learned

  • Three "six-pack" instruments are pitot-static (ASI, ALT, VSI); three are gyroscopic (AI, HI, TC).
  • A blocked pitot tube makes the ASI act like an altimeter; a blocked static port freezes the ALT and VSI.
  • Read a three-pointer altimeter short→medium→long and add the numbers; watch hands sitting just before a mark — read them as not yet arrived. Pressure altitude = 29.92" Hg in the Kollsman window; density altitude corrects that for temperature; true = above MSL; absolute = above the ground (AGL).
  • ANDS (accelerate→N, decelerate→S) shows up on E/W headings; UNOS (undershoot N, overshoot S) shows up rolling through N/S headings, worst at high latitude.
  • Gyroscopic instruments rely on rigidity in space and precession — the heading indicator isn't magnetic and drifts, so reset it against the compass about every 15 minutes, only in straight-and-level, unaccelerated flight.
  • The turn coordinator shows roll rate during roll-in, then rate of turn once stabilized (standard rate = 3°/sec, "2 MIN" for a full circle) — it's electric ("D.C. ELEC."), unlike the vacuum-driven AI/HI. Rolling into a turn causes adverse yaw (more induced drag from the down aileron on the rising wing), corrected with rudder in the direction of the turn; the ball checks coordination throughout — step on the ball.
  • Dual magnetos are independent of the electrical system (the engine runs with the master off). A small rpm drop on L/R is normal; no drop or an excessive drop is a squawk. Detonation = explosive burn after ignition (low fuel grade, high MP/low rpm, over-lean); preignition = mixture lit early by a hot spot. Fuel: 80 red · 100 green · 100LL blue · Jet A clear — substitute higher, never lower.
  • A glass cockpit's PFD/MFD does not show traffic without a separate ADS-B In/TCAS input.
  • Emergencies: aviate → navigate → communicate. Engine failure = best glide, pick a site, then troubleshoot; avoid the low-altitude "impossible turn." Engine fire = mixture cut-off + fuel OFF, ignition ON. Electrical fire = master OFF. Emergency descent = idle, drag out, 30–45° bank, respect VNE/VFE/VA.
