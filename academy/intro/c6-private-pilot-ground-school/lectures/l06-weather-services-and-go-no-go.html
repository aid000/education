Week 6 · Lecture 6

Weather Services & Go/No-Go

A METAR is a snapshot; a TAF is a forecast. Reading both fluently is what turns weather theory into an actual decision.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization only. Always pull live products from an official source (e.g. aviationweather.gov) for any real flight decision — never rely on a study-guide example.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Decode a METAR field-by-field.
  • Decode a TAF and explain how it differs from a METAR.
  • Locate area forecasts, winds aloft, and prognostic charts and state what each is for.
  • Build a personal-minimums framework and apply it to a go/no-go decision.

6.1Decoding a METAR

A METAR (Meteorological Aerodrome Report) is a routine, timestamped snapshot of actual conditions at an airport, usually issued hourly. Every field has a fixed position and format:

Sky coverCoverageCounts as a ceiling?
SKC / CLR0 oktas (clear)No
FEW1–2 oktasNo
SCT3–4 oktasNo
BKN5–7 oktasYes — lowest BKN/OVC layer defines the ceiling
OVC8 oktas (overcast)Yes

6.2Decoding a TAF

A TAF (Terminal Aerodrome Forecast) uses the same field vocabulary as a METAR but describes expected conditions over a period, typically 24–30 hours, issued 4 times daily. Change groups mark how conditions are expected to shift:

TAF KXYZ 121720Z 1218/1324 24010KT 6SM BKN020
  FM121900 26015G22KT 4SM SHRA BKN012
  PROB30 1220/1223 3SM TSRA BKN008CB
  BECMG 1300/1302 P6SM SCT250 Base forecast, then FM (rapid change from a set time), PROB30 (30% probability of these conditions in a window), and BECMG (a gradual change over a window). CB in a cloud group flags cumulonimbus (thunderstorm potential).

The key skill the test rewards: matching the valid time window of each change group to your planned departure/arrival time, so you evaluate the conditions that will actually exist when you fly — not just the base forecast at the top.

6.3Other Products: Area Forecasts, Winds Aloft, Prog Charts

  • Winds and Temperatures Aloft Forecast (FB): expected wind direction/speed and temperature at standard altitudes — directly feeds your Week 8 nav-log wind correction.
  • Graphical Forecasts for Aviation / Area Forecast products: broader regional outlook for clouds, visibility, and hazards beyond a single airport.
  • Surface Analysis & Prognostic ("Prog") Charts: depict current and forecast fronts, pressure systems, and precipitation across a wide area — useful for seeing the "big picture" a single METAR/TAF can't show.
  • AIRMETs / SIGMETs: in-flight weather advisories for hazards like moderate icing/turbulence (AIRMET) or severe/extreme conditions and convective activity (SIGMET/Convective SIGMET).

6.4Building Personal Minimums

The legal VFR minimums from Week 3 are a floor, not a target. A personal-minimums framework sets stricter numbers for yourself based on your actual experience, recency, and the specific flight — and you decide them before you're airborne and tempted to rationalize a marginal call.

AnalogyLegal minimums are like a speed limit sign; personal minimums are the speed you actually drive in the rain at night. The law permits more than a careful driver — or a careful pilot with limited experience — should actually use.

A simple framework: for a given flight, write down your minimum ceiling, minimum visibility, and maximum crosswind before checking the weather — then compare the actual METAR/TAF against those numbers, not against the bare legal minimum.

✦ Worked Example — a go/no-go call

Scenario: Your personal minimums are 3,000 ft ceiling / 5 SM visibility / 15 kt crosswind. The TAF for your destination shows: BECMG 1220/1222 6SM BKN015 22018G25KT, valid right when you'd arrive.

  1. Read the ceiling. BKN015 = a broken layer at 1,500 ft — a real ceiling, well below your 3,000 ft personal minimum.
  2. Read the wind. 220° at 18, gusting 25 — check that against the runway orientation for crosswind component; even before doing the math, an 18–25 kt wind is a flag against a 15 kt personal max.
  3. Compare to personal, not legal, minimums. 6SM visibility alone might be legal for the airspace, but your ceiling and crosswind numbers are both already broken.
  4. Decision: NO-GO (or delay/divert) — and this is exactly the kind of marginal-VFR case where personal minimums, set in advance, prevent an in-the-moment rationalization.

💬 Discussion 2 — Week 6

Prompt: You've decoded a METAR/TAF for your project route and the ceiling is marginal VFR. Walk through your go/no-go reasoning — what specific numbers would flip your decision, and why set a personal minimum stricter than the legal minimum?

✓ Saved

Model answer: A strong answer names concrete trigger numbers (e.g., "if the ceiling drops below 2,000 ft or visibility below 4 SM, I no-go") rather than a vague "if it looks bad." Personal minimums exist because legal minimums represent the floor of what's permitted, not what's prudent for a given pilot's actual experience, recency, and the specific aircraft/route — a low-time VFR pilot has far less margin for error in marginal conditions than the bare regulation assumes. Setting the number in advance, before any flight, also removes "get-there-itis" from the decision — you're checking today's METAR against a number you committed to when you weren't under any pressure to complete the flight.

❓ Lecture 6 Quiz

6 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A METAR reports "BKN008." What does this mean?
A. A broken ceiling at 800 ft
B. A few clouds at 8,000 ft, no ceiling
C. Clear skies below 8,000 ft
D. Overcast at 8 statute miles
Show answer

A. Cloud height in a METAR/TAF is given in hundreds of feet, so 008 = 800 ft. BKN (5–7 oktas) counts as a ceiling.

Q2. Which sky-cover abbreviations count as an official "ceiling"?
A. FEW and SCT
B. BKN and OVC
C. SKC and CLR
D. None — ceilings are only reported in TAFs
Show answer

B. The lowest layer reported as BKN (5–7 oktas) or OVC (8 oktas) defines the ceiling; FEW and SCT do not count as a ceiling.

Q3. In a TAF, a "PROB30" group indicates…
A. A guaranteed condition change
B. Wind gusting to 30 knots
C. A 30% probability of the stated conditions occurring in that time window
D. A 30-minute-only forecast
Show answer

C. PROB30 flags a 30% probability of the described conditions (often thunderstorms) occurring within the given time window.

Q4. Which product would you check specifically for expected wind direction/speed at your planned cruise altitude?
A. METAR
B. NOTAM
C. AIRMET only
D. Winds and Temperatures Aloft Forecast
Show answer

D. The Winds and Temperatures Aloft Forecast gives expected wind/temperature at standard altitudes — exactly what feeds a nav-log wind correction.

Q5. An advisory for severe or extreme turbulence, severe icing, or widespread duststorms/sandstorms would be issued as a…
A. SIGMET
B. AIRMET
C. METAR remark
D. TAF amendment only
Show answer

A. SIGMETs cover severe/extreme hazards (severe icing/turbulence, dust/sandstorms, volcanic ash); AIRMETs cover more moderate hazards.

Q6. Personal minimums should generally be…
A. Looser than the legal VFR minimums
B. At least as strict as, and often stricter than, the legal minimums, set in advance
C. Exactly equal to the legal VFR minimums
D. Irrelevant once you're an instrument-rated pilot
Show answer

B. Legal minimums are a floor; personal minimums, set before the pressure of an actual flight decision, should meet or exceed them based on the pilot's real experience and currency.

✓ What you learned

  • A METAR is a timestamped snapshot; a TAF is a 24–30 hour forecast using the same field vocabulary plus change groups (FM, BECMG, PROB30).
  • BKN and OVC are the only sky-cover codes that count as a ceiling.
  • Winds aloft, area forecasts, and prog charts fill in the bigger picture; AIRMETs/SIGMETs flag hazards by severity.
  • Personal minimums — set in advance — should meet or exceed the legal VFR floor, and remove in-the-moment rationalization from a go/no-go call.
