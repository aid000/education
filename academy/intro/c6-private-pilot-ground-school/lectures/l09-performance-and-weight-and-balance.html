Performance & Weight-and-Balance

The same airplane, the same runway, a hot afternoon instead of a cool morning — and suddenly the numbers don't work. This is why performance charts exist.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization only. Real performance and W&B decisions must use your specific aircraft's current POH/AFM data — never a generic study-guide number.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define pressure altitude and density altitude, and explain how each affects performance.
  • Read a takeoff/landing performance chart.
  • Distinguish VX (best angle) from VY (best rate) and know when each is used.
  • Explain short-field, soft-field, crosswind, and go-around technique and why each exists.
  • Calculate a basic weight-and-balance problem using moments.
  • Explain the CG envelope and the effect of an out-of-limits CG.

9.1Density Altitude

Pressure altitude is your altimeter reading with 29.92" Hg set — altitude in the "standard atmosphere." Density altitude then corrects pressure altitude for non-standard temperature: hot air is less dense, so it behaves like a higher altitude even if the airport elevation hasn't changed. Since engines, propellers, and wings all depend on air density, higher density altitude means longer takeoff rolls, reduced climb rate, and higher true airspeed for the same indicated airspeed — a hot, high-elevation airport on a summer afternoon is the classic hazard case.

Density Altitude = Pressure Altitude + [120 × (OAT − ISA Temp)]A commonly used rule-of-thumb formula. Always use your POH's actual density-altitude chart or an E6-B computation for real flight planning.
AnalogyThink of density altitude as the airport "feeling" like a different, higher elevation to the airplane. A sea-level airport at 100°F might present performance closer to a 2,500 ft airport on a standard day — the airplane doesn't know its true elevation, only how many air molecules it has to work with.

9.2Reading a Performance Chart

POH takeoff/landing distance charts typically walk through a sequence: enter with pressure altitude and temperature to find a baseline distance, then apply correction factors/lines for weight, headwind/tailwind component, and sometimes runway surface/slope. The test rewards being able to walk a chart's reference lines in the correct order — skipping a correction (e.g., forgetting the wind adjustment) is the most common error.

FactorEffect on takeoff/landing distance
Higher density altitudeIncreases distance (less thrust, less lift per unit speed)
Higher weightIncreases distance (higher stall/liftoff speed needed)
HeadwindDecreases distance
TailwindIncreases distance sharply — even a small tailwind matters a lot
Uphill slope / soft or high-grass surfaceIncreases distance

Interpolation: When Your Number Isn't on the Chart

Charts list round values — 2,000 ft and 3,000 ft, 10 °C and 20 °C — and your actual conditions almost never land on one. Interpolation is just proportional reasoning between the two bracketing rows.

✦ Worked Example — interpolating a takeoff distance

Scenario: The chart gives a takeoff distance of 1,000 ft at 2,000 ft pressure altitude and 1,150 ft at 3,000 ft. What's the distance at 2,400 ft?

  1. Find the span. 3,000 − 2,000 = 1,000 ft of altitude between the two rows.
  2. Find your fraction into it. 2,400 − 2,000 = 400, so you're 400/1,000 = 0.4 of the way.
  3. Find the output span. 1,150 − 1,000 = 150 ft of takeoff distance.
  4. Apply the fraction. 0.4 × 150 = 60 ft.
  5. Add to the lower value. 1,000 + 60 = 1,060 ft.

Sanity check every interpolation: the answer must fall between the two chart values. If it doesn't, you've inverted a subtraction somewhere. And when in doubt on a real flight, round toward the more conservative (longer distance, worse climb) figure.

Two Runway Hazards the Charts Assume Away

  • Runway surface and gradient. Chart numbers assume a dry, level, hard surface. Soft or wet grass, snow, or mud increases rolling resistance and lengthens the takeoff roll substantially. An upslope gradient lengthens takeoff and shortens landing; a downslope does the reverse. Neither is usually in the chart — you add margin yourself.
  • Hydroplaning. On standing water deeper than the tire tread, the tire can ride up on a film of water like a water ski. Braking and directional control become virtually impossible, and a locked wheel may simply skate. The classic estimate for dynamic hydroplaning speed is about 8.6 × √(tire pressure in psi) — so a tire at 36 psi can hydroplane near 52 knots. The practical defenses are landing on the numbers at minimum speed, avoiding hard braking until the tires bite, and treating a wet or contaminated runway as needing far more distance than the chart says.

9.3Climb Performance: VX vs. VY

Two different "best climb" speeds exist because there are two different things you might want to maximize, and they are not the same thing:

  • VX — best angle of climb. The most altitude gained per unit of horizontal distance — the steepest possible path over the ground. This is the obstacle-clearance speed. It's the slower of the two.
  • VY — best rate of climb. The most altitude gained per unit of time — gets you to cruise altitude soonest. It's the faster of the two, and it's the normal climb speed when nothing is in the way.

The physical driver is different for each: maximum angle occurs where the aircraft has the greatest excess thrust, while maximum rate occurs where it has the greatest excess power. That's why they land on different airspeeds in the same airplane.

VX — best angleVY — best rate
MaximizesAltitude per unit of distanceAltitude per unit of time
Driven byGreatest excess thrustGreatest excess power
Relative speedSlowerFaster
Use it forClearing an obstacle on departure — short-field takeoffNormal climb to cruise altitude
With altitudeIncreasesDecreases
The trade you're makingVX buys you a steeper path at the cost of climbing more slowly in time, less airflow over the engine (a cooling concern in a prolonged climb), and a higher deck angle that hurts forward visibility. Standard practice is to use VX only until the obstacle is cleared, then lower the nose and accelerate to VY for the rest of the climb.

9.4Takeoff & Landing Techniques

⚠ Knowledge onlyThese are the concepts behind each technique — what the written test asks. The actual flying is trained with a CFI, and the airspeeds always come from your POH.

The performance charts in §9.2 assume a specific technique was flown. Use the wrong one and the chart numbers stop meaning anything. Four named variants cover almost every question:

SituationThe problemTechnique logic
Short field
takeoff		Runway length is the limit; an obstacle sits off the endUse all available runway, flaps per POH, brakes held while power comes up. Rotate at the POH speed and climb at VX (§9.3) until the obstacle is cleared, then accelerate to VY
Soft field
takeoff		Grass, sand, mud or snow adds drag and may prevent reaching takeoff speed at allGet weight off the wheels onto the wings as early as possible: flaps per POH before rolling, keep moving (stopping may bog you down), hold a nose-high attitude so the airplane flies itself off early — then stay in ground effect to accelerate before climbing out
Short field
landing		Must stop in minimum distanceStabilized approach at the POH short-field speed (slower, full flaps), touch down at minimum speed on the intended point, then maximum braking
Soft field
landing		Must protect the nosewheel and avoid bogging downTouch down as slowly as possible and hold the nosewheel off with back pressure as long as elevator authority lasts; minimal braking, and keep rolling once down
The one-line contrast that resolves most questionsShort field is about distance; soft field is about weight on the wheels. Short-field technique wants firm, precise, minimum-distance performance. Soft-field technique wants the wheels unloaded as early as possible on takeoff and as long as possible on landing — which is why soft-field landings use minimal braking, the opposite of short field.

Crosswind Technique: Crab vs. Wing-Low

A crosswind wants to drift you off the extended centerline. Two methods correct for it, and most pilots use both in sequence:

  • Crab method — turn into the wind so the airplane's ground track follows the centerline while the nose points slightly upwind. Comfortable and coordinated, but the airplane is not aligned with the runway, so the crab must be removed with rudder just before touchdown to avoid a side load on the gear.
  • Wing-low (sideslip) method — lower the upwind wing to stop the drift and use opposite rudder to keep the nose straight down the runway. The airplane is aligned with the runway the whole way down, so it can be flown right to touchdown — landing on the upwind main wheel first.
  • In practice, a common approach is to crab on final for comfort, then transition to wing-low before the round out.
"Maximum demonstrated crosswind" is not a limitThe number in the POH is the strongest crosswind a test pilot demonstrated during certification — it's a data point, not a regulatory limitation. It says nothing about your current proficiency, which is the number that actually matters. If you run out of rudder trying to hold alignment, the crosswind exceeds what you and that airplane can do together — go somewhere else.

Wind Correction on the Ground: Taxiing

Before any of the above, the airplane has to survive the taxi to the runway — and wind can flip a light airplane on the ground the same way it can drift one in the air, just through a different mechanism: getting under a wing or the tail instead of pushing sideways off a centerline. The correction is aileron and elevator position, held throughout the taxi, and it depends on where the wind is coming from relative to your direction of travel.

Quartering headwind: aileron UP into the wind  ·  Quartering tailwind: aileron DOWN on the side the wind is from, elevator DOWN (dive away from it)"Climb into a headwind, dive away from a tailwind" — but the "climb" (elevator up) half only applies to tailwheel airplanes. See the callout below.

The logic behind each: a quartering headwind hits the upwind wing from the front and below, trying to lift it — raising that wing's aileron spoils its lift and holds it down. A quartering tailwind hits from behind and can get under the tail or an upwind wing, threatening to flip the airplane forward — lowering the aileron on the upwind side and pushing the elevator down counters both, since the goal has flipped from "hold this wing down against lift from ahead" to "don't let the tail come up from a push behind it."

The elevator half of the mnemonic depends on the gearThe full "climb into a headwind" (elevator up) only applies to tailwheel airplanes — pulling back keeps weight on the tailwheel for directional control. In a tricycle-gear airplane (the C-172/C-152 you're actually training in), the elevator stays neutral for a quartering headwind — there's no tailwheel to weight, and the nosewheel doesn't benefit the same way. The tailwind half is identical for both gear types: elevator down, upwind aileron down — that one's about keeping wind from getting under the tail or an upwind wing and flipping the airplane forward, which threatens tricycle- and tailwheel-gear airplanes alike.
Don't memorize the diagram — derive itTest questions describe wind as "quartering from the left rear" or similar and ask for the correct control position. Skip straight to which end is the wind pushing on (nose or tail) and which wing is it trying to lift (always the upwind one) — then hold the upwind aileron the direction that resists that wing being lifted, and set the elevator per the box above (checking gear type first). Rote-memorizing eight compass positions is more to get wrong than reasoning it through each time.

The Stabilized Approach & the Go-Around

A stabilized approach means being configured, on speed, on the intended glidepath, and with power set — all before a defined point on final, so the last part of the approach requires only small corrections. Most landing accidents trace back to an unstable approach that the pilot chose to continue.

The go-around is the answer whenever that isn't true, and it's a normal maneuver, not an admission of failure. The order matters and is worth memorizing as three words — power, attitude, configuration:

  1. Power — full power first, to stop the descent and get energy back.
  2. Attitude — pitch to arrest the sink and establish a climb; expect significant right rudder and nose-up trim forces (the §7.4 left-turning tendencies at high power and low speed).
  3. Configuration — retract flaps incrementally per the POH, not all at once; dumping full flaps instantly can sink the airplane into the ground.
Decide early, and decide onceThe most dangerous go-around is the late one, started low and slow after a bounce or a float. A go-around initiated from a stabilized approach that simply looks wrong is routine; the same maneuver begun after touchdown in a bounce is a different, far more demanding situation. Briefing a go-around point before every approach turns it from a judgment call into a pre-made decision.

9.5Weight & Balance: Moments and the CG

Every item in the airplane — empty airframe, pilot, passengers, baggage, fuel — has a weight and an arm (its distance from a reference datum). Multiplying weight × arm gives a moment; summing all moments and dividing by total weight gives the center of gravity (CG) — the single point where the airplane's weight effectively acts.

Moment = Weight × Arm     CG = ΣMoments ÷ ΣWeightCompute this for every loaded item, then check the result against the CG envelope for that total weight.

9.6Effects of Weight, CG & Configuration

Tying this lecture back to Week 7: higher weight directly raises stall speed (more lift needed at the same AoA requires more speed) and increases takeoff/landing distance. A CG shifted aft reduces the wing's natural nose-down pitching stability and can move the aircraft's stall/spin characteristics from mild to dangerous — which is exactly why the CG envelope, not just a max weight number, is a hard limit.

✦ Worked Example — a basic weight-and-balance calculation

Scenario: Empty weight 1,500 lb at arm 40". Pilot+front passenger 340 lb at arm 37". Rear passenger 170 lb at arm 73". Fuel 30 gal (180 lb) at arm 48". Find the loaded weight and CG.

  1. Compute each moment (weight × arm):
    Empty: 1,500 × 40 = 60,000
    Front seats: 340 × 37 = 12,580
    Rear seat: 170 × 73 = 12,410
    Fuel: 180 × 48 = 8,640
  2. Sum the weights: 1,500 + 340 + 170 + 180 = 2,190 lb
  3. Sum the moments: 60,000 + 12,580 + 12,410 + 8,640 = 93,630
  4. Compute CG: 93,630 ÷ 2,190 ≈ 42.75" aft of datum
  5. Check limits. Plot 2,190 lb and 42.75" on the aircraft's actual CG envelope graph (from its POH) — only then can you confirm it's actually within limits. Never skip this last step; the arithmetic alone doesn't tell you if you're legal.
✦ Worked Example — shifting weight to move the CG

Scenario: The airplane above is loaded to 2,190 lb with a CG at 42.75", but the aft limit is 42.5" — it's 0.25" out of limits. You move a 50 lb bag from the rear baggage area (arm 95") to the front seats (arm 37"). Where does the CG end up?

  1. Find the distance the weight moved. 95 − 37 = 58" forward.
  2. Apply the weight-shift formula. The CG moves in proportion to how much weight moved, how far, relative to total weight:
CG change = (Weight shifted × Distance moved) ÷ Total weightThe result is how far the CG travels — in the same direction the weight went.
  1. Compute. (50 × 58) ÷ 2,190 = 2,900 ÷ 2,190 ≈ 1.32".
  2. Apply the direction. The weight moved forward, so the CG moves forward: 42.75 − 1.32 = 41.43".
  3. Recheck. 41.43" is now inside the aft limit of 42.5". Note the total weight did not change — shifting moves the CG without changing gross weight, which is what makes it the fastest fix for an out-of-limits CG.

Two things the test checks: the CG always moves in the same direction as the weight you moved, and adding or removing weight changes both the CG and the total weight — so those problems need the full moment recomputation above, not this shortcut.

Q1. Density altitude is best described as…
A. Pressure altitude corrected for non-standard temperature
B. The altitude shown on the altimeter with the current local setting
C. The airport's published field elevation
D. True altitude above sea level
Show answer

A. Density altitude takes pressure altitude (29.92" Hg reference) and corrects it for actual temperature — hot air behaves like a higher, "thinner" altitude.

Q2. On a hot day at a high-elevation airport, compared to a standard-temperature day at the same field, takeoff performance will be…
A. Better — hot air is thinner and creates less drag
B. Worse — higher density altitude means longer takeoff roll and reduced climb
C. Unaffected — temperature doesn't matter for performance
D. Better only for jet aircraft
Show answer

B. Higher density altitude reduces air density, which reduces engine power, propeller efficiency, and lift — all worsening takeoff and climb performance.

Q3. On a takeoff-distance chart, a tailwind component compared to the same-speed headwind will…
A. Have no meaningful effect
B. Decrease the required distance
C. Increase the required distance significantly more than the headwind decreases it
D. Only matter for landing, not takeoff
Show answer

C. Tailwind components disproportionately increase takeoff/landing distance — POH charts typically warn that even a small tailwind has an outsized penalty.

Q4. A "moment," in weight-and-balance terms, is calculated as…
A. Weight divided by arm
B. Weight minus arm
C. Arm divided by total weight
D. Weight multiplied by arm
Show answer

D. Moment = weight × arm. Summing moments and dividing by total weight gives the center of gravity.

Q5. An aircraft loaded with its CG too far aft of the envelope's aft limit will tend to be…
A. Less pitch-stable, with potentially dangerous stall/spin characteristics
B. Unaffected in pitch stability
C. Extra nose-heavy and hard to flare
D. Automatically safer at high speed
Show answer

A. An aft CG reduces the wing's natural nose-down stability and can turn a normally mild stall into a dangerous one, potentially complicating recovery.

Q6. A CG envelope graph typically narrows as weight approaches…
A. Empty weight
B. Maximum gross weight
C. It never narrows — it's a constant width
D. Zero fuel weight only
Show answer

B. The safe CG range typically narrows near max gross weight, leaving less margin for loading error exactly when the airplane is heaviest.

Q7. After computing a loaded CG arithmetically, the required final step is to…
A. Round to the nearest whole number and move on
B. Nothing further is needed once the math is done
C. Plot the weight and CG on the aircraft's actual CG envelope to confirm it's within limits
D. Compare it only to the empty-weight CG
Show answer

C. The arithmetic alone doesn't confirm legality — the resulting weight/CG point must fall inside the aircraft's actual envelope graph from its POH.

🧩 Challenging Questions

Real test-bank questions logged while practicing — kept here for spaced review, separate from the lecture quiz above. More get added as they come up.

Q# 3302ACS PA.II.D.K4 — visual indicators for wind (§9.4, taxi wind correction)
When taxiing with strong quartering tailwinds, which aileron positions should be used?
A. Aileron down on the downwind side.
B. Ailerons neutral.
C. Aileron down on the side from which the wind is blowing.
Show answer

C. Straight off the §9.4 taxi-wind box: for a quartering tailwind, lower the aileron on the upwind side (the side the wind is coming from, not the downwind side A describes) — this holds that wing down and keeps the wind from getting under it or the tail, which is what threatens to flip a light airplane forward in a strong tailwind. B is wrong because neutral ailerons do nothing to counter that lifting force; this is one of the two taxi scenarios (along with a quartering headwind) where aileron position actually matters.

Q# 3305ACS PA.II.D.K4 — visual indicators for wind (§9.4, tricycle-gear quartering headwind)
(Refer to Figure 9, area A.) How should the flight controls be held while taxiing a tricycle-gear equipped airplane into a left quartering headwind?
A. Left aileron up, elevator neutral.
B. Left aileron down, elevator neutral.
C. Left aileron up, elevator down.
Show answer

A. This is exactly the gear-type distinction called out in the §9.4 warning box: for a quartering headwind, the upwind aileron goes up (left aileron up, since the wind is from the left) to hold that wing down against the wind trying to lift it — but in a tricycle-gear airplane the elevator stays neutral, unlike the tailwheel case (Q3306 below) where it goes up. There's no tailwheel to weight down, so the "climb into it" half of the mnemonic doesn't apply. B has the aileron backwards (down instead of up — that would help lift the wing, the opposite of what's needed). C borrows the tailwheel airplane's elevator position, which is wrong for this gear type.

Q# 3306ACS PA.II.D.K4 — visual indicators for wind (§9.4, tailwheel quartering headwind)
(Refer to Figure 9, area B.) How should the flight controls be held while taxiing a tailwheel airplane into a right quartering headwind?
A. Right aileron up, elevator up.
B. Right aileron down, elevator neutral.
C. Right aileron up, elevator down.
Show answer

A. The full "climb into a headwind" mnemonic, unabbreviated: in a tailwheel airplane, the upwind aileron goes up (right aileron, wind from the right) to hold that wing down, and the elevator goes up (back pressure) to keep weight on the tailwheel for directional control — the piece that tricycle-gear airplanes skip (compare Q3305, same wind geometry, elevator neutral instead). B gives the tricycle-gear elevator answer to a tailwheel question, and gets the aileron backwards besides. C mixes the correct aileron with the tailwind airplane's elevator position instead of the headwind one.

Q# 3308ACS PA.II.D.K4 — visual indicators for wind (§9.4, tricycle-gear quartering tailwind)
(Refer to Figure 9, area C.) How should the flight controls be held while taxiing a tricycle-gear equipped airplane with a left quartering tailwind?
A. Left aileron up, elevator neutral.
B. Left aileron down, elevator down.
C. Left aileron up, elevator down.
Show answer

B. This is the other half of the §9.4 warning box — the tailwind case, where gear type stops mattering: upwind aileron down (left, since the wind is from the left) and elevator down, identical for tricycle and tailwheel alike, because the danger (wind getting under the tail or an upwind wing and flipping the airplane forward) doesn't care what's under the nose. A gives the aileron position for a headwind instead (compare Q3305 — same "left aileron up" answer, but that was for area A, a headwind scenario) — wrong wind case entirely. C keeps the tailwind's correct elevator but pairs it with the headwind's aileron, mixing the two rules the warning box exists to keep separate.

✓ What you learned

  • Density altitude = pressure altitude corrected for temperature; higher DA worsens every performance number.
  • Performance charts must be walked in sequence — pressure altitude/temp, then weight, then wind, then surface/slope corrections.
  • Short field = distance (all runway, VX, max braking); soft field = weight off the wheels (flaps early, keep moving, nose high, minimal braking). Crosswind: crab then wing-low. Go-around = power, attitude, configuration. Taxi wind correction: upwind aileron up for a headwind / down for a tailwind; elevator down for a tailwind on any gear type, but up for a headwind only on tailwheel airplanes (neutral on tricycle gear).
  • VX = best angle (slower, steepest path, clears obstacles); VY = best rate (faster, altitude soonest). With altitude VX rises and VY falls until they meet at the absolute ceiling.
  • Moment = weight × arm; CG = total moment ÷ total weight — then check the result against the actual CG envelope.
  • An aft CG reduces pitch stability and can worsen stall/spin behavior; the envelope narrows near max gross weight.
  • Interpolate between bracketing chart rows; the answer must land between them. Weight shift: CG change = (weight × distance) ÷ total weight, and the CG moves the same direction the weight did. Charts assume dry/level/hard — add margin for slope, soft surfaces, and hydroplaning (≈8.6 × √tire psi).
