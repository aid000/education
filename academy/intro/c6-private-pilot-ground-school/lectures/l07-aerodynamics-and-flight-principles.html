Week 7 · Lecture 7

Aerodynamics & Flight Principles

Everything from Weeks 1–6 exists to manage four forces and one angle — this is the physics underneath the whole test.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization for the FAA knowledge test only. Stall/spin recognition and recovery must be trained hands-on with a CFI, never learned solely from a study guide.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the four forces acting on an aircraft in flight and their relationships in steady, unaccelerated flight.
  • Explain how a wing generates lift, and what angle of attack actually is.
  • Explain what a stall is, why critical AoA is fixed, and why airspeed at the stall varies.
  • Explain load factor's effect on stall speed and identify maneuvering speed (Va).
  • Distinguish parasite drag from induced drag and locate L/D-max.
  • Explain ground effect and how flaps change lift and drag.

7.1The Four Forces

Every aircraft in flight is a balance of exactly four forces: lift (up, from the wings), weight (down, from gravity), thrust (forward, from the engine/propeller), and drag (backward, resisting motion through the air). In steady, unaccelerated, straight-and-level flight, opposing pairs are equal: lift = weight, thrust = drag. Change that balance and the airplane accelerates, climbs, descends, or turns.

7.2How a Wing Makes Lift

A wing's cross-section (its airfoil) is shaped, and tilted relative to the oncoming air, so that air flowing over the top and bottom surfaces produces a net upward pressure difference. Two complementary explanations are both part of the real physics: Bernoulli's principle (air accelerated over the curved upper surface has lower pressure than the slower air beneath) and Newton's third law (the wing deflects air downward, and the reaction force pushes the wing up). Neither alone is the complete story — real lift is the net effect of the pressure field the wing creates all around itself.

7.3Angle of Attack & the Stall

The angle of attack (AoA) is the angle between the wing's chord line and the oncoming relative wind — not the same as pitch attitude relative to the horizon. As AoA increases, lift increases too — up to a point. Beyond the critical angle of attack, airflow separates from the upper surface faster than it can smoothly follow the wing's curve, lift collapses, and the wing stalls.

Note · a stall is an angle problem, not a speed problemThe single most-tested conceptual point in this lecture: pilots don't stall because they're "too slow" — they stall because they exceed the critical AoA. Low speed is simply the usual way pilots end up needing a high AoA to keep lift equal to weight, but a steep, abrupt pull-up at high speed can stall a wing just as easily.

7.4Load Factor & Maneuvering Speed

Load factor is the ratio of lift to weight, expressed in "G's." In a level, coordinated turn, the wings must generate more lift than weight alone to also supply the centripetal force that curves the flight path — so load factor increases with bank angle, and because more lift at the same airspeed requires a higher AoA, stall speed increases in a turn (a 60° bank roughly doubles load factor to 2G and raises stall speed by about 41%).

Maneuvering speed (Va) is the maximum speed at which full, abrupt control deflection won't overstress the airframe — at or below Va, the wing will stall (shedding excess load) before structural limits are exceeded; above Va, the structure could be damaged before the wing stalls. Va decreases with decreasing weight, which is why a lighter-than-max-gross airplane has a lower Va than published for max gross weight.

✦ Worked Example — total load in pounds from a load-factor chart

Scenario: A 3,300 lb airplane enters a level, coordinated 30° bank. What total load are the wings supporting?

  1. Read (or compute) the load factor. The exact formula gives n = 1/cos(30°) ≈ 1.1547 G. But the FAA written test hands you an actual load-factor chart to read by eye, not a formula — reading that chart at 30° gives approximately 1.2 G's, the rounded value the test expects.
  2. Apply the load formula. Total Load (lbs) = Weight (lbs) × Load Factor (G's).
  3. Compute. 3,300 lbs × 1.2 = 3,960 lbs — the wings are supporting almost 4,000 lbs, about 660 lbs more than the airplane actually weighs, from a 30° bank alone.

This is a different question style from the stall-speed worked example above it — same load factor, different final quantity (structural load in pounds, not stall speed in knots). Expect both styles on the real test, and expect chart-read values (≈1.2, 1.4, 2.0 G's at 30°/45°/60°) rather than the more precise trig values shown in the figure above (1.15, 1.41, 2.00).

7.5Drag: Parasite vs. Induced

This is why induced drag is highest exactly where stalls are most likely — slow flight, climbs, and steep turns all combine high AoA with high induced drag, demanding more thrust/power precisely when the airplane has the least excess performance to give.

The Region of Reversed Command

The U-shaped drag curve has a consequence that feels backwards the first time you meet it. On the right side of the curve — normal cruise, the region of normal command — going slower requires less power, exactly as intuition says. But on the left side, below L/D-max, induced drag rises so steeply as you slow down that going slower requires MORE power to hold altitude. That's the region of reversed command — "reversed" because the relationship between speed and required power has flipped.

  • Where you meet it: slow flight, short-final approach, and the climb-out after takeoff — all flown on the back side of the curve, by design.
  • Why it bites: the airplane becomes speed-unstable here. Let the nose rise and speed decay, and drag increases, which decays speed further — the situation worsens on its own instead of self-correcting. Pitching up to arrest a sink rate makes it worse, not better.
  • The fix is counterintuitive but simple: in the region of reversed command, power controls altitude and pitch controls airspeed. Sinking on final? Add power. Trying to pitch your way out of it is what turns a low approach into a stall — and it's the mechanism behind many base-to-final and go-around accidents.

7.6Flaps & Ground Effect

Flaps increase wing camber and (on some designs) area, which increases both lift and drag — more lift allows a slower approach speed, while the added drag helps maintain a steeper descent without excess airspeed.

Ground effect occurs within roughly one wingspan of the surface, where the ground disrupts wingtip vortex formation, reducing induced drag and slightly increasing lift for a given AoA. Practically: an airplane can float and delay touchdown when arriving too fast in ground effect, and can seem to fly at an airspeed on takeoff that won't sustain a climb once it leaves ground effect a few feet higher.

✦ Worked Example — why a steep turn raises stall speed

Scenario: An airplane stalls at 50 KIAS in level, 1G flight. What happens to its stall speed in a level 60° banked turn?

  1. Identify the load factor. A level, coordinated 60° bank requires roughly 2G to maintain altitude.
  2. Connect load factor to lift needed. The wings must produce about twice the lift they would in level flight — at the same airspeed, that requires a higher AoA.
  3. Apply the stall-speed/load-factor relationship. Stall speed scales with the square root of load factor: new stall speed ≈ 50 × √2 ≈ 50 × 1.41 ≈ 71 KIAS.
  4. Conclusion: the same airplane that's perfectly safe at 60 KIAS in level flight could stall at that same airspeed in a steep turn — the classic "base-to-final stall" accident scenario.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

8 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In steady, unaccelerated, straight-and-level flight, which relationship is true?
A. Lift = Weight and Thrust = Drag
B. Lift > Weight and Thrust > Drag
C. Lift = Drag and Thrust = Weight
D. All four forces are unrelated
Show answer

A. In steady, unaccelerated level flight the opposing force pairs balance: lift equals weight, thrust equals drag.

Q2. A stall occurs when…
A. Airspeed drops below a fixed number, regardless of AoA
B. The critical angle of attack is exceeded
C. The engine loses power
D. Flaps are retracted
Show answer

B. A stall is fundamentally an angle-of-attack event, not a fixed-airspeed event — you can stall at any airspeed if AoA is high enough.

Q3. What happens to the critical angle of attack as an aircraft's weight increases?
A. It increases proportionally
B. It decreases proportionally
C. It stays essentially the same — the airspeed needed to reach it changes instead
D. It becomes undefined
Show answer

C. Critical AoA is essentially fixed for a given wing/configuration; what changes with weight, load factor, or configuration is the airspeed at which that AoA is reached.

Q4. A 60° banked, level turn increases load factor to roughly 2G. The new stall speed is approximately…
A. The same as 1G stall speed
B. About 1.2x the 1G stall speed
C. About 2x the 1G stall speed
D. About 1.41x the 1G stall speed
Show answer

D. Stall speed scales with the square root of load factor: √2 ≈ 1.41, so stall speed rises about 41% in a 60° bank.

Q5. Which type of drag is the byproduct of lift and is worst at low airspeed / high angle of attack?
A. Induced drag
B. Parasite drag
C. Form drag
D. Interference drag
Show answer

A. Induced drag comes from wingtip vortices generated as a byproduct of producing lift, and is greatest at low speed/high AoA — the opposite of parasite drag's high-speed dominance.

Q6. Maneuvering speed (Va) is best described as…
A. The fastest speed the aircraft can legally fly
B. The maximum speed at which full, abrupt control deflection will stall the wing before the airframe is overstressed
C. The best glide speed
D. A fixed number that never changes with weight
Show answer

B. At or below Va, the wing stalls first and sheds the load before the structure is damaged; above Va, a full abrupt control input can overstress the airframe before the wing stalls. Va decreases as weight decreases.

Q7. Extending flaps generally does what to lift and drag?
A. Increases lift, decreases drag
B. Decreases both lift and drag
C. Increases both lift and drag
D. Has no effect on either
Show answer

C. Flaps increase camber (and sometimes area), increasing both lift (allowing a slower approach) and drag (allowing a steeper descent without excess speed).

Q8. Ground effect, occurring within about one wingspan of the surface, primarily…
A. Increases induced drag sharply
B. Only occurs at night
C. Has no aerodynamic effect, only a psychological one
D. Reduces induced drag and slightly increases lift for a given AoA
Show answer

D. The ground disrupts wingtip vortex formation, reducing induced drag and slightly increasing lift — which is why airplanes can float during a fast landing or seem to fly briefly at an airspeed that won't sustain a climb once out of ground effect.

🧩 Challenging Questions

Real test-bank questions logged while practicing — kept here for spaced review, separate from the lecture quiz above. More get added as they come up.

Q# 3263ACS PA.VII.C.K1 — aerodynamics associated with stalls
As altitude increases, the indicated airspeed at which a given airplane stalls in a particular configuration will
A. decrease as the true airspeed decreases.
B. decrease as the true airspeed increases.
C. remain the same regardless of altitude.
Show answer

C. In the same configuration, weight, and load factor, an airplane always stalls at the same indicated airspeed regardless of altitude — this is the §7.3 point that critical AoA is fixed, applied one level deeper. A and B are wrong the other way around: it's the true airspeed at the stall that increases with altitude (thinner air needs a higher true speed to generate the same dynamic pressure/indicated airspeed the wing needs to reach critical AoA), not indicated airspeed.

Q# 3316ACS PA.I.F.K3 — aerodynamics
During an approach to a stall, an increased load factor will cause the airplane to
A. stall at a higher airspeed.
B. have a tendency to spin.
C. be more difficult to control.
Show answer

A. Same idea as Q3263 and §7.4's load-factor discussion, from the opposite direction: the wing always stalls at the same critical AoA, but a higher load factor means that AoA is reached at a higher airspeed (VS(new) = VS × √n) — this is exactly the accelerated-stall / steep-turn scenario in the §7.4–7.5 worked example. B and C are wrong because load factor alone doesn't change spin tendency or controllability — those depend on other factors (yaw/roll coupling, control authority), not just how much load the wings are carrying.

Q# 3207ACS PA.I.F.K3 — aerodynamics (torque/left-turning tendencies — not yet covered in §7.1–7.6)
In what flight condition is torque effect the greatest in a single-engine airplane?
A. Low airspeed, high power, high angle of attack.
B. Low airspeed, low power, low angle of attack.
C. High airspeed, high power, high angle of attack.
Show answer

A. "Torque effect" here really means the combined left-turning tendencies: torque reaction, P-factor (asymmetric propeller disc loading), spiraling slipstream, and gyroscopic precession. All four are strongest together at high power (more torque reaction, more slipstream) and high AoA at low airspeed (P-factor comes from the descending propeller blade taking a bigger "bite" of air than the ascending blade when the prop disc is tilted relative to the relative wind — which is exactly what a high AoA does). That combination — low airspeed, high power, high AoA — is the takeoff/initial-climb configuration, which is why right rudder demand peaks right after liftoff, not in cruise. B is wrong because low power and low AoA minimize all four components; C is wrong because high airspeed reduces the AoA needed and aligns the prop disc more with the relative wind, weakening P-factor even though power is still high.

Q# 3208ACS PA.I.F.K3 — aerodynamics (P-factor mechanism — same gap as Q3207)
The left turning tendency of an airplane caused by P-factor is the result of the
A. clockwise rotation of the engine and the propeller turning the airplane counter-clockwise.
B. propeller blade descending on the right producing more thrust than the ascending blade on the left.
C. gyroscopic forces applied to the rotating propeller blades acting 90° in advance of the point the force was applied.
Show answer

B. This is the mechanism behind Q3207's answer, spelled out directly: at high AoA and high power, the propeller disc is tilted relative to the oncoming relative wind, so the descending blade (on the right, for a standard clockwise-rotating U.S. engine viewed from the cockpit) takes a bigger "bite" of air and produces more thrust than the ascending blade on the left — the asymmetric thrust yaws the nose left. A describes torque reaction (Newton's third law reaction to spinning the prop, a different one of the four left-turning tendencies) — real, but not what causes P-factor specifically. C describes gyroscopic precession (a third, separate left-turning tendency that shows up when the prop disc is pitched or yawed, e.g. raising the tail in a taildragger) — also real, also not P-factor.

Q# 3825ACS PA.I.F.K3 — aerodynamics (wingtip vortices — §7.2 downwash / §7.5 induced drag)
The greatest vortex strength occurs when the generating aircraft is
A. light, dirty, and fast.
B. heavy, dirty, and fast.
C. heavy, clean, and slow.
Show answer

C. Wingtip vortices are a byproduct of lift (§7.2's downwash, §7.5's induced drag) — they only exist because the wing is producing lift at all, and they get stronger with a higher angle of attack at the wingtips. A heavy airplane needs more lift (and thus more AoA) to stay level; a slow airplane needs even more AoA at that weight; and "clean" (flaps up) means no extra lift-producing surface area to spread the load across, concentrating it — and the AoA — right at the tips. So heavy + slow + clean stacks all three toward maximum AoA and maximum vortex strength; this is exactly why wake-turbulence avoidance briefings emphasize a heavy jet that's slow and clean (e.g., climbing out or on approach) as the worst case. A and B are wrong for the same reason in reverse: higher speed lowers the AoA needed for the same lift, which weakens the vortices regardless of weight or flap setting.

✓ What you learned

  • Four forces — lift, weight, thrust, drag — balance in pairs during steady, unaccelerated flight.
  • A wing makes lift via Bernoulli (pressure) and Newton (downwash) together; lift depends on AoA, airspeed, wing shape/area, and air density.
  • Critical AoA is fixed; the airspeed at which you reach it varies with weight, load factor, and configuration — you can stall at any airspeed.
  • Load factor rises with bank angle, raising stall speed; Va is the speed at or below which the wing stalls before the structure is overstressed.
  • Parasite drag rises with speed²; induced drag falls with speed; their sum's minimum is best-glide (L/D-max) speed.
  • Below L/D-max you are in the region of reversed command — slower now needs more power, and the airplane is speed-unstable. There, power controls altitude, pitch controls airspeed.
  • Flaps add lift and drag together; ground effect reduces induced drag near the surface.
