10.1Aeromedical Factors

  • Hypoxia — insufficient oxygen to the brain, from altitude. Early signs (euphoria, poor judgment, tingling) are easy to miss because judgment itself is what's impaired. Above roughly 12,500 ft MSL cabin altitude for extended periods, supplemental oxygen becomes required for the required crew under 14 CFR §91.211; the risk begins well before that threshold for some individuals.
  • Hyperventilation — over-breathing, often from anxiety, that flushes out too much CO₂, causing dizziness, tingling, and symptoms that can be mistaken for hypoxia. Treatment: consciously slow the breathing rate.
  • Spatial disorientation — the inner ear and body's sense of "up" can be flat-out wrong without visual reference (e.g., in clouds), producing illusions like the "graveyard spiral" (a gentle turn that feels like level flight). The only reliable fix is to trust the flight instruments over bodily sensation.
  • Fatigue — degrades judgment, reaction time, and attention similarly to alcohol; acute (one bad night) and chronic (accumulated sleep debt) fatigue both count.
  • Alcohol & drugs — 14 CFR §91.17 sets the "8 hours from bottle to throttle" rule, a blood alcohol limit of 0.04, and prohibits flying under the influence of any drug that affects faculties. Alcohol's effects (and residual "hangover" impairment) can last well past 8 hours; the regulation is a floor, not a target.

Trapped Gas: Ear & Sinus Block

Gas trapped in body cavities expands as you climb and compresses as you descend. If it can't escape or equalize, pressure builds and it hurts — sometimes badly.

  • The descent is the problem, not the climb. Climbing, expanding air vents out of the middle ear fairly easily. Descending, air must be forced back in through the Eustachian tube against a pressure difference — which is why ear pain almost always shows up on the way down.
  • Clearing it: swallow, yawn, or use the Valsalva maneuver — pinch the nostrils shut, close the mouth, and blow gently. That forces air up the Eustachian tube into the middle ear.
  • A cold changes everything. With congestion, an ear infection, or a sore throat, the tube may not open at all — a flight in that condition can be extremely painful and can rupture an eardrum. Sinus block works the same way through smaller openings, and hurts around the forehead or cheeks.
  • Careful with the fix. Nose drops or sprays may help minor congestion, but check with an AME first — and beware the decongestant that wears off mid-descent, leaving you blocked at the worst moment.

Motion Sickness, and Flying After Diving

  • Motion sickness comes from a conflict between what the eyes see and what the inner ear feels — the same sensory mismatch behind spatial disorientation. Symptoms run from general discomfort and sweating to nausea and vomiting, and it degrades performance well before that point. Practical mitigations: open fresh air vents, focus on a point outside the aircraft (looking down at charts makes it worse), avoid abrupt maneuvering, and — for a queasy passenger — give them something to do, like flying the airplane straight and level.
  • Flying after scuba diving. Diving forces extra nitrogen into your tissues; climbing afterward drops the pressure and lets it bubble out — decompression sickness, "the bends." Give the body time to off-gas. The standard waiting times: 12 hours after a dive that did not require a controlled ascent, and 24 hours after a dive that did require a controlled ascent — and 24 hours before any flight above 8,000 ft cabin altitude regardless of dive type.

10.3PAVE & the Five Hazardous Attitudes

PAVE organizes risk into four categories to check before every flight: Pilot (currency, IMSAFE), Aircraft (airworthiness, equipment for the flight), enVironment (weather, airspace, terrain, time of day), and External pressures (a schedule, passengers waiting, self-imposed "get-there-itis").

Hazardous attitudeThought patternAntidote
Anti-authority"Don't tell me what to do."Follow the rules; they're usually right.
Impulsivity"Do something — now!"Not so fast — think first.
Invulnerability"It won't happen to me."It could happen to me.
Macho"I can do it — watch this."Taking chances is foolish.
Resignation"What's the use?"I'm not helpless — I can make a difference.
AnalogyThe hazardous attitudes are like cognitive biases with a pilot's license — everyone has them to some degree, and the FAA doesn't expect you to eliminate them, just to recognize the thought as it happens and consciously apply the antidote before it turns into an action.

The DECIDE Model

PAVE organizes risk before the flight; DECIDE is the six-step loop for working a problem during it. It's a continuous loop, not a one-time checklist — after acting you evaluate, and if the situation hasn't improved you run it again.

StepThe question you're answering
DDetectSomething has changed — what is it? (The step pilots miss most, because it requires noticing at all)
EEstimateHow urgent is this? Does it need a response, and how soon?
CChooseWhat outcome do I want? (Choose the objective — e.g. "land safely somewhere" — before picking a tactic)
IIdentifyWhat actions could achieve that outcome?
DDoTake the action
EEvaluateDid it work? If not, loop back to Detect
CFIT — where all of this pointsControlled Flight Into Terrain is an accident in which a perfectly airworthy aircraft, under the pilot's full control, is flown into ground, water, or an obstacle. Nothing broke — the pilot simply didn't know where the terrain was. It's the outcome that the whole ADM apparatus exists to prevent, and it clusters around exactly the conditions in this lecture: night, reduced visibility, unfamiliar terrain, descending below a safe altitude, and continued VFR into deteriorating weather (§10.4). The defenses are unglamorous: know the MEF figures on your chart (§8.1), plan a route altitude with real terrain clearance, and treat any descent below a known-safe altitude without visual reference as unacceptable.

10.4Continued VFR into IMC — the Accident That Keeps Happening

Every hazardous attitude and every PAVE factor from §10.3 converges on one scenario more than any other: a VFR pilot pressing on into deteriorating weather. The FAA treats it as a genuine emergency, and the numbers explain why — a pilot without instrument training (or with eroded instrument skills) typically loses control of the airplane within about 10 minutes of being forced onto instruments alone.

About the "178 seconds" figureYou'll see a widely-quoted claim that a VFR pilot lasts 178 seconds in IMC. It comes from a 1954 University of Illinois study of non-pilots in a simulator, not from the current handbooks. The Airplane Flying Handbook's own figure is about 10 minutes. Either way the lesson is identical, but it's worth knowing which number is actually in FAA material.

The regulatory definition is broader than "flew into a cloud." Any time you cannot maintain attitude control by reference to the natural horizon — or cannot establish position by visual reference to surface landmarks — you are effectively in IMC, whatever the reported weather says. Haze, a moonless night over unlit terrain, or snow over a featureless surface all qualify.

If It Happens: the Three Steps

  1. Recognize and accept it immediately. This is the step pilots actually fail. Denial — "it'll clear up in a minute" — burns the altitude, fuel, and time that the other two steps need. Accept that it's an emergency now.
  2. Maintain control of the airplane. Trust the instruments over your inner ear, which will actively lie to you (§10.1's spatial disorientation). Level the wings using the attitude indicator and turn coordinator, hold altitude and heading, and make every input small and smooth. Steep or abrupt maneuvering is what turns a bad situation into a spiral.
  3. Get help. Confess to ATC on frequency (or 121.5), squawk 7700, and ask for vectors to VFR conditions or to an airport. Controllers have radar, terrain data, and current weather you don't — and §2.5's rule applies: declaring costs you nothing.
The 180° turnThe single most effective escape is also the earliest one: a gentle, level 180° turn back to the conditions you just came from — because you have direct evidence those were flyable. This works only while you still have a horizon to turn by, which is precisely why the decision has to be made early, before the situation is already unrecoverable.

10.5Night Operations

Night flying is legal for private pilots with no additional rating, but the human eye works differently in the dark, and most night-specific test questions come straight from that physiology.

How Night Vision Actually Works

Vision typeWhenWhat it uses
PhotopicDaylight / bright lightCones in the fovea (central vision) — sharp detail and color
MesopicDawn, dusk, full moonlightA mix of cones and rods — acuity and color perception both start degrading
ScotopicNight / low lightRods (peripheral vision) only — central vision becomes ineffective
  • The night blind spot. There are no rods in the fovea, so at night the central 5–10° of your visual field is effectively blind. Look directly at a dim object and it fades out entirely — and the blind spot hides larger objects the farther away they are.
  • Off-center viewing is the fix. Scan by looking slightly to the side of what you want to see, in short movements of about 10°, pausing a few seconds at each stop. Staring directly is exactly wrong at night.
  • Dark adaptation takes roughly 30 minutes and is undone in seconds by bright white light — which is why red or dim cockpit lighting is standard, and why you avoid staring at landing lights or strobes.

Night Illusions

  • False horizon — ground lights blend with stars, or a sloping cloud deck reads as level, and you align the airplane with something that isn't the real horizon.
  • Autokinesis — stare at a single stationary light in the dark for several seconds and it appears to move. Chasing it can put you in an unusual attitude. The defense is to keep scanning rather than fixating.
  • Black-hole approach (featureless terrain) — approaching over water or unlit ground with only runway lights visible, the lack of peripheral cues makes you feel high, so pilots tend to fly dangerously low. A VASI/PAPI (§2.7) is the antidote.
Night equipment & currencyUnder §91.205, VFR night flight requires everything on the day list plus — remembered as FLAPS — Fuses (spares), Landing light (only if flying for hire), Anticollision lights, Position lights, and a Source of electrical power. Position lights are the classic test item: red on the left wingtip, green on the right, white on the tail — which is how you judge another aircraft's direction of travel at night. And recall §2.1's currency rule: carrying passengers at night needs 3 takeoffs and full-stop landings at night within the preceding 90 days.
✦ Worked Example — spotting a hazardous attitude in real time

Scenario: Weather is marginal, but you told friends you'd make it to the fly-in today, and you think, "I've flown in worse than this before, I'll be fine."

  1. Identify the attitude. "I've handled worse, I'll be fine" is invulnerability — discounting personal risk based on past luck rather than present conditions.
  2. Notice the external pressure (PAVE). The friends/fly-in commitment is a classic External pressure factor pushing toward a "go" decision that the weather alone might not support.
  3. Apply the antidote. "It could happen to me" — reframe: today's actual ceiling/visibility numbers matter more than any past flight's outcome.
  4. Decide using the tools from Week 6, not the social pressure: compare today's METAR/TAF to your personal minimums, set in advance, precisely because this kind of pressure was foreseeable.

💬 Discussion 3 — Week 10

Prompt: Pick one hazardous attitude (anti-authority, impulsivity, invulnerability, macho, or resignation). Describe a scenario where it could push a pilot toward a bad decision, and what the matching "antidote thought" would be.

✓ Saved

Model answer (example — macho): A pilot with more experienced friends in the plane feels pressure to demonstrate skill by flying a tighter pattern or a faster approach than they'd normally choose, thinking "I can do it — watch this." The bad decision isn't the maneuver itself but performing it to prove something rather than because conditions call for it. The antidote — "taking chances is foolish" — reframes competence as matching technique to the actual situation, not to an audience; a genuinely skilled pilot is defined by consistently making the conservative choice available, not by the most impressive one.

Q1. Early hypoxia is especially dangerous because…
A. Its early symptoms (euphoria, impaired judgment) are hard to self-detect precisely because judgment is what's impaired
B. It causes immediate loss of consciousness with no warning
C. It only affects vision, never cognition
D. It cannot occur below 25,000 ft

A. Hypoxia impairs the very faculty (judgment) needed to notice it's happening — a dangerous, self-masking hazard.

Q2. Hyperventilation is typically caused by…
A. Too little cabin pressure
B. Over-breathing that flushes out too much CO2
C. Carbon monoxide poisoning exclusively
D. Excess oxygen intake from supplemental O2
Show answer

B. Hyperventilation, often anxiety-driven, over-expels CO2, producing symptoms (dizziness, tingling) that can mimic hypoxia. Slowing the breathing rate treats it.

Q3. The regulation on alcohol and flying (14 CFR §91.17) sets which minimum time and blood-alcohol limit?
A. 4 hours, 0.08
B. 12 hours, 0.02
C. 8 hours, 0.04
D. 24 hours, 0.00
Show answer

C. §91.17: no flying within 8 hours of consuming alcohol, with a blood alcohol limit of 0.04 — and effects can outlast 8 hours, so it's a floor, not a target.

A. Maintenance
B. Meteorology
C. Motivation
D. Medication
Show answer

D. IMSAFE = Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Eating — a personal, pre-flight self-check.
