10.1Aeromedical Factors

  • Hypoxia — insufficient oxygen to the brain, from altitude. Early signs (euphoria, poor judgment, tingling) are easy to miss because judgment itself is what's impaired. Above roughly 12,500 ft MSL cabin altitude for extended periods, supplemental oxygen becomes required for the required crew under 14 CFR §91.211; the risk begins well before that threshold for some individuals.
  • Hyperventilation — over-breathing, often from anxiety, that flushes out too much CO₂, causing dizziness, tingling, and symptoms that can be mistaken for hypoxia. Treatment: consciously slow the breathing rate.
  • Spatial disorientation — the inner ear and body's sense of "up" can be flat-out wrong without visual reference (e.g., in clouds), producing illusions like the "graveyard spiral" (a gentle turn that feels like level flight). The only reliable fix is to trust the flight instruments over bodily sensation.
  • Fatigue — degrades judgment, reaction time, and attention similarly to alcohol; acute (one bad night) and chronic (accumulated sleep debt) fatigue both count.
  • Alcohol & drugs — 14 CFR §91.17 sets the "8 hours from bottle to throttle" rule, a blood alcohol limit of 0.04, and prohibits flying under the influence of any drug that affects faculties. Alcohol's effects (and residual "hangover" impairment) can last well past 8 hours; the regulation is a floor, not a target.

10.3PAVE & the Five Hazardous Attitudes

PAVE organizes risk into four categories to check before every flight: Pilot (currency, IMSAFE), Aircraft (airworthiness, equipment for the flight), enVironment (weather, airspace, terrain, time of day), and External pressures (a schedule, passengers waiting, self-imposed "get-there-itis").

Anti-authority"Don't tell me what to do."Follow the rules; they're usually right.
Impulsivity"Do something — now!"Not so fast — think first.
Invulnerability"It won't happen to me."It could happen to me.
Macho"I can do it — watch this."Taking chances is foolish.

AnalogyThe hazardous attitudes are like cognitive biases with a pilot's license — everyone has them to some degree, and the FAA doesn't expect you to eliminate them, just to recognize the thought as it happens and consciously apply the antidote before it turns into an action.
✦ Worked Example — spotting a hazardous attitude in real time

Scenario: Weather is marginal, but you told friends you'd make it to the fly-in today, and you think, "I've flown in worse than this before, I'll be fine."

  1. Identify the attitude. "I've handled worse, I'll be fine" is invulnerability — discounting personal risk based on past luck rather than present conditions.
  2. Notice the external pressure (PAVE). The friends/fly-in commitment is a classic External pressure factor pushing toward a "go" decision that the weather alone might not support.
  3. Apply the antidote. "It could happen to me" — reframe: today's actual ceiling/visibility numbers matter more than any past flight's outcome.
  4. Decide using the tools from Week 6, not the social pressure: compare today's METAR/TAF to your personal minimums, set in advance, precisely because this kind of pressure was foreseeable.

💬 Discussion 3 — Week 10

Prompt: Pick one hazardous attitude (anti-authority, impulsivity, invulnerability, macho, or resignation). Describe a scenario where it could push a pilot toward a bad decision, and what the matching "antidote thought" would be.

✓ Saved

Model answer (example — macho): A pilot with more experienced friends in the plane feels pressure to demonstrate skill by flying a tighter pattern or a faster approach than they'd normally choose, thinking "I can do it — watch this." The bad decision isn't the maneuver itself but performing it to prove something rather than because conditions call for it. The antidote — "taking chances is foolish" — reframes competence as matching technique to the actual situation, not to an audience; a genuinely skilled pilot is defined by consistently making the conservative choice available, not by the most impressive one.

✓ Rubric: names a specific, plausible scenario (not just a definition); explains the underlying thought pattern in the pilot's own words; states the correct antidote thought for the chosen attitude.

7 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Early hypoxia is especially dangerous because…
A. Its early symptoms (euphoria, impaired judgment) are hard to self-detect precisely because judgment is what's impaired
B. It causes immediate loss of consciousness with no warning
C. It only affects vision, never cognition
D. It cannot occur below 25,000 ft
Show answer

A. Hypoxia impairs the very faculty (judgment) needed to notice it's happening — a dangerous, self-masking hazard.

Q2. Hyperventilation is typically caused by…
A. Too little cabin pressure
B. Over-breathing that flushes out too much CO2
C. Carbon monoxide poisoning exclusively
D. Excess oxygen intake from supplemental O2
Show answer

B. Hyperventilation, often anxiety-driven, over-expels CO2, producing symptoms (dizziness, tingling) that can mimic hypoxia. Slowing the breathing rate treats it.

Q3. The regulation on alcohol and flying (14 CFR §91.17) sets which minimum time and blood-alcohol limit?
A. 4 hours, 0.08
B. 12 hours, 0.02
C. 8 hours, 0.04
D. 24 hours, 0.00
Show answer

C. §91.17: no flying within 8 hours of consuming alcohol, with a blood alcohol limit of 0.04 — and effects can outlast 8 hours, so it's a floor, not a target.

Q4. In the IMSAFE checklist, "M" stands for…
A. Maintenance
B. Meteorology
C. Motivation
D. Medication
Show answer

D. IMSAFE = Illness, Medication, Stress, Alcohol, Fatigue, Eating — a personal, pre-flight self-check.

Q5. In the PAVE checklist, the "E" stands for…
A. External pressures
B. Elevation
C. Engine
D. Emergency procedures
Show answer

A. PAVE = Pilot, Aircraft, enVironment, External pressures — the last covers self-imposed pressures like schedules or passenger expectations.

Q6. A pilot who thinks "It won't happen to me" is displaying which hazardous attitude?
A. Anti-authority
B. Invulnerability
C. Resignation
D. Impulsivity
Show answer

B. Invulnerability — the antidote thought is "It could happen to me."

Q7. A pilot who feels overwhelmed and thinks "What's the use, nothing I do matters now" is displaying…
A. Macho
B. Impulsivity
C. Resignation
D. Anti-authority
Show answer

C. Resignation — the antidote is "I'm not helpless, I can make a difference," reasserting that deliberate action still changes the outcome.
