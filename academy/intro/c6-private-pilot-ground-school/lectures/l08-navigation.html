Navigation

Where the airplane points and where it actually goes are two different things — the wind triangle is how you reconcile them.

⚠ Educational only — not flight instruction Familiarization only — plan any real cross-country using current charts and an actual weather briefing, and always confirm your position visually and with any available navigation aid.

8.1Sectional Chart Symbology (Beyond Airspace)

Beyond the airspace rings from Week 3, sectionals depict the physical world you'll actually see out the window: airports (with runway layout icons and a data block giving elevation, frequencies, and lighting), obstructions (towers, given with elevation MSL and height AGL), terrain via color-shaded relief and spot elevations, and a scale/reference grid of latitude and longitude for plotting.

Obstruction Symbols: Height Category & Grouping

The shape of an obstruction symbol carries information before you even read its numbers (verified against the current FAA Chart Users' Guide):

A blunt way to remember MSL-over-AGL"The top number is what altimeter will read when you hit the obstruction; the bottom number is how far would you fall once you hit it." Morbid, but it makes the two numbers impossible to swap: the top one is an altitude (relative to sea level, same reference as your altimeter), the bottom one is a length (the object's physical height — literally the fall from its top to the ground right under it).

Airport Symbols: Runway Type & Beacons

The tower color (blue/magenta, above) is only half of what an airport's circle symbol tells you — its shape tells you what kind of runway is actually there. (Verified directly against the current FAA Chart Users' Guide — an earlier version of this figure had the "other than hard-surfaced" and "fuel" symbols backwards; corrected below.)

One more beacon fact worth knowingA rotating beacon operating during daylight hours at a towered (Class D) airport is a signal that weather is below basic VFR minimums — it doesn't replace an actual weather check, but seeing it running in daylight is a real, testable clue that something's off before you even call the tower.
One more circle symbolAn "R" printed inside the airport circle means restricted or private — a soft-surfaced runway, or a hard-surfaced runway shorter than 1,500 ft, not open for public use without specific authorization. The Chart Supplement note is blunt about it: "use only in emergency, or by specific authorization." Worth knowing as a real diversion option, not just a symbol to recognize.

Measuring Distance: Why Latitude, Never Longitude

The single most useful conversion in navigation is also the simplest: 1 minute of latitude = 1 nautical mile. This isn't an approximation to memorize — it's the actual definition of a nautical mile (one minute of arc along a meridian). It's also why the tick marks running up the left and right edges of every sectional are what you measure distance against, not the top/bottom edges: longitude lines converge toward the poles, so the spacing between them shrinks depending on latitude — using them for distance would give a different answer depending on where on the chart you measured. Latitude lines stay evenly spaced everywhere, so they're the only ruler on the chart that always tells the truth.

✦ Quick conversions
  1. 0°42' of latitude = 42 NM (each minute is 1 NM, so just read the minutes).
  2. 1°15' of latitude = 75 NM (1° = 60', so 60 + 15 = 75).
  3. 2°03' of latitude = 123 NM (2 × 60 = 120, plus 3 = 123).
Where this shows up on a GPSA GPS or EFB displays position in the same units a chart uses — degrees, minutes, and decimal minutes (e.g. N40°15.3'). Since 1' of latitude is always 1 NM, you can eyeball a rough distance directly from two sets of coordinates without touching a plotter: two waypoints at N40°15.3' and N40°42.1' are about 26.8 NM apart in the north-south direction alone — read straight off the minutes, no conversion factor needed. (Longitude minutes on a GPS are not 1 NM each, for the same convergence reason above — only latitude gives you that shortcut.)

8.2Dead Reckoning & the Wind Triangle

Dead reckoning means computing your position and heading from known starting data — course, distance, airspeed, time, and wind — rather than from outside references. The core tool is the wind triangle: three vectors (true course, wind, and true heading/groundspeed) that must close into a triangle.

TC + WCA (+ magnetic variation + compass deviation) = Compass HeadingTrue course, corrected for wind, then for magnetic variation, then for the individual compass's own deviation — each correction applied in sequence.
AnalogyCrossing a river by swimming is the same problem: if you aim straight at the far-bank landmark, the current sweeps you downstream past it. You have to aim upstream of your target by just enough that the current's sideways push and your own forward swim add up to a straight line across — exactly what the wind correction angle does for an airplane.

8.3VOR Navigation

A VOR (VHF Omnidirectional Range) ground station transmits a signal that varies by compass direction, letting an aircraft's receiver determine which radial (a magnetic course measured outward from the station) it's on.

A pilot dials a desired radial into the Omni-Bearing Selector (OBS); a centered Course Deviation Indicator (CDI) needle, with a "TO" or "FROM" flag, shows whether the aircraft is on that radial and heading toward or away from the station. A VOR-DME adds distance-measuring equipment, giving slant-range distance to the station alongside the radial.

8.4GPS & RAIM

GPS determines position by timing signals from multiple satellites and trilaterating a 3D fix — far more precise than VOR and not limited to defined radials. RAIM (Receiver Autonomous Integrity Monitoring) is the receiver's internal check that enough satellites are in view, in good enough geometry, to trust the position solution; a RAIM warning means the GPS position may not be reliable and should prompt a cross-check against other navigation sources.

8.5Lost Procedures & Diversion

If uncertain of position, the standard framework is: Climb (for a better view and better radio/nav reception), Communicate (contact ATC or Flight Service — they can help locate you via radar or DF), and Confess (state the situation plainly rather than wasting time or fuel hiding it). For a planned diversion (weather, fuel, an emergency ahead), quickly estimate a new heading and distance to the nearest suitable airport using dead-reckoning shortcuts rather than a full replot — approximate is far better than delayed-and-precise.

✦ Worked Example — solving a simple wind triangle

Scenario: True course 090°, true airspeed 100 kt, wind from 360° at 20 kt (a direct crosswind from the left). Roughly, what heading and groundspeed result?

  1. Visualize the geometry. The wind is blowing directly across your course from the north (left side, since you're heading east) — a pure crosswind, no headwind/tailwind component in this simplified case.
  2. Estimate the wind correction angle. With a 20 kt direct crosswind and 100 kt true airspeed, basic trigonometry (or an E6-B) gives a WCA of about 11–12° — you must crab into the wind (turn the nose toward the wind, i.e. slightly north of 090, toward 360) to track straight east.
  3. Compute the heading. True heading ≈ 090° − 12° ≈ 078° (crabbing toward the wind direction).
  4. Compute groundspeed. With a pure crosswind and no head/tailwind component in this simplified setup, groundspeed stays close to true airspeed — roughly 98 kt (slightly reduced because some airspeed is now used to counteract drift).
  5. On the real E6-B you set up in Week 1, this same problem is solved mechanically in under a minute — practice it before test day.

A. Indicated and true airspeed
B. True course and true heading
C. Magnetic heading and compass heading
D. Pressure altitude and density altitude

B. The WCA is the angle you must offset your heading from the true course line so that wind drift and your own track combine to actually fly the desired course over the ground.

🧩 Challenging Questions

Real test-bank questions logged while practicing — kept here for spaced review, separate from the lecture quiz above. More get added as they come up.

Q# 3571ACS PA.I.D.K3b — ETA, including conversion to UTC (Zulu)
(Refer to Figure 27 — Time Conversion Table.) An aircraft departs an airport in the eastern daylight time zone at 0945 EDT for a 2-hour flight to an airport located in the central daylight time zone. The landing should be at what coordinated universal time?
A. 1345Z.
B. 1445Z.
C. 1545Z.
Show answer

C. Not conceptually hard, just easy to fumble on the arithmetic — three steps, always in this order:

1. Convert the departure local time to UTC — using the departure zone's offset, not the destination's. This is the step that trips people up: the destination is in Central time, but that's irrelevant to the conversion itself. UTC is one single global clock; every local time converts to it independently of what zone anything else is in. Per the Figure 27 table, Eastern Daylight Time → add 4 hours: 0945 + 0400 = 1345Z (this is option A — it's the right first step, but it's departure time, not arrival, so stopping here is the classic trap).

2. Add the flight duration in real elapsed hours. A 2-hour flight is 2 hours of elapsed time no matter how many local time zones it crosses — crossing into Central time changes what the local clock reads on arrival, not how much time actually passed. 1345Z + 0200 = 1545Z.

3. Don't double-apply the zone change. Option B (1445Z) is what you'd get by only adding 1 hour instead of 2 — e.g. mistakenly treating the Eastern→Central zone shift (1 hour) as if it were the flight time, or subtracting it from the 2-hour duration. The zone difference affects only what the local arrival clock will read (1145 CDT, since Central is 1 hour behind Eastern) — it has no effect on the UTC arithmetic at all, which only ever needs: departure zone's offset, then add elapsed flight time.

Reference table for questions of this style (e.g. Figure 27 in the FAA supplement). The tedious part is never the concept — it's remembering to always key off the departure zone, then just add elapsed hours.

The reverse direction is just as tedious, and comes up just as often: given a required Zulu time, what's that in local? Same table, opposite arithmetic — going local → Zulu you add the offset; going Zulu → local you subtract it. Two worked examples, one each direction, including the part that actually causes mistakes: the calendar date rolling over.

✦ Worked Example — local departure time → Zulu ETA (forward rollover)

Scenario: You depart Chicago (Central Daylight Time) at 1650 CDT for a 3-hour-20-minute flight. What's your ETA in Zulu?

  1. Convert departure local → Zulu. CDT → add 5 hours: 1650 + 0500 = 2150Z.
  2. Add the flight duration. 2150 + 0320 = 2470 on the clock — which isn't a valid time, because it rolled past 2400.
  3. Handle the rollover. Subtract 2400 from the overflow: 2470 − 2400 = 0070, i.e. 0070 → 0110 (70 minutes past midnight = 01:10) — and because it crossed midnight, the Zulu date is now one day later than the local departure date.
  4. ETA: 0110Z, the following calendar day. This is exactly why flight plans list a Zulu date alongside the time — a late-evening domestic departure routinely lands "tomorrow" in Zulu even though it's the same local evening.
✦ Worked Example — required Zulu time → local arrival time (backward rollover)

Scenario: Your flight plan requires landing at KJFK (Eastern Daylight Time) no later than 0300Z. What's the latest local (EDT) arrival time?

  1. Find the zone's offset. EDT → add 4 hours to go local→Zulu, so go Zulu→local by subtracting 4 hours instead.
  2. Subtract. 0300 − 0400 = −0100 — negative, which isn't a valid time, because it rolled back past midnight.
  3. Handle the rollover. Add 2400 to the negative result: −0100 + 2400 = 2300 — and because it crossed midnight going backward, the local date is one day earlier than the Zulu date.
  4. Latest local arrival: 2300 EDT, the previous calendar day (i.e. 11:00 PM the evening before the 0300Z deadline). Easy to misread as "11 PM that day" if you don't track that the date, not just the time, shifted backward.

✓ What you learned

  • Sectional charts depict airports, obstructions, and terrain alongside airspace.
  • The wind triangle (true course, wind, true heading/groundspeed) is the core dead-reckoning tool; WCA corrects for crosswind drift.
  • VOR radials are measured outward from the station; a VOR-DME adds distance; GPS/RAIM gives more precise position with an integrity check.
  • Lost procedure: Climb, Communicate, Confess. Diversions favor quick estimates over slow precision.
