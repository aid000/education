Balanced vs Focused Portfolios & the Startup Mindset

Diversification protects you from being wrong; concentration is how fortunes are made — and lost. This lecture shows when each makes sense.

7.1Two Philosophies: Spreading Out vs Betting Big

By now you know index funds beat most active managers net of fees (Week 6). That argues for a balanced portfolio: own a little of everything so no single bad outcome can sink you. But the largest fortunes — founders, early employees, concentrated investors — almost always come from a focused bet: a big stake in one company or idea. These are not contradictions; they answer different questions.

  • Balanced / diversified. Goal: capture the market's return with the least avoidable risk. Hundreds or thousands of holdings, no single name decisive. This is the right default for retirement and money you cannot afford to lose.
  • Focused / concentrated. Goal: maximize upside on an edge you believe in. A few positions — or one — that can multiply, but can also go to zero. This is how wealth is created, and how it is most often destroyed.

The core trade-off is between expected return and variance (how spread out the outcomes are). Diversification lowers variance; concentration raises it in both directions.

7.2Why Diversification Is a "Free Lunch"

Diversification is special because it lowers risk without lowering expected return. The reason is that returns are not perfectly correlated — when one holding zigs, another zags, and the wiggles partly cancel. For n holdings with similar volatility σ and average pairwise correlation ρ, portfolio variance falls toward a floor set by ρ:

σp2 = (1/n)·σ2 + (1 − 1/n)·ρ·σ2The first term (idiosyncratic, single-company risk) vanishes as n grows; the second (systematic, market-wide risk) is the floor you cannot diversify away.

If holdings were independent (ρ = 0), risk would fall like 1/√n and you could in principle eliminate it. In reality ρ > 0, so a broad index removes company-specific (idiosyncratic) risk but leaves market (systematic) risk — the part you are actually paid to bear. The lesson: holding 20–30 well-spread names captures most of the benefit; a total-market index fund captures essentially all of it.

Analogy An insurance company doesn't know which one house will burn down, but across ten thousand policies the fraction is remarkably steady. One claim could ruin a single homeowner; spread across the pool it's just a predictable cost. Diversification does the same for your portfolio — it turns the gut-wrenching uncertainty of one company into the smooth, knowable average of many.

7.3The Case For Concentration — and Its Price

If diversification is free, why does anyone concentrate? Because diversification also caps your upside. To beat the market meaningfully you must hold something different from the market — and that means concentration. Founders and early employees become wealthy precisely because most of their net worth is one asset that grew enormously. Concentration is rational when you have a genuine edge: real information, skill, or control the average investor lacks.

The price is idiosyncratic risk you no longer share with anyone. Three forces define a concentrated bet:

  • High variance. Single-company outcomes are wide — zero is a live possibility, and most startups fail.
  • Illiquidity. Private equity often can't be sold for years; you can't rebalance out of a bad position the way you can with a public stock.
  • Correlation with your income. If you work and hold equity at the same firm, a single bad event hits your paycheck and your portfolio together — your eggs aren't just in one basket, they're in the basket you're carrying.

So the discipline is not "diversify or concentrate" but position sizing: concentrate only with money whose total loss won't end your game, and keep a diversified base underneath.

Common mistake Confusing a concentrated bet that worked with a good decision. Survivorship bias makes concentration look easy — we hear about the founder who got rich, never the nine who didn't. A bet can be smart and still lose, or dumb and still win. Judge the process and the sizing, not the single outcome, and never bet money you cannot afford to lose to zero.

7.4Startup Equity: Options, RSUs, Vesting & Illiquidity

The most common concentrated bet a young professional faces is equity compensation at a startup — part of your pay delivered as ownership instead of cash. Two main forms:

  • Stock options give you the right to buy shares later at a fixed strike price. They're valuable only if the company's share value rises above the strike; if it falls below, they're worthless ("underwater"). Exercising can trigger taxes — and for incentive options, an AMT trap — so this is an area to verify with a professional (as of 2026 — verify against current IRS rules).
  • RSUs (restricted stock units) are an outright promise of shares once they vest; they have value as long as the stock has any value at all, and are generally taxed as ordinary income when they vest.

Vesting is the schedule on which equity becomes yours — a classic grant vests over four years with a one-year "cliff" (nothing until month 12, then monthly). Its purpose is retention: leave early and you forfeit the unvested portion. And crucially, private startup shares are illiquid — there is usually no market to sell them in until an IPO or acquisition, which may be years away or never come.

7.5The Wealth-Building Mindset

Strip away the products and the actual engine of wealth for nearly everyone is unglamorous: spend less than you earn, invest the difference automatically, and let compounding run for decades. Concentration can accelerate it, but the base is built by habit, not heroics.

The three levers

  • Savings rate — the one lever you fully control. A high savings rate beats a high return for the first decade or two.
  • Time — compounding is exponential, so the early years you can't get back are the most valuable. Recall the Rule of 72 from Week 1: years-to-double ≈ 72 / return%.
  • Return — broadly captured for free by a low-cost index; chasing extra return is where most people lose, not gain.
FV = PV · (1 + r)t   |   years to double ≈ 72 / r%Compound growth (Week 1). At 7%/yr money doubles about every ~10 years; at 10%, about every ~7 — the gap compounds enormously over a career.

Pair this with the right mindset toward risk: keep an emergency fund and a diversified core so you can survive bad luck, then take asymmetric concentrated bets only with capital you can afford to lose. The goal is to stay in the game long enough for compounding and the occasional big bet to do their work.

Analogy Think of a casino owner versus a gambler. The gambler makes one big bet and rides the outcome; the house makes thousands of small, positively-edged bets and wins by sheer repetition and never risking ruin. A diversified investor who saves steadily is the house. Concentrated bets are fine — but make them as the house would: sized so no single loss can put you out of business.
✦ Worked Example — sizing a startup equity bet

Maya, 28, is offered a job at a startup. Cash salary is $20k below a safe corporate offer, plus an equity grant the company "values" at $200k over a 4-year vest (1-year cliff). How should she think about it?

  1. Discount the headline. The $200k is a paper figure on private, illiquid shares. Most startups fail, so the expected value is far below $200k. Treat it as a lottery ticket with a real edge, not a bank balance.
  2. Price the cost. She gives up $20k/yr in cash = $80k over four years (more after taxes and forgone investing). That is the certain price of the uncertain upside.
  3. Account for the cliff. If she leaves before month 12, she vests zero equity but has already taken the pay cut — the first year is the riskiest stretch.
  4. Check correlation. Her paycheck and her equity are the same company. A failure hits both at once, so this bet is riskier than the dollar amount alone suggests.
  5. Size it. Reasonable framing: take the bet if (a) she believes in the edge, (b) she can absorb the $80k cash hit without derailing her diversified base and emergency fund, and (c) she'd accept the equity going to zero. If any fails, she should negotiate more cash or pass — not bet money she can't lose.
  6. Takeaway: the decision is about position sizing and survival, not the seductive $200k. Concentrate only what you can afford to lose to zero.

🛠️ Hands-On: diversified index vs one concentrated stock

Reminder: you already set up Python + the data stack in Week 1 — just activate that environment. (If a download is blocked, the CSV-from-Sheets fallback from Week 5 works too.)

Pull a few years of a broad index and one single stock, then compare their return spreads. You should see the index's daily returns cluster far more tightly — that tighter spread is diversification at work.

# portfolio_spread.py — Lecture 7 (educational, not advice)
import yfinance as yf
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Broad index ETF vs a single concentrated name
data = yf.download(["VTI", "TSLA"], start="2019-01-01", end="2024-01-01")["Close"]
rets = data.pct_change().dropna()

# Annualized volatility (risk): single stock should be much higher
vol = rets.std() * np.sqrt(252)
print(vol.sort_values())          # VTI well below the single stock

# Histogram of daily returns: index is tall and narrow, stock is wide
rets.plot.hist(bins=80, alpha=0.5, figsize=(8,4))
plt.title("Daily returns: diversified index vs one stock")
plt.xlabel("daily return"); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Now build a tiny equal-weight basket of ~10 unrelated tickers and recompute its volatility. Predict before you run: it should land below the average of the individual stocks' volatilities — that gap is the diversification benefit from §7.2. (Past data is not a forecast; this is illustration, not advice.)

Q1. Diversification is often called a "free lunch" because it…
A. guarantees a higher return than any single stock
B. reduces risk without reducing expected return
C. eliminates all risk, including market risk
D. avoids all taxes and fees
Show answer

B. By combining imperfectly correlated assets, variance falls while expected return is unchanged. It cannot raise return (A) or remove systematic/market risk (C).

Q2. The risk that remains in a broad total-market index fund, which you cannot diversify away, is called…
A. idiosyncratic (company-specific) risk
B. liquidity risk
C. systematic (market) risk
D. round-off risk
Show answer

C. Systematic risk affects the whole market and is the part investors are actually paid to bear. Company-specific (idiosyncratic) risk (A) is the part diversification removes.

Q3. A startup grant "vests over 4 years with a 1-year cliff." If an employee leaves after 9 months, they keep…
A. 25% of the grant
B. a pro-rated 9/48 of the grant
C. nothing — the cliff hasn't been reached
D. the full grant
Show answer

C. With a 1-year cliff, nothing vests until month 12 (when 25% vests at once). Leaving at month 9 forfeits all of it.

Q4. Why is holding both your job and your equity at the same startup riskier than the dollar amount suggests?
A. Equity is always taxed twice
B. Your income and your investment are correlated — one bad event hits both at once
C. RSUs cannot be diversified by law
D. Options always expire worthless
Show answer

B. Concentration plus correlation with your paycheck means a company failure removes your salary and your savings simultaneously — the worst-case scenarios coincide.

Q5. Which best captures the wealth-building mindset emphasized in this lecture?
A. Find one stock that will 10× and put everything in it
B. Time the market to avoid every downturn
C. Save steadily into a diversified core, let compounding run, and size concentrated bets so a total loss can't end the game
D. Borrow as much as possible to amplify returns
Show answer

C. Survival plus compounding is the engine; concentrated bets are optional accelerants taken only with money you can afford to lose. (A) and (D) risk ruin; (B) is unreliable.

Q6. A friend got rich on a single concentrated bet, so they conclude concentration is clearly the smart move for everyone. The flaw in this reasoning is…
A. survivorship bias — we don't hear about the many similar bets that failed
B. the Rule of 72 doesn't apply to stocks
C. diversified portfolios have higher expected returns
D. concentrated bets are illegal for individuals
Show answer

A. One winning outcome doesn't make the decision good — judge the process and position sizing, not a single result drawn from a fat-tailed distribution where most outcomes were losses.
