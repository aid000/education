Account Types — IRA, Roth, Brokerage & 401(k)

Same dollars, very different outcomes — the account you put a dollar in can change its after-tax future value by tens of thousands.

⚠ Educational only — not advice This lecture is for education, not financial, tax, or investment advice. Every dollar figure here is illustrative and dated ("as of 2026"); contribution limits, income phase-outs, brackets, and the Social Security wage base change every year. Verify current numbers with the IRS (irs.gov) and SSA (ssa.gov) and consult a qualified professional before acting. 2.1Three Tax Treatments, One Decision Tree

In Week 1 we saw that compounding does the heavy lifting over decades. The single biggest lever on how much of that growth you keep is which tax wrapper you hold the investment in. Every U.S. account reduces to one of three treatments:

  • Tax-deferred (Traditional IRA, traditional 401(k)): you contribute pre-tax dollars — a deduction today — and pay ordinary income tax on every dollar you withdraw in retirement.
  • Tax-free (Roth IRA, Roth 401(k)): you contribute after-tax dollars — no deduction today — but qualified withdrawals, including all the growth, come out completely tax-free.
  • Taxable (a regular brokerage account): no special treatment going in, but you owe tax each year on dividends and on gains when you sell — at favorable long-term capital-gains rates if held over a year.
Analogy — the farmer's taxImagine a farmer who can be taxed on the seed or on the harvest. Traditional is "tax the harvest": plant freely now, hand over a share of the (much larger) crop later. Roth is "tax the seed": pay on the small bag of seed today, keep the entire harvest. If your harvest-time tax rate is lower than today's, taxing the seed (Roth) only makes sense when you expect to be richer or rates to rise later — exactly the decision in §2.4.

2.2Traditional IRA vs Roth IRA

An IRA (Individual Retirement Arrangement) is an account you open yourself at a broker — no employer needed. The two flavors differ only in when you pay tax, plus a few rules around income and withdrawals.

FeatureTraditional IRARoth IRA
ContributionsPre-tax (often deductible)After-tax (never deductible)
GrowthTax-deferredTax-free
Qualified withdrawalsTaxed as ordinary incomeTax-free (after age 59½ & 5-yr rule)

The headline numbers, as of 2026 — verify against current IRS figures:

IRA / Roth IRA limit ≈ $7,500  (+ ~$1,100 catch-up at age 50+)Combined across all your IRAs. The 2025 figure was $7,000 (+$1,000 catch-up); the 2026 numbers shown here are approximate — re-confirm at irs.gov.

Roth IRAs have an income phase-out: above a modified-AGI threshold your allowed Roth contribution shrinks to zero. As of 2026 (verify): roughly the high-$150Ks to ~$160Ks for single filers and the ~$240Ks–$250Ks for married-filing-jointly. (For 2025 the single range was about $150,000–$165,000 and MFJ about $236,000–$246,000.) Earn above the top of the range and you cannot contribute directly — a "backdoor Roth" is the common workaround, beyond our scope here.

Common mistakeThe contribution limit is per person, across all IRAs combined — not per account. Opening three IRAs does not triple your limit. Also, you can only contribute earned income, and contributions for a tax year can be made up until that year's tax-filing deadline the following April.

2.3The 401(k) and the Employer Match

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement plan. Money is deferred straight from your paycheck (traditional/pre-tax, or Roth/after-tax if offered), and the employee deferral limit is much larger than an IRA's. As of 2026 — verify against current IRS figures:

401(k) employee deferral ≈ $24,500  (+ ~$8,000 catch-up at 50+)2025 was $23,500 (+$7,500 catch-up); 2026 shown is approximate. Separate from — and on top of — the IRA limit. Re-confirm at irs.gov.

The defining feature is the employer match: many employers add money when you contribute — a classic formula is "100% of the first 3%, then 50% of the next 2%," giving up to a 4% match if you defer 5% of pay. That match is an immediate, guaranteed return on your contribution — there is no investment on earth that reliably pays you 50–100% the instant you buy it.

Analogy — leaving money on the tableSkipping the match is like declining a coupon that says "spend $5, get $5 free" at the register. If your match is 100% on the first 3% and you contribute 0%, you have voluntarily taken a pay cut equal to 3% of your salary, every single year.

Vesting is the catch: your own contributions are always 100% yours, but the employer's match may require you to stay employed for a few years before it fully belongs to you. A "graded" schedule might vest 20% per year over five years; "cliff" vesting gives you 0% until a date, then 100%. Leave early and you forfeit the unvested portion.

2.4The Math: Traditional vs Roth Is About Tax Rates

People agonize over Traditional vs Roth, but the algebra is clean. Let your contribution grow by a factor g over the years, with marginal tax rate tnow today and tlater in retirement. Starting from the same pre-tax dollar:

Traditional: 1 · g · (1 − tlater)    Roth: (1 − tnow) · gBoth grow by the same g; the only difference is which tax rate you multiply by.

Cancel g and the choice reduces to a single comparison:

Roth wins ⇔ tnow < tlater     Traditional wins ⇔ tnow > tlaterIf your tax rate is identical now and later, the two are mathematically equal — the growth factor g is irrelevant to the choice.

So the decision is not about returns or time horizon — it's a bet on whether your tax rate will be higher or lower in retirement. A student or early-career worker in a low bracket usually expects higher rates later → Roth. A peak-earner in a top bracket who expects a lower-income retirement usually leans Traditional. Many people split to hedge.

Note — taxable accounts still matterOnce you've captured the match and maxed tax-advantaged space, a taxable brokerage is the overflow. It has no contribution limit and full liquidity, and long-term gains (assets held > 1 year) are taxed at preferential long-term capital-gains rates of 0% / 15% / 20% (brackets as of 2026 — verify) rather than ordinary income rates. That's why "tax-efficient" index funds (low turnover, few taxable distributions) fit naturally in taxable accounts.

2.5A Sensible Funding Order

Given limited dollars, most personal-finance frameworks fund accounts in roughly this order — capturing the highest "guaranteed return" first:

  1. 401(k) up to the full employer match — the instant 50–100% return beats everything.
  2. High-interest debt — paying off a 22% credit card is a guaranteed 22% return (covered in Week 3).
  3. Max the IRA (Roth if you're eligible and expect higher future rates).
  4. Max the rest of the 401(k) up to the deferral limit.
  5. Taxable brokerage for anything beyond that.

The exact order is personal, but the first rule — always grab the full match — is nearly universal. Note also FICA payroll taxes fund Social Security (6.2% up to a wage base, ~$184,500 as of 2026 — verify with SSA) and Medicare (1.45%, no cap); these are separate from income tax and apply to wages regardless of retirement contributions.

✦ Worked Example — Roth or Traditional for a $6,000 contribution?

Maya, 25, is in the 12% federal bracket now and expects to retire in the 22% bracket. She can invest from $6,000 of pre-tax income, growing 8× over 35 years (≈ 6.1%/yr). Which IRA leaves her more after-tax money?

  1. Traditional: the full $6,000 pre-tax goes in (she takes a deduction now). It grows 8× to $48,000. At withdrawal she pays 22%: $48,000 × (1 − 0.22) = $37,440.
  2. Roth: she pays 12% tax on the $6,000 first → $5,280 invested. It grows 8× to $42,240, withdrawn tax-free: $42,240.
  3. Compare: Roth's $42,240 beats Traditional's $37,440 by ~$4,800 — because tnow (12%) < tlater (22%), exactly the rule from §2.4.
  4. Flip the rates (35% now, 15% later) and Traditional would win instead. The growth factor g (8×) never decides it — only the two tax rates do.
  5. Takeaway: a young, low-bracket saver who expects to earn more later almost always favors the Roth — the up-front tax is cheap and decades of growth come out tax-free.

🛠️ Hands-On: model Traditional vs Roth yourself

Reuse the Python + numpy/pandas/matplotlib environment from Week 1 (one-line reminder: source finance-env/bin/activate). No new installs needed.

Build a tiny function that returns the after-tax ending value of each account, then sweep the retirement tax rate to see exactly where the crossover sits.

# account_compare.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

pretax     = 6000      # pre-tax dollars available
years, r   = 35, 0.061 # horizon and annual return
t_now      = 0.12      # marginal rate today
g          = (1 + r) ** years           # growth factor (~8x)

def traditional(t_later):
    return pretax * g * (1 - t_later)   # tax the harvest

def roth(t_now):
    return pretax * (1 - t_now) * g     # tax the seed

t_later = np.linspace(0.05, 0.40, 50)
plt.plot(t_later, traditional(t_later), label="Traditional")
plt.axhline(roth(t_now), color="C1", ls="--", label="Roth (fixed)")
plt.xlabel("retirement tax rate"); plt.ylabel("after-tax $")
plt.legend(); plt.title("Crossover happens at t_later = t_now")
plt.show()
# The lines cross exactly where t_later == t_now (= 0.12 here).

Q1. Qualified withdrawals from a Roth IRA in retirement are…
A. taxed as ordinary income
B. completely tax-free, including the growth
C. taxed at long-term capital-gains rates
D. taxed only if you're under 59½
Show answer

B. Roth contributions are after-tax going in, so qualified withdrawals (after 59½ and the 5-year rule) — principal and all growth — come out tax-free. A Traditional IRA is what gets taxed as ordinary income (A).

Q2. Your employer matches 100% of the first 3% of pay you defer. The smartest minimum action is to…
A. skip the 401(k) and use only a Roth IRA
B. contribute 1% to keep things simple
C. contribute at least 3% to capture the full match
D. wait until you're fully vested before contributing anything
Show answer

C. The match is an instant ~100% return on those dollars — contributing below 3% leaves guaranteed money on the table. Your own contributions are always immediately yours regardless of vesting (D is wrong).

Q3. Starting from the same pre-tax dollar, a Roth beats a Traditional account precisely when…
A. your tax rate now is lower than your tax rate in retirement
B. the investment grows faster
C. your tax rate now is higher than in retirement
D. you have a longer time horizon
Show answer

A. The growth factor cancels; Roth wins iff tnow < tlater. Time horizon and return (B, D) don't decide it — only the two tax rates do.

Q4. The annual contribution limit for IRAs is…
A. per account, so more IRAs means a higher total limit
B. the same as the 401(k) employee deferral limit
C. unlimited as long as you have earned income
D. a single limit shared across all your IRAs combined
Show answer

D. It's one combined limit across all your IRAs (≈ $7,500 as of 2026 — verify), not per account. The 401(k) deferral limit is separate and much larger (B is wrong).

Q5. A regular taxable brokerage account's main tax advantage versus ordinary income is that…
A. contributions are tax-deductible like a Traditional IRA
B. long-term gains (held > 1 year) get preferential 0/15/20% rates
C. all withdrawals are tax-free like a Roth
D. it has an annual contribution limit
Show answer

B. Taxable accounts get no deduction (A) and aren't tax-free (C), but assets held over a year are taxed at favorable long-term capital-gains rates. They also have no contribution limit (D is wrong).

Q6. The FICA Social Security tax is 6.2% of wages up to an annual wage base, while the Medicare portion (1.45%)…
A. stops at the same wage base as Social Security
B. only applies to investment income
C. has no wage cap and applies to all wages
D. is waived if you contribute to a 401(k)
Show answer

C. The 1.45% Medicare tax has no wage cap (unlike Social Security's wage base, ~$184,500 as of 2026 — verify with SSA). Pre-tax 401(k) deferrals still owe FICA (D is wrong).
