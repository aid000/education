Taxes

Taxes are the single biggest expense most people will ever have — and the one almost nobody is taught to read. This lecture decodes the bill.

4.1Marginal vs Effective: How a Progressive System Actually Works

The most common tax myth is "a raise pushed me into a higher bracket, so I'll take home less." That is false, and understanding why is the foundation of everything else. The U.S. federal income tax is progressive and marginal: income is sliced into brackets, and only the dollars that fall inside a bracket are taxed at that bracket's rate.

Your marginal rate is the rate on your next dollar — the top bracket you reach. Your effective rate is the total tax divided by total income — a blended average that is always lower than your marginal rate. 4.2FICA: The Payroll Tax Hiding in Plain Sight

Before federal income tax even applies, your paycheck loses FICA — the Federal Insurance Contributions Act payroll tax that funds Social Security and Medicare. It is flat, not bracketed, and it comes off the top:

FICA (employee share) = 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65%Your employer pays a matching 7.65%, so the true cost on your labor is ~15.3%. As of 2026 — verify rates with the SSA/IRS.

Two crucial wrinkles, as of 2026 (verify against current SSA figures):

  • The Social Security 6.2% applies only up to the annual wage base — approximately $184,500 (as of 2026). Earnings above that pay no further Social Security tax, which makes this portion mildly regressive at high incomes.
  • The Medicare 1.45% has no cap — every dollar pays it. High earners owe an extra 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax above ~$200k (single).
  • Self-employed? You pay both halves (the ~15.3% "self-employment tax"), though half of it is deductible.
Common mistake People conflate "my tax bracket" with "what comes out of my check." For a typical worker, FICA (7.65%) often takes a bigger bite than federal income tax does, especially at lower incomes — because FICA hits the very first dollar while income tax is shielded by the standard deduction. Always count FICA and income tax and any state tax to see your real take-home.

4.3State, Local & Sales Tax — The Layers Beyond Federal

Federal income tax is only one floor of the building. On top of it:

State & local income tax

Most states levy their own income tax — some flat, some progressive — typically in the 0%–13% range depending on the state. A handful (e.g., several with no wage income tax) charge none, while a few cities add a local income tax on top. Where you live can change your total bill by many percentage points for identical income.

Sales tax

Sales tax is a consumption tax: a percentage added at checkout, set by state and locality (commonly ~0%–10% combined). Unlike income tax it is not bracketed — everyone pays the same rate on a purchase — which is why it is considered regressive: it consumes a larger share of a low earner's income, since they spend a higher fraction of what they earn.

Total tax wedge ≈ FICA + federal income + state/local income (on what you earn)
+ sales tax (on what you spend)Your "all-in" rate is the sum of these layers. The mix differs enormously by state and by spending habits.

4.4Capital Gains: Short-Term vs Long-Term

When you sell an investment for more than you paid, the profit is a capital gain. How it's taxed depends almost entirely on one thing: how long you held it.

  • Short-term (held ≤ 1 year): taxed as ordinary income — your regular marginal bracket (up to 37%).
  • Long-term (held > 1 year): taxed at the preferential 0% / 15% / 20% long-term rates — far lower for most people.

4.5Filing Basics: From Gross Pay to Tax Owed

"Filing taxes" is really just running one formula. Here is the skeleton of a Form 1040:

Gross income − adjustments = AGI
AGI − deduction (standard or itemized) = taxable income
taxable income → brackets → tax − credits = tax owedSubtract what your employer already withheld; the difference is your refund (over-withheld) or balance due (under-withheld).

The standard deduction is a flat amount everyone can subtract before brackets apply — it's why your first dollars of income are effectively tax-free. As of 2026 (verify with the IRS): roughly $16,100 single / $32,200 married filing jointly. You take the larger of the standard deduction or your itemized deductions (mortgage interest, state/local taxes capped, charitable gifts, etc.); most people take the standard.

A few distinctions worth burning in:

  • Deduction vs credit: a deduction reduces taxable income (worth your marginal rate per dollar); a credit reduces tax owed dollar-for-dollar (much more valuable).
  • Withholding vs the bill: your W-4 controls how much is withheld each paycheck. A big refund just means you lent the government money interest-free all year.
  • Filing status (single, married filing jointly, head of household) sets your bracket and deduction sizes.
Note — these numbers expire Every figure in this lecture — brackets, FICA wage base, standard deduction, capital-gains thresholds — is inflation-indexed and changes annually, and tax law itself changes. The amounts here are dated as of 2026 and are illustrative. Before you file or make a decision, confirm the current figures at irs.gov and ssa.gov.
✦ Worked Example — marginal vs effective rate on a real paycheck

Maya is single, earns $70,000 in wages, takes the standard deduction, and has no other income. Find her federal taxable income, federal income tax, FICA, and her effective rate. (Figures as of 2026 — verify.)

  1. Taxable income: $70,000 − $16,100 standard deduction = $53,900.
  2. Apply brackets to $53,900:
    • 10% on the first $12,400 = $1,240
    • 12% on $12,400→$50,400 ($38,000) = $4,560
    • 22% on $50,400→$53,900 ($3,500) = $770
  3. Federal income tax = $1,240 + $4,560 + $770 = $6,570.
  4. Marginal rate = 22% (her top bracket). Effective federal income-tax rate = $6,570 ÷ $70,000 = ≈ 9.4% — far below 22%.
  5. FICA = 7.65% × $70,000 = $5,355 (Social Security + Medicare, below the wage base).
  6. All-in federal payroll bite = $6,570 + $5,355 = $11,925, an effective ≈ 17% of gross — and that's before any state income or sales tax. Notice FICA is nearly as large as her income tax.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a progressive-tax calculator

One-line reminder: the Python + NumPy/pandas/matplotlib stack was set up in Week 1 — just activate that environment. (No yfinance needed today; it returns in the investing weeks.)

Encode the 2026 single-filer brackets, then write a function that pours income through them and reports the marginal and effective rates. Reproduce Maya's numbers, then try your own.

# taxes.py — Lecture 4 · progressive-tax calculator (figures as of 2026 — verify w/ IRS)
import numpy as np

# (lower_bound, rate) for single filer ordinary income, 2026
BRACKETS = [(0, .10), (12_400, .12), (50_400, .22), (105_700, .24),
            (201_775, .32), (256_225, .35), (640_600, .37)]
STD_DEDUCTION = 16_100   # single, 2026
FICA = 0.0765            # 6.2% SS (up to wage base) + 1.45% Medicare
SS_WAGE_BASE = 184_500   # 2026 Social Security cap

def income_tax(taxable):
    tax = 0.0
    for i, (lo, rate) in enumerate(BRACKETS):
        hi = BRACKETS[i + 1][0] if i + 1 < len(BRACKETS) else np.inf
        if taxable > lo:
            tax += (min(taxable, hi) - lo) * rate
        else:
            break
    return tax

def fica(wages):
    ss = 0.062 * min(wages, SS_WAGE_BASE)
    medicare = 0.0145 * wages
    return ss + medicare

wages = 70_000
taxable = max(0, wages - STD_DEDUCTION)
fed = income_tax(taxable)
print(f"taxable income: ${taxable:,.0f}")
print(f"federal income tax: ${fed:,.0f}")            # ~6,570
print(f"FICA: ${fica(wages):,.0f}")                  # ~5,355
print(f"effective income-tax rate: {fed/wages:6.1%}")# ~9.4%

Extension: sweep wages from $10k to $400k with NumPy, then matplotlib-plot the marginal rate (a step function) against the effective rate (a smooth curve that always stays below it). Add a line for FICA and watch it flatten once wages cross the Social Security wage base.

🔗 Where this is heading — taxes are the silent return killer

Taxes are why account type matters so much (Week 2): a Roth grows and withdraws tax-free, a traditional 401(k)/IRA defers tax, and a taxable brokerage pays capital-gains tax on realized gains. In the investing weeks (5–6) you'll see that minimizing taxable churn — buying low-cost index funds and holding for the long term — is one of the highest-return "free lunches" available, precisely because it keeps gains unrealized and untaxed while they compound. And in Week 9's rent-vs-buy analysis, the tax treatment of mortgage interest and capital gains on a home changes the NPV directly. Tax literacy isn't a side topic — it's a multiplier on every other decision in this course.

Q1. A raise pushes the last few thousand dollars of your income from the 12% bracket into the 22% bracket. What happens to your take-home pay?
A. It drops, because all your income is now taxed at 22%
B. It rises; only the dollars inside the 22% band are taxed at 22%
C. It stays exactly the same
D. It drops only if you're married
Show answer

B. Brackets are marginal — only the income within a bracket is taxed at that rate. A raise is always net positive; you never lose take-home by crossing a bracket line.

Q2. Your effective tax rate compared with your marginal rate is…
A. always higher
B. always equal
C. always lower (a blended average across all brackets)
D. unrelated to brackets
Show answer

C. Lower brackets tax earlier dollars at smaller rates, so the blended (effective) rate is always below the top (marginal) rate you reach.

Q3. The employee share of FICA, as of 2026, is approximately…
A. 7.65% (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare)
B. 15.3% on all wages
C. 22% on all wages
D. 1.45% total
Show answer

A. The employee pays 7.65%; the employer matches it (≈15.3% total). Social Security (6.2%) stops at the wage base; Medicare (1.45%) has no cap. Verify rates with the SSA/IRS.

Q4. You buy a stock, then sell it 8 months later at a profit. That gain is taxed as…
A. a long-term gain at 0/15/20%
B. tax-free because it's under a year
C. short-term — at your ordinary income rate
D. only subject to FICA
Show answer

C. Held one year or less = short-term, taxed as ordinary income (up to 37%). You must hold more than a year to get the preferential long-term 0/15/20% rates.

Q5. Why is sales tax often called regressive?
A. Because the rate falls as you spend more
B. Because it consumes a larger share of a lower earner's income (they spend a higher fraction of what they earn)
C. Because only the wealthy pay it
D. Because it is deductible for everyone
Show answer

B. The same flat rate hits a bigger share of income for those who spend most of what they earn, so lower earners bear a relatively heavier burden.

Q6. The difference between a tax deduction and a tax credit is…
A. They are two words for the same thing
B. A deduction lowers tax owed dollar-for-dollar; a credit lowers income
C. A deduction lowers taxable income (worth your marginal rate); a credit lowers tax owed dollar-for-dollar
D. Only businesses can use credits
Show answer

C. A $1,000 deduction saves you (rate × $1,000); a $1,000 credit saves the full $1,000 of tax. Credits are generally far more valuable per dollar.

Q7. Tana, single, has $40,000 of taxable income (after the standard deduction). Using the 2026 single brackets (10% to $12,400, 12% to $50,400), her federal income tax is closest to…
A. $4,550
B. $4,800
C. $8,800 (40,000 × 22%)
D. $1,240
Show answer

A. 10% × $12,400 = $1,240, plus 12% × ($40,000 − $12,400 = $27,600) = $3,312 → total ≈ $4,552. Applying a single flat 22% (C) is the classic marginal-rate mistake.

✓ What you learned

  • Your marginal rate (next-dollar rate) vs your effective rate (blended average) — and why a raise never lowers take-home pay.
  • The paycheck layers: federal income tax, FICA (6.2% Social Security up to the wage base + 1.45% Medicare), and state/local income tax.
  • Sales tax as a regressive consumption tax, and capital gains — short-term (ordinary rates) vs long-term (0/15/20%), driven by the one-year holding period.
  • The filing skeleton: gross → AGI → minus deduction → taxable income → brackets → minus credits → tax owed.
  • That every figure is inflation-indexed and dated "as of 2026" — always verify current numbers with the IRS/SSA.
