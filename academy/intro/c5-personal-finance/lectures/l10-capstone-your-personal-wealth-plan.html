Capstone: Your Personal Wealth Plan

Ten weeks of pieces — budget, projection, tax estimate, rent-vs-buy — snap into one document you can actually act on. This is where you build it.

⚠ Educational only — not advice This capstone is for education, not financial, tax, or investment advice. Every dollar figure is illustrative and dated ("as of 2026"); contribution limits, brackets, the standard deduction, and the Social Security wage base change every year. Verify all current numbers with the IRS (irs.gov) and SSA (ssa.gov), and consult a qualified professional, before acting on anything. The author could not auto-verify the 2026 figures from official sources at build time — treat them as starting points to re-confirm.

  • Order your money flows correctly: capture the match, fill tax-advantaged space, then overflow to taxable.
  • Connect a monthly budget surplus to a decades-long projection using compounding and the Rule of 72.

10.1From Ten Lectures to One Plan

You have spent nine weeks collecting tools. The capstone is not new theory — it is the assembly step. A personal wealth plan is just four documents that talk to each other:

  • A budget (Week 3) — where the money goes each month and, crucially, the surplus it produces.
  • A tax estimate (Weeks 1, 4) — what you actually keep after federal income tax, FICA, and capital gains.
  • A growth projection (Weeks 1, 5–6) — what that surplus, invested in low-cost index funds at a sensible allocation, compounds into.
  • A big-decision module (Weeks 7–9) — concentration vs diversification, fee awareness, and a rent-vs-buy NPV check for the largest choices.

The whole point is the links between them. Your budget surplus is the input to your projection. Your tax bracket decides whether a dollar belongs in a Roth or a traditional account. Your rent-vs-buy decision changes the surplus that feeds everything else. A plan is a system, not a stack of spreadsheets.

10.2Pillar 1 & 2 — Budget and Tax Estimate

The plan starts with a cash-flow budget (Week 3): list take-home income, fixed costs, variable costs, and what's left. The single most important output is the monthly surplus — the dollars you can route into savings and investments.

But "take-home" hides the tax layer, so Pillar 2 is a tax estimate built from Weeks 1 and 4. Recall the order of operations: gross income → adjustments → standard deduction → taxable income → tax → credits. The figures you'll plug in, as of 2026 — verify against current IRS/SSA tables:

Key 2026 planning figures (illustrative; verify at irs.gov / ssa.gov — all are inflation-indexed and change yearly). Sources: IRS annual inflation adjustments; SSA contribution & benefit base.

Standard deduction (single / MFJ)≈ $16,100 / $32,200
FICA (employee): Social Security + Medicare6.2% (to wage base) + 1.45% = 7.65%

Federal ordinary-income tax is marginal: each bracket's rate applies only to the income inside it, so your effective rate (total tax ÷ total income) is always below your top marginal rate. Capital gains on assets held over a year get the preferential 0/15/20% schedule instead of ordinary rates — which is why where you hold an asset matters as much as what you hold.

Common mistakeBuilding a projection on gross income. You can only invest what survives taxes. A $90k salary might leave well under $70k after federal income tax, FICA (7.65% on the first dollar), and any state tax — and only the surplus after living costs is investable. Always project from net-of-tax, net-of-spending dollars, and label every dollar figure "as of 2026 — verify."

10.3Pillar 3 — The Growth Projection

The projection turns a steady monthly surplus into a future balance using compounding. For a one-time principal the formula is FV = P(1 + r/m)mn; for a recurring contribution it's the future value of an annuity:

FV = C · [ (1 + r)n − 1 ] / rC = contribution per period, r = return per period, n = number of periods. Combine with FV = P(1+r)n if you also have a starting balance P.

You don't always need the full formula. The Rule of 72 estimates doubling time: divide 72 by the annual percentage return. At a 7.2% real return, money doubles roughly every 10 years — three doublings in 30 years is an 8× multiple before you add a single new dollar.

doubling years ≈ 72 / (rate in %)e.g. 72 / 7.2 ≈ 10 years per double. A back-of-envelope sanity check for any projection.

The two levers that dominate the outcome are your savings rate and your fees. From Week 6: a broad index fund might cost 0.03–0.10%/yr while an active fund charges 0.5–1.0%/yr, and SPIVA-style evidence shows the large majority of active U.S. equity funds trail their benchmark net of fees over 10–15 years. A 1% annual fee doesn't sound like much — but compounded over 30 years it can quietly consume a fifth or more of your final wealth.

AnalogyA projection is a weather forecast, not a clock. A clock tells you exactly what time it is; a forecast gives a likely range that gets fuzzier the further out you look. Quote your projection as a band ("roughly $0.8M–$1.4M at 5–8% real"), not a single confident number — and re-run it yearly with real data, the way a forecaster updates with each new reading.

10.4Pillar 4 — The Big-Decision Module

Most months, the plan runs on autopilot. The leverage is in a handful of large, infrequent decisions — and you now have the tools to handle them.

Rent vs Buy (Week 9)

Don't compare a mortgage payment to rent. Compare the net present value of each path, counting the opportunity cost of the cash you tie up. A down payment that could have grown in index funds is a real cost of buying; rent buys flexibility. Discount future cash flows back to today:

NPV = Σ CFt / (1 + r)tr = your discount rate (often your expected investment return). The option with the higher NPV — or lower net cost — wins on the numbers; lifestyle still gets a vote.

Concentration, fees & equity (Weeks 7–8)

Weeks 7–8 are warnings to carry into the plan. A focused or startup-heavy position (illiquid equity, options/RSUs that vest over years, a single employer holding most of your net worth) can pay off — but it concentrates risk in the exact place your paycheck already comes from. And hedge-fund "2 and 20" (2% of assets plus 20% of profits), leverage, and illiquidity stack fees and risk against you. The plan's job is to keep the boring core — diversified, low-cost, liquid — large enough that any concentrated bet can fail without sinking you.

Note — illiquidity is a cost, not a free upgradeStartup equity, RSUs before vesting, and hedge-fund lock-ups all share one feature: you can't spend them on the day you need cash. Treat illiquid or unvested holdings at a discount in your plan, never at headline value, and never let them substitute for an emergency fund.

10.5The Capstone Deliverable — "Build Your Personal Financial Model"

Your final project is to produce one model that links all four pillars. It can be a Python notebook (preferred) or a spreadsheet. Here is exactly what to submit:

  1. Budget sheet — monthly take-home, fixed + variable costs, and the computed surplus.
  2. Tax estimate — taxable income after the standard deduction, federal income tax by bracket, FICA, and your effective rate. Label every figure "as of 2026 — verify."
  3. Funding order — a stated priority list: emergency fund → full employer match → tax-advantaged space (IRA/Roth/401(k) within limits) → taxable brokerage overflow.
  4. Growth projection — the surplus invested at a chosen allocation, shown for at least three return assumptions (e.g. 4% / 6% / 8% real) over your horizon, with a Rule-of-72 sanity check.
  5. One big-decision analysis — a rent-vs-buy NPV (or an equivalent concentration/fee decision) with the discount rate and opportunity cost stated.
  6. A one-paragraph stress test + a half-page written summary — what happens if returns are 2% lower or your savings rate drops; then your plan in plain English.

Grading rewards linked, sourced, and honestly-hedged work: the surplus from (1) must actually flow into (4); every tax figure in (2) carries a "verify with IRS/SSA" note; the projection is a range, not a single number.

✦ Worked Example — Priya assembles her plan

Priya, single, earns $90,000 in wages, takes the standard deduction, and has a 401(k) with a 4% employer match. Walk the four pillars (figures as of 2026 — verify).

  1. Tax estimate (Pillar 2): taxable income ≈ $90,000 − $16,100 standard deduction = $73,900. FICA = 7.65% × $90,000 ≈ $6,885 (she's below the ~$184,500 wage base). After federal income tax + FICA (and ignoring state tax here), take-home lands roughly in the $66k–$68k range — illustrative, verify with current brackets.
  2. Budget (Pillar 1): living costs of ~$45k/yr leave a surplus of about $21k/yr ≈ $1,750/mo to deploy.
  3. Funding order: she first contributes enough to her 401(k) to capture the full 4% match (free money — Week 2), then fills a Roth IRA up to ≈ $7,500, then routes the rest back into the 401(k) toward the ≈ $24,500 deferral limit, with any overflow to a taxable brokerage.
  4. Projection (Pillar 3): investing ~$1,750/mo for 30 years at a 6% real return → FV = C·[(1+r)n−1]/r with monthly r = 0.5%, n = 360 → roughly $1.7M (illustrative). Rule-of-72 check: at 7.2% money doubles ~every 10 yrs, so the order of magnitude is sane. She reports it as a band, ~$1.2M–$2.4M at 4–8%.
  5. Big decision (Pillar 4): rent vs buy — she discounts both paths' cash flows at her 6% expected return; the down payment's opportunity cost makes renting+investing competitive in her city, so she rents for now and revisits annually.
  6. Stress test: at a 4% return and a job gap that cuts saving for a year, the projection drops toward the bottom of her band — survivable because her core is liquid and diversified. Plan stands.

🛠️ Hands-On: wire the four pillars into one model

Reuse the finance-env you built in Week 1 (pip install numpy pandas matplotlib yfinance — see Week 1 if you skipped it). This single script links budget → tax → projection and prints a return-assumption band. A spreadsheet works as backup; the logic is identical.

# wealth_plan.py — Lecture 10 capstone (all figures "as of 2026" — VERIFY w/ IRS/SSA)
import numpy as np

# --- Pillar 2: rough tax estimate (single, std deduction) ---
gross      = 90_000
std_ded    = 16_100          # as of 2026 — verify at irs.gov
fica       = 0.0765 * min(gross, 184_500)   # SS to wage base + Medicare
brackets   = [(0,.10),(12_400,.12),(50_400,.22)]   # illustrative 2026 single — verify
taxable    = max(0, gross - std_ded)
inc_tax = 0
for i,(lo,rate) in enumerate(brackets):
    hi = brackets[i+1][0] if i+1 < len(brackets) else taxable
    if taxable > lo:
        inc_tax += (min(taxable, hi) - lo) * rate
take_home  = gross - inc_tax - fica
print(f"take-home ~ ${take_home:,.0f}  (effective tax ~{(inc_tax+fica)/gross:.1%})")

# --- Pillar 1: budget surplus ---
living     = 45_000
surplus_mo = (take_home - living) / 12
print(f"monthly surplus ~ ${surplus_mo:,.0f}")

# --- Pillar 3: projection as a BAND, not one number ---
n = 360                                  # 30 years, monthly
for annual in (0.04, 0.06, 0.08):
    r  = annual / 12
    fv = surplus_mo * ((1+r)**n - 1) / r # future value of an annuity
    print(f"  @ {annual:.0%}: ~${fv:,.0f}")
print("Rule of 72 @7.2%: doubles ~every", round(72/7.2), "years")

Extension: add a fee term (subtract 0.01 from each annual return to model a 1% active fee) and watch the band shrink — that's the Week-6 fee drag made concrete. Then pull a real index ETF's history with yfinance to pick a defensible return range instead of guessing.

🔗 Beyond the course

A living plan is reviewed, not framed. Set a yearly ritual: re-pull the IRS/SSA limits (they re-index every year), re-run the projection with your real account balances, and re-test one big decision. From here the natural next steps are Monte-Carlo retirement modeling (simulate thousands of return paths instead of three fixed lines), tax-loss harvesting and asset location across account types, and — if you head toward the Quant or Computational Physics tracks — the same compounding, discounting, and risk-vs-return math reappears as discounted cash flow, stochastic processes, and portfolio optimization. The vocabulary you built here travels.

✓ What you learned

  • A wealth plan is one linked system of four pillars: budget, tax estimate, growth projection, and a big-decision module.
  • Project from net-of-tax, net-of-spending surplus — never gross income — using the annuity FV formula and the Rule of 72 as a check.
  • The right funding order: emergency fund → full match → tax-advantaged space (IRA/Roth/401(k), limits as of 2026 — verify) → taxable overflow.
  • Big decisions use NPV and opportunity cost (rent-vs-buy), keep a large low-cost diversified core, and discount illiquid/concentrated holdings (startup equity, RSUs, "2 and 20").
  • Report projections as a range, stress-test the assumptions, and label every dollar figure "as of 2026 — verify with IRS/SSA."
