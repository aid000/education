Investing I — Compounding, Index Funds & Allocation

You don't have to pick winners to build wealth — you can quietly own the whole market for almost nothing and let compounding do the work.

⚠ Educational only — not advice This lecture is for education only — it is not financial, tax, or investment advice. All figures are illustrative and dated. Tax and contribution numbers change every year; verify current figures with the IRS and SSA, and consult a qualified professional before acting.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Use the compound-interest formula and the Rule of 72 to estimate long-run growth.
  • Explain what an index fund is and why owning the total market (U.S. + international) beats stock-picking for most people.
  • Define diversification and show how it lowers risk without lowering expected return.
  • Build a simple asset allocation (stocks vs bonds, domestic vs international) appropriate to a time horizon.
  • Read an expense ratio and compute how much a fee quietly costs you over decades.

5.1Compounding: the Engine

Investing works because returns earn returns. Money left to grow follows the compound-interest formula, where P is the starting amount, r the annual return, and n the number of years:

A = P · (1 + r)nA = final amount. Each year multiplies by (1 + r), so growth is exponential, not linear.

The exponent is what makes this feel like magic late in life: the curve barely lifts off for a decade, then bends sharply upward. A fast mental shortcut is the Rule of 72 — the number of years to double your money:

years to double ≈ 72 / (annual return %)At 8% a year, money doubles in ≈ 72 / 8 = 9 years. At 6%, ≈ 12 years.
Analogy Compounding is a snowball rolling downhill. At the top it picks up almost nothing — a thin dusting per turn. But each turn the ball is bigger, so it grabs more snow per turn. By the bottom it's enormous, and that final size came mostly from the last few rotations. Time on the hill matters far more than how hard you push at the start.

5.2What an Index Fund Actually Is

An index fund is a single fund that buys a tiny slice of every company in a list (an "index") instead of trying to guess which few will win. A total U.S. stock market index fund holds thousands of American companies, weighted by size; a total international fund does the same for the rest of the world.

Because no human is choosing stocks, the fund is cheap to run, rarely trades (so it triggers fewer taxes), and simply earns whatever the market earns — by definition, the average. That sounds modest until you realize the average has beaten the large majority of professional stock-pickers over long periods (more on that in Week 6).

Common mistake "Index investing = boring = low return." Owning the whole market is not playing it safe in the return sense — historically broad stock indexes have delivered strong long-run growth. What's "boring" is the behavior: no tinkering, no headlines, no guessing. Confusing low cost and low activity with low return is one of the most expensive mistakes new investors make.

5.3Diversification — the One Free Lunch

Diversification means spreading money across many holdings so that no single failure can sink you. A single stock can go to zero; the whole market essentially never does. Economists call this "the only free lunch in finance" because it lowers your risk (the bumpiness of your returns) without lowering your expected return.

The reason it works: different companies and countries don't all move together. When one zigs, another zags, and the zigzags partly cancel. Mathematically, the variance of a blended portfolio depends on how correlated the parts are — less-than-perfectly-correlated assets blend to a smoother ride than any one alone.

5.4Asset Allocation: the Decision That Matters Most

Your asset allocation is the mix of broad asset classes you hold — chiefly stocks (growth, but bumpy) versus bonds (steadier, lower expected return), and within stocks, domestic vs international. Research consistently finds that this top-level mix explains the bulk of how a diversified portfolio behaves over time — far more than which specific funds you pick.

The main input is your time horizon. Money you won't touch for 30 years can ride out crashes, so it can hold mostly stocks. Money you need in 3 years should lean heavily on bonds/cash so a downturn doesn't force you to sell low. A common (illustrative, not prescriptive) starting heuristic is to hold roughly your age in bonds — younger investors stock-heavy, older investors bond-heavier.

portfolio = (stock % × stock return) + (bond % × bond return)A 80/20 portfolio's expected return and risk are a weighted blend of its parts. Shift the weights to shift the risk.
Analogy Asset allocation is choosing the vehicle for the trip; fund-picking is choosing its paint color. A sports car (100% stocks) is fast but tosses you around in turns; a sedan (60/40) is calmer. For a long highway trip you'll happily take the fast car's bumps; for a short errand you want the calm one. Pick the vehicle for the journey's length — the paint barely matters.

5.5Expense Ratios — the Fee That Compounds Against You

An expense ratio is the percentage of your money a fund charges every year to run itself, skimmed automatically. A broad index fund might charge 0.03%–0.10%; an actively managed fund often charges 0.5%–1.0% or more. It sounds like a rounding error. It is not.

Because the fee is taken every year, it compounds against you exactly the way your returns compound for you. A 1% annual fee doesn't cost you 1% of your final balance — over decades it can quietly eat a quarter or more of your total growth.

net return ≈ market return − expense ratioIf the market returns 7% and the fund charges 1%, you keep ~6%. That 1% gap, compounded for 30+ years, is enormous.

5.6Putting It in the Right Account

Index funds are what you buy; the account is where you hold them (covered in Week 2). Tax-advantaged accounts — a Roth IRA, traditional IRA, or workplace 401(k) — let the same index fund compound with less tax drag. As a reminder of the contribution ceilings:

⚠ Dated figures — verify with IRS As of 2025 (verify the current 2026 figures at irs.gov): the IRA / Roth IRA contribution limit was $7,000 (plus a $1,000 catch-up at age 50+), and the 401(k) employee deferral limit was $23,500. Roth IRA eligibility also phases out above certain incomes. Long-term capital-gains rates on a taxable brokerage account are 0% / 15% / 20% depending on income. These numbers change yearly — confirm against current IRS tables before relying on them.

The practical order most educators suggest (not advice): capture any 401(k) employer match first (free money), then fund a Roth/IRA, then a taxable brokerage — holding the same low-cost, diversified index funds throughout.

✦ Worked Example — what a 1% fee really costs

You invest $10,000 for 30 years. The market returns 7% per year. Compare a low-cost index fund (0.04% fee → keep 6.96%) with an active fund (1.0% fee → keep 6.0%). How much does the fee cost?

  1. Low-cost fund: A = 10,000 × (1.0696)30. Since 1.069630 ≈ 7.5, that's ≈ $75,000.
  2. Active fund: A = 10,000 × (1.06)30. Since 1.0630 ≈ 5.74, that's ≈ $57,400.
  3. The gap: 75,000 − 57,400 ≈ $17,600 — roughly a quarter of the low-cost balance — lost to a fee that looked like "just 1%."
  4. Sanity check with Rule of 72: at 7% money doubles in ≈ 72/7 ≈ 10.3 years, so ~30 years is almost 3 doublings (×8). At 6% it doubles in ≈ 12 years — only ~2.5 doublings. Fewer doublings = a smaller pile.
  5. Takeaway: you cannot control the market's return, but you can control your fee — and over decades the fee is one of the few levers that reliably moves the outcome.

Figures are illustrative; 7% is a rough long-run stock assumption, not a guarantee.

🛠️ Hands-On: see fees and diversification with real data

Reminder: this uses the Python + numpy / pandas / matplotlib (and yfinance) stack you installed in Week 1 — no need to reinstall. (Google Sheets works as a no-code backup: build the same fee table with =10000*(1+r)^n.)

# investing_i.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# --- 1. Fee drag: same market, different expense ratios ---
P, years, market = 10_000, 30, 0.07
for fee in [0.0004, 0.005, 0.010]:          # 0.04%, 0.5%, 1.0%
    net = market - fee
    final = P * (1 + net) ** years
    print(f"fee {fee*100:5.2f}%  ->  ${final:,.0f}")

# --- 2. Rule of 72 check ---
for r in [0.04, 0.06, 0.08]:
    print(f"{r*100:.0f}% return doubles in ~{72/(r*100):.1f} years")

# --- 3. Diversification: blend two assets (needs internet) ---
import yfinance as yf
data = yf.download(["VTI", "VXUS"], period="3y")["Close"]   # US + international
rets = data.pct_change().dropna()
blend = 0.7 * rets["VTI"] + 0.3 * rets["VXUS"]              # 70/30 mix
print(rets.std() * np.sqrt(252))                            # annualized risk of each
print("blend risk:", blend.std() * np.sqrt(252))            # usually lower than the riskier one

Predict before you run: will the 70/30 blend's volatility be the simple average of the two parts, or lower? (Hint: §5.3 — it's lower unless the two move in perfect lockstep.) Then change the weights to 100/0 and watch risk rise.

Q1. By the Rule of 72, money earning about 9% per year doubles in roughly…
A. 4 years
B. 8 years
C. 18 years
D. 30 years
Show answer

B. 72 / 9 = 8 years. The Rule of 72 is a quick mental estimate of the doubling time for a given annual return.

Q2. A "total U.S. stock market" index fund mainly aims to…
A. pick the 10 best-performing stocks each year
B. hold only safe, dividend-paying blue chips
C. own a slice of essentially every U.S. company, weighted by size, and earn the market average
D. time the market by moving to cash before crashes
Show answer

C. An index fund tracks a whole list of companies rather than picking winners, which keeps costs low and simply captures the market's return.

Q3. Diversification is called "the only free lunch in finance" because it can…
A. guarantee you never lose money
B. reduce risk without reducing expected return
C. double your return with no downside
D. eliminate all fees
Show answer

B. Blending less-than-perfectly-correlated assets smooths the ride (lowers risk) while the expected return stays the weighted average — a genuine free improvement. It does not remove losses or fees.

Q4. Which factor explains the most about how a diversified portfolio behaves over time?
A. which specific funds you picked
B. the day of the week you buy
C. your broker's logo
D. your overall asset allocation (stocks vs bonds, domestic vs international)
Show answer

D. Top-level allocation drives most of a diversified portfolio's long-run behavior — far more than fund selection or timing.

Q5. An expense ratio of 1.0% means that each year the fund…
A. charges you 1% of your invested assets, deducted automatically
B. guarantees a 1% return
C. charges 1% only when you sell
D. pays you a 1% bonus
Show answer

A. It's an annual fee skimmed from assets every year — so it compounds against you, unlike a one-time sales charge.

Q6. Two funds track the same market; one charges 0.04% and the other 1.0%. Over 30 years the cheaper fund will most likely…
A. end with a slightly lower balance
B. match the expensive fund exactly
C. end meaningfully ahead, because the fee gap compounds for decades
D. only matter for amounts over $1 million
Show answer

C. The ~0.96% fee difference compounds every year; over 30 years it can cost roughly a quarter of the ending balance (see the worked example).

✓ What you learned

  • Compounding via A = P(1+r)ⁿ, and the Rule of 72 for doubling time — time on the hill matters most.
  • An index fund owns the whole market (U.S. + international) cheaply and earns the average, which beats most pickers.
  • Diversification lowers risk without lowering expected return — the one free lunch.
  • Asset allocation (stocks vs bonds, domestic vs international) set by your time horizon drives most of your outcome.
  • Expense ratios compound against you — a 1% fee can quietly eat a quarter of your long-run growth.
