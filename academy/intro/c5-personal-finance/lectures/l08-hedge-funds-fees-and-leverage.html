Hedge Funds, Fees & Leverage

The most expensive, exclusive corner of investing — and why, for almost everyone, the boring index fund quietly wins.

⚠ Educational only — not advice This lecture is for education only — not financial, tax, or investment advice. Figures are illustrative and dated; verify current numbers with the IRS / SSA and a qualified professional before acting. Hedge funds, leverage, and private investments carry real risk of large or total loss.

  • Describe the main strategy families — long/short, global macro, and quantitative — in plain terms.

8.1What a Hedge Fund Actually Is

A hedge fund is a privately pooled investment partnership that takes money from a limited set of wealthy investors and trades it with very few restrictions. Unlike a public mutual fund or an index ETF, a hedge fund can short-sell, use leverage, hold illiquid private assets, and trade derivatives — tools the typical retail fund is barred from using freely.

The name is historical. The first such fund (Alfred Winslow Jones, 1949) literally hedged: it bought stocks it liked and shorted stocks it disliked, aiming to make money whether the market rose or fell. Today "hedge fund" is a legal-and-marketing label far more than a description of strategy — many modern funds barely hedge at all.

The defining feature is who is allowed in. To accept this freedom, U.S. funds rely on private-offering exemptions, so investors must generally be accredited investors or qualified purchasers — high income or high net worth (specific thresholds set by the SEC; as of 2026 — verify against current SEC rules). Minimum investments are often $250k–$1M+, and your money is frequently locked up for a year or more with limited windows to withdraw.

8.2What They Do: Strategy Families

"Hedge fund" covers wildly different businesses. A few of the big families:

  • Long/short equity. Buy (go long) undervalued stocks, short overvalued ones. If the manager's relative picks are right, the fund can profit even in a flat market.
  • Global macro. Bet on big-picture moves in currencies, rates, commodities, and indices based on macroeconomic views (the famous "broke the Bank of England" trade was macro).
  • Event-driven. Trade around mergers, bankruptcies, and spin-offs — e.g. merger arbitrage capturing the gap between a takeover price and the current price.
  • Quantitative ("quant"). Use statistical models, signals, and automation to trade — often thousands of small positions held briefly. This is where a computational-physics or math background actually shows up on a trading desk.

The quant idea in one sentence

A quant fund hunts for a small, repeatable statistical edge — a pattern that wins slightly more than half the time — and harvests it across enormous numbers of trades so the law of large numbers turns a tiny per-trade advantage into a steady aggregate return. The hard parts are finding edges that survive trading costs, that other firms haven't already arbitraged away, and that don't quietly stop working.

Analogy A quant edge is like a very slightly weighted coin that lands heads 51% of the time. Flip it once and you learn nothing. Flip it ten million times with disciplined bet-sizing and that 1% tilt compounds into reliable profit. The whole business is (a) finding genuinely weighted coins, (b) before everyone else, and (c) sizing bets so a streak of tails never bankrupts you.

8.3The Fees: "2 and 20"

The classic hedge-fund fee is "2 and 20": a 2% management fee on assets every year (charged win or lose) plus a 20% performance fee on profits. Fees have drifted lower at many funds since the 2010s, but 2-and-20 remains the reference point.

annual fee ≈ 2% × assets  +  20% × (profit above high-water mark)The 2% is paid even in a losing year; the 20% applies only to new profits.

A high-water mark protects the investor a little: the 20% performance fee only applies to gains above the highest value the fund has previously reached. If the fund loses money, it must climb back over the old peak before charging performance fees again — so you aren't charged twice for recovering the same dollar.

Why this matters so much: fees compound against you exactly the way returns compound for you (recall the Rule of 72 and compounding from Weeks 1 and 5). A 2% management fee alone is roughly 50–100× the expense ratio of a broad index fund (often 0.03–0.10%/yr). The 20% performance fee then skims the best years — the very years that drive long-run compounding.

Common mistake People compare hedge funds on gross (pre-fee) returns. What you actually keep is the net return, after the 2% drag, the 20% skim, trading costs, and (in a taxable account) often short-term capital-gains taxes at ordinary income rates rather than the lower long-term rates (0/15/20% brackets, as of 2026 — verify with the IRS). A fund can look brilliant gross and mediocre net.

8.4Leverage: Amplifying Both Directions

Leverage means investing with borrowed money so your exposure exceeds your own capital. If you put up $1 and borrow $2, you control $3 of assets — that's 3× leverage. Every percent the assets move now moves your equity by about three percent.

return on equity ≈ L × (asset return) − (L − 1) × (borrow cost)L = leverage ratio (total assets ÷ your equity). Gains and losses both scale with L.

Leverage is symmetric in a brutal way. At 3× leverage, a +10% asset move makes your equity jump ~+30%; a −10% move drops it ~−30%; and a −33% move wipes you out entirely. Worse, lenders demand collateral, so a falling price can trigger a margin call — forcing you to sell at the worst moment, which pushes prices down further. Leverage doesn't just magnify losses; it can convert a survivable drawdown into a permanent, total one.

8.5Why Access and Fees Matter — and Index Funds Usually Win

Put the pieces together. A hedge fund must clear a very high bar just to break even for you: it has to beat a cheap index fund by enough to cover the 2% management fee, the 20% performance skim, its trading costs, and the risk it takes with leverage and illiquidity. That's a lot of alpha to manufacture every year, forever.

The broad, repeated evidence (the SPIVA-style scorecards that track active vs. index performance) is that most active managers underperform a comparable low-cost index over long horizons, net of fees — and the share that lags grows the longer you measure. Some hedge funds are genuinely exceptional; the trouble is that you usually can't identify them in advance, the best are often closed to new money, and survivorship bias flatters the reported averages (failed funds quietly disappear from the data).

Add the access problem: even if a brilliant fund exists, accreditation rules, multi-hundred-thousand-dollar minimums, and lock-ups put it out of reach for almost everyone — and tie up money you might need. For the ordinary long-term investor, the lesson from Weeks 5–6 holds firmly: a diversified, low-cost index portfolio is hard to beat after fees.

Analogy Hiring a hedge fund is like betting on a star athlete to beat the field while spotting the field a huge head start — the fees. A few stars overcome it; most don't. The index fund just quietly runs the average pace with almost no entry cost, and over a 30-year race that thrifty steadiness usually finishes ahead of the expensive sprinters.
✦ Worked Example — what "2 and 20" really costs you

You invest $1,000,000. In one year the fund earns a 10% gross return (before fees). Fee terms: 2% management on assets, 20% performance on profit. Compare your net return to a 10% index fund charging 0.05%.

  1. Gross profit: 10% × $1,000,000 = $100,000, so gross assets are $1,100,000.
  2. Management fee (2%): 2% × $1,000,000 = $20,000, charged regardless of performance.
  3. Performance fee (20%): charged on profit. After the $20k management fee, net profit is $80,000, so 20% × $80,000 = $16,000. (Conventions vary; some charge on gross profit — verify the actual agreement.)
  4. Total fees: $20,000 + $16,000 = $36,000. Your net profit is $100,000 − $36,000 = $64,000 → a 6.4% net return. The manager took 36% of your gains.
  5. Index comparison: the same 10% gross at a 0.05% fee = $100,000 − $500 = $99,500 (≈ 9.95% net). The fund had to outperform by ~3.6 percentage points just to tie the index after fees.
  6. Now a bad year: suppose the fund loses 10%. You still pay the 2% management fee ($20k) on top of the $100k loss, and the high-water mark means no performance fees until the fund climbs back above its old peak. Fees don't pause when you're losing.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate fees + leverage over time

(Reminder: you set up Python + numpy/pandas/matplotlib + yfinance back in Week 1 — no need to reinstall. Google Sheets works as a backup if you prefer formulas.)

Model two investors over 20 years: one in a cheap index fund, one in a "2 and 20" hedge fund that earns a higher gross return. See whether the higher gross actually wins net of fees — then add leverage and watch volatility (and wipeout risk) explode.

# hedge_vs_index.py — Lecture 8 (illustrative; not advice)
import numpy as np

years = 20
start = 1_000_000

# Index: 8% gross, 0.05% fee
index = start * (1 + 0.08 - 0.0005) ** years

# Hedge fund: 10% GROSS, but 2% mgmt + 20% of profit each year
hf = start
for _ in range(years):
    gross = hf * 0.10
    mgmt  = hf * 0.02
    perf  = max(gross - mgmt, 0) * 0.20      # 20% of profit after mgmt fee
    hf   += gross - mgmt - perf

print(f"Index (8% gross): ${index:,.0f}")
print(f"Hedge (10% gross, 2&20): ${hf:,.0f}")
# Despite a 2-point higher GROSS return, fees can erase the hedge fund's edge.

# --- Leverage: same +/-10% asset moves, viewed at different L ---
for L in [1, 2, 3]:
    up   = L * 0.10
    down = L * -0.10
    print(f"{L}x leverage:  up move -> {up:+.0%},  down move -> {down:+.0%}")
# At 3x, a -33% asset move wipes equity to zero. Leverage cuts both ways.

Stretch goal: pull a real index series with yfinance (e.g. an S&P 500 ETF), apply a 2-and-20 fee each year vs. a 0.05% fee, and plot both ending balances. Then add a random "bad year" and watch the high-water mark delay performance fees on the recovery.

🔗 Where this connects

The illiquidity and access lessons here rhyme with the startup-equity world from Week 7: options and RSUs vest over years and are usually impossible to sell while a company is private — concentrated, illiquid, and high-variance, just like a hedge-fund stake, but with even sharper boom-or-bust outcomes. Both reward you for tying up capital and accepting risk you can't easily unwind. And the fee math you just ran is the same compounding engine from Weeks 1 and 5, simply pointed the wrong way: small annual percentages, left to compound for decades, decide enormous final differences. Q2. A high-water mark means the 20% performance fee applies…
A. to every dollar of assets, every year
B. only to gains above the fund's previous peak value
C. only when the broader market hits a new high
D. only to leveraged positions

B. After a loss, the fund must climb back above its prior peak before performance fees resume — so you aren't charged twice for recovering the same dollar.

Q3. At 3× leverage, roughly how large an adverse move in the underlying asset wipes out your equity?
A. about −10%
B. about −90%
C. about −33%
D. leverage cannot wipe out equity
Show answer

C. With $1 equity controlling $3 of assets, a ~−33% asset move erases your $1. Leverage scales losses as well as gains, and can trigger forced selling (margin calls).

D. Private-offering exemptions limit hedge funds to accredited investors or qualified purchasers, with large minimums and lock-ups. Thresholds are set by the SEC (verify current rules).

  • A hedge fund is a lightly regulated private partnership gated to accredited investors — flexible tools (shorting, leverage, derivatives), high fees, and lock-ups.
  • The main strategy families — long/short, global macro, event-driven, and quant (harvesting a tiny statistical edge across many trades).
  • The "2 and 20" fee structure and the high-water mark, and how fees compound against you the way returns compound for you.
  • Leverage scales gains and losses (ROE ≈ L × asset return − costs), so a −1/L move can wipe out equity.
  • Why access and fees mean most investors do better in a diversified low-cost index portfolio, net of fees.
