The Money Map — Accounts, Taxes & the Power of Compounding

Before you pick a single stock, you need a map of where every dollar can live — and why the dollars you invest early are worth far more than the ones you invest later.

⚠ Disclaimer — read first Educational only — not financial, tax, or investment advice. Every figure here is illustrative and dated. Tax limits, brackets, and wage bases change every year, so verify current numbers with the IRS (irs.gov) and SSA (ssa.gov) — and consult a qualified professional — before acting on anything. Where you see "as of 2025/2026," treat it as a starting point to confirm, not a quote.

  • State the time value of money and why a dollar today beats a dollar tomorrow.
  • Apply the compound-interest formula and the Rule of 72 to estimate doubling time.

1.1Where Dollars Live: The Money Map

Personal finance looks complicated because there are dozens of account names. But every dollar you earn follows one of three paths, and most account types are just different "rooms" along those paths. Your income arrives, taxes are taken out, and what's left is allocated to spend, save, or invest.

The key distinction is save vs invest. Saving is parking cash you may need within a few years (an emergency fund, a car down payment) — safe, liquid, low return. Investing is putting money to work for years or decades in assets like index funds; it carries short-term risk but is where long-term growth comes from. This course is mostly about the invest branch — but only after the spend and save branches are sound.

Analogy Think of your money like water flowing through a house. Spending is water you use and it's gone. Saving is a bucket by the door — easy to grab, but it just sits there. Investing is water you pipe out to a garden: you don't see it for a while, but it grows into something much bigger. The mistake beginners make is leaving everything in the bucket because the garden feels scary.

1.2The Time Value of Money

The single most important idea in finance is the time value of money: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future, because today's dollar can be invested and earn a return in the meantime. If you can earn an annual return r, then a dollar now becomes (1 + r) dollars in a year.

Run that backward and you get the present value of a future dollar — what a payment n years from now is worth to you today:

PV = FV / (1 + r)nFV = future value, r = annual return (discount rate), n = years. Future money is "discounted" back to today.

This is why "$1,000 in 10 years" is not the same as "$1,000 today," and why a return rate is also called a discount rate. Every later topic — investing, rent-vs-buy (Week 9), even why fees matter — is really an argument about the time value of money. The same engine that discounts future dollars backward also grows present dollars forward: that forward growth is compounding.

Note — inflation Time value isn't only about earning returns; it's also that money tends to lose purchasing power over time (inflation). Cash stuffed under a mattress doesn't stay still in real terms — it slowly shrinks. That's a second reason the save branch alone can't build wealth, and why "real" (inflation-adjusted) returns matter more than headline numbers. We'll separate nominal vs real returns in Week 5.

1.3Compound Interest — The Engine

With simple interest you earn a return only on your original principal. With compound interest you earn returns on your principal and on all the returns you've already earned — interest on interest. Over long horizons the difference is enormous. The compound-growth formula is:

FV = P · (1 + r/m)m·nP = principal, r = annual rate, m = compounding periods per year, n = years. With annual compounding (m = 1): FV = P·(1 + r)n.

Notice the exponent. Simple interest grows linearly (a straight line); compound interest grows exponentially (a curve that bends upward and accelerates). Early on they look almost identical — which is exactly why compounding is so easy to underestimate.

The Rule of 72

You rarely need a calculator to estimate compounding. The Rule of 72 is a famous shortcut: divide 72 by the annual percentage return to get the approximate number of years for money to double.

years to double ≈ 72 / r(%)e.g. at 8% per year, money doubles in ~72/8 = 9 years. At 6%, ~12 years. (An approximation of the exact ln(2)/ln(1+r); accurate for everyday rates of a few to ~10%.)

Flip it around and it's just as useful: at 7% returns, money doubles about every 10 years. So $10,000 today becomes ~$20k in 10 years, ~$40k in 20, ~$80k in 30 — three doublings, an 8× gain, with no extra deposits. That last doubling (from $40k to $80k) is bigger than the entire first 20 years combined. The biggest growth happens at the end — which is the whole argument for starting early.

1.4Why Starting Early Wins

Because compounding accelerates with time, the years your money is invested matter more than how many dollars you put in. A small amount invested in your twenties can outgrow a much larger amount invested in your forties, purely because it has more doublings ahead of it. The classic illustration:

  • Early Ellie invests $5,000/year from age 25 to 35 (10 years, $50,000 total), then stops and never adds another dollar.
  • Late Liam waits, then invests $5,000/year from age 35 to 65 (30 years, $150,000 total).

Assuming a steady 7% annual return, by age 65 Ellie — who invested one-third as much — often ends up with roughly the same or more than Liam, despite stopping 30 years earlier. Her early dollars simply had more time to double. (We compute the exact figures in the Hands-On and Worked Example below.)

Common mistake "I'll start investing once I earn more / pay off everything / understand the markets." Waiting is the single costliest beginner error, because each year of delay removes a doubling from the far end of the curve — the most valuable doubling of all. Time in the market beats timing the market. Starting small now almost always beats starting big later. (This is not advice to invest money you can't afford to risk — the emergency fund and high-interest debt come first.)

1.5The Tax Landscape (A Map, Not the Territory)

The "Taxes" box in §1.1 deserves its own preview, because where you hold investments changes how much compounding the tax system lets you keep. You'll study each piece in Weeks 2–4; here's the lay of the land. Every dollar amount below is dated and approximate — confirm against IRS/SSA before relying on it.

Payroll taxes (FICA)

On wages, FICA is withheld for Social Security and Medicare:

  • Social Security: 6.2% of wages, charged only up to an annual wage base (≈ $176,100 as of 2025 — verify the 2026 figure with SSA, it rises most years). Your employer pays a matching 6.2%.
  • Medicare: 1.45% of all wages (no cap), plus an extra 0.9% on high earners above a threshold. Employer matches the 1.45%.

Federal income tax — marginal brackets

Federal income tax is progressive and marginal: rates of 10%, 12%, 22%, 24%, 32%, 35%, and 37% apply to successive slices of income. A common myth is that crossing into a higher bracket taxes all your income at the higher rate — it doesn't; only the slice above each threshold is taxed at that bracket's rate.

Common mistake — "I don't want a raise, it'll push me into a higher bracket" False. Brackets are marginal. If a raise pushes part of your income into the 24% bracket, only the dollars above that threshold are taxed at 24% — every dollar below is still taxed at the lower rates. A raise always leaves you with more after-tax money.

Standard deduction

Before brackets apply, most people subtract the standard deduction (≈ $15,000 single / $30,000 married-filing-jointly, as of 2025 — verify the 2026 figures with the IRS). Only income above the deduction is taxed at all.

Long-term capital gains

Profits on investments held longer than a year get a separate, generally lower set of rates — 0%, 15%, or 20% depending on your taxable income (plus a possible 3.8% net investment income tax for high earners). Short-term gains (held ≤ 1 year) are taxed as ordinary income. This favorable treatment is another reason long-term investing wins.

⚠ Verify these numbers Contribution limits, wage bases, brackets, and deductions are indexed for inflation and change every year. The figures above are labeled "as of 2025" because that is the most recent set confirmed at the time of writing; the 2026 values were not yet verified here. Always pull current numbers from irs.gov and ssa.gov (or a tax professional). Weeks 2–4 use these as worked-example inputs only.
✦ Worked Example — Early Ellie vs Late Liam (the cost of waiting)

Both earn a steady 7% annual return. Ellie invests $5,000/year for 10 years (ages 25–35) then stops; Liam invests $5,000/year for 30 years (ages 35–65). Compare their balances at age 65.

  1. Rule-of-72 intuition first. At 7%, money doubles every ≈ 72/7 ≈ 10.3 years. From age 35 to 65 is ~3 doublings, so Ellie's age-35 pot grows roughly 8× while she sleeps.
  2. Ellie's pot at age 35. Ten yearly $5,000 deposits growing at 7% (a future-value-of-an-annuity) come to ≈ $69,000. She then adds nothing for 30 more years.
  3. Ellie grows untouched to 65. $69,000 × (1.07)30. Since (1.07)30 ≈ 7.6, that's ≈ $525,000. Total invested: $50,000.
  4. Liam's pot at age 65. Thirty yearly $5,000 deposits at 7% (annuity FV) come to ≈ $505,000. Total invested: $150,000.
  5. Takeaway. Ellie invested one-third as much ($50k vs $150k) yet ends up with more (~$525k vs ~$505k), simply because her dollars enjoyed more doublings. Every year of delay is permanently expensive. (Figures rounded for illustration — the exact totals depend on deposit timing; reproduce them in the Hands-On.)

🛠️ Hands-On: compute your own compounding curves

This course's tool is the free Python data stack — NumPy / pandas / matplotlib for the math and charts, plus yfinance to pull real market data in later weeks. (If you'd rather not install anything, a spreadsheet like Google Sheets is a fine backup — every calculation here is one formula.) Week 1 setup: install it once below, then reuse it all term and for the Week-10 capstone.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy / pandas / matplotlib / yfinance (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed (from python.org or your package manager), create an environment and install the stack:

python3 -m venv finance-env
source finance-env/bin/activate       # macOS / Linux
finance-env\Scripts\activate          # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install numpy pandas matplotlib yfinance jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import numpy, pandas, yfinance; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set.

Backup (no install): in Google Sheets, the future value of a steady deposit is =FV(rate, nper, -pmt) and a lump sum is =FV(rate, nper, 0, -pv).

Reproduce Ellie vs Liam and the Rule of 72, then change the numbers to your own situation.

# money_map.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

# --- Rule of 72 vs the exact doubling time ---
r = 0.07
print("Rule of 72 :", 72 / (r*100), "years")          # ~10.3
print("exact      :", np.log(2) / np.log(1 + r), "yr")  # ~10.24

# --- Future value of N yearly deposits, then growth ---
def annuity_fv(pmt, r, years):
    # deposits made at end of each year
    return pmt * ((1 + r)**years - 1) / r

ellie_at_35 = annuity_fv(5000, r, 10)          # 10 deposits, ages 25-35
ellie_at_65 = ellie_at_35 * (1 + r)**30        # grows untouched 30 yrs
liam_at_65  = annuity_fv(5000, r, 30)          # 30 deposits, ages 35-65

print(f"Ellie invested $50,000  -> ${ellie_at_65:,.0f}")
print(f"Liam  invested $150,000 -> ${liam_at_65:,.0f}")
# Ellie ends up ahead despite investing 1/3 as much — that's compounding.

Then plot both balances year-by-year with matplotlib and watch the curves. Try lowering the return to 5% (taxes/fees eat returns — Week 6) and see how much the ending gap shrinks: small rate differences compound into big dollars.

Q1. In the money map, what is the key difference between "saving" and "investing"?
A. Saving earns more than investing over decades
B. Saving is safe, liquid cash for near-term needs; investing puts money to work for long-term growth (with short-term risk)
C. They are two words for the same thing
D. Only investing is taxed
Show answer

B. Saving is low-risk, low-return money you may need soon; investing accepts short-term volatility in exchange for long-term growth. Compounding lives on the invest branch.

Q2. The "time value of money" says that…
A. a dollar today is worth more than a dollar in the future, because it can be invested to earn a return
B. money always loses value
C. all dollars are worth the same regardless of timing
D. future dollars are always worth more than present dollars
Show answer

A. Today's dollar can earn a return (and future dollars are eroded by inflation), so PV = FV/(1+r)ⁿ discounts future money back to today.

Q3. Using the Rule of 72, money earning 6% per year doubles in approximately…
A. 6 years
B. 72 years
C. 12 years
D. 36 years
Show answer

C. 72 / 6 = 12 years. The Rule of 72 estimates doubling time as 72 divided by the percentage rate.

Q4. Compared with simple interest, compound interest grows…
A. more slowly
B. at exactly the same rate forever
C. linearly (a straight line)
D. exponentially — you earn returns on prior returns, so the curve accelerates and the gap widens over time
Show answer

D. Compounding pays interest on interest, so growth is exponential (FV = P(1+r)ⁿ). Simple interest is linear; the two overlap early then diverge.

Q5. A raise pushes part of your income into the 24% federal bracket. How is your income taxed?
A. Only the income above the bracket threshold is taxed at 24%; lower slices keep their lower rates
B. All of your income is now taxed at 24%
C. You take home less than before the raise
D. The raise is tax-free
Show answer

A. U.S. federal brackets are marginal: each rate applies only to the slice of income within its band, so a raise always increases after-tax income.

Q6. Early Ellie invests for 10 years then stops; Late Liam invests three times as many total dollars but starts 10 years later. Same return. The usual result is that…
A. Liam always wins because he invested far more money
B. Ellie can match or beat Liam, because her early dollars had more time (more doublings) to compound
C. they always end exactly equal
D. neither grows without yearly deposits
Show answer

B. Time, not total dollars, dominates. Ellie's early contributions enjoy more compounding periods, so she can finish ahead despite investing one-third as much. Starting early wins.
