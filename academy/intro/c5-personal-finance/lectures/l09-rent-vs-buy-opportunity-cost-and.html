Rent vs Buy — Opportunity Cost & NPV

"Owning beats renting" is a slogan, not a calculation. This lecture turns the question into a model you can actually solve — and shows which three numbers decide the answer.

⚠ Educational only — not adviceThis lecture is for education, not financial, tax, or investment advice. Every dollar figure here is illustrative and dated. Tax rules, mortgage rates, and local prices change constantly — verify current numbers with the IRS / SSA and a qualified professional before making any real decision.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • List the true cost of owning a home — not just the mortgage payment.
  • Explain the opportunity cost of a down payment and why it belongs in the comparison.
  • Account for maintenance, transaction costs, and the value of mobility.
  • Build a rent-vs-buy NPV / break-even model and read its sensitivity.
  • Code a simple year-by-year simulator in Python and find the break-even horizon.

9.1The Payment Is Not the Cost

The single most common rent-vs-buy error is comparing rent to a mortgage payment as if they were the same kind of number. They are not. Rent is (almost) a pure expense. A mortgage payment is part expense and part forced savings — but it also drags along a list of costs renters never pay.

Economists split owning into recoverable costs (money you might get back, like principal you pay down and any appreciation) and unrecoverable costs — money that is simply gone each year, exactly like rent. The honest comparison is rent vs. the unrecoverable cost of owning, not rent vs. the mortgage.

unrecoverable owning cost ≈ property tax + maintenance + insurance + mortgage interest + opportunity cost of equityA common rule of thumb pegs total annual unrecoverable cost near ~5% of the home's value — illustrative only; it varies widely by market.

9.2The Opportunity Cost of a Down Payment

Suppose you put $80,000 down on a $400,000 home. That cash is now locked in walls. The crucial question a beginner forgets: what would that $80,000 have earned otherwise? If a low-cost index fund returns, say, ~6% real over the long run, the down payment carries an opportunity cost of roughly $4,800 in the first year alone — and it compounds.

This is the bridge back to Week 5's compounding. Money invested grows by the same engine whether it sits in a brokerage account or in home equity; the question is only which grows faster, net of all costs. A house can appreciate, but so can the portfolio you gave up to buy it. Ignoring the foregone return is the financial equivalent of leaving a term out of an equation.

opportunity cost (yr 1) = down payment × expected return
= $80,000 × 6% = $4,800 / yr (and growing)Return assumption is illustrative — markets are not guaranteed. The point is that the term is never zero.
AnalogyA down payment is like pulling your best player off the field to sit on the bench "for safety." The player (your capital) is still yours — but while benched they score nothing. The opportunity cost is every goal they would have scored. A house only wins the comparison if it scores more than that benched player would have.

9.3Maintenance, Transaction Costs & Mobility

Three more terms quietly tilt the math toward renting more often than buyers expect.

Maintenance

Roofs, water heaters, and HVAC systems fail on their own schedule, not yours. A widely cited rule budgets ~1% of the home's value per year for upkeep — $4,000/yr on a $400,000 home — averaged over time. As a renter, that bill is the landlord's problem.

Transaction costs

Buying and selling a home is expensive and slow. Closing costs on purchase plus selling costs (agent commissions, transfer taxes, repairs) can together run on the order of ~8–10% of the price. On a $400,000 home that is roughly $32,000–$40,000 that must be earned back through appreciation and saved rent before buying breaks even. This is why short stays almost always favor renting.

Mobility

Renting is liquid: give notice and leave. Owning is illiquid (recall Week 8's discussion of illiquidity in hedge funds and startup equity — the same idea). If a better job, a relationship, or a city change appears, an owner pays a steep cost to move. Mobility has real option value, especially early in a career.

Common mistake"Rent is throwing money away." So is mortgage interest, property tax, insurance, maintenance, and the foregone return on your down payment — none of which builds equity. In the early years of a mortgage, the vast majority of each payment is interest, not principal. Renting buys flexibility and frees capital; whether that is "wasted" depends entirely on the numbers, not the slogan.

9.4NPV & the Break-Even Horizon

The clean way to compare two streams of cash over time is Net Present Value (NPV): discount every future dollar back to today, because a dollar next year is worth less than a dollar now. The discount rate is usually your opportunity-cost return r.

NPV = Σt CFt / (1 + r)tCFt = the net cash flow in year t. Compare the NPV of total renting cost vs. total owning cost over your expected horizon.

Do this for both paths — including, for buying, the proceeds from selling at the end minus all selling costs — and whichever has the lower present-value cost wins. Because transaction costs hit up front and the equity/appreciation payoff arrives only at sale, owning starts behind and catches up over time. The year the two curves cross is the break-even horizon.

The headline result of every serious model: break-even is driven by time horizon, the gap between rent and total owning cost, expected appreciation, and the return on invested savings. Change any of those and the crossover year can swing from 3 years to never.

9.5The Three Numbers That Decide It

Hundreds of inputs, but three dominate the sensitivity:

  • How long you'll stay. The biggest lever. Transaction costs are fixed and large, so they amortize only over many years. Short stay → rent. Long stay → buy gains ground.
  • The rent-to-price ratio. Cheap rent relative to home prices (a low rent ÷ price) strongly favors renting; expensive rent favors buying. The old "price-to-rent ratio" captures this in one number.
  • Your assumed investment return vs. home appreciation. The discount/opportunity rate and the appreciation rate together decide whether benched capital or the house grows faster. Small changes here move break-even by years.

Notice what is not on the list: the emotional pull of "owning your own place." That is a real, valid preference — just keep it separate from the math, so you know exactly what the feeling is costing you.

✦ Worked Example — a 5-year break-even check

A $400,000 home vs. renting a comparable place for $2,000/mo ($24,000/yr). You'd put 20% ($80,000) down. Assume ~6% you could earn investing, ~1% maintenance, ~1.2% property tax, and ~3% home appreciation. Should you buy if you'll stay 5 years? (All figures illustrative — verify locally.)

  1. Up-front + selling costs: ~5% to buy and ~6% to sell ≈ 8–10% of price round-trip ≈ $36,000 of pure friction.
  2. Annual unrecoverable owning cost: maintenance $4,000 + property tax $4,800 + insurance ~$1,500 + mortgage interest (large early on, say ~$15,000 yr 1) + opportunity cost of the $80,000 down (6% ≈ $4,800) ≈ ~$30,000/yr, well above the $24,000 rent.
  3. Appreciation offset: 3% on $400,000 ≈ $12,000/yr of equity gain — real, but partly eaten by the round-trip $36,000 and is uncertain.
  4. 5-year tally (rough): renting's saved cash invested at 6% vs. buying's net equity after selling costs. With these inputs the two land close, with renting often slightly ahead — buying typically needs a longer horizon (or cheaper price / higher appreciation) to clearly win.
  5. Takeaway: at a 5-year horizon with a ~6% opportunity return and modest appreciation, this is roughly a tie — meaning the decision should hinge on mobility and preference, not on a claimed financial slam-dunk. Stay 10+ years and buying pulls ahead.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate the break-even year

Build a year-by-year cash-flow model in Python and find when buying overtakes renting. (Reminder: you set up Python + numpy/pandas/matplotlib back in Week 1 — just activate that environment.)

# rent_vs_buy.py — Lecture 9 (all inputs ILLUSTRATIVE — edit for your city)
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

price      = 400_000
down       = 0.20 * price
rate_mort  = 0.065          # mortgage rate — verify current
r_invest   = 0.06           # opportunity / discount rate
appr       = 0.03           # home appreciation
rent0      = 24_000         # annual rent, grows with inflation
rent_g     = 0.03
maint      = 0.01 * price
tax        = 0.012 * price
ins        = 1_500
buy_cost   = 0.05 * price   # closing
sell_cost  = 0.06           # fraction of sale price

years = np.arange(1, 16)
buy_pv, rent_pv = [], []
for n in years:
    # crude: unrecoverable owning ~ interest+tax+maint+ins; net out sale equity
    interest = rate_mort * (price - down)            # approx, level for simplicity
    annual_own = interest + tax + maint + ins
    own_cash = buy_cost + sum(annual_own/(1+r_invest)**t for t in range(1, n+1))
    sale = price*(1+appr)**n*(1-sell_cost) - (price-down)   # equity at sale
    own_cash -= sale/(1+r_invest)**n
    rent_cash = sum(rent0*(1+rent_g)**(t-1)/(1+r_invest)**t for t in range(1, n+1))
    rent_cash -= down*( (1+r_invest)**n - 1 )/(1+r_invest)**n  # invest the down pmt instead
    buy_pv.append(own_cash); rent_pv.append(rent_cash)

plt.plot(years, rent_pv, label="rent (PV cost)")
plt.plot(years, buy_pv,  label="buy (PV cost)")
plt.xlabel("years held"); plt.ylabel("present-value cost ($)"); plt.legend(); plt.show()
# Find the crossover: first year where buy_pv < rent_pv = your break-even.

Now do sensitivity: re-run with r_invest = 0.04, then 0.08, and watch the break-even year move. Try halving the rent. Which of the three numbers from §9.5 moved the answer most? (Sheets backup: build the same columns — year, owning PV, renting PV — and chart them.)

🔗 Where this connects

NPV and discounting are the same machinery used to value a bond, a business, or a startup's future cash flows — and the opportunity-cost idea is exactly why Week 5–6 argued for low-cost index investing over leaving cash idle. A house is just one asset in your portfolio, with unusual features: it's leveraged (the mortgage), illiquid (Week 8's theme), undiversified (one property, one city), and bundled with a place to live. Treat the "buy" decision as a portfolio decision and the slogans dissolve into numbers you can actually weigh.

✓ What you learned

  • The true cost of owning is the unrecoverable part — interest, tax, insurance, upkeep, opportunity cost — not the whole payment.
  • A down payment carries an opportunity cost: the return that capital would have earned invested elsewhere, compounding over time.
  • Maintenance, transaction costs, and mobility tilt the math toward renting more often than buyers expect.
  • NPV and break-even: discount both paths to today; buying starts behind on up-front costs and catches up at a break-even horizon.
  • Three numbers decide it — time horizon, rent-to-price ratio, and return vs. appreciation — which you simulated in Python.
