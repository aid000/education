Investing II — Risk, Return & Why Active Underperforms

Higher returns come bundled with bigger swings — and the people paid to beat the market mostly don't, once you count their fees.

6.1Risk and Return Are Joined at the Hip

In Investing I you saw compounding and why a low-cost, broadly diversified index fund is a sensible default. This week we look at the other side of the coin: the swings you must tolerate to earn those returns, and why the professionals paid to dodge those swings rarely beat the index after fees.

The central law of investing is simple: expected return is compensation for bearing risk. A U.S. Treasury bill is nearly risk-free, so it pays little. Stocks can drop 30–50% in a bad year, so over the long run they have paid more — a "risk premium" — to convince people to hold them through the scary parts.

expected return ≈ risk-free rate + risk premiumYou don't get paid for taking risk you could have diversified away — only for the risk that remains.

There's a crucial subtlety: you are only rewarded for non-diversifiable risk. The risk that one company blows up can be diluted by holding hundreds of companies, so the market doesn't pay you to concentrate in a single stock — it just pays you for the risk that all stocks move together (recessions, rate shocks). That's the deep reason diversification is "the only free lunch in finance."

AnalogyThink of return as wages and risk as how unpleasant the job is. Pleasant, safe jobs (a T-bill) pay little; jobs with stomach-churning ups and downs (stocks) pay a premium to get anyone to show up. But you only get hazard pay for hazards you can't avoid — nobody pays you extra for refusing to wear the free safety gear (diversification).

6.2Measuring Risk: Volatility & Drawdown

"Risk" is fuzzy, so we measure it two concrete ways.

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns — how widely returns scatter around their average. The broad U.S. stock market has historically had an annual volatility of roughly 15–20% (as of 2026 — varies by period; verify against current data). Volatility scales with the square root of time, which is why a single day looks calm and a single year looks wild:

σannual ≈ σdaily × √252252 ≈ trading days per year. A 1%/day market implies ≈ 1% × 15.9 ≈ 16% per year.

Drawdown is the more visceral measure: how far you are below your previous peak, right now. The maximum drawdown is the worst peak-to-trough fall over a period — the deepest the pain ever got before recovery.

drawdown(t) = (value(t) − peak-so-far) / peak-so-farAlways ≤ 0. The broad market has endured drawdowns worse than −50% (e.g., 2007–09) — historical, verify.
Common mistakeConfusing volatility with permanent loss. For a diversified index, a drawdown is a paper loss that historically recovered if you held on; it only becomes a realized loss if you sell at the bottom. The real danger isn't the drop — it's your reaction to it (see §6.4).

6.3Why Most Active Funds Underperform After Fees

An active fund pays managers to pick stocks and time markets, hoping to beat a benchmark like the S&P 500. A passive index fund just holds the whole market cheaply. The repeated, well-documented finding — published each period in the SPIVA U.S. Scorecard (S&P Dow Jones Indices) — is that the large majority of active U.S. equity funds underperform their benchmark over 10–15 year horizons (commonly on the order of 80–90%+ over 15 years, as of 2026 — verify against the latest SPIVA scorecard, figures shift each report).

This isn't bad luck or dumb managers. It's arithmetic, made famous by William Sharpe's "Arithmetic of Active Management":

  1. Before costs, the average actively managed dollar must earn exactly the market return — because together all investors are the market. For every active winner there is an active loser.
  2. So as a group, active investors earn the market return minus their costs. Passive investors earn the market return minus tiny costs.
  3. Therefore the average active dollar must, after fees, trail the average passive dollar. Not might — must.

The lever is fees. A typical active fund might charge 0.5–1.0%/yr; a broad index fund can charge 0.03–0.10%/yr (illustrative, as of 2026 — check current expense ratios). That gap compounds relentlessly against the active fund. A handful of managers do beat the index in any period — but identifying in advance which ones will keep doing so has proven extremely hard, and past winners tend not to persist.

6.4Behavioral Pitfalls — the Gap You Inflict on Yourself

Even an investor who picks a perfect low-cost index fund can underperform that very fund — because of how they behave. Studies of investor cash flows repeatedly find a "behavior gap": the return investors actually earn lags the return their funds report, because people buy high and sell low. The usual culprits:

  • Performance-chasing. Pouring money into whatever just went up (last year's hot fund, a soaring stock), which is often exactly when it's most expensive.
  • Panic-selling. Bailing out during a drawdown, converting a temporary paper loss into a permanent realized one — and usually missing the recovery.
  • Loss aversion. A loss hurts roughly twice as much as an equal gain feels good, so we make lopsided, fear-driven decisions.
  • Overconfidence & overtrading. Believing we can time the market or pick winners; frequent trading racks up costs and taxes and, on average, lowers returns.
  • Recency bias. Assuming the recent past (a long bull market, or a crash) will simply continue.
AnalogyInvesting is like a bar of soap: the more you handle it, the smaller it gets. Each "smart" intervention — chasing a winner, dodging a dip — usually subtracts return through bad timing, costs, and taxes. The best behavior is mostly boring inaction: automate contributions, then leave it alone.

6.5Putting Risk to Work: the Sharpe Ratio

If two strategies earn the same return, the one with smaller swings is better. The Sharpe ratio captures "return per unit of risk":

Sharpe = (Rportfolio − Rrisk-free) / σportfolioExcess return divided by volatility. Higher is better; it lets you compare a calm and a wild strategy fairly.

This is also why asset allocation (last week's topic) and diversification matter: mixing assets that don't move in lockstep can lower σ without giving up much return, raising the Sharpe ratio. A volatility tax also lurks here — big drawdowns drag down your compounded (geometric) return even when the simple average looks fine, which is why limiting the depth of losses matters as much as chasing the average.

geometric return ≈ arithmetic return − ½σ²The "volatility drag": the wilder the ride, the more your actual compounded growth falls below the simple average.
✦ Worked Example — what a 1% fee really costs

You invest $10,000 for 30 years. The market returns 7%/yr gross. Compare an index fund (0.03% fee → net 6.97%) with an active fund that matches the market gross but charges 1.0% (net 6.0%). Use the compound formula FV = P·(1+r)n.

  1. Index fund: FV = 10,000 × (1.0697)30 ≈ 10,000 × 7.55 ≈ $75,500.
  2. Active fund (same gross, higher fee): FV = 10,000 × (1.06)30 ≈ 10,000 × 5.74 ≈ $57,400.
  3. The fee gap: ≈ $75,500 − $57,400 ≈ $18,100 — roughly a quarter of the index outcome — handed over for a fund that merely matched the market before fees.
  4. And that's the optimistic case. Most active funds don't even match the market gross; SPIVA-style evidence says the majority trail it. So the realistic gap is usually worse, not better.
  5. Takeaway: a fee is a guaranteed, compounding loss; outperformance is a hope. Rule of 72 sanity check: at 7% money doubles in ~72/7 ≈ 10 years, so 30 years ≈ ~3 doublings ≈ ~8×, matching the index figure.

🛠️ Hands-On: measure real volatility & drawdown

Reminder: this uses the same Python + numpy/pandas/matplotlib stack from Week 1 (one-liner if needed: pip install numpy pandas matplotlib yfinance). No Python? A Sheets backup is noted at the end.

Pull a few years of a broad U.S. index ETF, then compute annualized volatility and the maximum drawdown — the two risk numbers from §6.2 — on real data.

# risk_metrics.py — Lecture 6
import yfinance as yf
import numpy as np

px = yf.download("VTI", start="2015-01-01")["Close"].dropna()   # total US market
ret = px.pct_change().dropna()

vol_annual = ret.std() * np.sqrt(252)            # annualized volatility
print(f"annualized volatility: {float(vol_annual):.1%}")   # ~15-20%, varies

peak = px.cummax()                                # running peak
drawdown = (px - peak) / peak                     # always <= 0
print(f"max drawdown: {float(drawdown.min()):.1%}")        # worst peak-to-trough

# Sharpe-ish: excess return per unit of risk (assume ~4% risk-free, illustrative)
mean_annual = ret.mean() * 252
sharpe = (mean_annual - 0.04) / vol_annual
print(f"approx Sharpe ratio: {float(sharpe):.2f}")

Now compare a single stock (e.g. "TSLA") to the index: its volatility and drawdown should be far larger — that's the undiversified, unrewarded risk from §6.1. A. compensation for bearing risk (a risk premium)
B. a guarantee written into securities law

A. Expected return is the price of bearing risk. Stocks swing hard, so they must offer a premium to attract holders — no guarantee involved.

Q2. Maximum drawdown measures…
A. the average yearly return
B. the annual dividend yield
C. the worst peak-to-trough drop over a period
D. the total fees charged by a fund
Show answer

C. Drawdown is how far below the previous peak you are; the maximum is the deepest such fall before recovery — the number that triggers panic.

Q3. According to the "arithmetic of active management," the average actively managed dollar, after costs, must…
A. beat the market
B. trail the average passive dollar
C. exactly match the market
D. earn the risk-free rate
Show answer

B. Before costs the average active dollar earns the market return; after subtracting higher fees it must trail low-cost passive investors. It's arithmetic, not luck.

Q4. An investor sells their index fund during a 30% drawdown, then buys back after it recovers. This mainly illustrates…
A. the benefit of diversification
B. a low expense ratio
C. panic-selling, turning a paper loss into a realized one
D. the Sharpe ratio
Show answer

C. A diversified index drawdown is usually a temporary paper loss; selling at the bottom realizes it and misses the recovery — the classic behavior gap.

Q5. The risk you are not rewarded for bearing is…
A. the risk that the whole market falls in a recession
B. interest-rate risk affecting all assets
C. the company-specific risk you could have diversified away
D. inflation risk
Show answer

C. Markets pay a premium only for non-diversifiable (systematic) risk. Single-company risk can be diluted by holding many names, so concentrating in one stock adds risk without added expected reward.

Q6. Two funds earn the same gross 7% return. Fund A charges 0.03%/yr, Fund B charges 1.0%/yr. Over 30 years, Fund B's investor will most likely end up with…

A. A higher fee is a guaranteed, compounding subtraction from return. As the worked example showed, ~1% can erode roughly a quarter of the final balance over 30 years.

Q7. The Sharpe ratio of a portfolio rises when you…
A. increase volatility while keeping return fixed
B. earn more excess return per unit of volatility (e.g., via diversification)

B. Sharpe = excess return ÷ volatility. Diversifying into assets that don't move together can cut σ without sacrificing much return, raising the ratio.
