Week 4 · Lecture 4

Estimation, Planning & Backlogs

A plan is not a list of dates — it's an ordered list of valuable, sized work the team can actually finish. Here's how Scrum builds one.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write a good user story using the "As a… I want… so that…" form and INVEST criteria.
  • Explain why teams estimate in relative story points instead of hours, and how the Fibonacci-style scale works.
  • Use velocity — story points completed per sprint — to forecast a release without pretending to predict exact dates.
  • Prioritize a backlog with MoSCoW (Must / Should / Could / Won't) and order it by value-per-effort.
  • State what a Definition of Done is and why it protects quality across every sprint.

4.1The Unit of Planning: a User Story

In Week 3 you met the Product Backlog — Scrum's single ordered list of everything that might be done. But "everything" has to be broken into pieces small enough to plan, estimate, and finish inside one Sprint. The most common piece is the user story: a short description of a feature told from the point of view of the person who wants it.

As a <type of user>, I want <some goal>, so that <some reason / value>.The "so that" clause is the most important part — it states the value, which is what you'll prioritize on.

A story is a placeholder for a conversation, not a spec. It is small enough that the Developers can discuss it, size it, build it, and demo it. A widely used checklist for whether a story is ready is the INVEST acronym:

AnalogyA user story is like an item on a restaurant order ticket, not the recipe. "Table 4: one medium-rare steak, so the guest can eat" tells the kitchen what and why — the chef negotiates the how. A ticket reading "make all the food" is too big to cook or time; you'd split it into dishes first. Stories work the same way.

4.2Why Estimate in Points, Not Hours

People are famously bad at estimating how long something takes in absolute hours — but surprisingly good at saying whether one thing is bigger than another. Story points exploit this: they are a unit of relative size (effort + complexity + uncertainty), not a unit of time.

You pick a small, well-understood story as a reference — say it's a "2" — and size everything else against it. Teams usually use a Fibonacci-like scale because the gaps widen as items get bigger, reflecting growing uncertainty:

1 · 2 · 3 · 5 · 8 · 13 · 20 · 40 · …There is no "11" — once a story is bigger than ~8, you can't tell 11 from 13 anyway, so the scale forces a coarser, honest guess.

A common technique is Planning Poker: each Developer privately picks a card, all reveal at once, and the high and low estimators explain their reasoning. The discussion — not the number — is the real product; it surfaces hidden complexity and shared understanding.

Common mistakeDo not convert story points to hours (e.g. "a point = 4 hours"). The moment you do, points become a disguised time estimate and lose their power — and managers start treating velocity as a productivity quota to inflate. Points are relative and team-specific: one team's "5" is not comparable to another team's "5", and that's fine.

4.3Velocity: Forecasting Without Fortune-Telling

Once a team finishes a few Sprints, you can measure velocity — the number of story points of done work it completes per Sprint. Velocity converts relative sizes into a forecast:

Sprints remaining ≈ (points left in backlog) ÷ (average velocity)Use a range from your recent best and worst Sprints, never a single "exact" number.

Crucially, velocity is a capacity measurement, not a target. It is descriptive ("we tend to finish about 20 points") rather than prescriptive ("you must finish 25"). Plotting remaining work against time gives a burndown (or burnup) chart that shows whether you'll hit the goal:

4.4Prioritization: Ordering the Backlog with MoSCoW

A backlog is ordered, not just a pile. The Product Owner is accountable for that order, and the goal is to put the most valuable, least costly work first. A simple, durable framework is MoSCoW:

  • M — Must have: non-negotiable; the release fails or is illegal/unsafe without it.
  • S — Should have: important and painful to omit, but the release survives without it (for now).
  • C — Could have: nice-to-have; included only if time and capacity allow.
  • W — Won't have (this time): explicitly out of scope for now — recorded so it isn't silently smuggled back in.

MoSCoW answers "is this in or out?". To order items inside the Musts and Shoulds, teams often rank by value-per-effort — roughly, business value divided by story points — so cheap, high-value work rises to the top:

priority score ≈ value ÷ effort (points)A "Weighted Shortest Job First" idea: do the small, high-value things first to deliver value early.
Common mistakeTreating every item as a "Must." If everything is a Must, you have not prioritized — you've just relabelled the wishlist. A healthy plan keeps Musts to a clear minority (a common rule of thumb is roughly 60% or less of effort) so there's slack to absorb surprises. The Won't column is doing real work: it makes the cut explicit and stops scope creep.

4.5Definition of Done: When Is "Done" Really Done?

The Definition of Done (DoD) is one of Scrum's three artifact commitments — it is the commitment attached to the Increment (the Product Goal commits the Product Backlog; the Sprint Goal commits the Sprint Backlog; the DoD commits the Increment). It is a shared, explicit checklist of what "complete" means for every item, so that "done" never quietly shifts to mean "done-ish."

Note the difference between the DoD and a story's acceptance criteria: acceptance criteria are specific to one story ("the login form rejects an empty password"), while the DoD is a universal quality bar applied to all work. An Increment is only usable if every item in it meets the DoD.

AnalogyThe Definition of Done is the health-inspection checklist a kitchen must pass before any plate leaves — clean station, correct temperature, allergens logged. Acceptance criteria are the individual diner's order ("no onions"). A dish must satisfy both the diner's order and the inspection standard before it's truly ready to serve.
✦ Worked Example — sizing a backlog and forecasting a release

A team is building a simple to-do app. Walk through estimating, prioritizing, and forecasting.

  1. Write the stories. Three items: (A) "As a user, I want to add a task, so that I can track it." (B) "As a user, I want to mark a task done, so that I see progress." (C) "As a user, I want to sync across devices, so that I can use my phone and laptop."
  2. Pick a reference. The team agrees story A is a small, familiar "2".
  3. Estimate relatively (Planning Poker). B feels about the same effort as A → also 2. C involves accounts, a server, and conflict handling — far bigger and far more uncertain → the team lands on 13. Total backlog = 2 + 2 + 13 = 17 points.
  4. Prioritize with MoSCoW. A = Must (no app without adding tasks). B = Should (very valuable, but you could ship a read-only first cut). C = Could (sync is great but not needed for v1). Order: A, B, C.
  5. Value-per-effort check. A and B are cheap (2 pts) and high value → top. C is 13 pts for one feature → goes last, and the team agrees to split it later (INVEST: it's not "Small").
  6. Forecast with velocity. Past Sprints averaged ~8 points/Sprint. Backlog is 17 points → 17 ÷ 8 ≈ ~2–3 Sprints. Report a range ("2 to 3 sprints"), not a false-precision date.
  7. Takeaway: No hours were ever estimated. Relative sizing + measured velocity produced a defensible forecast, and MoSCoW guaranteed the Must ships first.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a points-and-velocity backlog

No install needed for the board itself — use a free Trello or Notion board (one-line reminder: the Python/pandas setup lives in Week 1 if you'd rather compute). Make four lists: Backlog → To Do (this Sprint) → In Progress → Done.

Step 1 — cards as stories. Write each card in "As a… I want… so that…" form. Add a label for the point value (1/2/3/5/8/13) and a MoSCoW label (M/S/C/W).

Step 2 — a Definition of Done. Pin a card at the top of Done listing your DoD checklist; a card may only enter Done when it passes all items.

Step 3 — compute velocity & a forecast from your completed points with a few lines of pandas:

# backlog_forecast.py — Lecture 4
import pandas as pd

# story points finished in each past sprint
done = pd.Series([7, 9, 8, 10], name="points_done")
velocity = done.mean()                       # average points / sprint
print(f"avg velocity: {velocity:.1f} pts/sprint")

backlog = pd.DataFrame({
    "story":   ["add task", "mark done", "sync devices"],
    "points":  [2,           2,           13],
    "moscow":  ["M",         "S",         "C"],
})
remaining = backlog["points"].sum()          # 17
low, high = remaining / done.max(), remaining / done.min()
print(f"backlog: {remaining} pts -> ~{low:.1f} to {high:.1f} sprints")
# forecast a RANGE, never a single fake-precise date

Stretch: re-sort backlog by a value/points column you add, and watch which stories rise to the top. That is value-per-effort prioritization in two lines.

❓ Lecture 4 Quiz

7 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Which part of a user story ("As a… I want… so that…") states the value you prioritize on?
A. the "As a <user>" clause
B. the "so that <reason>" clause
C. the "I want <goal>" clause
D. the story's point estimate
Show answer

B. The "so that…" clause names the benefit/value, which is exactly what prioritization weighs. The first clause says who and the "I want" says what; the points (D) measure effort, not value.

Q2. Story points primarily measure…
A. relative size: effort, complexity, and uncertainty compared to a reference story
B. the exact number of hours a task will take
C. the dollar value of the feature
D. how many people will work on the story
Show answer

A. Points are a relative size, sized against a reference item — not hours (B), money (C), or headcount (D). Converting points to a fixed number of hours defeats their purpose.

Q3. A team's velocity is best described as…
A. a target every team must hit each Sprint
B. the number of stories (regardless of size) finished per Sprint
C. the average story points of done work completed per Sprint, used to forecast
D. the total points remaining in the backlog
Show answer

C. Velocity is a descriptive capacity measure — points of completed work per Sprint — used to forecast, not a quota (A). It counts points, not raw story count (B); remaining work (D) is the burndown.

Q4. In MoSCoW, the "W" stands for…
A. "Will have first" — the top priority
B. "Wait" — undecided items
C. "Weighted" — items scored by value
D. "Won't have (this time)" — explicitly out of scope for now
Show answer

D. "Won't have this time" records what is deliberately out of scope, which prevents silent scope creep. (The lowercase o's are just there to make the word pronounceable.)

Q5. Why do teams use a Fibonacci-like scale (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, …) for points?
A. Fibonacci numbers convert neatly into hours
B. it is required by the Scrum Guide
C. it lets you estimate any item down to one decimal place
D. the widening gaps reflect growing uncertainty for bigger items, forcing honest, coarser estimates
Show answer

D. Larger items carry more uncertainty, so a scale with no "11 vs 13" distinction matches our real ability to tell sizes apart. It isn't about hours (A), isn't mandated by the Scrum Guide (B), and is deliberately coarse, not precise (C).

Q6. The Definition of Done differs from a story's acceptance criteria because the DoD…
A. applies universally to every backlog item as a shared quality bar
B. is unique to each individual story
C. is decided by the customer, not the team
D. replaces the need for any testing
Show answer

A. The DoD is one shared checklist applied to all items (the commitment to the Increment), whereas acceptance criteria (B) are specific to one story. The team owns the DoD (not C), and it typically requires testing (not D).

Q7. A backlog has 24 points left and the team's average velocity is ~8 points/Sprint. A reasonable forecast to report is…
A. "done in exactly 3 sprints, guaranteed"
B. "done in 192 sprints" (24 × 8)
C. "roughly 3 sprints, give or take, based on a range of recent velocities"
D. "impossible to estimate without hour estimates"
Show answer

C. 24 ÷ 8 ≈ 3 Sprints, reported as a range — never a guaranteed exact date (A). You divide, not multiply (B), and points + velocity make the forecast without any hours (D).
