Scrum in Depth — Roles, Artifacts, Events

Scrum is a small, deliberate machine: three accountabilities, three artifacts, and five events that turn a fuzzy goal into working product every couple of weeks.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the three Scrum accountabilities — Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers — and what each is on the hook for.
  • Distinguish the three artifacts (Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment) and the commitment attached to each.
  • Walk through the five events (the Sprint plus Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) and what each produces.
  • Explain how the pieces form one closed feedback loop that delivers a usable Increment every Sprint.
  • Set up a simple Sprint board in a free tool and run a one-week mini-Sprint.

3.1The Whole Machine in One Picture

Last week you saw the predictive (PMP) backbone — five process groups and the up-front plan. Scrum is the adaptive opposite: instead of planning the whole journey once, you take a short, fixed-length trip, look at what you got, and re-plan. The Scrum Guide (the short, free, official rulebook by Schwaber and Sutherland) defines exactly three accountabilities, three artifacts, and five events — nothing more is required, and removing pieces breaks the loop.

3.2Three Accountabilities (the "roles")

A Scrum Team is small — typically 10 or fewer people — and has no sub-teams or hierarchy. It holds exactly three accountabilities. (The 2020 Scrum Guide deliberately says "accountabilities," not "roles," to stress they are responsibilities, not job titles.)

Product Owner — the "what" and "why"

One person accountable for maximizing the value of the product. The PO owns the Product Backlog: creating items, ordering them, and making the priorities clear so the team always works on the most valuable thing next. The PO decides what's in and what waits — a single, decisive voice, not a committee.

Scrum Master — the "how it runs"

Accountable for the team's effectiveness and for Scrum being understood and practiced. The Scrum Master is a coach and a remover-of-obstacles — they facilitate events, protect the team from interruptions, and help the organization adopt Scrum. Crucially, they are not a boss who assigns tasks.

Developers — the "how it's built"

Everyone doing the work to build the Increment — programmers, designers, testers, writers, whoever it takes. Developers are accountable for the Sprint Backlog, for quality (the Definition of Done), and they self-manage: they decide how to turn backlog items into working product.

Common mistakeThe Scrum Master is not a project manager who hands out assignments or a status-reporting middleman, and the Product Owner is not the Developers' boss. The team self-manages; the PO sets priority, the SM removes friction, and the Developers choose how to do the work. Collapsing these into "the manager tells people what to do" quietly turns Scrum back into mini-waterfall.

3.3Three Artifacts and Their Commitments

Each artifact carries a commitment — a target that makes the artifact transparent and measurable. Memorize the pairs:

Product Backlog → Product Goal  ·  Sprint Backlog → Sprint Goal  ·  Increment → Definition of DoneEach artifact (left) is anchored by its commitment (right) — the test of whether the artifact is doing its job.
  • Product Backlog — the single, ordered, ever-evolving list of everything that might improve the product. Its commitment is the Product Goal: the longer-term objective the team is heading toward.
  • Sprint Backlog — the slice the Developers pull in for this Sprint: the Sprint Goal, the selected items, and the plan to deliver them. Its commitment is the Sprint Goal: the single objective for the Sprint.
  • Increment — the concrete, usable sum of completed work this Sprint, added to all prior Increments. Its commitment is the Definition of Done: the shared quality bar an item must meet to count as "done."
AnalogyThink of a restaurant kitchen. The Product Backlog is the full menu the chef could cook (Product Goal = the kind of restaurant we want to be). Tonight's prep list is the Sprint Backlog (Sprint Goal = "nail the dinner service"). And a plate only leaves the pass when it meets the chef's standard — that standard is the Definition of Done.

3.4Five Events (and what each produces)

Every event is a timebox — a fixed maximum length — and every event exists to inspect something and adapt. The Sprint is the container; the other four happen inside it.

1. The Sprint — the heartbeat

A fixed length of one month or less (often two weeks). It holds all the other events. You don't change the Sprint Goal mid-Sprint, and a new Sprint starts the instant the previous one ends — no gaps. Consistent length makes the team's pace predictable.

2. Sprint Planning — opens the Sprint

The team answers three questions: Why is this Sprint valuable (the Sprint Goal)? What can be done (selected backlog items)? How will it get done (the plan)? Output: the Sprint Backlog. Timebox: up to ~8 hours for a one-month Sprint, less for shorter ones.

3. Daily Scrum — 15 minutes, every day

The Developers inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adjust the plan for the next 24 hours. It is for the Developers, by the Developers — not a status report to a manager. Timebox: 15 minutes.

4. Sprint Review — inspect the product

At the Sprint's end the team and stakeholders inspect the Increment and discuss what to do next; the Product Backlog may be adjusted. It's a working session, not a demo-for-applause. Timebox: up to ~4 hours for a one-month Sprint.

5. Sprint Retrospective — inspect the process

The team inspects how it worked — people, interactions, tools — and picks improvements to try next Sprint. It closes the Sprint. Timebox: up to ~3 hours for a one-month Sprint.

Note · verify timeboxesThe specific timebox maxima above (8h / 4h / 3h for a one-month Sprint, scaled down for shorter ones) are from the Scrum Guide — as of 2026, verify against the current official Scrum Guide at scrumguides.org, since the guide is revised periodically. The 15-minute Daily Scrum and the "one month or less" Sprint are long-standing.

3.5Why Events Map to Inspect-and-Adapt

Scrum is built on empiricism: decisions are based on what is observed, not on a forecast. Each event is one beat of an inspect-and-adapt loop, and notice that two events look at the product while two look at the process:

Planning (set goal) → Daily (adjust daily) → Review (inspect product) → Retro (inspect process)Drop any one event and a feedback channel goes dark — that is the usual cause of "we do Scrum but it isn't working."

This is also why a missing event hurts in a predictable way: skip the Retrospective and the team stops improving; skip the Review and you build the wrong thing for a whole Sprint; skip Planning and there's no shared Sprint Goal. The discussion below asks you to spot exactly this in a real project.

✦ Worked Example — diagnosing a broken "Scrum" team

A 6-person team runs two-week "Sprints." They do daily standups and planning, but skip the Review and Retrospective. Stakeholders complain features miss the mark, and the same delivery problems recur every Sprint. Walk through which Scrum pieces are missing and which single event you'd add first.

  1. Map what exists: Sprint (timebox) ✓, Sprint Planning ✓, Daily Scrum ✓. So the opening of the loop is intact.
  2. Find the dark channels: no Sprint Review → no structured inspection of the product with stakeholders → "features miss the mark." No Retrospective → no inspection of the process → "the same problems recur."
  3. Match symptom to cause: "miss the mark" is a product-feedback failure (Review); "same problems recur" is a process-feedback failure (Retro). Both are missing, but they cause different symptoms.
  4. Pick the highest-leverage fix: Add the Sprint Review first. Until stakeholders see the Increment each Sprint, the team keeps building the wrong thing — the most expensive error. The Retrospective is the close second.
  5. Takeaway: "We do Scrum" usually means "we kept the events that feel like work and dropped the ones that feel like meetings" — but the dropped ones are exactly the feedback loops.

🛠️ Hands-On: run a one-week mini-Sprint on a free board

Set up a Sprint board in Trello or Notion (both free) and run a real one-week Sprint on a small personal project. (No new installs needed — Week 1 already covered tool setup; a free Trello/Notion account is all you need here.)

# 1. Make a board with three lists / columns:
Product Backlog   |   Sprint Backlog (This Week)   |   Done

# 2. Fill the Product Backlog with 8–12 small items for your project.
#    Write a one-line Sprint Goal at the top of the board.
#    e.g. Sprint Goal: "Ship a working signup page."

# 3. SPRINT PLANNING (15 min): drag only what fits one week
#    into "Sprint Backlog". Add a Definition of Done checklist to each
#    card (e.g. tested, reviewed, merged).

# 4. DAILY SCRUM (2 min/day): move cards right; note any blocker
#    as a red label. Adjust toward the Sprint Goal.

# 5. End of week — SPRINT REVIEW: look at the "Done" column.
#    Did you hit the Sprint Goal? Show it to one other person.

# 6. RETROSPECTIVE (5 min): write 2 things to keep, 1 to change.

Optional: if you'd rather code it, track the same board as a tiny pandas table — one row per card with columns item, status, done_criteria — and print df[df.status=="Done"] at week's end to compute your "velocity" (items completed). We formalize velocity next week.

Q1. Who is accountable for maximizing the value of the product and for ordering the Product Backlog?
A. The Scrum Master
B. The Product Owner
C. The Developers, collectively
D. The stakeholders
Show answer

B. The Product Owner owns the "what/why" — maximizing value and keeping the Product Backlog ordered. The Scrum Master serves the team's effectiveness; Developers build the Increment.

Q2. The commitment attached to the Increment is the…
A. Sprint Goal
B. Product Goal
C. Definition of Done
D. Sprint Backlog
Show answer

C. Increment → Definition of Done. (Product Backlog → Product Goal; Sprint Backlog → Sprint Goal.)

Q3. The Daily Scrum is best described as…
A. a 15-minute event for the Developers to inspect progress toward the Sprint Goal and adjust their plan
B. a status report delivered to the manager
C. the meeting where the Increment is shown to stakeholders
D. the session where the Sprint Goal is set
Show answer

A. It's a 15-minute event by and for the Developers. Showing the Increment is the Review (C); setting the goal is Planning (D).

Q4. A team delivers features that repeatedly "miss the mark" with stakeholders. Which event most directly addresses that gap?
A. Sprint Retrospective
B. Daily Scrum
C. Sprint Planning
D. Sprint Review
Show answer

D. The Sprint Review inspects the product with stakeholders and adapts the Product Backlog. The Retrospective inspects the process, which fixes recurring delivery problems, not aim.

Q5. How long is the Sprint itself, per the Scrum Guide?
A. Exactly 30 calendar days, always
B. One month or less, with consistent length
C. However long the work takes
D. 15 minutes
Show answer

B. A Sprint is a fixed timebox of one month or less; teams keep the length consistent (often two weeks). 15 minutes (D) is the Daily Scrum.

Q6. Which statement about the Scrum Master is correct?
A. They assign tasks to Developers each morning
B. They own and order the Product Backlog
C. They are accountable for the team's effectiveness and for removing impediments, without bossing the team
D. They sign off whether each Increment meets the Definition of Done
Show answer

C. The Scrum Master coaches, facilitates, and removes obstacles — a servant-leader, not a task-assigner. Ordering the backlog (B) is the PO; meeting the Definition of Done (D) is the Developers' accountability.

  • The five events: the Sprint (container) plus Planning, Daily Scrum, Review, and Retrospective — each a timeboxed inspect-and-adapt beat.
  • How the pieces form one closed feedback loop — and how a missing event (Review or Retro) shows up as a specific failure.

Model response

Example: a student-club fundraiser. It was run predictively — we wrote one big plan in September (venue, date, tasks, owners) and only checked in when something broke. The plan looked tidy but reality drifted: a sponsor fell through in week three and we didn't notice the knock-on effects until close to the event.

The single event that would have helped most is the Sprint Review (or, scaled down, a short weekly review of what's actually done and usable). A regular review with the people who cared about the outcome would have surfaced the sponsor gap early, when we still had time to adapt the plan — that's the inspect-the-product feedback channel our one-shot plan was missing. (A reasonable alternative answer is the Retrospective, if your project's main pain was the same coordination mistakes recurring — that's the inspect-the-process channel.)

Self-check rubric

  • Classifies the project as predictive or agile with a concrete reason (up-front plan vs. iterate-and-adapt)
  • Names one specific Scrum event (not "more meetings")
  • Ties that event to the actual problem — product feedback (Review), process feedback (Retro), daily coordination (Daily), or shared goal (Planning)
