Theory of Constraints II — Throughput, Inventory & Drum-Buffer-Rope

Last week you found the constraint; this week you learn the three numbers that tell you whether any change actually helps — and how to schedule the whole system around its slowest part.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define Goldratt's three operational measures — Throughput, Inventory, and Operating Expense — and state what each one should do.
  • Explain why a local optimum (a busy machine) can hurt the global goal (money out the door).
  • Describe Drum-Buffer-Rope scheduling and name what the drum, buffer, and rope each are.
  • Compute a system's throughput and judge whether a proposed improvement is worth doing.
  • Model a simple work pipeline in a spreadsheet / pandas to see where inventory piles up.

6.1The Goal, Restated: Make Money

In Week 5 we read The Goal (Eliyahu Goldratt's business novel) and met the five focusing steps: identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, then go back to step one. But a plant manager can't run a factory on intuition about "the bottleneck." He needs numbers that connect a local decision — "should we buy this machine?" — to the global goal of making money.

Goldratt's breakthrough was to reject the traditional accounting view (where keeping every machine and worker busy looks like efficiency) and replace it with just three operational measures. Every action is judged by its effect on these three, in priority order:

Throughput ↑  ·  Inventory ↓  ·  Operating Expense ↓The three measures of The Goal. The arrows show the desired direction — and Throughput comes first.

6.2The Three Measures (T, I, OE)

Each measure has a precise definition. Get these straight and the rest of the lecture follows.

Throughput (T)

The rate at which the system generates money through sales. Crucial subtlety: it is money from sold goods, not produced goods. A warehouse full of finished product you haven't sold is not throughput — it's inventory. Roughly, T = sales revenue − truly variable cost (e.g. raw materials).

Inventory (I)

All the money the system has invested in things it intends to sell — raw materials, work-in-progress on the floor, and finished goods. In knowledge work the analog is the pile of started-but-unfinished tasks: half-written reports, code in review, tickets "in progress."

Operating Expense (OE)

All the money the system spends turning Inventory into Throughput — wages, rent, utilities, the cost of running the place.

Net Profit ∝ T − OE   ·   Return on Investment ∝ (T − OE) / IProfit and ROI are expressed entirely in T, I, and OE — which is why these three are enough.
Common mistakeTreating finished goods sitting in a warehouse as a "win." In traditional cost accounting, producing them looks like efficiency and even adds to reported asset value — but in ToC terms it is just Inventory, not Throughput, until a customer pays. Producing faster than you can sell raises I and OE while T stays flat: you are getting worse, not better.

6.3Local Optima vs the Global Goal

The deepest idea in The Goal is this: the sum of local optima is not the global optimum. If you optimize each machine, person, or department in isolation — keeping each one as busy as possible — you can easily make the whole system perform worse.

Why? Because a chain is only as strong as its weakest link. Any work a non-constraint produces beyond what the constraint can consume does not become Throughput. It piles up as Inventory in front of the bottleneck, inflating I and OE while T is unchanged. The non-constraint looks efficient (100% utilized!) but is actively harming the goal.

utilizing a non-constraint ≠ activating it usefullyGoldratt's distinction: a resource can be 100% "utilized" producing things the system can't yet sell. Only work the constraint can absorb counts.
AnalogyPicture a line of hikers on a narrow trail, tied loosely together. The group's speed is set entirely by the slowest hiker (the constraint). If the fast hikers at the front race ahead, the rope just stretches and a gap opens — that gap is Inventory. The group as a whole hasn't arrived any sooner. To go faster you must help the slowest hiker (carry some of their pack — that's "exploit"), and make everyone else march at their pace, not their own.

6.4Drum-Buffer-Rope: Scheduling the Whole System

So how do you make every non-constraint march at the constraint's pace? Goldratt's scheduling method is Drum-Buffer-Rope (DBR), and the hiking analogy maps onto it exactly.

🥁 Drum

The constraint sets the beat for the entire system. Its pace is the system's pace — there is no point planning anything to run faster. The constraint's schedule is the master schedule.

🛡️ Buffer

A small amount of protective inventory (or time) placed just in front of the constraint so it never starves. The constraint is the most expensive thing to leave idle — an hour lost there is an hour lost for the whole system — so we deliberately keep a little work waiting for it. (There is usually also a shipping buffer to protect the promised due dates.)

🪢 Rope

A signal that ties the release of new raw material to the constraint's consumption. Material is fed in at the front only as fast as the constraint pulls it through — like a rope keeping the lead hiker from sprinting away. This caps the work-in-progress and stops inventory from exploding everywhere upstream.

6.5Reading T, I, OE Together

A good improvement moves the three measures in the right directions — or at least improves the priority ones without harming the rest. Use this as a decision filter before spending money:

  • Does it raise Throughput? Only changes at (or that elevate) the constraint do this. This is the first question, always.
  • Does it lower Inventory? Faster flow and the rope reduce work-in-progress.
  • Does it lower Operating Expense? Cheaper to run, fewer rush costs.

The trap is judging by OE alone. A proposal that cuts cost (lower OE) but also cuts T because it slows the constraint is a bad deal — you saved a little and lost a lot. Throughput is unbounded upward; OE can only fall to zero. That asymmetry is why ToC ranks T first.

✦ Worked Example — should we buy the faster polisher?

A 4-station line makes one product. Capacities (units/hour): Cut 100, Drill 60, Polish 40, Pack 80. The product sells for $50, with $20 raw material. Operating Expense is $1,000/hour. A vendor offers a faster Drill (60 → 120) for $30k, or a faster Polish (40 → 70) for $30k. Which should you buy?

  1. Find the constraint: the slowest station sets system output. Polish at 40 u/hr is the bottleneck — the whole line makes 40 u/hr, no matter what the others can do.
  2. Throughput per unit: $50 sale − $20 material = $30 of Throughput per unit sold.
  3. Current Throughput: 40 u/hr × $30 = $1,200/hr. Net (T − OE) = 1,200 − 1,000 = $200/hr.
  4. Option A — faster Drill (60 → 120): Drill was not the constraint. The line is still capped by Polish at 40 u/hr. Throughput unchanged at $1,200/hr. This is a classic local improvement that does nothing for the global goal — money wasted.
  5. Option B — faster Polish (40 → 70): the new constraint becomes Drill at 60 u/hr. Throughput rises to 60 × $30 = $1,800/hr; net (T − OE) = 1,800 − 1,000 = $800/hr — a $600/hr gain that pays back $30k in ~50 hours.
  6. Decision: buy the Polisher. Then re-run the five focusing steps: the constraint has moved to Drill (60), so that is now where the next dollar of improvement belongs.

🛠️ Hands-On: find the bottleneck and watch inventory pile up

You can model a pipeline two ways — pick whichever you prefer.

Option 1 — Trello / Notion (no code, free): make a board with one list per stage (To Do → Cut → Drill → Polish → Pack → Done). Add 20 cards and move one stage per "tick," but each list can only complete its station's capacity per tick. After a few ticks, watch cards stack up in the list just before your slowest stage — that visible pile is Inventory, and the stage it's waiting on is your drum. (Free Trello/Notion accounts cover this; no install needed.)

Option 2 — Python + pandas (you set the stack up in Week 1 — no need to reinstall):

# bottleneck.py — Lecture 6: find the constraint & see WIP build up
import pandas as pd

line = pd.DataFrame({
    "station":  ["Cut", "Drill", "Polish", "Pack"],
    "capacity": [100,    60,      40,       80],   # units/hour
})

# the constraint = the minimum-capacity station
constraint = line.loc[line.capacity.idxmin()]
throughput = line.capacity.min()
print("Constraint (drum):", constraint.station, "@", throughput, "u/hr")

# with no rope, each station runs flat-out: WIP accumulates before the drum
arriving = throughput_in = 60   # units/hr fed by upstream Drill
wip_growth = arriving - throughput            # 60 - 40 = 20 u/hr piling up
print("WIP building before Polish:", wip_growth, "u/hr  <- inventory!")

# the 'rope': release only as fast as the drum consumes
release = throughput
print("Rope-paced release:", release, "u/hr  -> WIP stops growing")

Change the capacities and rerun. Notice that speeding up any station except the constraint leaves throughput unchanged — the same lesson as the worked example.

🔗 Where this is heading

Drum-Buffer-Rope is the ancestor of the work-in-progress limits you set on a Kanban board, and the "pull" idea behind Lean manufacturing. When you cap how many tickets can sit "in progress," you are putting a rope on your team and protecting your real constraint — usually a single reviewer, tester, or decision-maker. In the Week 10 capstone you'll combine this with Scrum: run sprints, but use ToC to find and protect the one step that actually limits your delivery rate. Next week we switch from systems to people — because very often the constraint isn't a machine, it's how a manager develops the talent around them.

Q1. In Goldratt's Theory of Constraints, Throughput is best defined as the rate at which the system…
A. produces finished goods
B. generates money through sales
C. keeps every machine busy
D. spends on wages and rent
Show answer

B. Throughput is money the system generates through sales — not production. Unsold finished goods are Inventory (A), not Throughput. (D) describes Operating Expense.

Q2. Money the system has invested in things it intends to sell (raw materials, work-in-progress, finished goods) is called…
A. Inventory
B. Throughput
C. Operating Expense
D. Net profit
Show answer

A. That's Inventory (I). Operating Expense is the money spent turning Inventory into Throughput.

Q3. A factory speeds up a machine that is not the constraint, running it at 100% utilization. The most likely result is…
A. system Throughput rises proportionally
B. the constraint disappears
C. work-in-progress piles up before the constraint while Throughput stays flat
D. Operating Expense falls
Show answer

C. Output beyond what the constraint can consume becomes Inventory, raising I (and often OE) with no gain in T. A busy non-constraint is a local optimum that hurts the global goal.

Q4. In Drum-Buffer-Rope, the drum is…
A. the protective inventory in front of the bottleneck
B. the signal that releases new material
C. the fastest station in the line
D. the constraint, whose pace sets the rhythm for the whole system
Show answer

D. The drum is the constraint; its pace is the master schedule. The buffer (A) protects it from starving; the rope (B) paces material release.

Q5. The purpose of the rope in DBR is to…
A. tie material release to the constraint's consumption so WIP doesn't explode
B. keep the constraint 100% busy at all costs
C. increase the speed of upstream stations
D. measure operating expense
Show answer

A. The rope links the release gate to the drum: new work enters only as fast as the constraint pulls it through, capping work-in-progress upstream.

Q6. A line is capped by its Polish station at 40 u/hr; each unit yields $30 of Throughput. A vendor's upgrade would double the (non-constraint) Drill station's speed. The effect on system Throughput is…
A. it doubles
B. it rises to 60 u/hr
C. it is unchanged — Polish still caps the line at 40 u/hr
D. it cannot be determined
Show answer

C. Improving a non-constraint does nothing for system output; Polish still limits the line to 40 u/hr. Spend the money on the constraint instead.

✓ What you learned

  • The three measures of The Goal — Throughput (money from sales), Inventory (money tied up), Operating Expense (money spent processing) — and that net profit and ROI are written entirely in T, I, OE.
  • Why the sum of local optima is not the global optimum: a busy non-constraint just builds Inventory.
  • Drum-Buffer-Rope — the constraint drums the beat, a buffer protects it, a rope paces material release.
  • How to use T-first as a decision filter, and to compute throughput and judge an improvement (the polisher example).
  • How to model a pipeline in Trello/Notion or pandas and watch inventory pile up before the constraint.

Model response

Take a morning coffee shop with one espresso machine. Identify: the line backs up not at the register or the milk station but at the single espresso machine — that's the constraint, the step everything waits on. Exploit (before spending a cent): make sure the machine is never idle — pre-grind and tamp the next shot while the current one pulls, stop pulling shots for orders that are still being modified, and don't let the barista leave the machine to bus tables. Stage cups and lids so no second is lost reaching for them. Subordinate: the register only takes orders as fast as the machine can serve them (a rope), and the milk-steaming is timed to finish exactly when the shot is ready (a buffer of one prepped order) so the machine never starves.

Elevate comes only after exploitation is maxed — that's when buying a second machine is justified. And then go back to step one: with two machines, the constraint may move to the single cashier or the cleanup sink, and the focus shifts there.

Self-check rubric

  • Names a single, specific constraint (not "everything is slow")
  • Lists concrete exploit actions that cost no money (eliminate idle time / waste at the constraint)
  • Subordinates non-constraints to the constraint's pace (a rope/buffer) before proposing to elevate (spend)
  • Mentions re-running step one because the constraint can move
