Theory of Constraints I — The Goal & the Five Focusing Steps

Every system has exactly one thing holding it back. Find it, and you can speed up the whole organization without working everyone harder.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the central idea of Goldratt's The Goal: a system's output is governed by its single weakest link, the constraint (bottleneck).
  • State the Theory of Constraints (ToC) goal of a for-profit organization and why local efficiencies can hurt the whole.
  • Walk through the Five Focusing Steps: identify → exploit → subordinate → elevate → repeat.
  • Recognize that an hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the entire system, while an hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage.
  • Apply the steps to a real workflow and track flow with a simple board or pandas table.

5.1A Factory That Was Busy but Losing Money

In 1984 Eliyahu M. Goldratt published The Goal — not a textbook but a business novel. Its hero, plant manager Alex Rogo, has 90 days to save a factory that is, by every local metric, working hard: machines humming, workers occupied, efficiency reports green. And yet orders ship late and the plant loses money. The book's quiet revolution is the question Alex is forced to answer: what is the goal?

For a for-profit company, Goldratt insists the goal is simply to make money, now and in the future. Everything else — high machine utilization, low cost per part, full staffing — is only useful if it moves that goal. The trap the plant fell into is mistaking activity for progress. A robot that runs 100% of the time looks efficient, but if it builds parts that pile up unsold in inventory, it has converted cash into stacks of metal and made the goal worse.

AnalogyThink of a hiking troop strung out along a trail. The whole troop arrives only when the slowest hiker arrives — sending the fastest kids sprinting ahead just spreads the line out; it does not get everyone to camp sooner. In the book this is "Herbie," the slow boy whose pace sets the troop's pace. Herbie is the constraint. Speeding up anyone behind him does nothing; speeding up Herbie speeds up the whole troop.

5.2The Bottleneck Governs Throughput

The first principle is purely arithmetic. In any chain of dependent steps, material (or work) can only flow as fast as the slowest step. That slowest step is the constraint, or bottleneck. No matter how fast the other steps run, the system's output rate equals the constraint's rate.

System throughput = capacity of the slowest stepA chain is exactly as strong as its weakest link — adding strength anywhere else changes nothing.

This leads to Goldratt's two famous rules of thumb:

An hour lost at the bottleneck = an hour lost for the whole system
An hour saved at a non-bottleneck = a mirage (it saves nothing)Effort is only "real" where the constraint is. Everywhere else, faster just means more idle waiting or more inventory.
Common mistakeThe instinct of most managers is to maximize utilization everywhere — keep every machine and every person 100% busy. The Goal shows this is backwards. Loading up non-bottlenecks past what the constraint can absorb does not add output; it only builds inventory (and operating expense) while throughput stays flat. A non-bottleneck running at less than 100% is often the correct, healthy state.

5.3The Five Focusing Steps

Goldratt turned the insight into a repeatable process of ongoing improvement. Rather than improving everything at once, you focus all attention on the one constraint, in a fixed order:

Step 1 — Identify the constraint

Find the one step that limits the whole system. In a factory it is the station with work piling up in front of it and starved stations downstream. In an office it might be a single approval, one over-booked specialist, or a market that simply will not buy more (a constraint can be external).

Step 2 — Exploit the constraint

Before spending any money, wring every drop out of the constraint as it already is. Make sure it never sits idle: stop it taking lunch breaks unmanned, stop it processing defective work (inspect before the constraint, not after), and stop it working on low-priority or unsold orders. The constraint's time is the most precious resource in the system.

Step 3 — Subordinate everything else to the constraint

Every other resource must serve the constraint's pace. Non-bottlenecks should release work only as fast as the constraint can consume it — no faster, even if that means they sit idle. This is the hardest step culturally, because it tells efficient people to stop producing when there is no point. (Next week's Drum-Buffer-Rope is the mechanism that makes subordination concrete.)

Step 4 — Elevate the constraint

Only now do you add capacity: buy another machine, hire, add a shift, offload work elsewhere. "Elevate" means raise the constraint's ceiling. It comes fourth on purpose — exploitation and subordination are often free and frequently make elevation unnecessary.

Step 5 — Repeat (and beware inertia)

Once you elevate enough, the constraint moves: some other step is now the slowest. Go back to step 1 and find it. Goldratt's warning attaches here: do not let inertia become the constraint. Policies and habits built around the old bottleneck (rules, schedules, metrics) often outlive their reason and quietly choke the new one.

5.4Why Local Efficiency Lies

Traditional cost accounting rewards each department for being "efficient" — low cost per unit, high utilization. ToC argues this local optimum almost never adds up to a global optimum. If a non-bottleneck department maximizes its own output, it simply manufactures inventory that waits in front of the constraint. You have spent money (operating expense) and tied up cash (inventory) without shipping anything more (throughput).

This is why ToC measures the whole system with just three numbers — Throughput (the rate the system generates money through sales), Inventory (money tied up in things it intends to sell), and Operating Expense (money spent turning inventory into throughput). The good move is the one that raises Throughput while holding or lowering the other two. We unpack all three next week.

AnalogyA highway is only as fast as its worst chokepoint. Widening every stretch except the one-lane tunnel just gets cars to the tunnel faster — where they queue exactly as long. The tunnel governs the commute, so the tunnel is the only place where roadwork actually shortens anyone's trip.
✦ Worked Example — debugging a slow software release pipeline

A 6-person team ships features through four stages. Daily capacity (features/day): Design 8 · Build 6 · Code Review 2 · Deploy 7. Leadership wants more features shipped and is about to hire two more builders. Apply the Five Focusing Steps.

  1. Identify: Throughput = the slowest stage = Code Review at 2/day. Tickets pile up "waiting for review"; Deploy is starved. Hiring builders (raising Build from 6→9) changes nothing — the line still ships 2/day.
  2. Exploit: Get the most from review as-is. Stop the two reviewers doing unrelated meetings during review hours, route only "ready, tests-passing" PRs to them (no half-done work wasting their time), and review highest-value features first. Suppose this lifts effective review to 3/day for free.
  3. Subordinate: Tell Design and Build to release work at review's pace, not their own. Builders pulling 6/day just inflate the review queue; they instead cap WIP and spend freed time writing tests that make reviews faster — serving the constraint.
  4. Elevate: Only now add capacity at the constraint: train a third reviewer or adopt pair-review tooling, lifting review to 6/day. Note the right hire was a reviewer, not a builder.
  5. Repeat: With review at 6/day, the new constraint is Build at 6/day (a tie) — exactly where the original plan would have spent money first, and wrongly. Restart at step 1 on Build, and retire the old "reviewers attend every standup" policy before inertia chokes the new bottleneck.

🛠️ Hands-On: find your real bottleneck

You don't need a factory — any board with stages will do. (Reminder: the free Trello / Notion setup from Week 1 is all you need; nothing new to install. Or use the Python + pandas snippet below.)

Board exercise (Trello or Notion, free):

  1. Make one list/column per stage of a real workflow you own (e.g. To Do · In Progress · Review · Done).
  2. For one week, just move cards and note how many sit in each column at day's end.
  3. The column where cards pile up (and the next one looks starved) is your constraint — your "Herbie."
  4. Pick one free exploit action for that stage before asking for more people or tools.

Prefer code? Compute throughput and spot the constraint from per-stage capacities:

# toc_bottleneck.py — Lecture 5
import pandas as pd

stages = pd.DataFrame({
    "stage":    ["Design", "Build", "Review", "Deploy"],
    "capacity": [8,        6,       2,        7],          # features/day
})

constraint = stages.loc[stages["capacity"].idxmin()]
system_throughput = stages["capacity"].min()

print(stages.to_string(index=False))
print(f"\nConstraint : {constraint['stage']} ({constraint['capacity']}/day)")
print(f"Throughput : {system_throughput}/day  <- the WHOLE line ships this")

# What-if: hiring builders does nothing; +review capacity is the lever
stages.loc[stages.stage == "Review", "capacity"] = 6
print(f"After elevating Review -> throughput = {stages['capacity'].min()}/day")
# 6/day, now tied with Build — the constraint has MOVED. Repeat step 1.

Change the numbers to match your own team or class project, then try "fixing" a non-bottleneck in the table and confirm the throughput line does not budge.

Q1. In The Goal, what does Goldratt say the goal of a for-profit organization is?
A. To keep every machine and worker 100% busy
B. To make money, now and in the future
C. To minimize the cost of each individual part
D. To ship the most units regardless of sales
Show answer

B. The goal is to make money now and in the future. High utilization (A) or low unit cost (C) are only good if they serve that goal — and often they don't.

Q2. A four-stage line has capacities 100, 100, 40, 100 units/hr. What is the system's throughput?
A. 340 units/hr (the sum)
B. 100 units/hr (the fastest stage)
C. 85 units/hr (the average)
D. 40 units/hr (the slowest stage)
Show answer

D. Throughput equals the capacity of the constraint — the slowest step. The line can ship only 40/hr no matter how fast the others run.

Q3. What is the first of the Five Focusing Steps?
A. Identify the system's constraint
B. Elevate the constraint by adding capacity
C. Subordinate everything to the constraint
D. Exploit the constraint
Show answer

A. The order is Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat. You must find the constraint before you can do anything else with it.

Q4. "Exploit the constraint" (step 2) primarily means…
A. Immediately buy a faster machine for that stage
B. Speed up the stages before and after it
C. Get the most out of the constraint as it already is, before spending money
D. Move the constraint to a different department
Show answer

C. Exploit means squeeze maximum value from the existing constraint (no idle time, no defective or low-priority work). Buying capacity is "Elevate," which comes later (step 4).

Q5. Which statement matches Goldratt's rules of thumb?
A. An hour saved at any station is an hour saved for the system
B. An hour lost at the bottleneck is an hour lost for the whole system
C. Non-bottlenecks should always run at 100% utilization
D. The bottleneck should be the least-watched resource
Show answer

B. An hour lost at the bottleneck is lost for the entire system; an hour "saved" at a non-bottleneck is a mirage. That's why non-bottlenecks running below 100% can be perfectly healthy.

Q6. After you successfully elevate a constraint, what does step 5 ("Repeat") warn you to do?
A. Stop improving, since the system is now balanced forever
B. Keep elevating the same stage indefinitely
C. Go back to step 1 for the new constraint, and don't let inertia become the constraint
D. Maximize utilization at every station equally
Show answer

C. Elevating moves the constraint elsewhere, so you restart at step 1 — and explicitly guard against old policies/habits (inertia) that no longer fit choking the new bottleneck.

✓ What you learned

  • Goldratt's The Goal reframes management around one question — what is the goal? — and answers it as "make money now and in the future."
  • Every system has a single constraint (bottleneck) that governs throughput; a chain is only as strong as its weakest link.
  • The Five Focusing Steps: identify → exploit → subordinate → elevate → repeat, done in that order and looped.
  • An hour lost at the bottleneck is lost for the whole system; an hour saved at a non-bottleneck is a mirage — so local efficiency can mislead.
  • You can spot a real bottleneck on a free board (work piling up / next stage starved) or with a tiny pandas table.
