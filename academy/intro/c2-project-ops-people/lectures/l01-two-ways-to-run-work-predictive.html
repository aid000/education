Week 1 · Lecture 1

Two Ways to Run Work — Predictive (PMP) vs Agile/Scrum

Before you pick a tool or a ritual, you pick a control model: do you plan the whole thing up front, or steer it a step at a time? That single choice shapes everything else.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why management exists at all — the coordination problem it solves.
  • Distinguish a project (temporary, unique) from operations (ongoing, repeatable).
  • Describe the predictive (waterfall / PMP) model and its assumption of a knowable plan.
  • Describe empirical (Agile / Scrum) control and its three pillars: transparency, inspection, adaptation.
  • Decide which model fits a given situation using a simple uncertainty test — and set up your Week-1 board.

1.1Why Management Exists at All

One person building one thing needs no "management" — they just do it. Management appears the moment work is too big for one head and one pair of hands. As soon as several people must produce one coherent result, three problems show up that no amount of individual effort solves:

  • Coordination — who does what, in what order, so pieces fit together instead of colliding.
  • Resource allocation — finite time, money, and people must be pointed at the highest-value work.
  • Uncertainty — the future is unknown, so plans must be made and revised as reality arrives.

Every management framework in this course — PMP, Scrum, the Theory of Constraints, the people practices in First, Break All the Rules — is ultimately a different answer to those three questions. The deepest fork is how each one handles the third one, uncertainty. That fork is the whole subject of this lecture.

AnalogyManagement is the conductor of an orchestra. The musicians can each play beautifully alone, but without a shared tempo and cue, fifty soloists produce noise, not a symphony. The conductor adds no notes — they add coordination. Notice they also don't play every instrument: good management is about the spaces between people, not doing the work for them.

1.2Projects vs Operations

Work comes in two flavors, and conflating them is the most common beginner mistake. A project is temporary and produces a unique result: it has a start, an end, and is "done." Building a bridge, launching an app, planning a wedding. Operations are ongoing and repeatable: they keep producing the same kind of output indefinitely. Running the toll booth on the bridge, keeping the app's servers up, catering weddings every weekend.

This distinction matters because they are optimized for opposite things. A project is judged on hitting its one-time goal (scope, schedule, budget). An operation is judged on its steady flow — throughput, defect rate, cost per unit. That is exactly why the back half of this course pivots from project frameworks to the Theory of Constraints, which is fundamentally a way to make a repeating system flow faster.

1.3The Predictive Model: Plan-Then-Execute (Waterfall / PMP)

The predictive approach — often called waterfall, and the heritage of the classic PMP / PMBOK discipline — assumes the destination is knowable in advance. You invest heavily up front to define scope, then execute the plan in ordered phases, each flowing into the next like water down steps:

Requirements → Design → Build → Test → DeliverEach phase finishes before the next begins; change is controlled and discouraged once the plan is baselined.

PMP organizes any project into five process groups — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing — that run across the project's life. It's worth a hedge here: recent guidance (the PMBOK 7th edition, as of 2026 — verify against current PMI sources) deliberately shifted away from a rigid recipe toward 12 principles and 8 performance domains, and explicitly embraces adaptive (Agile) approaches alongside predictive ones. The "five process groups" remain the canonical mental model and are still tested, but PMP today is no longer "waterfall only." We unpack the backbone next week.

Predictive shines when uncertainty is low and the cost of changing course later is high: regulated construction, hardware, aerospace, contracts with fixed deliverables. It fails when the requirements themselves are uncertain — you can write a flawless 200-page plan for the wrong product.

Common mistake"Waterfall" is not a slur and "Agile" is not automatically superior. The real error is a model–problem mismatch: forcing rigid up-front planning onto genuinely unknown work (you'll build the wrong thing precisely), or sprinkling stand-ups onto a fixed-scope bridge (you'll add ceremony without flexibility you can't use). Match the control model to the uncertainty, not to fashion.

1.4The Empirical Model: Steer-As-You-Go (Agile / Scrum)

When you can't know the full destination up front — most software, research, new products, anything with shifting requirements — predictive planning is a fiction. Agile replaces it with empirical process control: make decisions based on what is observed, not what was assumed. Scrum, the most widely used Agile framework, rests on three pillars:

Transparency → Inspection → AdaptationMake the real state of work visible; inspect it frequently against the goal; adapt the plan as soon as you learn something.

Scrum operationalizes this in short, fixed-length cycles called Sprints (typically one to four weeks). Per the Scrum Guide, the framework has exactly:

  • Three accountabilities — Product Owner (maximizes value / orders the work), Scrum Master (serves the team and the process), Developers (build the Increment).
  • Three artifacts — Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, Increment — each with a commitment: the Product Goal, the Sprint Goal, and the Definition of Done.
  • Five events — the Sprint (the container) holding Sprint Planning, the Daily Scrum, the Sprint Review, and the Sprint Retrospective.

We devote all of Week 3 to these, so don't memorize them now — just notice the shape: a tight feedback loop that produces something usable every Sprint and re-plans from real evidence each time.

1.5Choosing: a Simple Uncertainty Test

Neither model is "better." The honest question is: how much do we know about the destination, and how costly is change? A rough but useful rule:

High requirement uncertainty  OR  cheap-to-change  ⟶  Empirical (Agile/Scrum)
Low requirement uncertainty  AND  expensive-to-change  ⟶  Predictive (PMP/waterfall)Many real programs are hybrid — predictive at the contract/milestone level, Agile inside each delivery team.

The deeper principle is the one Scrum's pillars name directly: when you cannot predict, you must shorten the feedback loop so mistakes are caught in days, not at the final delivery. When you genuinely can predict, a long feedback loop is cheaper because it avoids the overhead of constant replanning. The skill is reading the uncertainty honestly rather than defending a favorite method.

✦ Worked Example — choosing a model for two real situations

You manage two efforts. (A) A city contract to replace a pedestrian bridge — fixed budget, safety-regulated, penalties for late delivery. (B) A startup's new mobile app whose features keep shifting as early users react. Pick a control model for each and justify it.

  1. Score the uncertainty (A, bridge): requirements are well understood (engineering codes), and change is very expensive once steel is cut. Both factors point one way.
  2. Decision (A): Predictive / PMP. Invest up front in design and a baselined plan; run the five process groups; control change formally. A retrospective every two weeks adds ceremony with no upside here.
  3. Score the uncertainty (B, app): requirements are unknown and moving; the cost of changing a screen is low. Both factors point the other way.
  4. Decision (B): Empirical / Scrum. Run short Sprints, ship an Increment users can touch, and let the Sprint Review feed real evidence back into the Product Backlog. A 200-page up-front spec would just document guesses.
  5. Reflection: the bridge could still borrow Agile inside a software-control subsystem; the app could still set predictive milestones for an investor deadline. Hybrid is normal — match the model to each part's uncertainty, not the whole program to one label.

🛠️ Hands-On: stand up your first board

This course's running tool is a Kanban board — the same artifact whether you go predictive or Agile. We'll use a free board app (Trello or Notion) for the visual work, and a tiny Python/pandas board for anyone who prefers code. Week 1 setup: do this once, then reuse it every lecture and for the Week-10 capstone.

⬇ One-time setup (Week 1): pick a board tool

Option 1 — no-code (recommended to start): create a free account on a board app such as Trello or Notion. Make one board with three lists/columns: To Do, Doing, Done. That's a working Kanban board.

Option 2 — code path: with Python 3 installed (from python.org), set up an environment and install pandas:

python3 -m venv pmwork-env
source pmwork-env/bin/activate        # macOS / Linux
pmwork-env\Scripts\activate           # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install pandas

Verify: run python -c "import pandas; print(pandas.__version__)" — a version string means you're set.

Now model the same five tasks two ways and feel the difference. The snippet below builds a minimal board in pandas, then prints a count per column — the seed of the Gantt/Kanban tooling we grow all course.

# board.py — Lecture 1: a 3-column Kanban as a DataFrame
import pandas as pd

board = pd.DataFrame([
    {"task": "Define product goal",   "column": "Done"},
    {"task": "Draft first backlog",   "column": "Done"},
    {"task": "Build login screen",    "column": "Doing"},
    {"task": "Set up payments",       "column": "To Do"},
    {"task": "Write release notes",   "column": "To Do"},
])

order = ["To Do", "Doing", "Done"]
print(board.groupby("column").size().reindex(order, fill_value=0))
# To Do  2 / Doing 1 / Done 2  -> work-in-progress is visible at a glance

Try it: now imagine running these as predictive (all five planned and ordered up front, no re-ordering) versus empirical (you may re-rank "To Do" every Sprint as you learn). Same board, opposite philosophy of when you decide.

🔗 Where this is heading

This single fork — predict vs steer — organizes the whole course. Weeks 2–4 go deep on the two project models: the PMP process-group backbone, then Scrum's roles, artifacts, and events, then estimation and backlogs. Everything converges in the Week-10 capstone, where you run a real project blending Scrum and the Theory of Constraints.

6 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The fundamental reason management exists is to solve…
A. the need to write longer documents
B. coordination, resource allocation, and uncertainty when work exceeds one person
C. the requirement to hold daily meetings
D. a legal obligation to appoint a manager
Show answer

B. Management appears when work is too big for one person; its job is coordination, allocating finite resources, and handling uncertainty — not paperwork or meetings for their own sake.

Q2. Which is the best example of operations rather than a project?
A. Building a new bridge
B. Launching a brand-new app
C. Running the toll booth on a bridge every day, indefinitely
D. Planning a one-time wedding
Show answer

C. Operations are ongoing and repeatable with no "done." A, B, and D are all temporary efforts with a unique end result — i.e., projects.

Q3. The predictive (waterfall / PMP) model assumes that…
A. the destination is largely knowable up front, so you plan then execute in ordered phases
B. requirements are unknown and must be discovered each cycle
C. no plan should ever be written
D. teams must always work in two-week Sprints
Show answer

A. Predictive invests heavily in up-front scope and runs ordered phases. Discovering requirements each cycle (B) and Sprints (D) describe the empirical/Scrum approach.

Q4. The three pillars of empirical process control in Scrum are…
A. Initiating, Planning, Closing
B. Scope, Schedule, Budget
C. Throughput, Inventory, Operating Expense
D. Transparency, Inspection, Adaptation
Show answer

D. Transparency → Inspection → Adaptation. A names PMP process groups; C names the Theory of Constraints measures (Weeks 5–6); B is the classic "triple constraint."

Q5. Per the Scrum Guide, Scrum's three accountabilities are…
A. Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers
B. Manager, Worker, Client
C. Sponsor, Architect, Tester
D. Initiating, Executing, Closing
Show answer

A. The three accountabilities are Product Owner, Scrum Master, and Developers. The three artifacts (Product/Sprint Backlog, Increment) and five events are covered in Week 3.

Q6. A team faces highly uncertain, fast-shifting requirements where changing course is cheap. The better-fitting control model is…
A. strict predictive waterfall with a frozen up-front plan
B. no model at all — just improvise without any cadence
C. empirical / Agile (Scrum), shortening the feedback loop to learn quickly
D. whichever is currently most fashionable
Show answer

C. High uncertainty plus cheap change calls for empirical control: short Sprints and frequent inspection/adaptation. A long up-front plan (A) would just document guesses.

✓ What you learned

  • Management exists to solve coordination, resource allocation, and uncertainty once work outgrows one person.
  • A project is temporary and unique (ends in "done"); operations are ongoing and repeatable.
  • The predictive / PMP model plans-then-executes in ordered phases and assumes a knowable destination — with PMBOK 7 now embracing adaptive work too (verify current PMI sources).
  • The empirical / Scrum model steers via Transparency → Inspection → Adaptation, with three accountabilities, three artifacts, and five events.
  • You choose by uncertainty: high uncertainty / cheap change → Agile; low uncertainty / costly change → predictive; hybrids are normal — and you built your Week-1 board.
