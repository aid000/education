Week 9 · Lecture 9

Feedback, Delegation & Difficult Conversations

The day-to-day mechanics of leading people: hand off work by outcome not instruction, give feedback that actually changes behavior, and run a retrospective that fixes the system.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Delegate by outcome — specify the result and the constraints, not the steps.
  • Give feedback that is specific, timely, and behavior-focused (and know why "praise sandwiches" backfire).
  • Run a blameless Sprint Retrospective that produces one or two concrete, owned improvements.
  • Manage up and sideways — make your manager and your peers your allies, not obstacles.
  • Prepare for a difficult conversation with a repeatable structure instead of improvising under stress.

9.1Delegation Is About Outcomes, Not Instructions

The most common reason managers stay overloaded is that they delegate tasks — "do this, then this, then this" — instead of outcomes. Outcome-based delegation hands over a clearly defined result, the constraints that bound it, and the authority to choose the method. This is the same instinct behind First, Break All the Rules (Buckingham & Coffman): the second of the four keys is "define the right outcomes, then let people find their own route to them." Great managers set the destination and let each person navigate using their own strengths.

A useful way to calibrate how much to hand over is to match the level of delegation to the person's competence and confidence for that specific task:

Whatever rung you pick, an outcome-based handoff names five things: the result (what "done" looks like), the why (so they can make trade-offs), the constraints (budget, deadline, non-negotiables), the decision rights (what they can decide alone), and the check-in cadence.

AnalogyDelegating is like ordering from a great chef versus dictating a recipe. You say "a light seafood dish, ready by eight, nut-free" (outcome + constraints) and trust the chef's skill — you don't stand at the stove calling out each stir. If you find yourself dictating every stir, you haven't delegated; you've just added a relay step to work you're still doing yourself.

9.2Feedback That Changes Behavior

Most feedback fails for one of two reasons: it's vague ("be more proactive") or it's late (raised at the annual review, months after the moment). Effective feedback is specific, timely, and aimed at behavior and impact rather than personality. A widely used scaffold is SBI — Situation, Behavior, Impact:

Situation → Behavior → Impact"In yesterday's standup (S), you cut off Maria twice before she finished (B); she went quiet for the rest of the meeting and we lost her input (I)."

SBI works because it stays observable. "You're rude" is a verdict the other person can dispute; "you interrupted twice and she went quiet" is a fact they can act on. Notice it works identically for praise — name the situation, the specific behavior, and the good impact, so the person knows exactly what to repeat.

Common mistakeThe "praise sandwich" — bury criticism between two compliments — usually backfires. People learn to brace for the bad news the moment praise arrives, which devalues your genuine praise, and the actual message gets softened until it's missed entirely. Be direct and kind: state the issue plainly using SBI, then collaborate on the fix. Save standalone praise for its own moment so it lands as real.

9.3The Sprint Retrospective: Fixing the System, Not the People

Per the Scrum Guide, the Sprint Retrospective is the last of the five events (Sprint, Sprint Planning, Daily Scrum, Sprint Review, Sprint Retrospective) and it closes the Sprint. Its purpose is narrow and powerful: inspect how the last Sprint went regarding people, relationships, process, and tools, and decide on improvements to the way the team works. It is the team's primary feedback loop on itself.

The cardinal rule is blamelessness. The retro assumes everyone did their best given what they knew; the target is the system — the process, the hand-offs, the tooling — not any individual. This echoes the operations mindset from the Theory of Constraints weeks: you improve a system by finding and fixing its constraint, not by exhorting people to "try harder."

A simple, durable format: (1) set the stage and restate the blameless rule; (2) gather what happened — "what went well / what didn't / what puzzled us"; (3) generate insights into why; (4) decide on one or two improvements with a named owner; (5) close. The discipline is restraint: a team that picks one improvement and actually ships it beats a team that lists fifteen and does none.

9.4Managing Up and Sideways

You manage your reports down, but most of your leverage comes from the people you don't control: your manager (up) and your peers in other teams (sideways). You have no authority over them, so influence runs entirely on clarity, reliability, and trust.

Managing up

Managing up is not flattery — it is making your manager effective and getting what you need. The mechanics: understand their goals and pressures, communicate in their preferred format and cadence, surface problems early (a bad-news-late surprise is the fastest way to lose trust), and bring a recommendation, not just a problem. Tell them how they can best support you.

Managing sideways

Managing sideways — peers, dependencies, other teams — is pure trust-banking. You make and keep small commitments, you give help before you need it, and you make your dependencies explicit early (the Drum-Buffer-Rope idea from Week 6 applies socially too: signal the upstream team before you're blocked, not after). When a conflict comes, you have a relationship to draw on instead of a cold ask.

influence = credibility × (relationship + clarity)A schematic, not a formula: with no authority, your ability to get things done scales with how reliable you've proven to be, the trust you've banked, and how clearly you ask.

9.5Difficult Conversations: a Repeatable Structure

The hardest conversations — underperformance, conflict, a missed commitment — go badly when we improvise under stress. A structure removes the improvisation. A reliable five-step skeleton:

  • Prepare: write the one core message in a single sentence before you walk in. If you can't, you're not ready.
  • Open with the facts, not the verdict: lead with observable behavior and its impact (SBI again), not a character judgment.
  • Ask and listen: genuinely seek their view — there is almost always context you don't have. This is where most difficult conversations are actually resolved.
  • Agree on a specific change: what will be different, by when, and how you'll both know.
  • Follow up: a calendar hold, not a vague "let's check in." Closing the loop is what makes the conversation real rather than theater.

Connecting it back to First, Break All the Rules: its fourth key is "find the right fit." Sometimes a difficult conversation reveals that the problem isn't effort or attitude — it's a person in a role that doesn't match their talents. The kind move and the effective move are the same: be honest early, and help them get to a role where their strengths are the job.

✦ Worked Example — delegating a release write-up by outcome

You manage a small team. Sam, a capable developer who has never written customer-facing copy, will own the next release notes. Walk through an outcome-based handoff and pick the delegation rung.

  1. Result: "A one-page release note our customers can read without us in the room — what changed, why it helps them, and any action they must take." That's the destination, not the steps.
  2. Why: "Three big customers asked for clearer notes; this directly affects renewals." Now Sam can make trade-offs (clarity over completeness) on their own.
  3. Constraints: "Ship by Thursday 3pm, no internal jargon, run it past Support for accuracy." Non-negotiables stated up front.
  4. Decision rights & rung: Sam is strong technically but new to this kind of writing, so this is not rung 5. Pick rung 3 — "recommend, I approve": Sam drafts and chooses the structure; you review once before it ships. Next release, if it goes well, climb to rung 4.
  5. Check-in cadence: "Send me the outline tomorrow morning, full draft Wednesday." Light enough to stay out of the way, frequent enough to catch a wrong turn early.
  6. Feedback after, in SBI form: "On the upgrade section (S) you led with the customer benefit before the technical detail (B); that's exactly the shift we needed and Support flagged zero edits (I)." Specific, timely, repeatable.

🛠️ Hands-On: run a retrospective board in Trello or Notion

You already set up your free Trello / Notion workspace back in Week 1 — reuse it here (no new install needed). Stand up a simple retrospective board and a delegation tracker, then practice the SBI scaffold.

Build a retro board (Trello list or Notion board) with four columns and time-box each step:

# Retrospective board — columns
[ Went well ]   [ Didn't go well ]   [ Puzzled us ]   [ ACTION (owner + when) ]

# Rules of the room (pin these to the board)
- Blameless: we critique the system, not the people.
- Everyone adds at least one card (silent writing, 5 min).
- Group similar cards, then dot-vote the top themes.
- Leave with ONE or TWO action cards — each has an owner and a date.
- An action card with no owner is a wish, not a decision.

Practice feedback — write three real SBI notes (one praise, two constructive) as cards. Each must fill in all three slots:

# SBI feedback template (one card each)
Situation:  when / where it happened (be specific)
Behavior:   what they actually did or said (observable)
Impact:     the effect on the work, the team, or you

# Self-check: if "Behavior" contains an adjective about
# the person ("lazy", "rude"), rewrite it as an action.

Move the winning ACTION card straight into next Sprint's backlog so it can't quietly vanish. That single habit — retro actions become backlog items — is what separates teams that improve from teams that just vent.

🔗 Where this connects

Everything here is the human face of the systems you've studied all course. The retrospective is Scrum's inspect-and-adapt loop (Weeks 1, 3) pointed at the team itself; blameless improvement is the Theory of Constraints mindset (Weeks 5–6) — fix the constraint in the system, not the worker. Outcome-based delegation and strengths-based feedback come straight from the four keys of First, Break All the Rules (Weeks 7–8). Next week you put all of it together: you'll run a real project, hold real retros, and make real delegation and feedback decisions under the constraints of a live plan.

✓ What you learned

  • Outcome-based delegation: hand over the result, why, constraints, decision rights, and cadence — and match the delegation rung to the person and task.
  • Feedback that works is specific, timely, and behavior-focused via SBI — and why the praise sandwich backfires.
  • The Sprint Retrospective as a blameless inspect-and-adapt loop that yields one or two owned improvements pulled into the next Sprint.
  • Managing up and sideways — influence without authority, built on reliability, early signals, and banked trust.
  • A repeatable structure for difficult conversations: prepare, lead with facts, listen, agree on a change, follow up.

Model response

I largely agree, with a boundary. First, Break All the Rules (Buckingham & Coffman) found that great managers treat enduring talents as fixed and pour their energy into matching people to roles where those talents shine — that's the third and fourth keys, "focus on strengths" and "find the right fit." The argument isn't that people never improve; it's that the highest-leverage move is amplifying a genuine strength rather than grinding a weakness up to mediocre.

Concrete example: a developer who was a slow, reluctant public speaker but an exceptional written communicator and code reviewer. The old instinct was to send him to presentation training. Instead the team made him the owner of design docs and asynchronous review — work that ran on his strength. Decision quality rose, onboarding got faster from his docs, and he was far more engaged. A small amount of presentation coaching still helped him clear a basic bar, but the win came from building the role around the strength, not from fixing the weakness.

The boundary: a weakness that's a true threshold requirement can't be ignored. If a role genuinely demands the missing skill (a pilot must land the plane), you either manage around it, partner the person with a complement, or — honestly and early — help them find a better fit. Strengths-based management isn't an excuse to skip hard conversations; it's a smarter place to aim your limited energy.
