Week 10 · Lecture 10

Capstone: Run a Real Project (Scrum + ToC)

Nine weeks of theory now collapse into one habit: pick a real goal, run it as a four-week Scrum, find the constraint, and manage the humans doing the work. This is where the course becomes yours.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Synthesize PMP framing, Scrum mechanics, Theory of Constraints, and great-manager principles into a single operating model.
  • Choose a real goal, write a Product Goal and Definition of Done, and slice it into a small Product Backlog.
  • Run four week-long Sprints with the five Scrum events, then locate and exploit the project's one constraint.
  • Read a burndown and a cumulative-flow diagram to see whether your system is actually flowing.
  • Assemble the capstone deliverable and know exactly what to submit.

10.1One Picture: How the Four Frameworks Fit Together

This course handed you four lenses that look unrelated on a syllabus but click together in practice. The trick of the capstone is to stop treating them as separate "topics" and see them as four altitudes of the same project.

  • PMP / PMBOK sets the frame. Are you predictive (plan-driven, fixed scope) or adaptive (steering with feedback)? Even an Agile project still passes through the five process groups — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing — just in tight, repeating loops rather than one long arc.
  • Scrum is the engine. Three accountabilities, three artifacts, five events: a lightweight cadence that turns a backlog into shipped increments one Sprint at a time.
  • Theory of Constraints is the diagnosis. Of all the work in flight, one step is the bottleneck. Goldratt's question — "what limits throughput right now?" — tells you where to spend your scarce attention.
  • Great-manager principles are the people layer. Who does what, and are they in the seat where their talents shine? Frameworks don't ship anything; people do.
AnalogyThink of running a project like driving a car on a long trip. PMP is the road map and the rules of the road — where you're going and how decisions get made. Scrum is the engine and the steering wheel — the thing that actually converts effort into forward motion, adjusted at every turn. Theory of Constraints is the dashboard warning light that says "the engine is fine, but you're low on one specific fluid" — it points at the single thing slowing you down. And the great-manager keys are about having the right driver, navigator, and mechanic, each doing what they're best at. A great map with the wrong driver still ends in a ditch.

10.2The Capstone Project: "Run a Real Goal as a 4-Week Scrum + ToC Analysis"

Here is the deliverable this whole course has been building toward. You will take a real goal you actually care about — not a toy — and run it as a four-week Scrum while keeping a Theory-of-Constraints log. The goal should be something that genuinely takes four weeks of part-time effort: launch a small side project, plan and execute an event, write a short course or zine, ship a portfolio site, train for a measurable fitness target, or run a small process for a club or team.

The shape of the project

  • Initiate (Day 0). Write a one-paragraph Product Goal — the outcome that makes the whole thing worthwhile. Name your "team," even if it's just you plus one helper.
  • Plan a backlog. Slice the goal into a Product Backlog of small, valuable items, ordered by priority. Write a Definition of Done that says what "finished" means for every item.
  • Run four Sprints (one week each). Each Sprint: a Sprint Planning that sets a Sprint Goal and pulls items into the Sprint Backlog; short Daily Scrums (even a two-line daily note counts solo); a Sprint Review of the Increment; and a Retrospective on the process.
  • Keep a ToC log. Each week, name the one step that is the constraint (what's the work piling up behind?) and what you did to exploit or elevate it.
  • Close & reflect. Did you hit the Product Goal? What did the four keys teach you about who should have done what?

Notice the PMP process groups are all present — Initiating and Closing happen once, while Planning, Executing, and Monitoring & Controlling repeat inside every Sprint. That is the adaptive frame in miniature.

10.3Wiring Theory of Constraints into Your Sprints

Scrum tells you to inspect and adapt; Theory of Constraints tells you what to adapt. Goldratt's The Goal (a business novel, not a textbook) frames every operation around three measures and a repeating procedure. Recall them:

Throughput (rate the system produces goal-units) · Inventory (money tied up in unfinished work) · Operating ExpenseThe three ToC measures. You improve a system by raising Throughput while holding or lowering the other two.

And the five focusing steps you ran in Weeks 5–6:

1. IDENTIFY the constraint  →  2. EXPLOIT it  →  3. SUBORDINATE everything else to it  →  4. ELEVATE it  →  5. REPEAT (don't let inertia become the constraint)After elevating, the constraint usually moves somewhere else — so you go back to step 1.

In a capstone Scrum, the constraint is whatever your work-in-progress is piling up behind. If three items are "waiting for review" and nothing is waiting to be coded, your constraint is review, not coding — and pulling in more coding work only grows inventory. That is the lesson of Drum-Buffer-Rope: the slowest step (the drum) sets the pace, a buffer protects it from starving, and a rope ties the release of new work to the drum's actual capacity so you don't flood the system.

Common mistakeThe most common capstone error is optimizing the wrong step. People speed up the part of the work they enjoy — usually not the bottleneck — and feel productive while the project moves no faster. If you make a non-constraint faster, you have not improved throughput; you have only built a bigger pile of inventory in front of the real constraint. Always ask "where is work waiting?" before you ask "how do I work faster?"

10.4Reading the Flow: Burndown & Cumulative-Flow

You can't manage a system you can't see. Two simple charts make your project's health visible without any special tooling. A burndown chart tracks work remaining in a Sprint against time — a straight line down to zero is the ideal; a flat shelf means you're blocked. A cumulative-flow diagram (CFD) stacks the count of items in each state over time; a band that keeps widening is inventory piling up — your constraint, made visible.

Read together, these charts connect Scrum and ToC: the burndown tells you whether the Sprint is on track, and the cumulative-flow band tells you where the constraint lives. A capstone that includes one hand-drawn burndown plus a one-line CFD reading per week earns full marks for "made the system visible."

10.5The People Layer: Who Does What, and Why

A project plan with the wrong people in the wrong seats still fails. The capstone asks you to apply the four keys from Buckingham & Coffman's First, Break All the Rules — even if your "team" is small:

  • Select for talent. When you assign backlog items, match each to the natural strength that the item rewards — the person who loves detail does the detail-heavy work.
  • Define the right outcomes, not the steps. Your Definition of Done states the result; let people find their own road there.
  • Focus on strengths. Spend your management energy growing what people are good at, not grinding on weaknesses you can manage around.
  • Find the right fit. If someone keeps stalling on a class of work, that may be a fit problem, not an effort problem — re-deploy rather than re-scold.

There is a deep link to Theory of Constraints here, and it's worth stating plainly: the manager often sits at the constraint of the whole people system. The Q12 engagement survey makes this measurable. A few representative items, paraphrased: "Do I know what is expected of me at work?", "Do I have the materials and equipment I need to do my work right?", and "Do I have the opportunity to do what I do best every day?" The book's claim, backed by a large body of data, is that a team's average Q12 score predicts that team's outcomes — and that the items are things a manager can actually change.

✦ Worked Example — diagnosing a stalled capstone Sprint with all four lenses

Your goal is to launch a small online store in four weeks. End of Sprint 2: the burndown flat-lined for two days, and your board shows 5 items in "Ready to Photograph," 0 in "Writing Descriptions," and 1 in "Publishing." You and one teammate run it. Walk the four lenses to decide what to do next.

  1. PMP frame (Monitoring & Controlling): The Sprint Goal — "publish 6 products" — is at risk. This is a steering moment, not a re-plan-everything moment; adjust inside the Sprint.
  2. ToC — identify the constraint: Work is piling up behind photography (5 waiting), while writing is starved (0 in queue). Photography is the drum. Speeding up writing would only grow inventory — the classic wrong-step trap.
  3. ToC — exploit & subordinate: Make the photography step never idle: batch-shoot all 5 products in one session, pre-stage the backdrop, and have the writer feed product facts to the photographer instead of starting new writing. The rope: stop pulling new "to photograph" items until the queue drains.
  4. People (four keys): Your teammate is a natural writer and dreads fussy photo setups (a fit problem). Assign all photography to the partner who enjoys it and let the writer draft descriptions from the photos as they land — strengths matched to outcomes.
  5. Close the loop (Retrospective): The retro item: "add a WIP limit of 2 on the Photograph column next Sprint so we surface the constraint earlier." You've turned a stall into a process improvement — the fifth focusing step.

🛠️ Build Your Capstone Board (and a 30-second flow check)

Use the free tool you set up in Week 1 — Trello or Notion (free tier) — for the board; one-line reminder only: a board is just columns (states) and cards (backlog items). No new install needed here.

Step 1 — Board (Trello / Notion, free). Create five columns: Backlog · This Sprint · Doing · Review · Done. Add a card per backlog item. Set a WIP limit (e.g. "max 2") on Doing and Review — when a column hits its limit, you must finish before starting, which forces the constraint into the open.

Step 2 — Daily note. Each working day, jot three lines (the solo Daily Scrum): done yesterday · plan today · blockers. A blocker that repeats is your constraint.

Step 3 — Flow check in pandas. Export your card counts per state and spot where inventory is piling up — that column is the constraint.

# capstone_flow.py — count work-in-progress per column to find the constraint
import pandas as pd

# one row per card: its current state and story-point size
cards = pd.DataFrame({
    "item":  ["shoot p1","shoot p2","shoot p3","shoot p4","shoot p5",
              "publish p0","write p0"],
    "state": ["Review","Review","Review","Review","Review",
              "Doing","Done"],
    "points":[2,2,2,2,2,3,3],
})

wip = cards.groupby("state")["points"].agg(["count","sum"])
print(wip)
# the state with the biggest 'count' that ISN'T 'Done' is where work is
# waiting — your current constraint (here: Review, 5 items stuck).
constraint = wip.drop("Done", errors="ignore")["count"].idxmax()
print("Constraint this week:", constraint)
# Constraint this week: Review

Run this at the end of each Sprint. Paste the one-line result into your ToC log: "Week N constraint = ___, exploited by ___." Four such lines are your Theory-of-Constraints analysis.

10.6What to Submit — the Capstone Deliverable

Keep it tight. The capstone is one short document (2–4 pages or a single board export plus a page of notes) with these six parts. Treat this as your Definition of Done for the project itself:

  • 1. Product Goal & Definition of Done — one paragraph each. What outcome, and what "finished" means.
  • 2. Product Backlog — your ordered item list (a board screenshot is fine), with sizes/estimates.
  • 3. Four Sprint records — for each Sprint: the Sprint Goal, what you shipped (the Increment), and one Retrospective improvement.
  • 4. Flow evidence — at least one burndown (hand-drawn is fine) and a one-line cumulative-flow reading per week.
  • 5. Theory-of-Constraints log — four lines: the weekly constraint and how you exploited or elevated it.
  • 6. People reflection — a short paragraph on how the four keys shaped who did what, and what you'd change.
AnalogyYour capstone document is a flight log, not a flight plan. Nobody expects a perfect, turbulence-free trip — they want to see that you knew your altitude (PMP), kept the engine running each leg (Scrum), spotted the warning light and acted on it (ToC), and flew with the right crew (the four keys). A messy project that you can read beats a tidy project you can't explain.

🔗 Where this goes next

Everything here scales. The same loop — frame, drive, diagnose the constraint, manage the people — runs a two-person side project or a hundred-person program; only the artifacts get heavier. From here you can go deeper into any one lens: PMP/PMBOK certification for the predictive frame, the broader Scrum and SAFe literature for scaling Agile, Goldratt's later works for ToC in supply chains and finance, and Buckingham's follow-ups for strengths-based leadership. But resist collecting frameworks for their own sake. The operators who get things done are the ones who can hold all four lenses lightly and switch between them in the same conversation — which is exactly what your capstone proves you can now do.
