The PMP Backbone — Process Groups & Knowledge Areas

Behind the certification jargon, predictive project management is a simple loop and a short checklist of things never to forget. Learn the skeleton once and the rest hangs off it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Name the five PMBOK Process Groups (Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing) and explain why they form a loop, not a straight line.
  • Map the core knowledge areas — scope, schedule, cost, risk, stakeholders — onto that loop.
  • Explain what a project charter is, what it must contain, and why nothing else should start without one.
  • Distinguish a process group from a project phase, and predictive ("plan-driven") management from adaptive/Agile.
  • Draft a one-page charter and a tiny status check for a real project.

2.1From "Two Ways" to the Predictive Backbone

Last week (Lecture 1) you saw the big fork: predictive management plans the whole thing up front and then controls against the plan, while adaptive/Agile management works in short cycles and re-plans constantly. This week we open up the predictive side — the world the PMP credential and the PMBOK Guide describe — because its vocabulary is the lingua franca of project work everywhere.

The Project Management Institute's PMBOK Guide (Project Management Body of Knowledge) organizes predictive project work two ways at once. The Process Groups answer "what kind of work are we doing right now?" and the knowledge areas answer "what subject is that work about?" Think of them as the rows and columns of a single grid.

Note — frameworks evolveAs of 2026, recent editions of the PMBOK Guide have shifted emphasis toward principles and performance domains rather than the classic five-process-group / ten-knowledge-area matrix. The five Process Groups remain the clearest mental model for learners and are still widely taught and tested, so we use them here — but verify the exact structure against the current official PMI publication before quoting it on an exam or résumé.

2.2The Five Process Groups — a Loop, Not a Line

A common beginner mistake is to picture the five groups as five sequential phases: first you Initiate, then you Plan, then you Execute, and so on, like a relay. They are not phases. They are types of work, and several of them run at the same time. The one that truly runs throughout is Monitoring & Controlling — it wraps everything.

Initiate → Plan ⇄ Execute  ( all under )  Monitor & Control  → ClosePlan and Execute iterate against each other; Monitoring & Controlling surrounds both and can send you back to re-plan.

Here is each group in one line:

  • Initiating — authorize the project and name who's in charge. Output: the project charter.
  • Planning — decide scope, schedule, budget, risks, and who's involved before money is spent at scale.
  • Executing — do the work, build the deliverables, coordinate the team.
  • Monitoring & Controlling — measure progress against the plan and correct course; runs the entire time.
  • Closing — formally accept the result, release the team, and capture lessons learned.

2.3The Knowledge Areas — What the Work Is About

If the Process Groups are when, the knowledge areas are what about. The classic PMBOK lists ten, but five carry most of the day-to-day weight for a small project. The first three are often called the triple constraint because moving one usually moves the others.

Scope

What is, and is not, included. The deliverables and the work to produce them. Uncontrolled growth of scope — scope creep — is the single most common way small projects quietly die.

Schedule

When things happen and in what order. Built from activities, their dependencies, and estimates (Lecture 4 goes deep on estimation). The classic artifacts are the network diagram and the Gantt chart.

Cost

The budget and how spending is tracked against it. The headline tool is earned value, which compares planned, actual, and earned amounts to flag overruns early.

Risk

Anything uncertain that could help or hurt the project. You don't eliminate risk; you identify, rank, and plan responses for it, then watch the top items in every status check.

Stakeholders

Everyone affected by or able to affect the project — sponsor, users, team, neighbors. The job is to know who they are, what they need, and how often to communicate. Most "scope" fights are really un-managed stakeholder expectations.

AnalogyThink of the Process Groups as the days of a build and the knowledge areas as the trades on site. Every day (Initiating, Planning, Executing…) the plumber, electrician, and framer (scope, schedule, cost, risk, stakeholders) all show up — but how busy each trade is depends on which day it is. You don't "finish plumbing" then "start scheduling"; both run across all the days, in different amounts.

2.4The Project Charter — the Document That Starts Everything

The single most important output of Initiating is the project charter: a short document that formally authorizes the project, names the project manager, and gives that person authority to use resources. No charter, no project — it is the green light, signed by the sponsor.

A good one-page charter answers six questions:

  • Why? — the business case / problem being solved.
  • What? — high-level scope and the main deliverables (and what's explicitly out).
  • Who? — sponsor, project manager (named, with authority), key stakeholders.
  • When? — milestones and a target end date.
  • How much? — a budget or order-of-magnitude cost.
  • What could go wrong? — the top known risks and assumptions.
Common mistakeA charter is not a project plan. Beginners stuff it with detailed task lists and Gantt charts — that's Planning work, done later. The charter is deliberately thin: it grants authority and direction, fits on a page, and is signed before detailed planning begins. If you can't say who the project manager is and who signs off, you don't have a charter yet.
✦ Worked Example — chartering a 6-week community food-drive app

A volunteer team wants a simple web app so local pantries can post needs and donors can claim them. Walk the Initiating decision step by step and produce the charter's bones.

  1. Why (business case): Pantries currently coordinate by group text; needs get missed. A shared board would cut waste and double donor reach. That justifies the effort — Initiating confirmed.
  2. What (scope): In — post a need, claim a need, basic login. Out — payments, delivery routing, native mobile app. Writing the "out" list now prevents scope creep in week 4.
  3. Who: Sponsor = the food-bank director (signs the charter). Project manager = Maya (named, with authority to assign the four volunteers). Stakeholders = pantry staff, donors, the IT volunteer hosting it.
  4. When: Milestones — design (wk 1), claimable board working (wk 3), pilot with two pantries (wk 5), launch (wk 6).
  5. How much: $0 cash (free hosting tier) + ~120 volunteer-hours. That order-of-magnitude figure is enough for a charter; detailed estimates come in Planning.
  6. Top risks: (1) volunteer availability drops near exams; (2) pantries won't adopt a new tool. Both get an owner and a watch item — not a solution yet.
  7. Result: A one-page, signed charter. Only now does Maya move into Planning to build the real schedule and backlog. Monitoring & Controlling — a weekly check against these six answers — starts the moment work does.

🛠️ Hands-On: charter + a one-glance status board

Pick your tool from Week 1 — Trello or Notion (both free). One-line reminder: if you set these up last week, just open your existing workspace; no reinstall needed.

Part A — Charter (Notion or a Trello card description). Create one page titled Project Charter and fill the six prompts. Keep it to one screen:

PROJECT CHARTER  —  <project name>
  Why .............. one-sentence business case / problem
  What (in) ........ 3-5 main deliverables
  What (out) ....... what we will NOT do  <-- the scope-creep shield
  Who .............. Sponsor: ___   PM: ___ (authority)   Stakeholders: ___
  When ............. milestone -> target date  (x3-4)
  How much ......... budget or rough effort
  Top risks ........ risk -> owner  (x2-3)
  Signed: __________________ (sponsor)        Date: ______

Part B — Map a board to the Process Groups. In Trello, make four lists: Planning · Executing · Monitoring · Closing (Initiating already happened — it produced the charter). Drop a card per task and move it rightward as it progresses. The Monitoring list is your weekly review queue, not a dead end.

Optional — a tiny Python status check (pandas). If you prefer code, track tasks in a table and print a one-line health read:

# status.py — knowledge-area health snapshot
import pandas as pd

tasks = pd.DataFrame([
    {"task": "Design board UI",   "area": "scope",    "status": "done"},
    {"task": "Wire claim button", "area": "scope",    "status": "doing"},
    {"task": "Pilot w/ 2 pantries","area": "schedule", "status": "todo"},
    {"task": "Volunteer backup",  "area": "risk",     "status": "todo"},
])

by_status = tasks["status"].value_counts()
print(by_status)                                # todo / doing / done counts
pct_done = (tasks["status"] == "done").mean() * 100
print(f"complete: {pct_done:.0f}%")             # a crude % for Monitoring
print(tasks.groupby("area")["status"]
           .apply(lambda s: (s == "done").mean())) # progress per knowledge area

Add a "cost"-area row and a "stakeholder" communication task so all five areas appear — then your one-glance board really does cover the whole backbone.

🔗 Where this is heading

The predictive backbone is one answer to "how do we run work." Next week we open the other answer in detail: Scrum replaces the big up-front plan with short Sprints, swaps the project-manager-with-authority for three accountabilities (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Developers), and turns the charter's scope list into a living Product Backlog. Many real teams blend the two — a lightweight charter to authorize the work, then Agile cycles to deliver it. Later in this course, the Theory of Constraints (Weeks 5–6) will give you a different lens again: instead of asking "are we on plan?", it asks "where is the one bottleneck choking the whole flow?"

Q1. Which of the five Process Groups runs continuously, alongside all the others?
A. Initiating
B. Closing
C. Monitoring & Controlling
D. Planning
Show answer

C. Monitoring & Controlling spans the whole project, measuring progress against the plan and triggering corrections (or re-planning). The others are concentrated at specific times.

Q2. The project charter is primarily produced during which Process Group?
A. Initiating
B. Planning
C. Executing
D. Closing
Show answer

A. Initiating authorizes the project; its key output is the charter, which names the project manager and grants authority. Detailed plans come later, in Planning.

Q3. "Scope, schedule, cost" linked so that changing one usually forces a change in the others is known as the…
A. definition of done
B. triple constraint
C. drum-buffer-rope
D. burndown chart
Show answer

B. The triple constraint (scope–schedule–cost, with quality balancing in the middle) is the core trade-off of predictive planning. The others belong to Scrum (A, D) and the Theory of Constraints (C).

Q4. A stakeholder keeps adding "just one more feature" mid-project until the deadline slips. This is best described as…
A. earned value
B. a risk response
C. scope creep driven by un-managed stakeholder expectations
D. a milestone
Show answer

C. Uncontrolled growth of scope is scope creep, and it usually traces back to stakeholder expectations that weren't agreed and written down — which is why the charter lists what's explicitly out.

Q5. Which statement about the Process Groups is most accurate?
A. They are five strictly sequential phases; each must fully finish before the next begins
B. They are types of work that overlap in time, and Planning/Executing can iterate
C. They only apply to software projects
D. They replace knowledge areas entirely
Show answer

B. Process Groups are categories of work, not a relay of phases. Planning and Executing cycle, and Monitoring & Controlling can send you back to re-plan. Knowledge areas (the "what about") run across all of them.

Q6. Your charter draft contains a 40-row Gantt chart and a detailed task breakdown. What's wrong?
A. Charters should never mention dates at all
B. Nothing — that's exactly what a charter is
C. Charters belong to Closing, not Initiating
D. That detail is Planning work; a charter stays thin — authority, direction, milestones, top risks
Show answer

D. Detailed schedules and task breakdowns are produced in Planning. The charter is deliberately one page: it authorizes the work and points the direction, with milestones (not a full Gantt).

✓ What you learned

  • The five Process Groups — Initiating, Planning, Executing, Monitoring & Controlling, Closing — are types of work that overlap, with Monitoring & Controlling running throughout.
  • The core knowledge areas — scope, schedule, cost, risk, stakeholders — are the "what about," cutting across every group; scope/schedule/cost form the triple constraint.
  • The project charter authorizes the project, names the PM, and answers why/what/who/when/how-much/what-could-go-wrong — on one page, signed before detailed planning.
  • A Process Group is not a phase, and a charter is not a plan — confusing either is the classic beginner error.
  • You drafted a real one-page charter and mapped tasks to a Process-Group board (Trello/Notion or a pandas snapshot).
