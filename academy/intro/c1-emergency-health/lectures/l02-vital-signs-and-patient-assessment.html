Week 2 · Lecture 2

Vital Signs & Patient Assessment

Numbers and a few sharp questions turn a vague "they look bad" into a working picture of what's wrong — and whether it's getting worse.

⚠ Educational only — not medical advice This lecture is exam-prep familiarization with the kind of material on the U.S. NREMT EMT cognitive exam. It is NOT EMT certification, not a license to treat, and not clinical guidance. Nothing here qualifies you to assess or treat a real patient. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the U.S.) and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Take a focused history with the SAMPLE and OPQRST mnemonics and say what each letter buys you.
  • Define the four core vital signs — pulse, respirations, blood pressure, SpO₂ — and recall approximate normal adult ranges.
  • Explain how each vital is measured and what a high or low reading suggests.
  • Read a set of vitals as a trend, choosing a reassessment interval based on how stable the patient is.
  • Tabulate and plot a series of vital signs in Python with pandas + Matplotlib to spot deterioration early.

2.1Where Assessment Sits: After Size-Up, Before Everything Else

In Week 1 you learned the primary assessment (ABCDE) — the 60-second sweep for immediate life threats: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure. Once that sweep finds no uncorrected killer, you move to the secondary assessment: a focused history and a full set of vital signs. This is where assessment turns from "is this patient dying right now?" into "what is the problem, and which direction is it heading?"

The NREMT EMT cognitive exam organizes its questions around five content domains; patient assessment skills like the ones in this lecture cut across all of them. As of 2026 the approximate domain split — verify against the current NREMT test plan before relying on exact percentages — is roughly:

2.2SAMPLE — the History in Six Letters

You rarely have time for a leisurely interview. SAMPLE is a checklist that captures the history that actually changes decisions, in the order you'd usually ask it:

  • S — Signs & Symptoms. What you observe (signs) plus what the patient reports (symptoms). "What's bothering you most right now?"
  • A — Allergies. Medications, foods, environmental. Critical before giving or assisting with any drug.
  • M — Medications. Prescription, over-the-counter, supplements. Often the fastest clue to a patient's underlying conditions.
  • P — Past pertinent history. Relevant prior illnesses, surgeries, or similar episodes. "Has this happened before?"
  • L — Last oral intake. When and what they last ate or drank — matters for surgery, diabetes, and aspiration risk.
  • E — Events leading up. What was happening just before symptoms began. Sets the whole scene in context.
AnalogySAMPLE is the "intake form" a detective fills out at a crime scene: who is the victim, what are they sensitive to, what's in their system, what's their backstory, when did they last "check in," and what just happened. Each letter is a line you'd hate to leave blank when handing the case to the next investigator (the hospital).

2.3OPQRST — Interrogating a Symptom

Where SAMPLE covers the whole patient, OPQRST drills into one complaint — classically pain, but it works for shortness of breath, dizziness, almost anything. It is the history-of-present-illness, turned into six questions:

O · P · Q · R · S · TOnset — Provocation/Palliation — Quality — Region/Radiation — Severity — Time
  • O — Onset: "What were you doing when it started? Sudden or gradual?"
  • P — Provocation / Palliation: "What makes it worse? What makes it better?"
  • Q — Quality: "Describe it — sharp, dull, crushing, burning?" (Use their words.)
  • R — Region / Radiation: "Where exactly? Does it move or spread anywhere?"
  • S — Severity: "On a scale of 0 to 10, how bad is it now? How bad at its worst?"
  • T — Time: "When did it start? Has it been constant or come and gone?"

A complaint of crushing chest pain that radiates to the left arm, rated 8/10, that started 20 minutes ago at rest, is a very different story from sharp pain that worsens with a deep breath — and OPQRST is what surfaces the difference quickly.

Common mistakeDon't lead the patient. Asking "Is it a crushing pain that goes down your arm?" plants the answer. Ask open questions ("What does it feel like?") and let them describe it. Leading questions corrupt the one data source — the patient — that you can't get anywhere else.

2.4The Four Core Vital Signs

Vital signs are objective, repeatable measurements of the body's most basic functions. The four you take first — and what each roughly means in a resting adult — are below. These are approximate adult reference ranges for orientation only; real ranges vary by age, fitness, and source.

Pulse 60–100 /min  ·  Respirations 12–20 /min
BP ≈ 120/80 mmHg  ·  SpO₂ 95–100 % Approximate resting-adult norms (as of 2026 — verify against current clinical references). Children run faster pulse and respirations.

Pulse (heart rate)

Palpate the radial artery (wrist) in a conscious adult, or the carotid (neck) if you can't feel a radial. Count for 15 seconds and multiply by 4, or 30 seconds × 2 if the rhythm is irregular. Note rate, rhythm (regular/irregular), and strength (strong/weak/thready). A fast pulse (tachycardia) can signal shock, fever, pain, or anxiety; a very slow one (bradycardia) can signal a cardiac or neurologic problem.

Respirations

Count without the patient knowing — people change their breathing when watched. Count chest rises for 30 seconds × 2. Note rate, effort (easy vs labored, accessory-muscle use), and quality (clear vs wheezing/gurgling). This vital ties directly into Week 3's airway and ventilation material.

Blood pressure

Reported as systolic / diastolic in mmHg — the pressure when the heart contracts over the pressure when it relaxes. Measured with a cuff (sphygmomanometer) and stethoscope, or an automated monitor. A falling systolic pressure is a late and ominous sign of shock; very high pressure raises concern for stroke or other emergencies.

SpO₂ — oxygen saturation

A pulse oximeter clips on a fingertip and estimates the percentage of hemoglobin carrying oxygen. 95–100% is typical; below ~94% suggests hypoxia and prompts oxygen and a closer look at the airway. Beware false readings: cold fingers, poor perfusion, nail polish, and carbon-monoxide poisoning (which can read falsely normal) all fool it.

2.5One Reading Is a Snapshot — a Trend Is the Story

A single set of vitals tells you where the patient is now. The real diagnostic power comes from repeating them and watching the direction of travel. A pulse that climbs from 88 → 104 → 122 while systolic pressure slides 124 → 110 → 96 is the classic fingerprint of compensated shock tipping toward decompensation — even if no single reading looks alarming on its own.

How often you reassess depends on how sick the patient is. A widely taught convention:

Unstable patient → reassess ≈ every 5 minutes
Stable patient → reassess ≈ every 15 minutes Common teaching guideline — local protocols and the patient's trajectory always override the clock.

"Unstable" means an abnormal or changing vital, an altered mental status, or a serious mechanism of injury. When in doubt, treat as unstable and reassess sooner.

AnalogyOne set of vitals is a single frame from a movie; reassessment is playing the frames in sequence. A still photo of a car can't tell you if it's parked or about to crash — but three frames showing it accelerating toward a wall tell you everything. Trends, not snapshots, predict the ending.
✦ Worked Example — reading a trend on a real complaint

A 58-year-old reports chest discomfort. Walk the assessment and decide your reassessment interval. (Educational scenario — not a treatment protocol.)

  1. OPQRST: Onset 30 min ago at rest; Provocation — nothing helps; Quality — "pressure, like a weight"; Region — center chest, radiating to the left jaw; Severity — 7/10; Time — constant. The pattern is concerning for a cardiac cause.
  2. SAMPLE: Signs/Symptoms — pale, sweaty; Allergies — none; Medications — a blood-pressure pill; Past history — high blood pressure; Last intake — lunch 2 h ago; Events — was watching TV. The history reinforces cardiac concern.
  3. First vitals: Pulse 96 (regular), Resp 20, BP 148/92, SpO₂ 95%. Borderline-high BP and a brisk pulse fit pain and stress.
  4. Classify stability: Concerning symptom + abnormal-trending vitals → treat as potentially unstable → reassess every ≈5 minutes.
  5. Second vitals (5 min later): Pulse 110, Resp 22, BP 132/86, SpO₂ 94%. Pulse up, BP drifting down, SpO₂ slipping — the trend is the alarm, even though no value is yet "critical." This is exactly when you escalate and keep reassessing, not when you relax.

🛠️ Hands-On: log a set of vitals and plot the trend

Reuse the Python environment you set up in Week 1 (one-line reminder: activate it, e.g. source health-env/bin/activate). Here we put a series of reassessments into a pandas table and let Matplotlib show the trend at a glance — the same idea that powers the Week 10 protocol dashboard.

# vitals_trend.py — Lecture 2 (synthetic teaching data, not real patient data)
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# each row = one reassessment, minutes since first contact
vitals = pd.DataFrame({
    "t_min":  [0, 5, 10, 15],
    "pulse":  [96, 110, 118, 124],
    "sbp":    [148, 132, 118, 104],   # systolic BP
    "resp":   [20, 22, 24, 26],
    "spo2":   [95, 94, 93, 92],
})

# a crude "is this trending toward shock?" flag: pulse rising while BP falls
vitals["shock_index"] = vitals["pulse"] / vitals["sbp"]   # >~0.9 is worrying
print(vitals)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(vitals["t_min"], vitals["pulse"], "-o", color="#f43f5e", label="pulse")
ax.plot(vitals["t_min"], vitals["sbp"],   "-o", color="#60a5fa", label="systolic BP")
ax.set_xlabel("minutes since contact"); ax.set_ylabel("value")
ax.set_title("Vital-sign trend"); ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
# Watch the lines cross — pulse climbing past a falling BP is the shock fingerprint.

Exercise: print only the rows where shock_index > 0.9, then add a fifth reassessment of your own and predict — before you plot it — whether the patient is improving or deteriorating.

🔗 Where this is heading

The skill of turning observations into a defensible decision recurs all course. Week 3 reads respirations and SpO₂ as airway/ventilation problems; Week 4 turns "no pulse, not breathing" into the CPR/AED algorithm (compressions 100–120/min, at least 2 in / 5 cm deep, 30:2 single-rescuer); Week 5 reads pulse-plus-blood-pressure trends as hemorrhagic shock you control with pressure, packing, or a tourniquet. The very same time-series mindset — log it, plot it, flag the trend — becomes the Week 8–9 personal-health analytics (sleep, heart-rate, focus) and the Week 10 capstone dashboard. Numbers over time, honestly interpreted, are the through-line of the whole specialization.

Q1. In the SAMPLE history, the L stands for…
A. Level of consciousness
B. Last oral intake (when/what they last ate or drank)
C. Location of pain
D. Length of symptoms
Show answer

B. SAMPLE = Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last oral intake, Events. "L" is last intake — relevant for surgery, diabetes, and aspiration risk.

Q2. A patient says their chest pain is "crushing, like a weight." Which letter of OPQRST does that answer?
A. O — Onset
B. R — Region/Radiation
C. Q — Quality
D. T — Time
Show answer

C. Quality is how the symptom feels in the patient's own words — sharp, dull, crushing, burning. Where it is would be R; when it started would be O/T.

Q3. Approximately which range is a normal resting adult heart rate?
A. 60–100 beats per minute
B. 12–20 beats per minute
C. 100–140 beats per minute
D. 30–50 beats per minute
Show answer

A. ~60–100/min is the usual resting-adult range. 12–20 (B) is the respiratory rate. Children normally run faster than adults.

Q4. Why should you count respirations without telling the patient?
A. It's faster to skip the explanation
B. The pulse oximeter requires silence
C. People consciously change their breathing when they know it's being watched
D. Talking lowers blood pressure
Show answer

C. Breathing is partly voluntary, so awareness biases the count. A common trick is to keep your fingers on the wrist as if still taking the pulse while you count chest rises.

Q5. Over three sets of vitals, a patient's pulse rises 90 → 108 → 124 while systolic BP falls 130 → 116 → 100. This trend most suggests…
A. the patient is improving
B. equipment error only
C. a normal response to lying down
D. developing shock — escalate and reassess more often
Show answer

D. Rising pulse with falling blood pressure is a classic shock trend. The direction of the numbers, not any single value, is the warning.

Q6. A patient is classified as unstable. A common teaching guideline says to reassess vital signs about every…
A. 30 minutes
B. 5 minutes
C. 60 minutes
D. only once on arrival
Show answer

B. Roughly every 5 minutes for an unstable patient and every ~15 for a stable one — but local protocol and the patient's trajectory always override the clock.

✓ What you learned

  • Take a focused history with SAMPLE (whole patient) and OPQRST (one symptom) — and ask open, non-leading questions.
  • The four core vital signs and their approximate adult norms: pulse 60–100, respirations 12–20, BP ≈ 120/80, SpO₂ 95–100%.
  • How each vital is measured — and the traps (counting respirations covertly, SpO₂ fooled by cold/CO).
  • A single reading is a snapshot; the trend across reassessments tells the story — rising pulse with falling BP signals shock.
  • Reassess roughly every 5 min if unstable, 15 min if stable, and log/plot vitals as a time series.
