10.1The Whole Course on One Page

You started this course not knowing where to stand at a chaotic scene. You now carry an entire decision system. The capstone's job is to make that system visible — to you and to anyone grading your project — as a single pipeline rather than ten disconnected weeks.

Everything you learned hangs off one spine: assess, then act, then reassess. Scene size-up (W1) decides whether it is even safe to approach; the ABCDE primary assessment finds what will kill the patient first; vital signs and patient assessment (W2) quantify how sick they are; and the body-system weeks (W3–W6) give you the specific intervention once you know the problem.

Weeks 8–9 then turned the lens inward: sleep and circadian rhythm (W8) and focus, energy, supplements, and addiction neuroscience (W9) are the same loop applied to your own baseline — measure, intervene, reassess. The capstone literally bolts these two halves together.

10.2Mapping to the NREMT Exam Blueprint

If you ever pursue real certification, the entry-level cognitive exam is the NREMT EMT test — a computer-adaptive exam built around five content domains. Knowing the blueprint tells you where to spend study time, because the domains are not weighted equally.

Verify before you rely on itThe percentages below are approximate and reflect the published NREMT EMT blueprint as of 2026 — verify against the current official NREMT test plan before any real exam, as weightings and pediatric splits are periodically revised. A large share of every domain (often around 85%) is weighted toward adult patients, the remainder pediatric.

Notice the alignment: your heaviest course weeks (cardiac/CPR in W4, medical emergencies in W6) line up with the heaviest exam domains. That is not a coincidence — it reflects what most often kills patients in the field.

10.3The Capstone Project — What You Are Building

Your deliverable is a 4-Week Personal "Protocol Dashboard" + Emergency Scenario. It has two panels that mirror the course's two halves.

Panel A — Emergency-action reference

A one-screen, dependable cheat-sheet of the life-saving facts from W1–W7, stated in their correct current form. These are the numbers you must never get wrong:

Adult CPR: rate 100–120/min · depth ≥ 2 in (5 cm) · single-rescuer ratio 30:2 · attach AED ASAPPush hard, push fast, allow full recoil, minimize interruptions; switch on the AED and follow its prompts.

Other anchors to include verbatim: tourniquet or wound-packing for life-threatening external hemorrhage; FAST / BE-FAST for suspected stroke; naloxone for opioid overdose; epinephrine auto-injector for anaphylaxis; oral glucose for a conscious hypoglycemic patient (never anything by mouth if they cannot protect their airway).

Panel B — Your 4-week health-protocol log

From W8–W9 you pick 2–4 protocols (e.g., morning sunlight, caffeine-delay, consistent sleep/wake time) and log them for four weeks alongside an outcome you can rate daily (sleep quality 1–10, subjective focus, resting heart rate). The dashboard then plots adherence vs. outcome. You are not proving causation in a sample of one — you are practicing the measure → intervene → reassess loop honestly.

AnalogyThink of the dashboard like an aircraft's instrument panel. Panel A is the emergency checklist bolted to the yoke — fixed, validated, used only when something goes wrong. Panel B is the flight instruments — your live readouts that you scan every day to keep the aircraft trimmed. A good pilot trusts the checklist exactly and reads the instruments skeptically. Same here.

10.4Frontier Medicine — Real, Promising, and Hype

Your capstone closes by looking outward at where medicine is going. AI is genuinely reshaping parts of the field — but the gap between a research result and an approved therapy is enormous, and headlines routinely collapse it. Here is the honest map.

Protein structure: AlphaFold

AlphaFold (from DeepMind) predicts a protein's 3-D folded structure from its amino-acid sequence, a problem that resisted biology for fifty years. This is real and transformative: a public database now holds predicted structures for essentially the entire known protein universe, and the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry recognized the work (as of 2026 — verify). What it is not: a drug. A predicted structure is a starting map for research, not a cure, and predictions still carry confidence scores you must respect.

Generative molecular / drug design

Generative models can now propose novel candidate molecules with desired properties, compressing the early "what should we even try?" stage of drug discovery. Several AI-designed candidates have entered clinical trials. But entering a trial is the beginning, not the end — most drug candidates fail in trials regardless of how they were discovered, and no disease has been "solved" by generative design as of 2026.

AI in oncology

The most clinically real wins are in pattern recognition: AI systems that flag tumors on mammograms, CT, and pathology slides, often matching or aiding specialists and triaging workloads. These are decision-support tools — a clinician stays in the loop. "AI that designs personalized cancer cures" remains largely aspirational; immunotherapy and neoantigen-vaccine research is promising but early.

Veterinary oncology — the dog-cancer idea

One genuinely exciting frontier is using AI-driven design to tackle cancer in dogs. Dogs develop spontaneous cancers biologically similar to human ones, so this could both help pets and inform human medicine. Be precise about its status: this is an aspiration and active research direction, not a product you can buy. Frame it that way in your capstone.

Common mistake — the hype filterWhen you meet any "AI cures X" claim, do not ask "is it impressive?" — ask "what stage is it at?" A prediction or a paper is not a trial; a trial is not approval; approval for one population is not a universal cure. Most of frontier medicine lives at the early stages. Promising ≠ proven. Use that filter on your own capstone language too: hedge what is preliminary.
✦ Worked Example — a full capstone scenario walk-through

Your project must include one written emergency scenario showing the pipeline end-to-end. Here is the model to imitate. Scenario: you find a ~50-year-old man slumped at a bus stop, unresponsive.

  1. Scene size-up (W1): Scan for danger first — traffic, electrical, the patient's surroundings. It is safe. Note one patient, no obvious mechanism of trauma. Form a general impression: looks critically ill.
  2. Responsiveness + call for help: Tap and shout — no response. Call your local emergency number now (or direct a bystander to), and ask for an AED.
  3. ABCDE / circulation: No normal breathing and no definite pulse within ~10 s → treat as cardiac arrest. Begin CPR: compressions at 100–120/min, depth ≥ 2 in (5 cm), full recoil, ratio 30:2 if trained to ventilate.
  4. AED: The moment it arrives, power on, apply pads, let it analyze, and follow prompts — shock if advised, then resume compressions immediately.
  5. Differential thinking (W6): If he revives or a cause becomes clear, match it: opioid signs (pinpoint pupils, slow breathing) → naloxone; known diabetic and now conscious + low sugar → oral glucose; sudden facial droop/arm weakness/speech change → run BE-FAST and note the time of onset.
  6. Reassess + hand off (W7): Reassess continuously; on EMS arrival give a clean handoff and your documentation: what you found, when, and what you did. Note consent/Good-Samaritan context.
  7. Takeaway: Every step is a course week. A grader can read this scenario and see the whole pipeline fire in order — that is exactly what your written scenario should demonstrate.

🛠️ Hands-On: build the Protocol Dashboard (pandas + Matplotlib)

Reminder: this uses the same free Python + pandas + Matplotlib (Jupyter) stack you installed back in Week 1 — no new install needed. Below is the core of Panel B: load your four-week log, then plot adherence against your daily outcome so the relationship (if any) is visible.

# protocol_dashboard.py — Capstone Panel B
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Your daily log: one row per day for 4 weeks (28 rows).
# Columns: date, sunlight_min, caffeine_delay_min, sleep_hours, sleep_quality (1-10)
df = pd.read_csv("my_protocol_log.csv", parse_dates=["date"])

# Adherence = did I hit each protocol's target today?
df["sunlight_ok"]  = df["sunlight_min"] >= 10        # morning light target
df["caffeine_ok"]  = df["caffeine_delay_min"] >= 90 # ~90-120 min delay
df["adherence"]    = df[["sunlight_ok", "caffeine_ok"]].mean(axis=1)  # 0..1

# 7-day rolling averages smooth out daily noise
df["adherence_7d"]   = df["adherence"].rolling(7, min_periods=3).mean()
df["sleepqual_7d"]   = df["sleep_quality"].rolling(7, min_periods=3).mean()

fig, ax1 = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 4))
ax1.plot(df["date"], df["adherence_7d"]*100, color="#f43f5e", label="adherence % (7d)")
ax1.set_ylabel("adherence %", color="#f43f5e")
ax2 = ax1.twinx()
ax2.plot(df["date"], df["sleepqual_7d"], color="#60a5fa", label="sleep quality (7d)")
ax2.set_ylabel("sleep quality 1-10", color="#60a5fa")
ax1.set_title("Protocol adherence vs. sleep quality — 4 weeks")
fig.autofmt_xdate(); fig.tight_layout()
plt.savefig("dashboard_panelB.png", dpi=130)

# Honest read: a SINGLE person's correlation is NOT proof of cause.
print(df[["adherence", "sleep_quality"]].corr().iloc[0, 1])

Q1. For an adult in cardiac arrest, the correct single-rescuer CPR is…
A. rate 100–120/min, depth ≥ 2 in (5 cm), ratio 30:2
B. rate 60–80/min, depth 1 in, ratio 15:2
C. rate 140–160/min, depth 4 in, ratio 5:1
D. compressions only are forbidden under all circumstances
Show answer

A. Push hard and fast at 100–120/min, at least 2 in (5 cm) deep, 30:2 for a single trained rescuer, with full recoil and minimal interruptions. (Hands-only CPR is in fact encouraged for untrained bystanders, so D is false.)

Q2. Which two NREMT EMT content domains carry the largest approximate weight (verify 2026)?
A. EMS Operations and Trauma
B. Airway and Trauma
C. Medical/OB-GYN and Cardiology & Resuscitation
D. Trauma and EMS Operations
Show answer

C. Medical/OB-GYN (~27%) and Cardiology & Resuscitation (~22%) dominate the blueprint, which is why W4 and W6 carry so much of this course. Numbers are approximate — re-verify against the current official test plan.

Q3. AlphaFold's genuine, real contribution is best described as…
A. an approved cancer cure available in clinics today
B. predicting protein 3-D structure from sequence — a research map, not a drug
C. a generative model that designs finished medicines automatically
D. an AED replacement for cardiac arrest
Show answer

B. AlphaFold predicts folded structures from amino-acid sequences — transformative for research, but a predicted structure is a starting map, not a therapy.

Q4. A conscious patient with known diabetes is shaky, sweaty, and confused with low blood sugar. The appropriate first-aid step is…
A. apply a tourniquet
B. give an epinephrine auto-injector
C. withhold everything and wait
D. give oral glucose (because they are conscious and can protect their airway)
Show answer

D. Oral glucose is for the conscious hypoglycemic patient who can swallow and protect their airway. If they were unresponsive, you would give nothing by mouth. Epinephrine is for anaphylaxis; a tourniquet is for life-threatening bleeding.

Q5. The "AI-designed therapy for cancer in dogs" idea should be described in your capstone as…
A. a product available for purchase at any vet
B. an aspiration / active research direction, not a finished product
C. an FDA-approved standard of care
D. proven to cure human cancer
Show answer

B. It is genuinely promising — dogs get spontaneous cancers similar to humans' — but it is an aspiration and research direction, not something you can buy. Hedge it that way.

Q6. Your 4-week personal protocol log shows higher adherence weeks correlate with better sleep scores. The honest interpretation is…
A. a single-person correlation is suggestive at best, not proof of cause
B. it proves the protocol causes better sleep for everyone
C. correlation always equals causation
D. the data should be hidden because it is imperfect
Show answer

A. An n-of-1 correlation can be confounded (travel, illness, season) and is suggestive, not causal — the same skepticism you apply to frontier-medicine headlines.
