Bleeding Control & Trauma Assessment

Most preventable trauma deaths come from blood loss you could have stopped with your hands and a tourniquet. This lecture is the playbook.

⚠ Read first — educational only, not medical advice This lecture is exam-prep familiarization built around current first-aid and EMT concepts — it is NOT EMT certification, not a license to treat, and not a substitute for hands-on training (e.g., an accredited course). Real skills require supervised practice and assessment. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US) and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Apply the hemorrhage-control hierarchy: direct pressure → wound packing → tourniquet — and know when to skip straight to a tourniquet.
  • Place a tourniquet correctly (location, tightness, time) and pack a wound for junctional/deep bleeding.
  • Define shock as hypoperfusion and recognize early ("compensated") vs late ("decompensated") signs.
  • Run a rapid trauma survey after the ABCDE primary assessment to find life threats fast.
  • Use Python + pandas + Matplotlib to model how blood-pressure compensation masks blood loss until it suddenly fails.

5.1First Principles: Why Bleeding Kills, and What "Trauma" Means on the Exam

An adult holds roughly 5 liters of blood. Lose enough of it and the heart has nothing left to pump — cells stop getting oxygen, and the patient dies. Massive hemorrhage is the leading cause of preventable death in trauma, and most of it happens before the patient ever reaches a hospital. That single fact reorders everything you learned in Lecture 1: the classic "ABC" priority is, for bleeding trauma, often taught as "MARCH" / "C-ABC" — catastrophic bleeding gets handled first, because a perfect airway is useless in an empty circulatory system.

On the NREMT EMT cognitive exam, this material sits in the Trauma content domain. The exam is organized into roughly five domains — Airway/Respiration/Ventilation; Cardiology & Resuscitation; Trauma; Medical/Obstetrics/Gynecology; and EMS Operations — with each weighted at a published percentage range (as of 2026 — verify the exact current weightings against the official NREMT EMT test plan, as they are revised periodically). Trauma is consistently one of the larger blocks, and hemorrhage control is its highest-yield single skill.

Analogy Think of the body as a pressurized plumbing system. The pump (heart) only works if the pipes stay full. A burst pipe (bleed) drops the pressure everywhere — and just like a real leak, your first move is not to inspect the pump or the upstairs faucet, it's to get a hand on the hole and stop the loss. Everything else can wait sixty seconds; the leak cannot.

5.2The Hemorrhage-Control Hierarchy

External life-threatening bleeding follows a clear, ordered protocol. Modern bleeding-control programs teach it as a short ladder you climb only as far as you need to:

Direct pressure — the foundation

The majority of bleeding stops with firm, focused, continuous direct pressure applied right on the bleeding point. Use a gloved hand and gauze/cloth; press hard and do not lift to look — peeking disrupts the clot you are trying to form. If blood soaks through, add more dressing on top and keep pressing; don't remove the soaked layer.

Wound packing — for deep and "junctional" bleeds

Where a limb meets the torso — the groin, armpit (axilla), and neck — you cannot place a tourniquet. For deep bleeding wounds here, you pack gauze tightly into the wound, all the way down to the bleeding vessel, then hold firm pressure for several minutes. Hemostatic (clot-promoting) gauze is preferred where available, but plain gauze packed tightly also works.

Tourniquet — for life-threatening limb bleeding

A tourniquet is a band cinched around an arm or leg, 2–3 inches above the wound (never on a joint), tightened until the bright-red bleeding stops — which usually means it hurts, because you are compressing the artery. Tourniquets are now standard, life-saving equipment; the old fear that "a tourniquet costs the limb" is largely outdated for the timeframes of modern EMS response. Write down the time of application — it is the single most important piece of documentation for the receiving hospital.

Common mistakes (1) A loose tourniquet is worse than none — it stops venous return but not arterial inflow, so the limb keeps bleeding into itself. Tighten until bleeding stops. (2) Never loosen or "release" a tourniquet in the field to "let blood through" — once it's on for life-threatening bleeding, it stays on. (3) Don't place a tourniquet over a joint (knee/elbow) or directly over a wound. (4) Always note the application time.

5.3Shock: Hypoperfusion in Plain Terms

Shock is not "being upset." Clinically, shock is hypoperfusion — inadequate delivery of oxygenated blood to the tissues. In trauma the usual cause is hemorrhagic (hypovolemic) shock: not enough volume in the tank. Other classes exist (cardiogenic, distributive/septic, obstructive), but the trauma exam centers on volume loss.

The body fights back. As volume drops, the nervous system raises the heart rate and clamps down the blood vessels (vasoconstriction) to defend the pressure to brain and heart. This is compensated shock — and it is dangerous precisely because blood pressure can look normal while the patient is already in serious trouble. The classic teaching:

MAP ≈ CO × SVR  and  CO = HR × SVMean arterial pressure depends on cardiac output (CO) and systemic vascular resistance (SVR). Lose stroke volume (SV) to bleeding, and the body raises HR and SVR to hold MAP — until it can't.

Early / compensated signs: anxiety or restlessness, fast weak pulse (tachycardia), pale–cool–clammy skin, rapid breathing, delayed capillary refill, thirst — with a still-normal blood pressure. Late / decompensated signs: falling blood pressure, altered mental status, very weak/absent peripheral pulses — an ominous, late finding. Treatment basics: stop the bleeding, keep the patient warm (cold worsens clotting), give high-flow oxygen per local protocol, position appropriately, and transport rapidly — definitive care for hemorrhagic shock is in the operating room, not the field.

5.4The Rapid Trauma Survey

After the ABCDE primary assessment from Lecture 1 (and after stopping any catastrophic bleeding), a significant-mechanism trauma patient gets a rapid trauma survey: a fast, head-to-toe sweep — about 60–90 seconds — to find the injuries that will kill in the next few minutes. A common memory aid for each region is DCAP-BTLS:

D-C-A-P · B-T-L-SDeformities · Contusions · Abrasions · Punctures/penetrations — Burns · Tenderness · Lacerations · Swelling. Look and feel for each, region by region.

Sweep in order, calling out what you find: head → neck → chest → abdomen → pelvis → extremities → back (log-roll). The point is not a complete diagnosis — it's a triage of life threats so you treat the worst first and transport. Findings like a rigid abdomen, an unstable pelvis, or distended neck veins redirect your priorities immediately.

Analogy The rapid trauma survey is a preflight walk-around, not a full inspection. A pilot circling the aircraft isn't rebuilding the engine — they're looking for the one thing that will bring the plane down in the next ten minutes: a flat tire, a fuel leak, a missing panel. You do the same to the body: fast, systematic, hunting for the immediately lethal, then go.
✦ Worked Example — a decision walk-through

Scenario: a cyclist is struck by a car. You've confirmed the scene is safe and you're wearing gloves. There is bright-red blood pulsing from a deep gash on the mid-thigh, and a smaller steady ooze from the left armpit. The patient is awake but anxious. Walk the decision.

  1. Catastrophic bleeding first (the "C" before ABC). The spurting thigh wound is the immediate killer. The thigh is a limb → it can take a tourniquet.
  2. Apply a tourniquet high and tight, 2–3 in above the wound, not over the knee, tighten until the bright-red bleeding stops. Note the time (e.g., "TK 14:07" — write it on the device or the patient's skin).
  3. The armpit (axilla) is junctional — you can't tourniquet it. Pack the wound with gauze down to the source and hold firm direct pressure for several minutes.
  4. Now the primary assessment / ABCDE: airway open? breathing adequate? With bleeding controlled, reassess. The anxiety + likely blood loss flags early compensated shock.
  5. Treat for shock: keep the patient warm, oxygen per protocol, monitor pulse/skin/mental status, and arrange rapid transport — a normal blood pressure here is reassuring only on the surface (recall §5.3's cliff).
  6. Rapid trauma survey en route (DCAP-BTLS, head-to-back) to catch the chest/abdomen/pelvis injuries a car strike loves to cause. Reassess the tourniquet and packing.

Takeaway: the order — stop the bleed, then ABCDE, then survey — is the whole exam in one case.

🛠️ Hands-On: model the compensation cliff

Reminder: this uses the same Python + pandas + Matplotlib (Jupyter) stack you set up in Week 1 — no reinstall needed (pip install pandas matplotlib if you skipped it).

We'll build a toy model of §5.3: as blood volume drops, the body holds mean arterial pressure (MAP) flat by raising heart rate and resistance — until a reserve runs out and MAP collapses. Plot both curves and see why a rising pulse is the early alarm.

# shock_compensation.py — Lecture 5 (toy teaching model, NOT clinical)
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

loss = np.linspace(0, 40, 200)          # % blood volume lost
reserve = 30                            # compensation runs out ~30%

# Heart rate climbs steadily to defend output
hr = 70 + 2.2 * loss

# MAP held near-flat while compensating, then falls off a cliff
comp = np.clip((reserve - loss) / reserve, 0, 1)   # 1 -> 0 as reserve depletes
map_mmHg = 60 + 30 * comp + 0.1 * loss             # ~90 -> collapse

df = pd.DataFrame({"loss_pct": loss, "HR": hr, "MAP": map_mmHg})
print(df.iloc[[0, 100, 150, 199]].round(1))        # peek at key rows

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(7, 4))
ax.plot(df.loss_pct, df.MAP, color="#f43f5e", lw=2.5, label="MAP (mmHg)")
ax.plot(df.loss_pct, df.HR, color="#60a5fa", lw=2, ls="--", label="Heart rate (bpm)")
ax.axvline(reserve, color="#fbbf24", ls=":", label="decompensation")
ax.set_xlabel("blood volume lost (%)"); ax.set_ylabel("vital sign")
ax.set_title("Compensated shock hides blood loss until it doesn't")
ax.legend(); fig.tight_layout(); plt.show()

Try it: lower reserve to model a frail or elderly patient (less compensation) and watch the cliff move left — they crash sooner. This is a conceptual model to build intuition, not a physiologic simulator.

6 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself. (Educational review only.)

Q1. For most external bleeding, the first intervention you should attempt is…
A. applying a tourniquet immediately
B. firm, focused, continuous direct pressure on the bleeding point
C. elevating the limb and waiting
D. cleaning the wound thoroughly first
Show answer

B. Direct pressure is the foundation of the hierarchy and stops the majority of bleeds. Escalate to packing or a tourniquet only as needed (or start at a tourniquet for obvious life-threatening limb hemorrhage).

Q2. A wound to the groin is bleeding heavily. The most appropriate technique is…
A. a tourniquet placed around the upper thigh on the joint
B. a quick peek every 30 seconds to check progress
C. tightly packing the wound with gauze and holding firm pressure
D. a loosely applied dressing taped in place
Show answer

C. The groin is a junctional site — you can't tourniquet it. Pack the wound down to the bleeding vessel and hold firm pressure. Peeking (B) disrupts the forming clot.

Q3. Clinically, "shock" is best defined as…
A. inadequate perfusion (oxygen delivery) of the tissues — hypoperfusion
B. an emotional reaction to a traumatic event
C. any drop in heart rate below 60
D. a sudden rise in blood pressure
Show answer

A. Shock = hypoperfusion: the tissues aren't getting enough oxygenated blood. In trauma the common cause is hemorrhagic (hypovolemic) shock from blood loss.

Q4. A trauma patient has a fast, weak pulse and pale, cool, clammy skin, but a normal blood pressure. This most likely indicates…
A. the patient is fine and not bleeding
B. decompensated (late) shock with imminent recovery
C. a measurement error you should ignore
D. compensated (early) shock — the body is holding pressure but is in trouble
Show answer

D. A normal BP with tachycardia and poor skin signs is the hallmark of compensated shock. BP falling is a late sign — don't be reassured by a "normal" pressure.

Q5. Once a tourniquet is correctly applied for life-threatening limb bleeding, you should…
A. note the time of application and leave it in place
B. loosen it every few minutes to let some blood through
C. place it directly over the nearest joint for stability
D. remove it as soon as bleeding slows
Show answer

A. Record the application time and leave it on. Do not periodically loosen a tourniquet placed for life-threatening hemorrhage, and never place it over a joint or directly on the wound.

Q6. "DCAP-BTLS" during the rapid trauma survey is a memory aid for…
A. the steps of CPR
B. the findings to look and feel for in each body region
C. the classes of shock
D. the layers of a pressure dressing
Show answer

B. Deformities, Contusions, Abrasions, Punctures — Burns, Tenderness, Lacerations, Swelling: what you inspect and palpate for, region by region, in the rapid head-to-toe survey.
