Airway, Respiration & Ventilation

Everything downstream — the heart, the brain, every cell — is starved if air can't get in. Airway is the "A" of ABCDE because nothing else matters until the pipe is open.

⚠ Educational only — not medical advice This lecture is exam-prep familiarization. It is NOT EMT certification, not a license to treat, and not clinical guidance. Nothing here qualifies you to manage a real airway or ventilate a real patient — those are hands-on skills taught and supervised in a certified course. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the U.S.) and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Choose between head-tilt/chin-lift and jaw-thrust based on trauma suspicion.
  • State when an OPA vs an NPA is appropriate — and the gag-reflex / basilar-fracture cautions.
  • Describe bag-valve-mask (BVM) technique and the adult ventilation rate, and recognize over-ventilation.
  • Recognize inadequate breathing and connect SpO₂ to a basic oxygen-delivery decision.

3.1Why Airway Comes First

In Week 1 the primary assessment marched through A → B → C → D → E: Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure. The order is not alphabetical convenience — it is a priority of survival. A perfect pulse and beautiful blood pressure mean nothing if no oxygen is reaching the lungs, because the blood is just circulating the same depleted air. Airway is the gate. Open it before you worry about anything past it.

A patent airway is one that is open and unobstructed — air can move freely from the mouth and nose to the lungs and back. In an unresponsive person, the most common cause of obstruction is embarrassingly simple: the tongue. When muscle tone is lost, the tongue falls back against the rear of the throat and seals it off. The good news is that the same loss of tone makes the fix mechanical and immediate — lift the jaw forward and the tongue comes with it.

3.2Opening the Airway: Head-Tilt/Chin-Lift vs Jaw-Thrust

There are two manual maneuvers, and the choice between them hinges on a single question: could there be a spinal injury?

Head-tilt / chin-lift — the default for non-trauma

One hand on the forehead tilts the head back; the fingers of the other hand lift the bony part of the chin. This straightens the airway and lifts the tongue. Use it for any unresponsive patient where trauma is not suspected — for example, a medical collapse or an overdose with no fall.

Jaw-thrust — when you suspect a spine injury

Kneeling above the head, you place fingers behind the angles of the jaw and push the jaw forward without tilting the head. This opens the airway while keeping the cervical spine in a neutral, in-line position. Use it whenever the mechanism of injury could have hurt the neck — a fall, a car crash, a diving accident, any significant blunt trauma above the chest.

No trauma suspected → Head-tilt / chin-lift
Trauma / spine suspected → Jaw-thrust (keep the neck neutral) The deciding question is always: could the neck be injured? When unsure, protect the spine.
Common mistakeDon't tilt the head of a trauma patient just because it's the maneuver you remember best. Head-tilt on a patient with a possible cervical-spine injury can worsen a spinal-cord injury. The trade-off in EMT teaching is clear: protect the spine with a jaw-thrust — but a patent airway always wins, so if a jaw-thrust fails to open the airway, opening it remains the priority.

3.3Airway Adjuncts: OPA and NPA

Manual maneuvers work, but they tie up your hands. Airway adjuncts are simple devices that help hold the airway open so you can do other things. There are two basic types, and they are not interchangeable — the deciding factor is the patient's gag reflex.

Oropharyngeal airway (OPA)

A curved plastic device inserted into the mouth that holds the tongue off the back of the throat. Because it sits deep in the oropharynx, it is only for patients with NO gag reflex — typically deeply unresponsive patients. Put an OPA in someone with an intact gag reflex and they may gag, vomit, and aspirate. Size it from the corner of the mouth to the angle of the jaw (or earlobe).

Nasopharyngeal airway (NPA)

A soft flexible tube passed through a nostril into the back of the throat. Because it does not press on the same gag-triggering structures, it is generally tolerated by semi-responsive patients who still have a gag reflex. The big caution: avoid an NPA when you suspect a basilar skull fracture (signs can include clear fluid from the nose/ears or bruising around the eyes), because the tube could pass in the wrong direction.

AnalogyThink of the adjuncts like a door wedge. The manual maneuver is your hand holding the door open; an adjunct is the wedge that keeps it open so your hands are free. But you only kick the wedge in if the door (the throat) won't slam back on it — that's the gag reflex. An OPA is a stiff wedge for a door that won't fight back; an NPA is a soft one a half-awake door can tolerate.

3.4Ventilation: The Bag-Valve-Mask (BVM)

An open airway lets air move; it doesn't make a patient breathe. When someone is not breathing or is breathing inadequately, you must ventilate them — push air in for them. The standard tool is the bag-valve-mask (BVM): a self-inflating bag attached to a one-way valve and a mask that seals over the nose and mouth. Squeeze the bag and air goes in; release and it refills (and can be fed supplemental oxygen).

Technique is mostly about the mask seal. A common one-rescuer grip is the "E-C" hold: thumb and index finger form a "C" pressing the mask down, while the other three fingers form an "E" lifting the jaw up into the mask. Two-rescuer BVM (one seals with both hands, one squeezes) is more effective when help is available.

Rate and volume — and the danger of doing too much

For an adult in respiratory arrest who still has a pulse, a commonly taught rate is roughly one breath every 5–6 seconds (about 10–12 breaths per minute), each breath delivered over about 1 second, just until you see the chest visibly rise. As of 2026, confirm exact rates against current resuscitation guidelines and local protocol.

Adult, respiratory arrest WITH a pulse:
≈ 1 breath every 5–6 s  (~10–12 /min) · each ~1 s to visible chest rise Approximate teaching values — verify against current guidelines. Rates differ for children and for full cardiac arrest with CPR.
Common mistake — over-ventilationThe instinct in an emergency is to squeeze fast and hard. Don't. Over-ventilation — too many breaths or too much volume — raises pressure in the chest, can reduce blood return to the heart, and forces air into the stomach, which causes vomiting and aspiration. Slow, gentle breaths just to visible chest rise are safer and more effective. More is not better.

3.5Recognizing Inadequate Breathing & Delivering Oxygen

Before you ventilate, you have to notice that breathing is failing. Adequate breathing is regular, unlabored, and moves enough air. Inadequate breathing shows up in three dimensions:

  • Rate. A normal resting adult breathes about 12–20 times per minute. Too slow (e.g. <8–10) or too fast moves too little usable air.
  • Depth (tidal volume). Shallow breaths that barely move the chest don't ventilate the lungs even at a normal rate.
  • Effort & signs of distress. Use of accessory muscles (neck and shoulder muscles straining), nasal flaring, tripod positioning, only being able to speak a word or two at a time, and especially cyanosis — a bluish tint to lips, fingertips, or skin signaling low oxygen.

SpO₂ from a pulse oximeter helps quantify the problem: a common target is ≥ 94%. A reading below that, or any signs of inadequate breathing, prompts oxygen. Delivery escalates with severity — broadly, a nasal cannula for low-flow supplemental oxygen in a breathing patient with mild need, and a non-rebreather mask for high-flow oxygen when need is greater. A patient who is not breathing adequately on their own moves past masks to assisted ventilation with a BVM. Specific flow rates and device thresholds are protocol-dependent — verify locally.

AnalogySpO₂ is the fuel gauge; rate, depth, and effort are the sound of the engine. A driver doesn't wait for the gauge to hit empty before reacting — a sputtering, straining engine (accessory muscles, gasping, cyanosis) tells you there's trouble even if the needle hasn't dropped yet. Treat the patient in front of you, not just the number.
✦ Worked Example — an airway decision walk-through

You find an unresponsive adult who fell from a ladder. Walk the airway decisions. (Educational scenario — not a treatment protocol.)

  1. Scene & ABCDE order: You complete scene size-up, then start the primary assessment at A — Airway. Nothing past A matters until the airway is open.
  2. Trauma? Yes. A fall from height means a possible spinal injury, so you open the airway with a jaw-thrust, keeping the neck neutral — not head-tilt/chin-lift.
  3. Adjunct choice: The patient is deeply unresponsive with no gag reflex, so an OPA is appropriate to keep the airway open and free your hands. You check first for signs of a basilar skull fracture, which would have ruled out an NPA anyway.
  4. B — Breathing: You look, listen, and feel: breaths are slow and shallow with neck-muscle straining and bluish lips. This is inadequate breathing.
  5. Ventilate: You begin BVM ventilation — roughly one breath every 5–6 seconds, each over about 1 second, stopping at visible chest rise. You consciously avoid squeezing fast/hard to prevent over-ventilation, and attach supplemental oxygen to the bag.

🛠️ Hands-On: model BVM ventilation rate and SpO₂ response

Reuse the Python environment from Week 1 (one-line reminder: activate it, e.g. source health-env/bin/activate). Here we tabulate a ventilation log and plot a synthetic SpO₂ recovery to build intuition for "breaths spaced ~5–6 s, watch the saturation come up."

# bvm_rate.py — Lecture 3 (synthetic teaching data, not real patient data)
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

target_interval = 5.5   # seconds between breaths (≈10-12/min adult, with a pulse)
print("breaths/min ≈", round(60 / target_interval, 1))

# each row = a check during assisted ventilation
log = pd.DataFrame({
    "t_min":      [0, 1, 2, 3, 4],
    "breaths_min":[6, 11, 11, 12, 11],   # started too slow, then corrected
    "spo2":       [82, 88, 92, 94, 96],  # saturation recovering
})

# flag intervals where rate is outside the ~10-12/min teaching window
log["rate_ok"] = log["breaths_min"].between(10, 12)
print(log)

fig, ax = plt.subplots()
ax.plot(log["t_min"], log["spo2"], "-o", color="#f43f5e", label="SpO₂ (%)")
ax.axhline(94, color="#34d399", linestyle="--", label="≈94% target")
ax.set_xlabel("minutes of assisted ventilation"); ax.set_ylabel("SpO₂ (%)")
ax.set_title("SpO₂ response to ventilation"); ax.legend()
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()
# Watch SpO2 cross the dashed target line as effective ventilation continues.

Q1. Why is Airway the "A" — the very first step — in ABCDE?
A. It is alphabetically first and easy to remember
B. No oxygen can reach the body without a patent (open) airway, so nothing downstream matters until it's open
C. It is the least urgent step, so it's done first to get it out of the way
D. Airway problems are rare, so they're checked once and skipped
Show answer

B. A perfect pulse and pressure are useless if no oxygen is getting in. Airway is the gate — open it before assessing anything past it.

Q2. An unresponsive patient who fell off a roof needs the airway opened. Which maneuver is preferred?
A. Head-tilt / chin-lift, tilting the head fully back
B. No maneuver — leave the airway closed to protect the spine
C. Jaw-thrust, keeping the cervical spine neutral and in-line
D. Roll the patient prone first
Show answer

C. A fall suggests possible spinal injury, so use a jaw-thrust to open the airway without tilting the neck. Head-tilt is the default only when trauma is not suspected.

Q3. An oropharyngeal airway (OPA) should be used only in a patient who…
A. has NO gag reflex (deeply unresponsive)
B. is awake and talking
C. has a strong, intact gag reflex
D. has a suspected basilar skull fracture
Show answer

A. An OPA sits deep in the oropharynx; a patient with a gag reflex may gag, vomit, and aspirate. It's for patients with no gag reflex.

Q4. When is a nasopharyngeal airway (NPA) specifically to be avoided?
A. Whenever the patient is unresponsive
B. Any time supplemental oxygen is in use
C. In a patient who is fully alert
D. When a basilar skull fracture is suspected
Show answer

D. With a suspected basilar skull fracture the tube could pass in the wrong direction. Otherwise an NPA is often tolerated by semi-responsive patients with a gag reflex.

Q5. For an adult in respiratory arrest who still has a pulse, a commonly taught BVM ventilation rate is about…
A. one breath every 1 second (≈60/min)
B. one breath every 5–6 seconds (≈10–12/min)
C. one breath every 30 seconds
D. as many breaths as fast as possible
Show answer

B. Roughly one breath every 5–6 seconds (~10–12/min), each over about 1 second to visible chest rise. Verify exact values against current guidelines.

Q6. What is the main danger of over-ventilation with a BVM?
A. It is harmless; more air is always better
B. It only wastes oxygen and nothing else
C. It raises chest pressure (reducing blood return) and forces air into the stomach, risking vomiting/aspiration
D. It cools the patient too quickly
Show answer

C. Too many/too forceful breaths raise intrathoracic pressure, can reduce blood return to the heart, and inflate the stomach. Breathe slowly, just to visible chest rise.

Q7. Which finding is a sign of inadequate breathing?
A. A regular, easy respiratory rate of 16/min
B. SpO₂ reading of 98%
C. Speaking in full sentences comfortably
D. Accessory-muscle use with cyanosis (bluish lips/fingertips)
Show answer

D. Straining neck/shoulder muscles and a bluish tint signal the body is working hard and not oxygenating well. A, B, and C describe adequate breathing.
