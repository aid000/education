Scene Size-Up & the Primary Assessment (ABCDE)

The first 60 seconds decide everything. Before you touch a patient, you read the scene; before you treat anything, you find the thing that kills first.

⚠ Educational only — not medical advice This lecture is exam-prep familiarization with how emergency responders think — it is NOT EMT certification, not a license to treat, and not a substitute for a hands-on, accredited course. Nothing here authorizes you to perform any procedure on another person. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the U.S.) and follow the dispatcher's instructions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Form a general impression ("sick vs not sick") in the first few seconds.

1.1Scene Safety & BSI Come Before the Patient

The most counter-intuitive rule in emergency care: your first patient is yourself. A rescuer who rushes into traffic, a fire, a downed power line, or a violent scene becomes a second casualty and removes a helper from the system. So every response begins with a deliberate scene size-up performed from the doorway or curb, before approaching.

The size-up answers five quick questions:

  • Is the scene safe? Traffic, fire, electricity, water, unstable structures, weapons, aggressive bystanders or animals, hazardous materials.
  • BSI / PPE — body-substance isolation. At minimum gloves; add eye protection and a mask when splash or airborne risk exists. Bloodborne and respiratory pathogens are invisible.
  • MOI or NOI? A mechanism of injury (a fall, a crash) points to trauma; a nature of illness (chest pain, trouble breathing) points to a medical problem. They steer the rest of the assessment.
  • How many patients? One quiet patient can hide three more. Count before you commit.
  • What resources? More ambulances, fire, police, power company, extrication — call early; help takes minutes to arrive.
AnalogyScene safety is the airplane oxygen-mask rule: "secure your own mask before assisting others." It feels selfish in the moment, but a responder who is hurt, contaminated, or trapped can't help anyone — and now two people need rescuing. Calm, deliberate hesitation at the threshold is a feature, not a flaw.

1.2The General Impression: "Sick or Not Sick?"

Once the scene is safe, you form a general impression in the first few seconds — a gestalt of how sick the patient looks before any measurement. Experienced clinicians describe it as the "doorway diagnosis." You're reading age, sex, position, obvious distress, skin color, and whether the person is interacting normally.

A useful pediatric tool that generalizes well is the Pediatric Assessment Triangle: three sides — Appearance, Work of Breathing, and Circulation to skin. Any side abnormal means "sick — move faster."

1.3Level of Consciousness: AVPU

The fastest measure of brain function — and a proxy for whether the airway is at risk — is AVPU, a four-rung ladder of responsiveness:

A · V · P · UAlert → responds to Voice → responds to Pain → Unresponsive — descending levels of consciousness.
  • A — Alert. Eyes open, interacting. Often qualified by orientation (person, place, time, event): "alert and oriented ×4."
  • V — Voice. Not spontaneously alert, but responds to your spoken voice (a moan, eye opening, movement).
  • P — Pain. Responds only to a painful stimulus (e.g. a trapezius pinch).
  • U — Unresponsive. No response to voice or pain. This patient cannot protect their own airway — the single most urgent finding on the scale.
Note · the airway linkA patient at P or U may lose the muscle tone that keeps the tongue off the back of the throat — the most common airway obstruction in an unconscious person. A dropping AVPU level is a red flag to immediately re-check the airway. AVPU is a screen, not a diagnosis; in-hospital and many EMS systems also use the more detailed Glasgow Coma Scale (covered later).

1.4The Primary Assessment: ABCDE

The primary assessment is a fixed-order hunt for things that kill within minutes. The order is the whole point: it is sorted by how fast each problem is fatal. You move down the list, and you stop and treat the moment you find a life threat — you do not skip ahead to the interesting injury.

A — Airway (with C-spine consideration)

Is the airway open and clear? In an unresponsive patient, open it with a head-tilt/chin-lift — or a jaw-thrust if you suspect spinal injury from the mechanism. Clear obvious obstructions (blood, vomit, foreign body). An airway you cannot open or keep open is the highest-priority problem on the scene.

B — Breathing

Is the patient breathing adequately — rate, depth, effort, and symmetry? Look, listen, feel. Inadequate or absent breathing means assisting ventilations and giving oxygen. (Cyanosis and accessory-muscle use are warning signs.)

C — Circulation

Is blood circulating, and is any of it leaving the body? Check a pulse and skin signs (color, temperature, moisture), and scan for and stop major bleeding. Severe external hemorrhage is one of the few things that can outrank parts of B in practice — many systems teach a "C-A-B" or "<C>ABCDE" (catastrophic-hemorrhage-first) order in high-bleed trauma.

D — Disability

A quick neuro check: AVPU (or GCS), pupils, and gross movement of all four limbs. This is where stroke and head-injury clues surface.

E — Exposure / Environment

Expose enough skin to find hidden injuries (a small entrance wound, a soaked pant leg), then protect the patient from heat loss and preserve dignity. Hypothermia worsens bleeding and outcomes.

1.5Where This Sits in the EMT Exam

The U.S. NREMT EMT cognitive exam is a computer-adaptive test built from five content domains. Scene size-up and the primary assessment are foundational across all of them — every domain assumes you've already secured the scene and run ABCDE. The approximate weightings are roughly:

Airway · Respiration · Ventilation  ≈ 18–22%
Cardiology & Resuscitation  ≈ 20–24%
Trauma  ≈ 14–18%
Medical · Obstetrics / Gynecology  ≈ 27–31%
EMS Operations  ≈ 10–14% Approximate domain weightings as of 2026 — verify against current official NREMT exam specifications. A large share of items applies to adults, with a portion on pediatric patients.

Notice the ABCDE order maps onto the two biggest-weight clinical domains (airway/breathing and cardiology/resuscitation) — which is exactly why the primary assessment is Week 1.

✦ Worked Example — a doorway-to-decision walk-through

Scenario (familiarization only): You arrive at a single-car collision. A driver is slumped over the wheel, not moving. Walk the assessment in order.

  1. Scene size-up. Park safely, scan for traffic, fuel/fire, downed lines, and the airbag/vehicle stability. Don gloves and eye protection (BSI). MOI = significant — a crash. Count patients: confirm only one. Call for additional resources (fire/extrication) early.
  2. General impression. Adult, slumped, not interacting → looks sick. Move with urgency.
  3. D-of-AVPU at the door / on contact. Speak loudly: no response. Trapezius pinch: a faint moan → P on AVPU. A P-level patient may not protect their airway → airway is now my immediate worry.
  4. A — Airway. Because the crash suggests possible spinal injury, open the airway with a jaw-thrust (not head-tilt). Clear visible blood. Maintain manual in-line stabilization of the head.
  5. B — Breathing. Breathing is shallow and slow → inadequate. This is a life threat I found, so I stop here and act: assist ventilations / apply high-flow oxygen before moving on.
  6. C — Circulation. Once B is managed, check pulse and skin, and scan for major bleeding — a soaked left thigh would mean immediate direct pressure / tourniquet (Week 5).
  7. D & E. Re-confirm AVPU and pupils; then expose to find hidden injuries and cover the patient to prevent heat loss.
  8. Decision: the key habit is that I treated inadequate breathing the instant I found it at step 5 — I did not jump ahead to the dramatic thigh wound. Life threats are handled in the order found.

🛠️ Hands-On: log your first scene-size-up dataset

This specialization uses the free Python + pandas + Matplotlib (Jupyter) stack — by Week 10 you'll build a protocol dashboard with it. Week 1 setup: install it once below, then reuse it every lecture and for the capstone.

⬇ Install Python + pandas / Matplotlib / Jupyter (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed (from python.org or your package manager), create an environment and install the data stack:

python3 -m venv health-env
source health-env/bin/activate        # macOS / Linux
health-env\Scripts\activate           # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install pandas matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import pandas; print(pandas.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. Launch the notebook with jupyter notebook.

Encode a few practice patients as an AVPU/ABCDE checklist, then count how often each life threat appears. Treating the assessment as structured data is exactly how the Week-10 dashboard will work.

# scene_log.py — Lecture 1
import pandas as pd

# each row = one practice patient's primary assessment (familiarization only)
patients = pd.DataFrame([
    {"id": 1, "scene_safe": True,  "avpu": "P", "airway_ok": False, "breathing_ok": False, "major_bleed": False},
    {"id": 2, "scene_safe": True,  "avpu": "A", "airway_ok": True,  "breathing_ok": True,  "major_bleed": True},
    {"id": 3, "scene_safe": False, "avpu": "U", "airway_ok": False, "breathing_ok": False, "major_bleed": True},
])

# AVPU as an ordered severity scale (A best -> U worst)
order = {"A": 0, "V": 1, "P": 2, "U": 3}
patients["avpu_severity"] = patients["avpu"].map(order)

# count life threats per patient (each False/True flag that is "bad")
patients["life_threats"] = (
    (~patients["airway_ok"]).astype(int)
    + (~patients["breathing_ok"]).astype(int)
    + patients["major_bleed"].astype(int)
)

print(patients.sort_values("avpu_severity", ascending=False))
# the most unresponsive patient with the most life threats sorts to the top

Extend it: add a bsi_donned column and a rule that refuses to "treat" any row where scene_safe is False — encoding the lecture's first principle (you are your own first patient) directly into the data pipeline.

A. A (Alert)
B. V (Voice)
C. P (Pain)
D. U (Unresponsive)

C. P = responds to Pain only. A is alert/interacting, V responds to voice, and U has no response to either voice or pain.

Q3. The "A" in the ABCDE primary assessment stands for…
A. Airway
B. Alert
C. Assessment
D. Ambulance
Show answer

A. Airway — checked first because a blocked airway is fatal within minutes. (Alert is a separate AVPU term.)

Q4. You are running ABCDE and discover inadequate breathing at step B, but you can also see a dramatic deformed forearm. What do you do?
A. Splint the forearm first because it's obvious
B. Skip ahead to E to expose everything
C. Treat the inadequate breathing now, before moving past B
D. Ignore both and recheck the scene
Show answer

C. Life threats are treated in the order found. Inadequate breathing kills far faster than a forearm fracture, so you manage B before stepping down to C/D/E.

Q5. Why does the primary assessment use a fixed A→B→C→D→E order rather than letting the responder choose?

B. The sequence is a triage of lethality — airway loss kills in minutes, environmental exposure takes far longer. The mnemonic is convenient, but the ordering is clinical.

✓ What you learned

  • A scene size-up comes first: scene safety, BSI/PPE, MOI vs NOI, number of patients, and resources — you are your own first patient.
  • The general impression ("sick vs not sick") is a wordless, equipment-free read in the first seconds.
  • AVPU grades consciousness — Alert, Voice, Pain, Unresponsive — and a falling level threatens the airway.
  • ABCDE (Airway, Breathing, Circulation, Disability, Exposure) is ordered by speed-to-death; you treat each life threat in the order found.
  • This structure underlies every NREMT EMT domain, and you can encode it as structured data with pandas.
