EMS Operations, Legal & Ethics, Documentation

Clinical skill keeps a patient alive in the moment; the call's structure, the law of consent, and an honest report are what keep the system — and you — defensible afterward.

⚠ Read first — educational only This lecture is educational familiarization only — not medical or legal advice. It is exam-prep orientation to how EMS works; it is NOT EMT certification, scope of practice, or a license to treat or to give legal counsel. Protocols, statutes, and certification rules vary by state, agency, and medical director and change over time. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number (911 in the US) and follow the directions of trained responders.

7.2Phases of a Call — the Operational Backbone

Every EMS response follows a predictable arc. Naming the phases is not bureaucratic trivia — each phase has a safety and a quality reason for existing, and exam questions love to ask "what comes first."

Dispatch & en route give you the nature of the call and time to prepare mentally and physically. Scene size-up — covered in Week 1 — is the hard gate: scene safety and standard (BSI) precautions come before patient contact, every time. Then comes patient care (primary then secondary assessment), transport and transfer of care, and finally the post-run phase: completing the report, decontaminating, and restocking so the unit is ready for the next call.

Analogy Think of a call like a commercial flight. Dispatch and en route are pre-flight briefing and taxi. Scene size-up is the captain's "is the runway clear?" — non-negotiable before takeoff. Patient care is the flight itself. Transfer of care is the handoff to the arrival crew. And the post-run report is the flight log: the plane isn't truly "done" until the paperwork that lets the next crew trust the aircraft is complete.

7.3Consent & Refusal — You Need Permission to Touch

In most jurisdictions, touching or treating a competent adult without permission can be battery. So before care, you need consent. Three flavors appear on the exam:

  • Expressed consent — a competent adult, after being told what you propose and the risks, verbally or non-verbally agrees. This is the default for an alert, oriented patient.
  • Implied consent — the law assumes a reasonable person would consent when they cannot (unconscious, severely altered, a true emergency). It applies only as long as the patient can't decide for themselves.
  • Informed consent — the underlying principle: consent is only valid if the patient understood the nature of the care, the risks, the benefits, and the alternatives, including the risk of declining.

For minors, consent normally comes from a parent or guardian; if none is reachable in an emergency, implied consent covers necessary care. Rules for emancipated minors and "mature minor" doctrines vary by state — verify locally.

Common mistake — the refusal trap A competent adult has the right to refuse any or all care, even care you're certain they need. But "competent" is doing heavy lifting: a patient who is hypoxic, hypoglycemic, intoxicated, head-injured, or suicidal may not have decision-making capacity. The exam-favorite error is accepting a refusal from someone who cannot legally give one. A valid refusal generally requires: the patient is an alert, oriented, competent adult; they were informed of the risks of refusing (including death); and the refusal is documented and witnessed. When in doubt, contact medical direction.

7.4Scope of Practice, Standard of Care & Negligence

Scope of practice is the set of skills and interventions you are legally permitted and trained to perform — defined by your certification level, your state, and your medical director's protocols. Acting outside it (e.g., an EMT performing a paramedic-only procedure) is a problem even if the patient does fine. Standard of care is what a similarly trained, reasonable provider would do under the same circumstances.

Confidentiality of patient information is protected by law (in the US, HIPAA); share it only with those involved in care or as the law requires.

7.5The Patient Care Report — Your Legal Record

The patient care report (PCR) is the official, often legal, record of the encounter. It has clinical functions (continuity of care, transfer of information, billing, quality improvement, research) and a legal function: months or years later, the PCR may be the only credible account of what happened. A widely quoted maxim captures it:

If it wasn't documented, it wasn't done.Not literally true in court — but a complete, contemporaneous record is far stronger evidence than memory of a routine call.

Good PCR narratives are accurate, objective, complete, legible, and timely. Record what you observed and did — not conclusions you can't support. Write "patient stated 'I had three beers,' breath had an alcohol odor, speech slurred" rather than the conclusion "patient was drunk." If you make an error, never erase or hide it: draw a single line through it, initial and date, and write the correction — falsifying or altering a record is far more damaging than the original mistake.

Many narratives are organized so nothing is missed. A common structure pairs the SAMPLE history (Signs/symptoms, Allergies, Medications, Past history, Last oral intake, Events leading up) with OPQRST for pain (Onset, Provocation, Quality, Region/Radiation, Severity, Time), plus serial vital signs and the interventions and the patient's response to each.

Common mistake — documenting refusals weakly The single most litigated PCR is a refusal. A bare "patient refused, signed AMA" is dangerously thin. A defensible refusal note shows the patient had capacity (alert, oriented, vitals noted), that you explained the specific risks of refusing (up to and including death), that you offered alternatives and to return, and that a witness and the patient signed. Document the patient's own words.
✦ Worked Example — a refusal decision walk-through

You're dispatched for a "fall." A 58-year-old man tripped on a curb, has a bleeding forearm laceration, is alert and oriented, vitals normal, no head strike, no alcohol. He says: "Just bandage it, I'm not going to the hospital." Walk the decision.

  1. Scene & primary first: scene safe, standard precautions on. Primary assessment: airway/breathing/circulation intact, bleeding controlled with direct pressure (Week 5). No immediate life threat.
  2. Capacity check: Is he a competent adult? Alert, oriented ×4, no intoxication, no hypoxia, no hypoglycemia, no head injury, not suicidal → he appears to have decision-making capacity.
  3. Inform of risks: You explain specific risks of declining transport — wound infection, possible tendon/nerve injury needing sutures, tetanus risk — and that he can call EMS back or go to the hospital himself any time.
  4. He still refuses transport: A competent, informed adult may refuse. You provide what he consents to (clean and dress the wound) — treating within his consent, not beyond it.
  5. Document defensibly: PCR records vitals, mental status, the specific risks explained in your words and his ("understands may need stitches, declines"), alternatives offered, a witnessed AMA signature, and instructions to return if worse.
  6. If facts changed: had he been confused, hypoxic, or intoxicated, he would likely lack capacity — implied consent and contacting medical direction would govern, not his refusal.

🛠️ Hands-On: turn PCRs into an operations dashboard

Beyond the single call, agencies analyze aggregated PCR data to spot quality and operations trends — response times, call types, documentation completeness. You'll build the same idea with Python + pandas + Matplotlib. (Reminder: the one-time Python/pandas/Matplotlib install lives in Week 1 — reuse that environment here.)

Below, a tiny synthetic set of "calls" is checked for refusal documentation completeness and charted by call type. This is the seed of your Week 10 protocol dashboard.

# pcr_dashboard.py — Lecture 7 (synthetic, illustrative data only)
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# Each row = one anonymized PCR. response_min = chute-to-scene time.
calls = pd.DataFrame([
    {"type": "Cardiac",  "response_min": 6.2, "refusal": False, "risks_documented": None},
    {"type": "Trauma",   "response_min": 8.9, "refusal": True,  "risks_documented": True},
    {"type": "Medical",  "response_min": 5.1, "refusal": True,  "risks_documented": False},  # incomplete!
    {"type": "Trauma",   "response_min": 7.4, "refusal": False, "risks_documented": None},
    {"type": "Medical",  "response_min": 9.8, "refusal": True,  "risks_documented": True},
])

# QA flag: refusals MUST show that risks were documented.
bad = calls[(calls.refusal) & (calls.risks_documented != True)]
print("Refusals missing risk documentation:", len(bad))   # 1 — flag for review

# Operations view: median response time by call type.
med = calls.groupby("type")["response_min"].median()
print(med)

med.plot(kind="bar", color="#f43f5e", edgecolor="#fb7185")
plt.ylabel("median response (min)")
plt.title("Response time by call type")
plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("response_by_type.png", dpi=120)

Extend it: add a capacity_documented column and write one QA rule that flags any refusal lacking capacity, risk, OR witness fields. That is exactly the kind of "did the chart support the decision?" audit a medical director runs.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

6 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. You arrive to find an unconscious adult who is not breathing normally. Under which principle may you begin care?
A. Expressed consent
B. Implied consent — the law assumes a reasonable person would consent when they cannot
C. Informed consent only
D. No consent is ever needed for any patient
Show answer

B. Implied consent covers a patient who cannot decide for themselves in an emergency. Expressed consent (A) requires a competent, communicating patient; it would apply once they regain capacity.

Q2. Which set of conditions best supports a valid refusal of care?
A. The patient is intoxicated but says "no"
B. A family member refuses on behalf of an alert adult
C. The patient is an alert, oriented, competent adult, informed of the risks of refusing, and the refusal is witnessed and documented
D. The patient is unconscious
Show answer

C. A valid refusal needs capacity (alert, oriented, competent), informed understanding of the risks, and proper documentation/witness. Intoxication (A) typically removes capacity; an unconscious patient (D) cannot refuse.

Q3. "Scope of practice" most accurately refers to…
A. the skills you are legally permitted and trained to perform at your certification level, in your state, under your medical director
B. whatever the patient asks you to do
C. the geographic area your agency covers
D. the total number of calls you can run per shift
Show answer

A. Scope of practice is the legally and medically authorized set of interventions for your level. It is distinct from standard of care (what a reasonable similar provider would do).

Q4. A negligence claim requires proving all four of which elements?
A. Consent, refusal, transport, billing
B. Dispatch, scene, transport, post-run
C. Airway, breathing, circulation, disability
D. Duty, breach, causation, damages
Show answer

D. Duty, breach (of the standard of care), causation, and damages must all be present. Break any one link and negligence is not established.

Q5. Which PCR narrative entry is best — accurate and objective?
A. "Patient was obviously drunk and being difficult."
B. "Patient stated 'I had three beers'; breath had alcohol odor; speech slurred; oriented to name only."
C. "Probably nothing serious, refused everything."
D. "Did the usual stuff, patient fine."
Show answer

B. Document observations and the patient's own words, not unsupported conclusions or labels. "Obviously drunk" (A) is a conclusion; C and D are vague and indefensible.

Q6. Leaving a patient without transferring care to a provider of equal or higher level is called…
A. abandonment
B. implied consent
C. a Good Samaritan act
D. standard of care
Show answer

A. Abandonment is terminating care without ensuring an equal-or-higher-level provider takes over. Good Samaritan laws (C) concern protection for good-faith off-duty aid, a different concept.
