CPR, AED & Cardiac Emergencies

When a heart stops, the only treatment that buys time is blood you push with your own hands — and the only cure is a shock delivered fast.

⚠ Read first — educational only This lecture is exam-prep familiarization, not medical advice and not EMT certification or a license to treat. Reading it does not qualify you to perform any procedure. To act in real emergencies, take a hands-on certified course (e.g., a Basic Life Support / first-aid class) with a live instructor. In a real emergency, call your local emergency number immediately.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the 30:2 compression-to-ventilation ratio and when it applies.
  • Describe how an AED works and why early defibrillation is the single biggest survival lever.

4.1The Problem: a Pump That Stopped

In sudden cardiac arrest, the heart stops moving blood — most often because its electrical rhythm has degenerated into a chaotic quiver called ventricular fibrillation (VF). No circulation means no oxygen delivery; the brain begins sustaining injury within minutes. Two interventions reverse this, and only two: push blood manually (CPR) and reset the rhythm (defibrillation). Everything in this lecture serves those two goals.

Survival falls steeply with each passing minute. A widely-cited rule of thumb is that the chance of survival from a shockable arrest drops on the order of 7–10% for every minute that defibrillation is delayed when no CPR is given — bystander CPR substantially slows that decline by keeping the heart and brain perfused until a shock arrives.

Survival ≈ S₀ − k · tdelayHeuristic, not a precise law: each minute without defibrillation costs roughly 7–10 percentage points absent CPR. Treat the numbers as motivational, not clinical — verify against current resuscitation council guidance (as of 2026 — verify against current official sources).

This is why the order of operations is fixed and drilled until it is reflex. We capture it as the Chain of Survival: recognition + call → early CPR → early defibrillation → advanced care → recovery. The first three links are entirely in a bystander's hands.

4.2High-Quality Compressions

CPR does not restart the heart. It is a manual pump: each compression squeezes the heart and chest, pushing oxygenated blood to the brain and coronary arteries; each release lets the chest refill. The quality of that pump is measurable, and small lapses cost lives. Five metrics define high-quality compressions for an adult:

  • Rate: 100–120 per minute. Faster than ~120 leaves too little time to refill the heart between pushes; slower than 100 underperfuses.
  • Depth: at least 2 inches (5 cm) for an adult, and not more than ~2.4 in (6 cm). Shallow compressions don't move blood.
  • Full recoil: let the chest rise completely between pushes — leaning on the chest raises pressure and blocks venous return.
  • Minimize interruptions: aim to spend a high fraction of time actively compressing (high "chest-compression fraction"). Every pause drops perfusion pressure that takes several compressions to rebuild.
  • Avoid over-ventilation: too many or too forceful breaths raise chest pressure and reduce blood return.
rate ∈ [100, 120] min⁻¹  ·  depth ≥ 2 in (5 cm)  ·  full recoil  ·  minimize pausesThe five quality metrics for adult compressions (current first-aid consensus; confirm pediatric/infant variants separately). As of 2026 — verify against current official sources.
AnalogyThink of the heart's coronary arteries as a garden hose you have to re-pressurize from scratch every time you let go. Each compression builds pressure; each pause lets it bleed back to zero. That's why interruptions are so costly: you're not pausing a steady flow, you're resetting the pump and have to climb the pressure hill all over again. "Push hard, push fast, don't stop" is the whole game.

4.330:2 — Compressions and Breaths

For a single lay rescuer (or two rescuers) doing CPR on an adult with a barrier or bag-mask, the standard cycle is 30 compressions : 2 breaths. Compress 30 times at 100–120/min, then deliver 2 breaths (each over about one second, just enough to see the chest rise), then immediately resume compressions.

Why a ratio at all? Because compressions and ventilations compete for time on the chest. The 30:2 ratio is a tuned compromise: enough compressions to keep perfusion pressure up, with periodic breaths to refresh oxygen — while accepting that any pause for breaths costs perfusion.

Common mistakeTwo errors dominate real arrests. (1) Hyperventilation — blasting big, fast breaths. This raises intrathoracic pressure, chokes off venous return, and actively lowers the blood you just worked to circulate; give just enough to make the chest rise. (2) Long pauses for breaths or pulse checks. If you are an untrained or unwilling-to-ventilate bystander, current guidance supports continuous compression-only ("hands-only") CPR for an adult who collapsed suddenly — doing something well beats doing the "complete" version badly. (As of 2026 — verify the exact ratios and exceptions against current official sources.)

The 30:2 ratio is the default for adults; ratios and technique differ for children and infants, and change once an advanced airway is placed (continuous compressions with asynchronous breaths). Those variants are tested separately on the NREMT cognitive exam.

4.4The AED & Early Defibrillation

An Automated External Defibrillator (AED) is a public-access device designed so an untrained person can use it. It reads the heart's rhythm through adhesive pads, decides whether the rhythm is shockable (VF or pulseless ventricular tachycardia), and — if so — charges and prompts you to deliver a shock. A defibrillation shock briefly stops all electrical activity in the heart, giving the heart's natural pacemaker a chance to resume an organized beat.

The single most important fact about AEDs: do exactly what it says, and minimize pauses around the shock. The universal flow:

Practical points the exam tests: ensure no one is touching the patient when the AED analyzes or shocks ("I'm clear, you're clear, everyone clear"); dry the chest and avoid pads over a pacemaker bump or medication patch; for a very hairy chest you may need to shave the pad sites; pediatric pads/attenuators are used for young children if available. After any shock, go straight back to compressions for ~2 minutes before the next rhythm check.

4.5Chest Pain & Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)

Not every cardiac emergency is an arrest. Far more common is the conscious patient with chest pain, which may signal acute coronary syndrome (ACS) — a spectrum from unstable angina to myocardial infarction (heart attack), caused by a coronary artery that is partially or fully blocked. Recognizing and rapidly transporting these patients prevents many arrests before they happen.

Recognizing ACS

Classic presentation: pressure, squeezing, or heaviness in the center of the chest, possibly radiating to the arm, jaw, or back, with shortness of breath, sweating (diaphoresis), nausea, or a feeling of doom. Atypical presentations are common and dangerous — women, older adults, and people with diabetes may have only fatigue, shortness of breath, or upper-abdominal discomfort. When in doubt, treat as cardiac.

Prehospital response (at the EMT scope)

  • Activate EMS early and prepare to acquire/transmit a 12-lead ECG so a hospital can ready the cath lab — the goal is fast reperfusion.
  • Position of comfort, reassurance, supplemental oxygen only if hypoxic (routine high-flow O₂ is no longer recommended for normally-oxygenated patients).
  • Aspirin: for suspected ACS without contraindication/allergy, chewable aspirin is commonly indicated (it inhibits platelets, limiting clot growth) — given per local protocol.
  • Nitroglycerin: may be assisted per protocol if prescribed and blood pressure permits — but it is contraindicated with recent erectile-dysfunction medications and in low blood pressure.

These medication specifics vary by jurisdiction and protocol and change over time — they are the kind of detail you confirm against your current local protocol and resuscitation guidelines (as of 2026 — verify against current official sources). Critically: a conscious chest-pain patient can deteriorate to arrest, so the AED stays at the bedside and you stay ready to start CPR.

AnalogyACS is a partly clogged drain; arrest is the drain fully blocked and the basin overflowing. Aspirin is like stopping more gunk from sticking to the clog, and rapid transport is calling the plumber who can actually clear the pipe (open the artery). Acting at the "slow drain" stage is far easier than after the flood.

4.6Where This Sits on the NREMT Exam

The national EMT cognitive (knowledge) exam is organized into content domains, each carrying an approximate weight. As of 2026, the major domains are: Airway, Respiration & Ventilation; Cardiology & Resuscitation; Trauma; Medical / Obstetrics & Gynecology; and EMS Operations. Today's material lives squarely in Cardiology & Resuscitation, one of the most heavily weighted clinical domains.

✦ Worked Example — a collapse in a public space (decision walk-through)

You witness an adult suddenly collapse in a transit station. Walk the decision tree at the bystander level (familiarization only — not a substitute for hands-on certification).

  1. Scene size-up (Week 1): is the scene safe? No traffic, fire, or live electrical hazard — approach.
  2. Check responsiveness & breathing: tap and shout. No response, and no normal breathing (gasping does not count) → treat as cardiac arrest.
  3. Activate the system: call your emergency number (or direct a specific bystander: "You in the red jacket — call now and get the AED"). Naming a person prevents the diffusion-of-responsibility freeze.
  4. Start compressions: heel of the hand on the center of the chest, push ≥ 2 in (5 cm) at 100–120/min, allow full recoil. Trained & willing → add 2 breaths every 30 compressions; otherwise → hands-only continuous compressions.
  5. AED arrives: power on, attach pads, stop touching the patient while it analyzes. "Clear!" If it advises a shock, ensure everyone is clear and deliver it.
  6. Immediately resume compressions for ~2 minutes; the AED re-analyzes. Continue until the person moves/breathes normally or EMS takes over.
  7. Takeaway: the high-leverage moves are early call, early compressions, and early AED — and minimizing every pause in between. None of the steps are exotic; the difficulty is doing the simple things fast and without hesitation.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a compression-rate metronome & quality chart

You can't certify yourself in a browser — but you can internalize the target rate and visualize why the 100–120/min window exists. We'll generate an audible-style metronome timing and plot how cardiac output (a stylized model) peaks inside the recommended band. (Reminder: the Python + pandas + Matplotlib install lives in Week 1 — reuse that environment here.)

# cpr_quality.py — Lecture 4 (educational model, NOT clinical)
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# 1) Metronome: time stamps for compressions at a chosen rate
rate = 110                      # target compressions per minute (in 100-120 band)
beats = np.arange(30)           # one cycle of 30 compressions
t = beats * (60.0 / rate)       # seconds at which each push should land
print(f"At {rate}/min, 30 compressions take {t[-1]:.1f}s "
      f"(+ ~5s for 2 breaths -> ~{t[-1]+5:.0f}s per 30:2 cycle)")

# 2) Stylized 'output vs rate' curve: too slow underperfuses,
#    too fast leaves no refill time. Peak sits inside 100-120.
rates = np.arange(60, 161, 5)
refill = np.clip(1 - (rates - 60) / 140, 0, 1)   # less refill as rate climbs
output = rates * refill                            # flow ~ rate x stroke (toy model)
df = pd.DataFrame({"rate": rates, "rel_output": output / output.max()})
print(df[(df.rate >= 100) & (df.rate <= 120)])

plt.figure(figsize=(6, 3.4))
plt.plot(df.rate, df.rel_output, color="#f43f5e", lw=2)
plt.axvspan(100, 120, color="#34d399", alpha=0.18, label="target 100-120/min")
plt.xlabel("compression rate (per min)"); plt.ylabel("relative output (toy)")
plt.title("Why 100-120/min? (illustrative, not clinical)")
plt.legend(); plt.tight_layout(); plt.savefig("cpr_rate.png", dpi=120)
print("saved cpr_rate.png")

The model is intentionally crude — its only job is to show why a window exists rather than a single magic number. Try shifting the refill penalty and watch the peak move; then compare the famous 100-beat songs people are taught to compress to and check they land inside the band.

🔗 Where this is heading

Cardiac care is becoming a data problem. Drone-delivered AEDs, smartwatch arrhythmia detection, and dispatcher-assisted CPR all shorten the links in the Chain of Survival you mapped today, and machine-learning models are being studied to flag deteriorating ACS from ECG patterns earlier — promising, but still being validated, not proven to replace clinician judgment. In Week 10's capstone you'll wire several of these threads into a personal "protocol dashboard," and the same instinct you built here — recognize fast, act on the highest-leverage step, measure quality — is exactly the mindset behind the frontier-medicine survey that closes the course.

Q1. The recommended compression rate for adult CPR is approximately…
A. 60–80 per minute
B. 100–120 per minute
C. as fast as physically possible
D. 140–160 per minute
Show answer

B. 100–120/min. Slower underperfuses; faster than ~120 leaves too little time for the heart to refill between compressions.

Q2. Minimum compression depth for an adult is about…
A. half an inch (1 cm)
B. 1 inch (2.5 cm)
C. at least 2 inches (5 cm)
D. at least 4 inches (10 cm)
Show answer

C. At least 2 in (5 cm), and not more than ~2.4 in (6 cm). Shallow compressions fail to move meaningful blood.

Q3. For single-rescuer adult CPR with ventilations, the standard compression-to-breath ratio is…
A. 15:2
B. 5:1
C. 30:2
D. 50:5
Show answer

C. 30 compressions then 2 breaths for an adult. (Ratios differ for children/infants and once an advanced airway is in place.)

Q4. When an AED says "Analyzing rhythm," you should…
A. ensure no one is touching the patient and stop compressions
B. keep compressing through the analysis
C. remove the pads and re-check the pulse
D. deliver a shock immediately yourself
Show answer

A. Movement and contact corrupt the rhythm analysis and risk shocking a rescuer. Everyone clears while it analyzes; then resume compressions the instant it's done.

Q5. Immediately after an AED delivers a shock, the correct next action is to…
A. wait two minutes before touching the patient
B. resume chest compressions right away
C. check for a pulse for a full 30 seconds first
D. remove the AED pads
Show answer

B. Restart compressions immediately and continue ~2 minutes before the next rhythm check. Minimizing the post-shock pause raises the chance of a perfusing rhythm.

Q6. A conscious adult reports central chest pressure radiating to the arm, with sweating and nausea. At the EMT scope, an appropriate early action (per local protocol) is to…
A. have them walk briskly to "work it out"
B. withhold all care until pain resolves on its own
C. give routine high-flow oxygen regardless of oxygen saturation
D. activate EMS, place in a position of comfort, and consider aspirin if not contraindicated
Show answer

D. Suspect ACS: early activation, comfort/reassurance, oxygen only if hypoxic, and aspirin per protocol absent allergy/contraindication. Exertion (A) is harmful; routine high-flow O₂ (C) is no longer recommended for normally-oxygenated patients.
