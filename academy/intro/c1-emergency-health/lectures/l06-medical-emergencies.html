6.2Diabetic Emergency & Oral Glucose

The brain runs almost entirely on glucose and stores none, so when blood sugar drops (hypoglycemia) the brain "browns out" within minutes. Classic findings: sudden confusion, sweating, shakiness, pale clammy skin, hostility or drunk-like behavior — often in someone known to have diabetes who took insulin but skipped a meal.

The rule of thumb that resolves almost every field dilemma:

When in doubt → treat for LOW sugarUntreated low sugar harms the brain in minutes; brief extra sugar in a high-sugar patient does comparatively little harm.

First-line treatment for a conscious patient who can swallow and protect their airway is oral glucose — gel, tablets, or a sugary drink. It is absorbed through the gut and raises blood sugar within minutes.

Common mistake — the airway trapNever put oral glucose (gel, food, or drink) into the mouth of an unresponsive patient or anyone who can't swallow — it can be aspirated into the lungs. An unresponsive low-sugar patient is an airway/ALS problem: position, protect the airway, and get advanced help (IV dextrose or glucagon).

6.3Seizures — Protect, Don't Restrain

A generalized seizure is a storm of abnormal electrical activity in the brain. During the active (tonic-clonic) phase the patient is unresponsive with stiffening and rhythmic jerking; afterward comes the postictal phase — confused, sleepy, slowly clearing.

For most single seizures the EMT job is simple and almost entirely protective:

  • Move hard or sharp objects away; cushion the head. Do not restrain the limbs and do not put anything in the mouth.
  • When jerking stops, roll to the recovery position and manage the airway (suction secretions).
  • Time it. A seizure lasting more than ~5 minutes, or back-to-back seizures without recovery (status epilepticus), is a true emergency needing urgent advanced care.
Myth to dropYou cannot "swallow your tongue," and forcing an object into the mouth breaks teeth and fingers. The old advice is wrong — protect the head, don't pry the jaw.

6.4Stroke — FAST / BE-FAST and "Time Is Brain"

A stroke is a sudden loss of blood flow to part of the brain — usually a clot blocking an artery (ischemic), sometimes a bleed (hemorrhagic). Either way, downstream brain tissue is dying. The famous estimate: an ischemic stroke can destroy on the order of ~1.9 million neurons per minute, which is why the field mantra is "time is brain."

The screening tool is FAST, extended to BE-FAST to catch posterior-circulation strokes that plain FAST misses:

The EMT does not treat the stroke itself — the treatment lives in the hospital. The job is: recognize fast, get the last-known-well time, keep the airway safe, check the blood sugar (low sugar can mimic stroke), and move to a stroke-capable center while pre-notifying. Every minute saved widens the window for definitive therapy.

6.5Opioid Overdose & Naloxone

Opioids (heroin, fentanyl, prescription painkillers) suppress the brainstem's drive to breathe. The lethal pathway is respiratory: breathing slows and stops, oxygen falls, and the heart follows. The classic triad is pinpoint pupils + slow/absent breathing + decreased responsiveness.

Naloxone (often by intranasal spray) is a competitive antagonist: it knocks opioids off their receptors and can restore breathing within minutes. But the priority order matters — oxygen and ventilation come first.

Airway / rescue breaths → naloxone → reassess, repeat as neededPeople die of opioid overdose from not breathing; naloxone helps only if oxygen is also being delivered. Effects can wear off before the opioid does — patients can re-sedate.
AnalogyAn opioid is like a key jammed in the "breathe" lock so it won't turn. Naloxone is a stronger key that pops the jammed one out and frees the lock — but it's a short-acting key. When it falls out (30–90 min), the original opioid, if still in the body, can jam the lock again. That's why every reversed patient still needs monitoring and transport.

6.6Anaphylaxis & the Epinephrine Auto-Injector

Anaphylaxis is a rapid, body-wide allergic reaction (foods, stings, drugs). It is dangerous because it hits two systems at once: the airway swells (throat tightness, wheeze, drooling, stridor) and the circulation collapses (hives, flushing, dropping blood pressure, faintness). Suspect it whenever a skin reaction appears together with breathing trouble or signs of shock.

The single first-line drug is epinephrine, delivered by an auto-injector into the outer thigh (intramuscular). It does the exact opposite of the reaction: tightens blood vessels (raises pressure), opens the airways, and calms the swelling.

Common mistake — waiting for antihistaminesAntihistamines and inhalers do not treat true anaphylaxis fast enough. Epinephrine is the treatment; give it promptly per protocol. Inject into the outer thigh (not the buttock or a vein), hold per device instructions, and arrange transport — symptoms can return (biphasic reaction).

✦ Worked Example — pattern-match the right first move

Scene: a 60-year-old at a dinner table suddenly can't speak clearly and the right side of the face droops. No injury, breathing fine, skin normal. Walk the decision.

  1. Scene & primary check: safe scene, airway open, breathing adequate, pulse present — not an arrest, not an obvious overdose (pupils normal, breathing fine).
  2. Pattern: sudden focal deficit (speech + face) = run BE-FAST. Face droop ✓ and speech ✓ are positive → high suspicion of stroke.
  3. Rule out the great mimic: check a blood glucose — hypoglycemia can imitate a stroke and is instantly treatable with oral glucose if conscious and able to swallow. Here, say glucose is normal.
  4. Critical data: ask bystanders for the last-known-well time — when was the patient last seen normal? This number drives hospital eligibility for clot-busting/retrieval.
  5. Action: keep airway safe, monitor, and transport now to a stroke-capable center with pre-notification. No food/drink (swallowing may be impaired). The first move is activate the stroke system fast — "time is brain."

🛠️ Hands-On: build a triage decision table & a "time-to-treat" chart

Reminder: this uses the Python + pandas + Matplotlib (Jupyter) stack you installed in Week 1 — no new install needed.

First encode the five patterns as a lookup table, then plot how each emergency's danger rises with delay — a visual for "why speed matters."

# medical_emergencies.py — Lecture 6
import pandas as pd
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# 1) Pattern -> single first-line move (familiarization, not protocol)
table = pd.DataFrame([
    ["Hypoglycemia (conscious)", "confusion, sweaty, shaky", "Oral glucose"],
    ["Seizure",                  "jerking then postictal",   "Protect head, time it, recovery position"],
    ["Stroke",                   "BE-FAST positive",         "Last-known-well + rapid transport"],
    ["Opioid overdose",          "pinpoint pupils, slow breathing", "Ventilate, then naloxone"],
    ["Anaphylaxis",              "hives + airway/shock",     "Epinephrine auto-injector (thigh)"],
], columns=["Emergency", "Key clue", "First-line move"])
print(table.to_string(index=False))

# 2) Illustrative "harm vs delay" curves (conceptual, not clinical data)
minutes = range(0, 11)
curves = {
    "Stroke (neurons lost)": [m for m in minutes],          # ~linear, steep
    "Opioid (hypoxia)":      [m**1.4/3 for m in minutes],   # accelerating
    "Anaphylaxis (airway)":  [m**1.3/3 for m in minutes],
}
for label, y in curves.items():
    plt.plot(list(minutes), y, marker="o", label=label)
plt.xlabel("minutes of delay"); plt.ylabel("relative harm (illustrative)")
plt.title("Why speed matters: harm climbs with every minute")
plt.legend(); plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

B. Opioid overdose kills by stopping breathing, so airway/ventilation comes first; naloxone reverses the opioid but only helps if oxygen is also delivered. Naloxone can also wear off before the opioid does.

Model response

The fastest separator is the breathing-and-pupils picture combined with the skin. An opioid overdose announces itself with pinpoint pupils and slow/absent breathing — that respiratory pattern is almost unique among the three. Anaphylaxis shows a skin reaction (hives/flushing) plus airway swelling or shock appearing together over minutes after an exposure. A stroke typically has normal breathing and pupils but a sudden focal neurological deficit — one-sided face droop, arm drift, or slurred speech (BE-FAST). So a quick scan of "how are they breathing, what do the pupils look like, is the skin involved?" steers you immediately.

Speed matters for different reasons: in stroke, brain tissue dies every minute and the last-known-well time controls treatment eligibility ("time is brain"); in opioid overdose, the patient is hypoxic right now and minutes without oxygen cause arrest, so ventilation plus naloxone can't wait; in anaphylaxis, the airway can close and blood pressure crash within minutes, and epinephrine reverses both — delay for antihistamines costs lives. A good answer also notes the universal safety net: check blood glucose, because hypoglycemia mimics all of these.
