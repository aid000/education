Week 8 · Lecture 8

Tool-Specific Mastery — Claude, ChatGPT & Grok

Three frontier assistants, three personalities. Knowing what each is built to be good at beats brand loyalty every time.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the durable capability strengths of Claude, ChatGPT, and Grok — and why they differ.
  • Explain what a long context window, artifacts/Projects, custom GPTs, an explicit reasoning mode, and real-time search grounding each do.
  • Map a task's properties (length, freshness, reusability, depth) to the tool most likely to win.
  • Use a simple decision checklist to pick a tool — and know when to verify across two.
  • Run a hands-on comparison on free tiers and log the results in Jupyter.

8.1Same Foundation, Different Choices

By now you know the shared machinery: every one of these tools is a large transformer trained on next-token prediction, then aligned with fine-tuning and RLHF (Weeks 1–2). So why do they feel different? Because the model weights are only part of the product. Around the raw model each company wraps a distinct set of scaffolding — context handling, memory, tools, search, and interface — and tunes the model's behaviour toward a particular ideal of "helpful."

Those choices are the durable part. Specific version numbers, prices, and context-window sizes change almost monthly, so this lecture stays at the capability level: what kind of thing each tool is built to do well. Any concrete figure below is labelled "as of 2026 — verify against current official sources."

AnalogyThink of three professional kitchens that all start from the same pantry. One specialises in banquets — it can hold an enormous amount of ingredients in mind at once and plate a coherent multi-course meal (long context). One runs a custom-order counter where you can save reusable recipes and ask for slow, careful preparation (custom GPTs + reasoning). One is a news-stand café wired to the wire services, serving what's happening right now (real-time search). Same pantry, very different best orders.

8.2Claude — Long Context, Projects & Artifacts

Claude's signature is its comfort with large, coherent bodies of text. Its context window — the amount of text it can attend to at once — is large enough to hold whole documents, long codebases, or many chat turns. (As of 2026 the window is on the order of hundreds of thousands of tokens — verify against current official sources.) That matters because everything inside the window is available to attention; anything outside it is, to the model, gone.

window (tokens) ≈ system + history + your_files + replyEvery part of the conversation competes for the same fixed token budget. A long window means more room before older content must be dropped or summarised.

Projects let you attach reference files and standing instructions that persist across many chats, so the model "remembers" your style guide or codebase without you re-pasting it each time. Artifacts are a side panel where generated code, documents, or small apps render and can be iterated on in place — rather than scrolling back through chat to find the latest version.

Net effect: Claude tends to shine on tasks that are long and consistency-sensitive — reviewing a 60-page contract, refactoring across many files, or drafting a report that must stay coherent end to end.

Note — a big window is not a free passA large context window lets the model see a lot, but accuracy can still sag for facts buried in the middle of very long inputs (the "lost-in-the-middle" effect, observed in long-context research). Put the most important instructions and material near the start or end, and don't assume "I pasted it, so it used it." Verify on long inputs.

8.3ChatGPT — Custom GPTs & Reasoning Modes

ChatGPT's distinguishing moves are reusability and explicit reasoning. A custom GPT is a saved configuration — a system prompt, optional reference files, and optional tool/action hooks — that you build once and reuse, or share with others. It is the prompt-engineering of Weeks 4–5 packaged so you don't retype it: a "Citation Checker," a "Lab-Report Formatter," a tutor that always answers in your preferred structure.

The second move is a reasoning mode — a setting (or separate model) that spends extra computation "thinking" before answering, generating a longer internal chain of reasoning. This is chain-of-thought (Week 4) made native: it trades latency and cost for better performance on multi-step problems like math, logic, and planning. For a quick factual lookup it is overkill; for a tricky proof or a careful plan it can be decisive.

quality ≈ f(model, prompt, thinking time)Reasoning modes turn "thinking time" into a dial you can turn up — more internal steps for harder problems, at the cost of speed.

ChatGPT also tends to carry the broadest ecosystem of integrations and a built-in image/file pipeline, making it a strong general default when you're unsure where a task will go.

8.4Grok — Real-Time & Search-Grounded

A pretrained model's knowledge has a training cutoff: it does not know events after the date its training data ends, and asking it about "today" invites a confident-sounding hallucination. Grok's signature is tightening that gap by being search-native — it is designed to pull in live web and social-feed results and ground answers in what is happening now.

answer = model(prompt + retrieved_live_results)Search grounding (a form of retrieval-augmented generation) injects fresh, dated sources into the context so the model can cite current events instead of guessing from stale training data.

That makes Grok well suited to freshness-critical questions: breaking news, "what are people saying about X right now," fast-moving prices or scores. The flip side: live sources can be wrong, biased, or unverified, and a real-time feed can amplify rumours. Grounding reduces stale-knowledge errors but does not eliminate hallucination or guarantee the sources are reliable — always check the citation, not just the claim. (We dig into this in Week 9.)

8.5Picking the Right Tool

Skip brand loyalty; match the task to the capability. Score your task on four axes, then pick the tool whose signature strength lines up:

  • Length / coherence — many pages or files that must stay consistent → favour long context (Claude).
  • Reusability — a workflow you'll run again and again → build a custom GPT (ChatGPT).
  • Depth — multi-step math, logic, planning → turn on a reasoning mode (ChatGPT, or any model with extended thinking).
  • Freshness — depends on what's happening today → use search grounding (Grok, or any tool with live web access on).

Two rules sit above the chart. First, for any high-stakes answer, cross-check across at least two tools — agreement raises your confidence, disagreement flags something to verify. Second, the differences are tendencies, not laws: capabilities converge quickly, so re-test your own real tasks rather than trusting last quarter's benchmark.

AnalogyPicking an AI tool is like picking a vehicle. You don't ask "which car is best?" — you ask "best for what trip?" A moving van (long context) for hauling a lot at once; a car with a saved GPS route and cruise control (custom GPT + reasoning) for a commute you repeat; a live traffic app on your phone (search grounding) for "what's the fastest way right now." The winner depends entirely on the journey.
✦ Worked Example — routing four real tasks

You have four jobs this week. Walk each through the four-axis checklist and pick a tool. (Tools are interchangeable in principle — we pick the one whose default strength fits best.)

  1. "Summarise this 80-page lab manual and flag inconsistencies." High length + coherence, low freshness. → Long context (Claude): the whole manual fits in one window so cross-page contradictions are visible to attention.
  2. "Every day, reformat my raw notes into the same structured study sheet." High reusability, low depth/freshness. → Custom GPT (ChatGPT): encode the format once, run it daily without re-prompting.
  3. "Prove this number-theory result and check each step." High depth, low freshness/length. → Reasoning mode (ChatGPT / extended thinking): spend extra thinking time on the multi-step logic.
  4. "What's the current reaction to today's product launch?" High freshness. → Search grounding (Grok / live web): it can retrieve and cite sources from the last few hours.
  5. Cross-check: the math proof (task 3) is high-stakes, so re-run it on a second tool. If both reach the same proof, confidence is high; if they diverge, you've found the step to scrutinise.

🛠️ Hands-On: a three-tool bake-off (free tiers)

Free tiers of each assistant are plenty for this. (Reminder: your Python/Jupyter environment was set up in Week 1 — no reinstall needed; we only use Jupyter here as a structured log.)

Run the same four prompts through each tool you can access, then score them. Use this checklist:

# Bake-off checklist — run identical prompts in each tool
P1 (long):    Paste a long doc -> "Summarise + list contradictions."
P2 (reuse):   "Reformat these notes into a study sheet: [template]."
P3 (depth):   A multi-step word problem -> ask to show reasoning.
P4 (fresh):   "What happened with [a topic] in the last 24 hours? Cite sources."

# Score each 1-5 on: correctness, coherence, freshness, citations.
# Note: did it hallucinate? Did it cite a checkable source?

Log your scores in a Jupyter cell so the comparison is reproducible:

Re-run this bake-off in a few months. Because capabilities converge fast, your table is the only benchmark that's truly up to date for your tasks.

🔗 Where this is heading

The same routing instinct scales up. In multi-model agentic systems (Weeks 6–7), an orchestrator can dispatch each sub-task to the model that's strongest at it — long-context model for document analysis, reasoning model for planning, search-grounded model for fresh facts — then combine the results. The four-axis checklist you just used by hand becomes a literal routing function in code. And once a system is choosing tools and sources automatically, knowing when not to trust the output becomes the critical skill — exactly where Week 9 picks up.

Q1. Why do Claude, ChatGPT, and Grok behave differently if they're all transformers trained with next-token prediction?
A. They use completely different mathematics from each other
B. The scaffolding around the model — context handling, memory, tools, search, tuning — differs
C. Only one of them is actually a neural network
D. They are the same product with different logos
Show answer

B. The shared engine is similar; the durable differences come from the product wrapper — context window, Projects/memory, custom configs, reasoning modes, and search grounding — plus alignment tuning.

Q2. A large context window primarily lets a model…
A. permanently learn new facts from your chat
B. run faster on short prompts
C. attend to more text at once (whole documents, long histories) within a single request
D. connect to the live internet
Show answer

C. The window is how much text is visible to attention in one request. It does not retrain the model (A), speed up short prompts (B), or grant web access (D).

Q3. A "lost-in-the-middle" effect on very long inputs means you should…
A. always trust facts buried mid-document because the window is large
B. place the most important instructions/material near the start or end, and verify
C. never paste documents longer than one page
D. assume the model ignores everything you paste
Show answer

B. A big window means the model can see a lot, but recall can dip for content in the middle of long inputs. Put key material at the edges and verify rather than assuming it was used.

Q4. A custom GPT is best described as…
A. a brand-new model trained from scratch on your data
B. a saved, reusable configuration (system prompt + optional files/tools) over an existing model
C. a way to make the model run offline
D. a hardware upgrade
Show answer

B. It's packaged prompt engineering — instructions, reference files, and optional actions saved for reuse — not retraining the underlying model.

Q5. Turning on an explicit reasoning mode mainly trades…
A. accuracy for privacy
B. context size for speed
C. latency/cost for better performance on multi-step problems
D. nothing — it changes nothing about the output
Show answer

C. Reasoning modes generate a longer internal chain of thought, spending more time/compute to do better on math, logic, and planning. It's overkill for a quick lookup.

Q6. For "what's happening with X in the last few hours?", the most relevant capability — and its key caveat — is…
A. real-time search grounding; but live sources can be wrong, so check the citation, not just the claim
B. a bigger context window; live data appears automatically
C. reasoning mode; thinking longer reveals today's news
D. a custom GPT; saved prompts include current events
Show answer

A. Search grounding retrieves fresh, dated sources past the training cutoff — but it reduces, not eliminates, hallucination, and the retrieved sources themselves still need verifying.

✓ What you learned

  • All three tools share a transformer base; their durable differences come from the scaffolding wrapped around the model.
  • Claude leans on a long context window, Projects, and artifacts — strong on long, coherence-sensitive work.
  • ChatGPT offers reusable custom GPTs and an explicit reasoning mode that trades time for depth.
  • Grok is search-native, grounding answers in live sources to beat the training cutoff — with citations still to be verified.
  • Route tasks by length, reusability, depth, and freshness; cross-check high-stakes answers; re-test as capabilities converge.
