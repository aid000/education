Week 1 · Lecture 1

Why AI Works Now — Compute, Data & the Transformer

The same big ideas existed for decades. Three things finally arrived at the same time — and that, not magic, is why AI suddenly works.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why deep learning was effectively "impossible" before roughly 2012 despite the math being old.
  • Name the three unlocks — compute, data, and architecture — and what each one contributed.
  • Describe the AlexNet (2012) moment and why GPUs mattered.
  • Explain at an intro level what the Transformer (2017) and attention changed about training and context.
  • State what "scaling" means and read the rough scaling relationship for model loss.

1.1The Ideas Were Old — So Why Now?

It's tempting to think modern AI is built on a recent invention. It isn't. The core math is decades old: artificial neural networks go back to the 1940s–50s, backpropagation (the algorithm that trains them) was popularized in the 1980s, and convolutional networks for images existed in the late 1980s and 1990s. Yet for a long time these methods underperformed simpler techniques and were widely considered a dead end.

The reason is mundane, not mysterious. A neural network is a giant function with millions or billions of adjustable numbers (parameters). To make it useful you need three things at once: enough computing power to do the arithmetic, enough data to pin down all those parameters, and a network design that the hardware can train efficiently. Until about 2012, all three were missing or mismatched. When they finally lined up, the field went from "impossible" to "inevitable" in a few years.

AnalogyThink of a sailing ship. The idea of a sail is ancient, but a sail alone does nothing. You also need wind (compute), an ocean wide enough to cross (data), and a hull shaped to catch the wind (architecture). Modern AI is the moment all three arrived together — the math was always the sail, just waiting for weather.

1.2The Three Unlocks

Almost everything in this course traces back to three simultaneous shifts. Hold onto this triangle — we'll return to it every week.

Unlock 1 — Compute (parallel hardware)

Training a network means multiplying huge grids of numbers (matrices) billions of times. A regular CPU does a few of these at a time. A GPU (graphics processing unit), built to shade millions of pixels at once, does thousands in parallel — and matrix math is exactly the kind of work it's good at. Repurposing GPUs from video games to neural networks gave researchers a roughly 10–100× speedup, turning experiments that would take months into ones that take days.

Unlock 2 — Data at scale

A model with millions of parameters needs a correspondingly large number of examples or it just memorizes. The release of ImageNet — a labeled dataset of over a million images across a thousand categories — gave vision researchers that scale for the first time. Later, the entire crawlable web became the training set for language models. Data is the "teacher": more of it, of higher quality, lets a network learn patterns instead of trivia.

Unlock 3 — Architecture

Finally, the network's shape has to be trainable at scale. Convolutional networks made vision tractable; the Transformer (Section 1.4) did the same for language and unlocked the scaling era. A good architecture is one whose computation maps cleanly onto parallel hardware — which is why architecture and compute are deeply linked.

1.3The AlexNet Moment (2012)

If modern AI has a "big bang," it's 2012. A deep convolutional network now known as AlexNet entered the annual ImageNet image-recognition competition and won by a startling margin — its error rate was several percentage points below the next-best entry, a gap that had previously moved by fractions of a percent per year. Crucially, it was trained on GPUs, which made the network's size and depth practical.

AlexNet itself invented little that was brand-new; its power came from combining the three unlocks: a deep convolutional architecture, the large ImageNet data, and GPU compute. That combination is the template for everything since. The result convinced the field — almost overnight — that deep learning was not a dead end but the main road.

Common misconceptionPeople often say "AI was invented in 2012" or "in 2017." Neither is true. The algorithms were already there; what changed was that hardware and data made them work. Be precise: 2012 (AlexNet) proved deep learning at scale for vision; 2017 (the Transformer) unlocked scaling for language. Treat any single "invention date" as a red flag for hype.

1.4The Transformer & Attention (2017)

Vision had its breakthrough, but language lagged. The networks used for text — recurrent networks — read one word at a time, like reading with a finger on the page. That made them slow to train (you can't parallelize a sequence you must process in order) and forgetful over long passages.

In 2017 a paper titled "Attention Is All You Need" introduced the Transformer. Its key mechanism is attention: instead of reading strictly left to right, every word can directly "look at" every other word and weigh how relevant each is. To process the word "it" in a sentence, the model can attend back to the noun it refers to, no matter how far away.

Two consequences made this revolutionary. First, attention is computed for all positions in parallel, so Transformers train beautifully on GPUs — architecture and compute clicking together. Second, the direct connections handle long-range context far better than reading word by word. Transformers are the backbone of essentially every frontier model today (the "GPT" in ChatGPT stands for Generative Pre-trained Transformer).

1.5The Scaling Era

Once the Transformer made training scalable, researchers noticed something with big economic consequences: making models bigger — more parameters, more data, more compute — kept making them better, in a smooth, predictable way. These observations are called scaling laws. Roughly, a model's error (its prediction "loss") falls as a power law in each ingredient:

loss ≈ (constant) × (compute)−aA power law: error drops steadily as compute, data, and parameters grow together. Small positive exponent a — gains slow but keep coming. (Schematic; exact constants are model-specific.)

This predictability is why so much money and hardware flowed into the field: if buying 10× the compute reliably buys a better model, scaling becomes an engineering plan rather than a gamble. It also explains emergence — at large enough scale, models begin doing things (translation, arithmetic, coding) they were never explicitly taught, simply as a side effect of getting very good at prediction. We'll unpack how that prediction works in Week 2.

Stay honest about scalingScaling laws are powerful but not infinite. Gains slow as the exponent bites; high-quality data is finite; and bigger models cost more energy and money. As of 2026, whether scaling alone keeps paying off — versus needing new ideas — is an open, actively debated question. Verify current claims against primary sources before repeating them.

✦ Worked Example — diagnosing a "why now" claim

A friend says: "AI just got invented — some genius wrote a new algorithm in 2023." Walk through how to evaluate that claim using this lecture, step by step.

  1. Check the algorithm timeline. Neural nets (1940s–50s), backprop (1980s), CNNs (late 1980s–90s), Transformer (2017). The core ideas are years to decades old — so "just invented" is wrong on its face.
  2. Identify what actually changed. Apply the triangle: did compute, data, or architecture shift? For 2012 it was GPUs + ImageNet + a deep CNN (AlexNet). For 2017 onward it was the Transformer + web-scale data + ever more compute.
  3. Locate 2023 correctly. The 2022–2023 chatbot boom was a product and scaling milestone (a usable interface on top of a large pretrained Transformer), not the invention of the underlying method.
  4. Reframe the claim accurately. "AI works now because compute, data, and the Transformer architecture lined up — culminating in scaled-up models that became useful to the public around 2022–2023."
  5. Takeaway: almost every "AI breakthrough" headline is really a story about one corner of the triangle moving. Naming which corner is the fastest way to cut through hype.

This course's "lab equipment" is the AI models themselves. You'll use the free tiers of a few assistants plus a simple prompt log so you can see how phrasing changes results. Week 1 setup: do this once, then reuse it every lecture and for the capstone.

2. Install Python + Jupyter (your prompt log lives here). With Python 3 installed:

python3 -m venv ai-env
source ai-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux
ai-env\Scripts\activate          # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install jupyter
jupyter notebook                 # opens in your browser

Verify: a notebook tab opens. Create one named prompt-log.ipynb. We use it purely as a tidy place to paste prompts and the answers you got — no AI runs inside the notebook (that's fine).

Now run your first experiment by hand: ask the same question two ways and log both. Notice that a vague prompt and a specific prompt produce very different answers — the seed of all the prompting skills in Weeks 4–5.

# prompt-log.ipynb — Week 1, cell 1 (just records your runs; edit the strings)
log = []

# Paste each prompt you tried and the model's reply, then run the cell.
log.append({
    "model":  "your assistant",
    "prompt": "Tell me about AI.",                 # vague
    "reply":  "<paste the answer you got>",

log.append({

Try a third prompt that adds a role ("You are a patient tutor…") and log that too. Keep this notebook open all term — by Week 10 it becomes the raw material for your personal AI operating system.

Q1. Why did deep learning underperform for decades despite the math being old?
A. The algorithms were secret until 2012
B. It lacked enough compute and data for the ideas to work at scale
C. Neural networks hadn't been invented yet
D. The internet did not exist
Show answer

B. The ideas (neural nets, backprop, CNNs) existed; what was missing was enough parallel compute and large datasets to train them — plus, later, scalable architectures.

Q2. What are the "three unlocks" behind modern AI?
A. Compute, data, and architecture
B. Cloud, mobile, and social media
C. Speed, accuracy, and cost
D. Vision, language, and robotics
Show answer

A. Parallel compute (GPUs), data at scale (e.g. ImageNet, web corpora), and trainable architectures (CNNs, then the Transformer). All three are necessary.

Q3. The AlexNet result in 2012 is most significant because it…
A. invented the neural network
B. was the first chatbot
C. combined a deep CNN, ImageNet data, and GPU compute to win an image contest by a wide margin
D. proved scaling laws mathematically
Show answer

C. AlexNet's power came from combining the three unlocks, not from a new algorithm. It convinced the field that deep learning at scale worked for vision.

Q4. What is the key mechanism the Transformer (2017) introduced?
A. Reading text strictly one word at a time
B. Convolution over image pixels
C. Random guessing with feedback
D. Attention — letting every token weigh its relevance to every other
Show answer

D. Attention computes how much each token should "look at" every other, in parallel — enabling fast GPU training and strong long-range context handling.

Q5. "Scaling laws" describe the observation that…
A. model error falls in a smooth, roughly power-law way as compute, data, and parameters grow
B. models always reach zero error eventually
C. bigger models are always cheaper
D. small models are strictly better than large ones
Show answer

A. Loss drops as a power law in each ingredient — predictable enough to justify building larger models. But gains slow and the strategy has real limits.

Q6. A headline reads "New AI breakthrough invented overnight!" The fastest honest way to evaluate it is to…
A. assume it's false because all AI is hype

B. Most "breakthroughs" are one corner of the triangle shifting. Naming which one (and noting that core methods are old) cuts through the hype quickly.
