Your two nervous systems · the HPA axis · the vagal brake · Week 1
1. Which branch of the autonomic nervous system dominates during the "rest-and-digest" state, slowing the heart and supporting recovery?
Answer: A. The parasympathetic branch handles "rest-and-digest"; the sympathetic branch drives "fight-or-flight."
2. Put the HPA axis in order. A stressor activates the hypothalamus, which signals the pituitary, which then signals the adrenal glands. The hormones involved, in that sequence, are roughly:
Answer: C. Hypothalamus → Pituitary (releases ACTH) → Adrenal (releases cortisol). The "H-P-A" name follows the cascade.
3. The vagus nerve is often called a "brake" on the body's stress response because it…
Answer: B. The vagus is the major parasympathetic nerve; high vagal tone lets the body slow the heart and downshift arousal quickly — a "brake."
Cortisol, adrenaline & recovery · Week 2
4. "Allostatic load" best describes…
Answer: D. Allostatic load is the long-term cost of the body repeatedly mobilizing for stress without adequate recovery — the chronic, not the acute, picture.
5. A healthy daily cortisol rhythm typically looks like…
Answer: D. Cortisol normally peaks in the morning (the cortisol awakening response) and tapers toward night, helping you wake and wind down.
6. Why does the course argue that recovery — not stress alone — is what builds resilience?
Answer: C. Like training a muscle, the stressor is the signal but the adaptation occurs during recovery. No recovery means accumulating allostatic load instead of resilience.
The physiological sigh, cyclic sighing & reframing · Week 3
7. The "physiological sigh" (cyclic sigh) is performed as…
Answer: D. A physiological sigh is a sharp inhale, a second top-up inhale, then a slow extended exhale — the pattern studied in cyclic sighing.
8. A 2023 study comparing brief daily breathing practices found that cyclic sighing produced the greatest improvement in mood and reduction in arousal. The proposed mechanism centers on…
Answer: B. The extended exhale increases parasympathetic (vagal) activity and slows the heart, while the double inhale helps offload CO₂ — shifting the body toward calm.
9. Slow, deliberate breathing can calm the body partly because breathing connects to brainstem centers that set heart rate. This illustrates that breathing is a…
Answer: A. Breathing runs automatically but can also be controlled voluntarily, giving us a conscious "dial" into otherwise involuntary autonomic state via brainstem pathways.
10. Cognitive reframing reduces a stress reaction mainly by…
Answer: B. Reframing works at the appraisal stage: how we interpret an event shapes the body's response, so a "challenge" reading is less taxing than a "threat" reading.
Amygdala vs prefrontal cortex · Week 4
11. The "amygdala hijack," a term popularized by Daniel Goleman, describes a situation where…
Answer: C. In an amygdala hijack the rapid threat-detection circuit drives behavior before the slower prefrontal "thinking" brain can weigh in.
12. "Top-down regulation" of emotion refers to…
Answer: D. Top-down regulation is the "higher" prefrontal cortex reinterpreting and reining in the "lower" amygdala-driven response.
13. Why does poor sleep make emotional control harder?
Answer: A. Too little sleep amplifies amygdala responses and reduces the prefrontal cortex's ability to regulate them — a worse threat-to-brake ratio.
Cognitive biases under pressure · Week 5
14. "Loss aversion," documented by Kahneman and Tversky, is the tendency for…
Answer: B. Roughly, "losses loom larger than gains" — a loss hurts more than an equal-sized gain feels good, biasing decisions.
15. "Negativity bias" means that, all else equal, the brain tends to…
Answer: C. Negativity bias: bad news, threats, and criticism grab disproportionate attention and weight — useful for survival, costly for balanced judgment.
16. How does physiology bias decisions under pressure?
Answer: D. Under threat the body shifts toward fast, defensive responses; attention narrows and biases like loss aversion and negativity bias get amplified.
Oxytocin, mirror neurons & attachment · Week 6
17. Oxytocin is most accurately described in this course as a hormone/neuropeptide associated with…
Answer: A. Oxytocin is linked to bonding, trust, and affiliation — but its effects depend on context, so it shouldn't be oversimplified as a pure "love hormone."
18. Which statement about mirror neurons is most scientifically careful?
Answer: B. Mirror neurons are a real and intriguing finding, but their precise function in humans remains debated — claims that they "explain everything" overstate the evidence.
19. Why does the course treat status and belonging as biological needs, not just preferences?
Answer: C. The social brain treats rejection and status loss as genuine threats, engaging the same stress circuitry — which is why they feel so physically real.
Understanding difficult people & the playbook · Weeks 7–8
20. The course advises "reading state, not just behavior" in a difficult person. This means…
Answer: A. Behavior is the output; reading state means noticing the threat/arousal driving it, which lets you respond to the cause rather than escalate the symptom.
21. From a threat-physiology view, why do people often escalate during conflict?
Answer: D. Escalation is usually a threat response: feeling attacked or diminished spikes sympathetic arousal and pulls behavior into defensive, fight-mode territory.
22. The "gray-rock" method is best used to…
Answer: B. Gray-rock means giving boring, neutral, low-emotion responses so a provocateur gets no reaction to escalate — a targeted, situational tool, not a way to treat everyone.
23. Which response best reflects de-escalation grounded in threat-response biology?
Answer: C. De-escalation first lowers the other person's threat arousal — calm tone, slower pace, acknowledgment — so their prefrontal cortex can re-engage before you tackle the problem.
Antifragile habits, recovery systems & capstone · Weeks 9–10
24. Which set of daily inputs does the course highlight as pillars of a personal recovery system?
Answer: A. The recovery pillars are sleep, daylight exposure (especially morning), regular movement, and genuine social connection — the inputs that support autonomic balance.
25. The capstone asks you to analyze your own tracker data and build a scenario playbook. The main point of tracking and reviewing your data is to…
Answer: B. A self-experiment is about your own n-of-1 patterns — spotting personal triggers and what reliably helps — not proving universal claims or replacing professional help.
