Week 2 · Lecture 2

The MHD Equations

Four equations — one for mass, one for momentum, one for the magnetic field, one for pressure — are the entire machinery of this course. Everything else is solving them under different assumptions.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write down the continuity, momentum, induction, and pressure-closure equations of MHD.
  • Derive the induction equation from Faraday's law and a generalized Ohm's law.
  • Identify the physical role of each term in the momentum equation, especially the J×B force.
  • Define the magnetic diffusivity and the magnetic Reynolds (Lundquist) number.
  • Compute a Lundquist number for a real plasma and interpret what it means for the physics.

2.1The Four Equations, at a Glance

Lecture 1 argued that a strongly magnetized plasma can be treated as one electrically conducting fluid. Four equations describe that fluid completely: a statement that mass is conserved, a statement that momentum changes under pressure and magnetic forces, a statement for how the magnetic field itself evolves, and a closure relation that pins down the pressure. In SI units, with ρ the mass density, v the bulk fluid velocity, p the scalar pressure, and B the magnetic field:

∂ρ/∂t + ∇·(ρv) = 0    (continuity — mass conservation)No sources or sinks of mass: whatever density change happens at a point is exactly accounted for by fluid flowing in or out.
ρ(∂v/∂t + v·∇v) = J×B − ∇p    (momentum)The fluid accelerates under the net Lorentz force density J×B and the usual pressure-gradient force, exactly like Euler's equation for a neutral fluid with one new force added.
∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) + (η/μ₀)∇²B    (induction)How B evolves: swept along by the flow (first term) and smoothed out by resistive diffusion (second term). Derived below.
d/dt( p ρ^(−γ) ) = 0    (adiabatic closure, γ = 5/3)In the absence of heat conduction or dissipation, each fluid element's pressure and density change together adiabatically — the same closure used for a neutral gas.

Two auxiliary relations complete the set. First, Ampère's law with the displacement current dropped (valid because MHD flows are far slower than the speed of light, so ∂E/∂t contributes negligibly): J = ∇×B/μ₀. Second, the solenoidal constraint ∇·B = 0, which is not an independent evolution equation but a condition the induction equation automatically preserves if it holds initially. Together, these six relations are "the MHD equations" — the object this entire course studies.

2.2Deriving the Induction Equation

The induction equation is not an independent postulate — it falls straight out of Faraday's law combined with a statement of how current relates to the electric field inside the moving fluid (a generalized Ohm's law). Faraday's law, exact and unchanged from vacuum electromagnetism, reads:

∂B/∂t = −∇×EFaraday's law of induction.

The physics specific to MHD enters through Ohm's law in the fluid's own frame. In a frame moving with the local fluid velocity v, the plasma is just a conductor obeying E′ = ηJ (η = resistivity). Transforming back to the lab frame (non-relativistically, E′ = E + v×B) gives the generalized Ohm's law:

E + v×B = ηJ    ⇒     E = −v×B + ηJη = 0 recovers "ideal" Ohm's law, E = −v×B — the fluid is a perfect conductor. η > 0 is resistive MHD.

Substitute this E into Faraday's law, and use J = ∇×B/μ₀ from Ampère's law to eliminate J in favor of B:

∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) − ∇×(ηJ) = ∇×(v×B) − (η/μ₀)∇×(∇×B)Substituting E = -v×B + ηJ into ∂B/∂t = -∇×E.

Using the vector identity ∇×(∇×B) = ∇(∇·B) − ∇²B, and ∇·B = 0, the last term becomes −∇²B, flipping the overall sign and producing the induction equation quoted above:

∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) + (η/μ₀)∇²BAdvection (first term) competes with resistive diffusion (second term, with diffusivity D_η = η/μ₀).
AnalogyThink of B as a dye pattern carried by a moving fluid. The ∇×(v×B) term says the dye is swept along and stretched by the flow, exactly like ink streaks in a stirred cup. The (η/μ₀)∇²B term says the dye also slowly diffuses and blurs, exactly like ink spreading through still water by molecular diffusion. Ideal MHD (η→0) is the "no blurring, pure stirring" limit — the subject of Lecture 3.

2.3The Momentum Equation: Reading the J×B Force

The momentum equation is Newton's second law applied to a fluid element: mass density times acceleration equals the net force density. Besides the familiar pressure-gradient force −∇p, the new ingredient is the Lorentz force density J×B — the same qv×B force from single-particle motion (Course 1), now written in terms of the current density the whole fluid carries rather than one particle's velocity. Using Ampère's law to write J in terms of B alone removes current from the problem entirely:

ρ(dv/dt) = (1/μ₀)(∇×B)×B − ∇pd/dt = ∂/∂t + v·∇ is the convective (material) derivative — the acceleration felt by a fluid element riding along with the flow.

This single vector term, (∇×B)×B/μ₀, is doing an enormous amount of physical work: it is simultaneously responsible for magnetic pressure pushing field lines apart, magnetic tension pulling curved field lines straight, and — through its balance against ∇p — the entire theory of MHD equilibrium that Lecture 4 builds on. Lecture 5 will split it algebraically into its pressure and tension pieces; for now, simply note that wherever J×B balances ∇p exactly, the fluid is in static equilibrium, and wherever it does not, the fluid accelerates — the seed of every wave and instability in this course.

Common mistakeDo not treat ρ, v, p, and B as independent, freely choosable fields. They are coupled: B is tied to v through the induction equation, J is tied to B through Ampère's law, and the force on the fluid in the momentum equation is tied right back to B through that same J×B term. A change anywhere propagates everywhere — which is exactly why MHD supports waves (Lectures 6–7) instead of just sitting still.

2.4Magnetic Diffusivity and the Lundquist Number

The induction equation has the mathematical form of an advection-diffusion equation, with magnetic diffusivity Dη = η/μ₀ (units m²/s, exactly like a thermal or viscous diffusivity) playing the role of the diffusion coefficient. Comparing the two terms' relative importance over a length scale L and flow speed v defines the dimensionless magnetic Reynolds number, more often called the Lundquist number S when v is taken to be the Alfvén speed (Lecture 6):

S = (Lv)/D_η = μ₀Lv/ηS ≫ 1: advection dominates, the field is nearly "frozen" into the fluid (Lecture 3). S ≪ 1: diffusion dominates, the field slips through the fluid like heat through a solid.

Spitzer's classical resistivity for a fully ionized plasma scales as η ∝ Te−3/2 — hotter plasmas are dramatically better conductors, because fast electrons barely notice the Coulomb collisions that create resistivity in the first place. Combined with the typically enormous length scales of astrophysical and fusion plasmas, this scaling is why S is almost always huge: tens of thousands in a modest laboratory pinch, and 10¹²–10¹⁴ in the solar corona. (Different textbooks quote the Spitzer coefficient with slightly different O(1) numerical prefactors depending on convention — only the T−3/2 scaling and the resulting order of magnitude of S matter for this course.)

✦ Worked Example — Lundquist number at a tokamak edge

A tokamak edge region has electron temperature Te = 50 eV, resistivity η ≈ 5.1×10⁻⁶ Ω·m (from the Spitzer T−3/2 scaling), deuterium density ni = 1×10¹⁹ m⁻³, B = 2 T, and a gradient length scale L = 0.3 m. Using μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷, estimate the Lundquist number.

  1. Mass density: ρ = nimi = 10¹⁹ × (2 × 1.673×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 3.35×10⁻⁸ kg/m³.
  2. Alfvén speed (preview of Lecture 6): v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ) = 2/√(4π×10⁻⁷ × 3.35×10⁻⁸) ≈ 9.76×10⁶ m/s.
  3. Lundquist number: S = μ₀Lv_A/η = (4π×10⁻⁷ × 0.3 × 9.76×10⁶)/(5.1×10⁻⁶) ≈ 7.3×10⁵.
  4. Interpretation: S ≫ 1 by five orders of magnitude — on the dynamical (Alfvén-crossing) timescale, this plasma's magnetic field is essentially frozen into the fluid, and resistive diffusion only matters on timescales 10⁵–10⁶ times longer. That is exactly the regime where ideal MHD (Lecture 3) applies.

🛠️ Hands-On: compute the Lundquist number in Python

# lundquist_number.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7
m_p = 1.67262192369e-27

def alfven_speed(B, n_i, m_i=2*m_p):
    rho = n_i*m_i
    return B/np.sqrt(mu0*rho)

# tokamak-edge example
B, n_i, L, eta = 2.0, 1e19, 0.3, 5.1e-6
vA = alfven_speed(B, n_i)
S  = mu0*L*vA/eta
print(f"v_A = {vA:.3e} m/s")
print(f"S   = {S:.3e}")

# --- actual output ---
# v_A = 9.755e+06 m/s
# S   = 7.259e+05

Try this: set η two orders of magnitude smaller (a hotter, η ∝ T−3/2 plasma) and recompute S — see how quickly the Lundquist number climbs into the millions, pushing the plasma even deeper into the ideal regime.

🔗 Where this is heading

With S almost always enormous in fusion and astrophysical plasmas, Lecture 3 takes the η → 0 limit seriously and derives the frozen-in-flux theorem — the statement that field lines move with the fluid, as if painted onto it. Lecture 4 then uses the momentum equation's static limit (dv/dt = 0) to derive MHD equilibria, the force-balance configurations that every pinch, tokamak, and stellarator is designed around. Every later topic — waves, instabilities, numerical solvers — is a variation on perturbing or solving these same four equations.

Q1. Which physical law, combined with a generalized Ohm's law, produces the MHD induction equation?
A. Newton's third law
B. Faraday's law of induction
C. The ideal gas law
D. Conservation of angular momentum
Show answer

B. Substituting Ohm's law E = −v×B + ηJ into Faraday's law ∂B/∂t = −∇×E, then using Ampère's law to write J in terms of B, produces the induction equation directly. The other laws play no role in this derivation.

Q2. In the induction equation ∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) + (η/μ₀)∇²B, what does the second term represent?
A. The magnetic field being advected (carried along) by the fluid flow
B. Resistive diffusion of the magnetic field through the fluid
C. The pressure gradient force
D. Gravitational acceleration
Show answer

B. (η/μ₀)∇²B has the mathematical form of a diffusion term with diffusivity η/μ₀, describing the field slipping/diffusing through a resistive fluid. (A) is the first term, ∇×(v×B). (C, D) do not appear in the induction equation at all.

Q3. In the MHD momentum equation ρ(dv/dt) = J×B − ∇p, the J×B term is written using Ampère's law as (∇×B)×B/μ₀. Physically, this term is…
A. A fictitious mathematical artifact with no physical meaning
B. The net Lorentz force density acting on the conducting fluid
C. Identical to the thermal pressure-gradient force
D. Only relevant when the fluid is at rest
Show answer

B. J×B is the same Lorentz force from single-particle physics, now expressed per unit volume for the whole current-carrying fluid. (A) it is a real, measurable force. (C) it is a separate magnetic force, distinct from −∇p. (D) it matters whenever current flows, at rest or in motion.

Q4. A Lundquist number S ≫ 1 (e.g. S ~ 10⁵–10¹⁴, typical of lab and astrophysical plasmas) means…
A. The magnetic field diffuses through the fluid almost instantly
B. Advection dominates over resistive diffusion — the field is nearly frozen into the fluid
C. The plasma has no magnetic field
D. The resistivity η must be infinite
Show answer

B. S = μ₀Lv/η compares advective transport to resistive diffusion; S ≫ 1 means advection wins by a huge margin, so the field behaves as if frozen into the moving fluid. (A) is the opposite (that would require S ≪ 1). (C, D) are not implied by a large S.

Q5. Why can the displacement current be dropped from Ampère's law in MHD, leaving J = ∇×B/μ₀?
A. Because electric fields never exist in a plasma
B. Because MHD flow speeds are non-relativistic (≪ c), making the displacement-current term negligible compared to the current term
C. Because magnetic fields do not exist in MHD
D. Because the plasma is always in a vacuum
Show answer

B. This "pre-Maxwell" or "MHD-ordering" approximation is valid because the fluid velocities and wave speeds considered are tiny compared to the speed of light, so ∂E/∂t contributes negligibly relative to ∇×B/μ₀. (A) electric fields do exist, e.g. E = −v×B. (C, D) are both false — B and a real conducting fluid are central to MHD.
