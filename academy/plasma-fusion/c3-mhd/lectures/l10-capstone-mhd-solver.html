10.1Ten Weeks, One Script

Trace the ingredients back: Lecture 4 gave the force-balance equation and a θ-pinch equilibrium; Lecture 5 split the magnetic force into pressure and tension and defined β; Lectures 6–7 derived the Alfvén and magnetosonic wave speeds from exactly that tension and pressure; Lecture 8 used the same field components to derive a stability criterion; Lecture 9 gave you the numerical tools — finite differences and the CFL condition — to solve any of this on a grid when no closed form exists. The capstone project wires all five pieces into one coherent deliverable with two parts: an equilibrium solver (Part A, extending Lecture 4's pure-pressure θ-pinch to the genuinely coupled Z-pinch case) and a wave-propagation solver (Part B, numerically evolving the Alfvén wave equation from Lecture 6 under the CFL discipline of Lecture 9).

10.2Part A: The Bennett Pinch, Solved Exactly and Checked Numerically

Lecture 4 flagged that the Z-pinch's force-balance equation, d/dr(p+Bθ²/2μ₀) = −Bθ²/(μ₀r), has no simple conserved quantity the way the θ-pinch did — but it does have a famous exact closed-form solution, the Bennett pinch, which this capstone adopts as its equilibrium profile:

p(r) = p₀ / (1 + (r/a)²)²     B_θ(r) = √(2μ₀p₀) · (r/a) / (1 + (r/a)²)p₀ = centerline pressure, a = pinch scale radius. Substituting both into the pinch equation satisfies it identically — verified symbolically for this course.

This solution also satisfies a clean global relation — the Bennett relation — connecting the total pinch current I to the plasma's line-integrated thermal content, for an isothermal Bennett pinch (p = nk_BT with T constant across the profile):

N k_B T = μ₀ I² / (8π)N = line density (particles per unit length along z). A compact, exact statement of how much current a given thermal content needs to pinch itself into equilibrium.

With a chosen minor radius a and an added uniform axial field Bz, the equilibrium's edge value Bθ(a) plugs directly into Lecture 8's Kruskal–Shafranov safety factor, q(a) = aBz/(RBθ(a)) — closing the loop between this lecture's equilibrium and the stability criterion from two lectures ago.

10.3Part B: Numerically Propagating an Alfvén Wave

The second half of the capstone revisits Lecture 6's shear Alfvén wave equations, ∂vy/∂t = (B₀/μ₀ρ₀)∂By/∂z and ∂By/∂t = B₀∂vy/∂z, but solves them numerically rather than analytically: discretize z into a grid, use finite differences for the spatial derivatives (Lecture 9), and march vy and By forward in time together, respecting the CFL condition with v_A as the relevant signal speed. Starting from a localized Gaussian pulse in By, the pulse should split into two counter-propagating wave packets, each sliding along z at exactly ±v_A — a direct numerical confirmation of Lecture 6's analytic dispersion relation, obtained with none of that lecture's algebra, purely by marching the discretized equations forward in time.

AnalogyThis is the numerical equivalent of plucking a guitar string (Lecture 6's analogy made literal in code): release a localized pluck, and watch two wavefronts race away from the pluck point in opposite directions, each moving at the string's wave speed. Measuring how far the simulated wavefront has traveled after a known elapsed time, and dividing, hands you back v_A — with no analytic derivation required, just a grid, a timestep, and patience.
✦ Worked Example — equilibrium, stability, and wave speed, numbers checked together

A Bennett pinch has centerline pressure p₀ = 1.0×10⁵ Pa and scale radius a = 0.02 m. An axial field Bz = 1.5 T is added, with periodicity length analog R = 0.1 m. Separately, a wave-propagation test region has background density ρ₀ = 1×10⁻⁷ kg/m³ and field Bz0 = 0.4 T.

  1. Bennett edge field coefficient: B₁ = √(2μ₀p₀) = √(2 × 4π×10⁻⁷ × 10⁵) ≈ 0.5013 T.
  2. B_θ at r = a: B_θ(a) = B₁ × (1)/(1+1) = 0.5013/2 ≈ 0.2507 T.
  3. Safety factor: q(a) = aB_z/(RB_θ(a)) = (0.02 × 1.5)/(0.1 × 0.2507) = 0.03/0.02507 ≈ 1.197 — above 1, so this configuration is kink-stable (with Bz = 1.0 T instead, q ≈ 0.798 < 1, kink-unstable — a vivid demonstration of how much the field balance matters).
  4. Alfvén speed for the wave test: v_A = B_{z0}/√(μ₀ρ₀) = 0.4/√(4π×10⁻⁷ × 10⁻⁷) ≈ 1.128×10⁶ m/s.
  5. Numerical check: after 600 timesteps (Δt ≈ 8.88×10⁻⁹ s, total elapsed t ≈ 5.33×10⁻⁶ s), the predicted pulse shift v_A·t ≈ 6.015 m matches the simulation's measured shift of ≈ 5.99 m to within 0.4% — well within the expected numerical dispersion error of this simple finite-difference scheme.

🛠️ Hands-On: the complete capstone project in Python/SciPy

This is the full project deliverable. Everything uses the free Python + NumPy + SciPy stack from Lecture 1 — no new installs needed.

# mhd_capstone.py — Capstone (Lecture 10)
import numpy as np

mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7

# ===== PART A: Bennett pinch equilibrium + stability =====
p0, a = 1.0e5, 0.02                 # Pa, m
B1 = np.sqrt(2*mu0*p0)              # exact Bennett coefficient

def p_profile(r):   return p0/(1+(r/a)**2)**2
def Bt_profile(r):  return B1*(r/a)/(1+(r/a)**2)

# numerical force-balance residual check at an interior radius
r0, dr = 0.015, 1e-7
lhs = ((p_profile(r0+dr)+Bt_profile(r0+dr)**2/(2*mu0))
     - (p_profile(r0-dr)+Bt_profile(r0-dr)**2/(2*mu0))) / (2*dr)
rhs = -Bt_profile(r0)**2/(mu0*r0)
print(f"[Part A] force-balance residual: lhs={lhs:.4e}  rhs={rhs:.4e}  (should match)")

I_total = 2*np.pi*a*B1/mu0
print(f"[Part A] total pinch current I = {I_total:.3e} A")

Btheta_a = Bt_profile(a)
Bz, R = 1.5, 0.1
q = (a*Bz)/(R*Btheta_a)
print(f"[Part A] B_theta(a)={Btheta_a:.4f} T, q(a)={q:.3f} -> "
      f"{'kink-stable' if q>1 else 'KINK-UNSTABLE'}")

# ===== PART B: 1-D Alfven wave propagation =====
rho0, Bz0 = 1e-7, 0.4
vA = Bz0/np.sqrt(mu0*rho0)
print(f"[Part B] v_A = {vA:.4e} m/s")

L, N = 10.0, 400
x = np.linspace(0, L, N); dx = x[1]-x[0]
dt = 0.4*dx/vA; nsteps = 600

vy = np.zeros(N)
By = 0.01*Bz0*np.exp(-(x-2.0)**2/(2*0.3**2))   # initial pulse

def step(vy, By, dt):
    vy_new = vy + dt*(Bz0/(mu0*rho0))*np.gradient(By, dx)
    By_new = By + dt*Bz0*np.gradient(vy_new, dx)
    return vy_new, By_new

peak0 = x[np.argmax(By)]
for _ in range(nsteps):
    vy, By = step(vy, By, dt)
peak1 = x[np.argmax(np.abs(By))]

t_elapsed = nsteps*dt
print(f"[Part B] predicted shift v_A*t = {vA*t_elapsed:.3f} m, "
      f"measured shift = {peak1-peak0:.3f} m")

# --- actual output ---
# [Part A] force-balance residual: lhs=-3.0720e+06  rhs=-3.0720e+06  (should match)
# [Part A] total pinch current I = 5.013e+04 A
# [Part A] B_theta(a)=0.2507 T, q(a)=1.197 -> kink-stable
# [Part B] v_A = 1.1284e+06 m/s
# [Part B] predicted shift v_A*t = 6.015 m, measured shift = 5.990 m

Q1. The Bennett pinch profiles p(r) = p₀/(1+(r/a)²)² and B_θ(r) = √(2μ₀p₀)(r/a)/(1+(r/a)²) are significant because…
A. They are rough approximations that only work near the center
B. They satisfy the Z-pinch force-balance equation exactly, for every radius, without approximation
C. They apply only to the θ-pinch, not the Z-pinch
D. They ignore the magnetic field entirely
Show answer

B. Substituting both profiles into d/dr(p+B_θ²/2μ₀) = −B_θ²/(μ₀r) satisfies it identically at every r — an exact, closed-form equilibrium. (A) it is exact everywhere, not just near the center. (C) it is specifically the Z-pinch (B_θ-only) solution. (D) B_θ(r) is central to the whole profile.

Q2. In the capstone's safety-factor check, raising B_z from 1.0 T to 1.5 T (same geometry) changed q(a) from about 0.80 to about 1.20. What does this show?
A. q(a) does not depend on B_z
B. A modest change in the stabilizing axial field can move a configuration from kink-unstable to kink-stable
C. The plasma pressure must have changed too
D. Kink stability has nothing to do with B_z
Show answer

B. q(a) = aB_z/(RB_θ(a)) is directly proportional to B_z, so a 50% increase in B_z was enough to cross the q=1 threshold from unstable to stable in this example. (A, D) directly contradict the formula and the numeric result. (C) the pressure profile (and hence B_θ(a)) was unchanged between the two cases.

Q3. The capstone's wave solver starts from a Gaussian pulse in B_y and measures how far it has moved after a known number of timesteps. This is used to…
A. Compute the plasma beta
B. Independently check that the measured propagation speed matches the analytic Alfvén speed v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ)
C. Determine the Kruskal–Shafranov safety factor
D. Measure the resistivity of the plasma
Show answer

B. Dividing the measured pulse shift by the elapsed time gives a numerical estimate of the propagation speed, which the worked example shows matches v_A to within about 0.4%. (A) beta requires pressure and field magnitude, not wave propagation timing. (C) that's a separate, equilibrium-based calculation (Part A). (D) this wave solver is purely ideal (η=0); no resistivity appears.

Q4. Why does the capstone's wave solver choose its timestep as 0.4×Δx/v_A rather than simply Δx/v_A?
A. To intentionally violate the CFL condition
B. To keep a safety margin below the CFL stability limit (Lecture 9), since real schemes have less margin than the idealized bound suggests
C. Because v_A is irrelevant to the timestep choice
D. Because a larger timestep is always more accurate
Show answer

B. Using a Courant number of 0.4 (below the C=1 hard limit) keeps the explicit scheme comfortably stable, exactly the practice recommended in Lecture 9. (A) the point is to respect, not violate, CFL. (C) v_A is precisely the speed the CFL condition is built around here. (D) larger timesteps reduce accuracy and risk instability, not improve it.

Q5. Looking back across the whole course, which single equation from Lecture 4 underlies both the equilibrium solver (Part A) and, indirectly, the wave solver's background state (Part B)?
A. The ideal gas law alone
B. The static force-balance equation, ∇p = J×B
C. The CFL condition
D. Ohm's law
Show answer

B. ∇p = J×B is the equilibrium Part A solves exactly (as the Bennett profile), and it is also what defines the uniform background state that Part B then perturbs with a wave. (A) the ideal gas law is one piece of the pressure closure, not the organizing equation. (C) CFL governs the numerical timestep, not the physical equilibrium. (D) Ohm's law underlies the induction equation (Lecture 2–3), not the static force balance.
