MHD Equilibria & the Pinch

Set the fluid's acceleration to zero and the momentum equation collapses into one force-balance condition — the single algebraic statement that every confinement device, from a lab pinch to a tokamak, is designed to satisfy.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set dv/dt = 0 in the MHD momentum equation to obtain the static force-balance equation ∇p = J×B.
  • Derive the general radial force-balance equation for a cylindrically symmetric "screw pinch," d/dr(p + B²/2μ₀) = −Bθ²/(μ₀r).
  • Solve the θ-pinch special case analytically and find the magnetic field profile Bz(r) for a given pressure profile.
  • Numerically verify the θ-pinch equilibrium with SciPy's ODE solver and confirm it matches the analytic result.
  • Begin the course project: an equilibrium solver that will be extended into the full capstone in Lecture 10.

4.1The Static Limit: ∇p = J×B

An equilibrium is a configuration that does not accelerate: every fluid element sits still (or moves at constant velocity) because the net force on it is exactly zero. Setting dv/dt = 0 in Lecture 2's momentum equation collapses it from a dynamical PDE into a single algebraic (vector) condition relating the pressure and the magnetic field:

∇p = J×BEvery equilibrium configuration — pinch, tokamak, stellarator — is a solution of this one equation, with J tied to B through Ampère's law J = ∇×B/μ₀.

Read it as a statement of balance: wherever the plasma pressure wants to push outward (down its gradient), the magnetic force J×B must push back exactly as hard. There is no unique solution — ∇p = J×B is one vector equation relating two unknown fields (p and B), so an entire zoo of equilibria exists, distinguished by geometry and by how much of the confining job is done by the field's pressure versus its tension (Lecture 5 makes that split precise). This lecture works the simplest nontrivial case: a cylindrically symmetric plasma column, the pinch.

Common mistake"Equilibrium" does not mean the plasma is static forever, or that nothing interesting is happening. It means the net force is zero at this configuration. A perfectly valid equilibrium can still be unstable — a small perturbation can grow explosively even though ∇p = J×B is satisfied exactly at t = 0. Whether an equilibrium is stable against such perturbations is a completely separate question, taken up in Lecture 8.

4.2The Screw-Pinch Equation

Take a plasma column along the z-axis with cylindrical symmetry: everything depends on radius r alone, and the magnetic field has an azimuthal component from any axial current, Bθ(r), plus possibly an imposed axial component Bz(r). This general configuration — B = (0, Bθ(r), Bz(r)) — is called a screw pinch (the field lines spiral like screw threads). Ampère's law gives the two current components that produce this field, Jθ = −(1/μ₀)dBz/dr and Jz = (1/(μ₀r))d(rBθ)/dr, and the radial component of ∇p = J×B becomes, after expanding and collecting terms (a short but careful bit of algebra):

d/dr( p + B²/(2μ₀) ) = − B_θ²/(μ₀r)    where B² = B_θ² + B_z²The master screw-pinch radial equilibrium equation. Verified independently via symbolic algebra for this course.

The left side is the gradient of total pressure — thermal plus magnetic. The right side is a pure consequence of the azimuthal field's curvature: a nonzero Bθ(r) wraps around the axis, and that curvature produces an extra inward "hoop-stress" term (magnetic tension, formalized in Lecture 5) that a straight Bz field does not have. Two special cases make this equation tractable by hand.

4.3The θ-Pinch: Pure Magnetic-Pressure Confinement

Set Bθ = 0, leaving only an axial field Bz(r) produced by azimuthal (θ-directed) surface currents — hence "θ-pinch." The right-hand side of the screw-pinch equation vanishes identically, and the equation integrates immediately:

p(r) + B_z(r)²/(2μ₀) = const = B₀²/(2μ₀)B₀ = the vacuum field value at the plasma edge, where p → 0. Total pressure — thermal + magnetic — is the same at every radius.

This says something very physical: wherever the plasma is hottest or densest (highest p), the magnetic field must be correspondingly weaker, because the plasma's own diamagnetic currents partially expel the field from its interior. Given any pressure profile p(r), the field profile follows immediately: Bz(r) = √(B₀² − 2μ₀p(r)).

AnalogyThink of the θ-pinch as a tube of toothpaste with a fixed total squeeze. Where the paste (thermal pressure) bulges up, the tube wall (magnetic pressure) must be correspondingly slacker; where the paste thins out near the cap, the wall pushes in harder. The total squeeze — paste plus wall tension — stays the same all the way along, exactly like p + B_z²/2μ₀ staying constant across the pinch.

4.4A Glimpse of the Z-Pinch

The opposite extreme sets Bz = 0, leaving only an azimuthal field Bθ(r) produced by a current flowing along the axis — the Z-pinch. Now the right-hand side of the screw-pinch equation does not vanish: d/dr(p + Bθ²/2μ₀) = −Bθ²/(μ₀r), a genuinely nonlinear ODE coupling p and Bθ that cannot simply be integrated into a conservation law the way the θ-pinch could. It does, however, have a famous exact closed-form solution — the Bennett pinch — which we will derive and use directly as the equilibrium profile for the capstone's numerical solver in Lecture 10. For now, the key qualitative point is that the extra −Bθ²/(μ₀r) term is a direct consequence of the azimuthal field's curvature around the axis, and it is exactly the magnetic-tension effect that Lecture 5 isolates algebraically.

✦ Worked Example — a θ-pinch pressure and field profile

A laboratory θ-pinch has edge (vacuum) field B₀ = 0.5 T, radius a = 0.05 m, and a parabolic pressure profile p(r) = p₀(1 − r²/a²) with centerline pressure p₀ = 2.0×10⁴ Pa. Using μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷, find Bz(r) at the center and at the edge, and the centerline plasma beta.

  1. Magnetic pressure at the edge: B₀²/(2μ₀) = 0.5²/(2 × 4π×10⁻⁷) ≈ 9.95×10⁴ Pa.
  2. Field at the center (r = 0, where p = p₀): B_z(0) = √(B₀² − 2μ₀p₀) = √(0.25 − 2×4π×10⁻⁷×2×10⁴) = √(0.25 − 0.0503) ≈ 0.4469 T — weaker than the edge value, as expected.
  3. Field at the edge (r = a, where p = 0): B_z(a) = √(B₀² − 0) = B₀ = 0.500 T, consistent by construction.
  4. Field at r = a/2 (p = 0.75p₀ = 1.5×10⁴ Pa): B_z = √(0.25 − 2×4π×10⁻⁷×1.5×10⁴) ≈ 0.4608 T — smoothly interpolating between center and edge.
  5. Centerline beta: β₀ = p₀/(B₀²/2μ₀) = 2.0×10⁴/9.95×10⁴ ≈ 0.20 (20%) — a substantial fraction of the confining pressure is thermal rather than magnetic at the hot core. Plasma beta is the subject of Lecture 5.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the θ-pinch equilibrium with SciPy (project checkpoint)

This is the first piece of the course project: an equilibrium solver you'll extend into the capstone (Lecture 10). Rather than use the analytic shortcut, integrate the force-balance equation as an ODE — the technique you'll need for pressure profiles without a clean closed form.

# theta_pinch_equilibrium.py — Lecture 4 project checkpoint
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7
B0, a, p0 = 0.5, 0.05, 2.0e4     # T, m, Pa

def p_profile(r):
    return p0*(1 - (r/a)**2)

def dpdr(r):
    return -2*p0*r/a**2

# theta-pinch ODE: mu0*dp/dr + Bz*dBz/dr = 0  =>  dBz/dr = -mu0*dp/dr / Bz
def rhs(r, y):
    Bz = y[0]
    return [-mu0*dpdr(r)/Bz]

# integrate inward from the known edge value Bz(a) = B0
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, [a, 1e-6], [B0], dense_output=True, rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-12)

Bz_center_numeric  = sol.y[0, -1]
Bz_center_analytic = np.sqrt(B0**2 - 2*mu0*p0)
print(f"Bz(0) numeric  = {Bz_center_numeric:.6f} T")
print(f"Bz(0) analytic = {Bz_center_analytic:.6f} T")

# --- actual output ---
# Bz(0) numeric  = 0.446917 T
# Bz(0) analytic = 0.446917 T   (agreement to 2e-11 T)

🔗 Where this is heading

You now have the master equation behind every magnetic-confinement device ever built — tokamaks and stellarators both solve a (two- or three-dimensional) generalization of ∇p = J×B. Lecture 5 pulls apart the J×B force itself into a magnetic-pressure piece and a magnetic-tension piece, giving physical names to the two mechanisms this lecture's screw-pinch equation already contains implicitly, and introduces the single number — plasma beta — that tells you which one is doing the confining.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Setting dv/dt = 0 in the MHD momentum equation gives the equilibrium condition…
A. ∇p = 0
B. ∇p = J×B
C. J = 0
D. B = 0
Show answer

B. With no acceleration, the pressure-gradient force must be exactly balanced by the Lorentz force density J×B. (A) would forbid any pressure variation. (C, D) would remove the magnetic confinement entirely, which is not required for equilibrium.

Q2. In a θ-pinch (B_θ = 0, only B_z(r)), the equilibrium relation p(r) + B_z(r)²/2μ₀ = const means…
A. The thermal pressure is always zero
B. Wherever thermal pressure is high, the local magnetic field strength must be correspondingly lower, and vice versa
C. The magnetic field is uniform everywhere in the plasma
D. Density and pressure are unrelated to the magnetic field
Show answer

B. Total (thermal + magnetic) pressure is constant, so a peak in p(r) must be compensated by a dip in B_z(r)² — the physical picture of diamagnetic field exclusion from the hot core. (A) p(r) is generally nonzero and peaked at the center. (C) B_z(r) varies with radius precisely to compensate p(r). (D) the whole equation ties them together.

Q3. The general screw-pinch equation has an extra term −B_θ²/(μ₀r) on the right-hand side that is absent in the pure θ-pinch. This term arises because…
A. B_θ wraps around the axis, and that curvature produces an additional inward "hoop stress" (tension) contribution
B. The plasma has no pressure in a screw pinch
C. Ampère's law does not apply to azimuthal fields
D. The axial field B_z always vanishes in a screw pinch
Show answer

A. An azimuthal field, unlike a straight axial one, is intrinsically curved around the axis; that curvature is exactly the geometric origin of the extra term — a preview of magnetic tension (Lecture 5). (B) pressure is still present and still balances the full right-hand side. (C) Ampère's law gives J_z from B_θ perfectly well. (D) a screw pinch can have both components nonzero.

Q4. A θ-pinch has edge field B₀ = 0.5 T and centerline pressure p₀ = 2.0×10⁴ Pa. The centerline field B_z(0) = √(B₀² − 2μ₀p₀) is…
A. Greater than B₀
B. Equal to B₀
C. Less than B₀, about 0.447 T
D. Zero
Show answer

C. Subtracting a positive 2μ₀p₀ from B₀² before the square root always reduces the field below its vacuum value; the numeric result here is ≈ 0.447 T, matching both the analytic formula and the SciPy ODE solve. (A) would violate the pressure-balance relation. (B) only holds where p = 0 (the edge). (D) would require 2μ₀p₀ = B₀², which is not the case here.

Q5. Why is an MHD equilibrium (a solution of ∇p = J×B) not automatically guaranteed to be physically realizable in a real device?
A. Because satisfying force balance says nothing about whether the equilibrium is stable against small perturbations
B. Because ∇p = J×B is never satisfied by any real configuration
C. Because equilibria require infinite pressure
D. Because Ampère's law does not hold in a plasma
Show answer

A. Force balance is necessary but not sufficient — an equilibrium can be exactly correct at one instant and still be violently unstable, growing away from that state the moment it is perturbed (Lecture 8). (B) equilibria are routinely achieved in real pinches and tokamaks. (C, D) are not required or true.
