Alfvén Waves

Pluck a magnetic field line like a guitar string and a disturbance races away along it at a single, clean speed — the Alfvén speed — set entirely by the field's tension and the fluid's inertia.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Linearize the ideal MHD momentum and induction equations about a uniform, field-aligned equilibrium.
  • Derive the shear Alfvén wave equation and its dispersion relation, ω = k v_A, for propagation along B₀.
  • Define the Alfvén speed v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ) and justify the "wave on a tensioned string" analogy quantitatively.
  • State the wave's defining properties: transverse, incompressible, non-dispersive, polarized perpendicular to both B₀ and k.
  • Compute v_A for a tokamak-core plasma and the solar wind, and compare to other characteristic speeds.

6.1Perturbing a Uniform, Magnetized Fluid

Take the simplest possible background: a uniform plasma of density ρ₀ threaded by a uniform field B₀ = B₀ẑ, with no equilibrium flow and (for this lecture) no equilibrium pressure gradient to worry about. Perturb it slightly — density ρ₀+ρ₁, velocity v₁, field B₀+B₁ — and ask how a small disturbance evolves under the ideal MHD momentum and induction equations, keeping only terms linear in the (small) perturbations.

Restrict attention to a disturbance that is purely transverse: velocity and field perturbations vy, By perpendicular to both B₀ and the direction of propagation ẑ, with no density or pressure perturbation at all (ρ₁ = p₁ = 0). This is a self-consistent solution of the linearized equations — it is the shear Alfvén wave, the simplest and, as you'll find in Lecture 7, the most fundamental of all MHD wave branches.

6.2Deriving the Wave Equation

The linearized momentum equation (y-component, keeping only the magnetic-tension piece from Lecture 5's decomposition, since this transverse perturbation carries no pressure gradient) reads:

ρ₀ ∂v_y/∂t = (B₀/μ₀) ∂B_y/∂zThe restoring force is pure magnetic tension: B₀ along z is curved by the y-displacement, and that curvature pulls back.

The linearized induction equation, ∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B₀) for this geometry, gives the companion equation:

∂B_y/∂t = B₀ ∂v_y/∂zThe field is stretched by the y-velocity gradient along z — exactly frozen-in flux (Lecture 3) at work.

These are two coupled first-order equations in vy and By — differentiate the first with respect to t and substitute the second to eliminate By, and you get a single, clean wave equation:

∂²v_y/∂t² = v_A² ∂²v_y/∂z²    v_A ≡ B₀/√(μ₀ρ₀)The classical wave equation, with v_A playing the role of wave speed. Solutions travel unchanged in shape at speed ±v_A along z.

Assuming a plane-wave solution vy ∝ exp[i(kz − ωt)] turns this directly into the dispersion relation ω² = k²v_A², or simply ω = k v_A: a perfectly linear, non-dispersive relation between frequency and wavenumber.

6.3The Alfvén Speed as a "String" Speed

The formula v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ) is not just formally similar to the speed of a wave on a stretched string — it is exactly that formula, generalized to three dimensions. A string under tension T (newtons) with mass per unit length μ (kg/m) carries transverse waves at v = √(T/μ). In MHD, the analogous "tension per unit cross-sectional area" is the magnetic tension B²/μ₀ (a pressure, from Lecture 5), and the analogous "mass per unit volume" is simply the mass density ρ:

v_A = √( (B²/μ₀) / ρ ) = B/√(μ₀ρ)Field lines behave exactly like strings under tension B²/μ₀ and mass density ρ — a literal, quantitative analogy, not just a loose one.
AnalogyPluck a guitar string: the wave speed is set by how tightly it's strung (tension) versus how heavy it is (mass per length) — tighten the string or use a lighter string, and the pitch (wave speed) rises. A magnetic field line is a "string" with tension B²/μ₀, and the plasma riding on it is the "mass." Stronger B or lower density ρ ⇒ faster Alfvén waves, exactly as a guitarist would expect.

6.4Properties of the Shear Alfvén Wave

Four properties define this wave and distinguish it sharply from ordinary sound waves:

Transverse: both v₁ and B₁ point perpendicular to B₀ — exactly like a wave on a string, which displaces the string sideways, not along its length. Incompressible: there is no density or pressure perturbation at all (ρ₁ = p₁ = 0 for this mode); the restoring force is pure tension, with no compressional component. Non-dispersive: ω = kv_A is perfectly linear in k, so the phase speed ω/k and the group speed dω/dk are both exactly v_A, independent of wavelength — a wave packet of any shape travels at v_A without spreading. Field-aligned: for propagation at an angle θ to B₀, the more general dispersion relation (derived rigorously in Lecture 7) is ω = k v_A cos θ — the wave only cares about how much of its wavevector points along the field; propagating exactly perpendicular to B₀ (θ = 90°), this mode does not exist at all.

💬 Discussion 2 — the solar wind is "super-Alfvénic"

At 1 AU, the solar wind's bulk flow speed is roughly 400 km/s, while its Alfvén speed (computed in the worked example below) is only about 49 km/s — the flow is far faster than v_A. Disturbances in a magnetized fluid can only propagate (relative to the fluid) at up to the local wave speed. Given that, can a disturbance generated at 1 AU — say, a spacecraft's bow shock — ever travel back upstream toward the Sun through the solar wind? Model response

No. A wave can propagate upstream at up to v_A relative to the fluid it's embedded in — but that fluid itself is being swept outward at 400 km/s. In the Sun's (inertial) frame, a disturbance trying to move straight upstream at v_A still gets carried outward at a net speed of v_flow − v_A ≈ 400 − 49 = 351 km/s, which is positive (outward). Since the flow speed exceeds v_A by roughly a factor of 8, there is no direction in which the wave's relative motion can overcome the bulk advection — the disturbance is swept away no matter which way it points.

The implication: once the wind becomes super-Alfvénic (flow speed > v_A, which happens close to the Sun, at the "Alfvén point"), no MHD-wave signal generated farther out can ever propagate back across that point. It behaves like a one-way causal horizon — exactly analogous to a sonic horizon for ordinary sound waves. So the inner solar system (and the Sun itself) cannot receive any advance "warning" via MHD waves about disturbances happening further out in the super-Alfvénic wind; information about that region only propagates outward, never back in.

✦ Worked Example — Alfvén speed in a tokamak core and the solar wind

Using μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ and mp = 1.673×10⁻²⁷ kg, compute v_A for (a) a tokamak core with deuterium density ni = 1×10²⁰ m⁻³ and B = 5 T, and (b) the solar wind at 1 AU with proton density n = 5×10⁶ m⁻³ and B = 5 nT.

  1. Tokamak mass density: ρ = ni(2mp) = 10²⁰ × 3.345×10⁻²⁷ ≈ 3.345×10⁻⁷ kg/m³.
  2. Tokamak Alfvén speed: v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ) = 5/√(4π×10⁻⁷ × 3.345×10⁻⁷) ≈ 7.71×10⁶ m/s (about 7712 km/s).
  3. Solar-wind mass density: ρ = n·mp = 5×10⁶ × 1.673×10⁻²⁷ ≈ 8.36×10⁻²¹ kg/m³.
  4. Solar-wind Alfvén speed: v_A = (5×10⁻⁹)/√(4π×10⁻⁷ × 8.36×10⁻²¹) ≈ 4.88×10⁴ m/s (about 49 km/s).
  5. Comparison: the tokamak's Alfvén speed (~7700 km/s) is set by a strong field and a dense plasma; the solar wind's (~49 km/s) by an extremely weak field and tenuous plasma. Both are routine, correctly-ordered results for their respective environments.

🛠️ Hands-On: compute Alfvén speeds in Python

# alfven_speed.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np

mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7
m_p = 1.67262192369e-27

def alfven_speed(B, n, m=m_p):
    rho = n*m
    return B/np.sqrt(mu0*rho)

vA_tokamak = alfven_speed(B=5.0,   n=1e20, m=2*m_p)   # deuterium
vA_sw      = alfven_speed(B=5e-9, n=5e6,  m=m_p)      # protons
print(f"v_A (tokamak core) = {vA_tokamak:.3e} m/s = {vA_tokamak/1e3:.0f} km/s")
print(f"v_A (solar wind)   = {vA_sw:.3e} m/s = {vA_sw/1e3:.1f} km/s")

# --- actual output ---
# v_A (tokamak core) = 7.712e+06 m/s = 7712 km/s
# v_A (solar wind)   = 4.877e+04 m/s = 48.8 km/s

Try this: the real solar wind bulk speed near Earth is about 400 km/s — far above the v_A you just computed. Divide the two to get the Alfvénic Mach number M_A ≈ 8.2, and keep that number in mind for Discussion 2.

🔗 Where this is heading

The shear Alfvén wave you derived here is the pure-tension, zero-compression limit of a richer family. Lecture 7 restores the pressure-gradient (sound-wave) physics that was switched off here, and shows that for general propagation angles, tension and pressure cooperate to produce two new wave branches — fast and slow magnetosonic waves — with the Alfvén wave reappearing as one well-defined piece of the full dispersion relation.

Q1. The restoring force for the shear Alfvén wave is…
A. Gravity
B. Magnetic tension — the field line resisting being bent
C. Thermal pressure
D. Electric charge repulsion
Show answer

B. The wave carries zero density/pressure perturbation; its momentum equation contains only the magnetic-tension piece from Lecture 5's decomposition. (A, C, D) play no role in this derivation.

Q2. The Alfvén wave dispersion relation ω = kv_A is "non-dispersive." What does that mean?
A. The phase speed ω/k is the same (v_A) for every wavelength — a wave packet travels without changing shape
B. The wave cannot propagate at all
C. The wave only exists at one specific frequency
D. The wave speed depends strongly on wavelength
Show answer

A. Since ω is exactly linear in k, phase and group speed both equal v_A regardless of wavelength — the defining feature of a non-dispersive wave. (B) the wave propagates perfectly well. (C) it exists across a continuum of (ω,k) pairs related by ω=kv_A. (D) the opposite is true — v_A is wavelength-independent.

Q3. v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ) is analogous to the speed of a wave on a stretched string, v = √(T/μ). In this analogy, what plays the role of the string's tension T?
A. The plasma density ρ
B. The magnetic tension B²/μ₀
C. The thermal pressure p
D. The permeability μ₀ alone
Show answer

B. v_A = √((B²/μ₀)/ρ), directly matching v=√(T/μ) with T ↔ B²/μ₀ and μ ↔ ρ. (A) density plays the role of the string's mass per length. (C) pressure does not appear in this (incompressible) wave at all. (D) μ₀ alone is not the tension — B²/μ₀ is.

Q4. A tokamak core (B=5 T, deuterium n=10²⁰ m⁻³) has v_A ≈ 7700 km/s, while the solar wind (B=5 nT, n=5×10⁶ m⁻³) has v_A ≈ 49 km/s. The huge difference is driven mainly by…
A. A difference in the fundamental physics between the two environments
B. The solar wind having no magnetic field at all
C. The tokamak's far stronger field and higher density compared to the extremely tenuous, weakly magnetized solar wind, combined through v_A = B/√(μ₀ρ)
D. Random measurement error
Show answer

C. Both plasmas obey exactly the same formula; the enormous difference in B and ρ between a tokamak core and the distant solar wind explains the result. (A) the same physics (and formula) applies to both. (B) the solar wind does carry a (weak) field. (D) this is a real, reproducible physical difference, not noise.

Q5. For propagation at angle θ to B₀, the shear Alfvén dispersion relation becomes ω = kv_A cos θ. What happens at θ = 90° (propagation exactly perpendicular to B₀)?
A. The wave speed doubles
B. Nothing changes; ω = kv_A regardless of angle
C. The wave becomes a sound wave
D. ω → 0 — the shear Alfvén wave does not propagate perpendicular to the field at all
Show answer

D. cos(90°) = 0, so ω = 0 for any k — the shear Alfvén wave only exists along the field-aligned component of k and vanishes for purely perpendicular propagation. (A) the opposite happens — the speed (along k) goes to zero. (C) a different wave branch, not sound, takes over perpendicular propagation (Lecture 7). (B) the angle dependence is essential, not absent.
