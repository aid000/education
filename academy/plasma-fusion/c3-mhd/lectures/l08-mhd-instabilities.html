MHD Instabilities: Kink & Sausage

An equilibrium satisfying ∇p = J×B is not automatically safe — perturb it and some configurations snap back, while others run away. Two named failure modes of the pinch show exactly how to tell which.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain, qualitatively, why force balance alone does not guarantee stability.
  • Describe the sausage (m=0) instability of a pure Z-pinch and why it runs away without an axial field.
  • Describe the kink (m=1) instability — a helical displacement of the whole column — and how an axial field stabilizes it.
  • State the Kruskal–Shafranov safety-factor criterion for kink stability, q(a) > 1.
  • Compute the safety factor for a screw-pinch geometry and classify it as kink-stable or -unstable.

8.1Equilibrium Is Not the Same as Stability

Every configuration in Lecture 4 satisfies ∇p = J×B exactly — force balance holds at every radius, with zero net force on every fluid element. But Lecture 4 already flagged the catch: force balance says nothing about what happens if that configuration is nudged. Push a ball resting at the bottom of a bowl, and it rolls back — a stable equilibrium. Balance a pencil on its tip, and the slightest nudge sends it falling away from equilibrium entirely — an unstable one. Both configurations satisfy "net force = 0" at the starting instant; only a stability analysis — asking whether a small perturbation grows or decays — tells you which kind of equilibrium you actually have.

The intuition that governs almost every MHD instability is an energy argument: if a small displacement can lower the total (magnetic plus thermal) potential energy of the system, that displacement will grow, releasing the freed energy into kinetic motion — nature always has access to that downhill path. If every possible displacement raises the energy, the equilibrium is stable, and any small perturbation simply oscillates (as one of Lectures 6–7's waves) rather than running away. The kink and sausage modes below are two of the clearest, most consequential examples of this principle at work in a cylindrical pinch.

8.2The Sausage (m=0) Instability

Consider a pure Z-pinch: only an azimuthal field Bθ(r), produced by current flowing along the axis, with no axial field at all. Now imagine a small, axisymmetric constriction — the column's radius dips slightly at one point along z, like a pinch in a sausage casing. At fixed total enclosed current I, Ampère's law says Bθ at the (now smaller) local surface radius rs is Bθ = μ₀I/(2πrs) — and shrinking rs raises Bθ there. A larger Bθ means a larger inward pinching force exactly at the already-constricted spot, squeezing it further, raising Bθ still more — a self-reinforcing runaway. This is the sausage (or m=0) instability: named for its axisymmetric, bead-like shape, and loosely in the same family as the classic Rayleigh–Taylor instability in that a small perturbation finds a downhill energy path and accelerates itself.

A purely azimuthal-field pinch is unconditionally sausage-unstable. The fix is exactly the ingredient the Z-pinch was missing: thread an axial field Bz through the column. Because that field is frozen into the fluid (Lecture 3), squeezing the column's cross-section compresses Bz's flux into a smaller area, which (by flux conservation) raises Bz locally — adding extra magnetic pressure that resists exactly the constriction that was running away. A sufficiently strong trapped Bz (or, in laboratory pinches, a close-fitting conducting wall that similarly traps flux) stabilizes the sausage mode.

8.3The Kink (m=1) Instability

The second classic failure mode is a helical, off-axis displacement of the whole column — not a local constriction, but the entire pinch bending sideways like a slack rope starting to coil, labeled the m=1 (kink) mode. Wherever the column bends, field lines on the concave (inner) side of the bend are compressed closer together, raising Bθ there relative to the convex (outer) side — and that pressure imbalance pushes the bend further in the same direction it already started. Exactly as with the sausage mode, a pure Bθ pinch has no mechanism to resist this and is kink-unstable.

An axial field Bz again provides the cure, but through tension rather than pressure this time: bending the column helically also bends the Bz field lines frozen into it, and magnetic tension (Lecture 5) resists that bending exactly like a straightened guitar string resists being kinked sideways. Whether that tension is strong enough to overcome the destabilizing Bθ pressure imbalance is a competition between the two field components — quantified by a single number.

AnalogyA phone cord with no internal stiffness kinks and coils at the slightest disturbance — nothing resists a sideways bend. Add a stiff internal wire (the axial field B_z) and the same cord resists kinking, springing back straight unless you bend it past some threshold. The Kruskal–Shafranov criterion below is exactly that threshold, stated for a magnetized pinch.

8.4The Kruskal–Shafranov Safety Factor

For a screw pinch of minor radius a, periodic along its length with period L (the cylindrical stand-in for "once around a torus," L = 2πR for a device of major radius R), define the safety factor at the plasma edge as the ratio of how many times a field line winds the "short way" (azimuthally) to how many times it winds the "long way" (axially) per unit length — equivalently, the ratio of the two field components weighted by geometry:

q(a) = ( a B_z ) / ( R B_θ(a) )q is dimensionless. Large q means the field is "mostly B_z, gently twisted"; small q means the field is "mostly B_θ, tightly wound."

The Kruskal–Shafranov criterion, derived from a full energy-principle stability analysis (beyond this course's scope to re-derive, but stated here as the standard, well-established intro-level result), says the m=1 kink mode is stable against this simplest helical perturbation when:

q(a) > 1    (kink-stability criterion)q(a) < 1 means B_θ is too strong relative to B_z for the given aspect ratio a/R — the configuration is kink-unstable.

This single inequality is why every tokamak is designed and operated with q well above 1 at the edge (typically q(a) ≳ 2–3 in practice, for extra margin) — it is one of the handful of numbers a tokamak operator watches most closely, and violating it (or related higher-mode safety-factor thresholds) is directly implicated in disruptions, the sudden, violent loss of plasma confinement that every tokamak design must guard against.

✦ Worked Example — checking kink stability with the safety factor

A screw-pinch device has major radius R = 1.0 m, minor radius a = 0.3 m, and axial field Bz = 2.0 T. Check kink stability for two cases of edge azimuthal field: (a) Bθ(a) = 0.3 T, and (b) Bθ(a) = 0.7 T.

  1. Case (a): q(a) = (aB_z)/(RB_θ(a)) = (0.3 × 2.0)/(1.0 × 0.3) = 0.6/0.3 = 2.0.
  2. Case (a) verdict: q = 2.0 > 1 ⇒ kink-stable, with a comfortable margin.
  3. Case (b): q(a) = (0.3 × 2.0)/(1.0 × 0.7) = 0.6/0.7 ≈ 0.857.
  4. Case (b) verdict: q ≈ 0.857 < 1 ⇒ kink-unstable — the azimuthal field is too strong relative to B_z for this geometry.
  5. Lesson: the same axial field and geometry can be stable or unstable purely depending on how much azimuthal (pinching) field is driven through the plasma — exactly the design trade-off every pinch and tokamak confronts.

🛠️ Hands-On: check the Kruskal–Shafranov criterion (project checkpoint)

# safety_factor.py — Lecture 8 project checkpoint
import numpy as np

def safety_factor(a, R, Bz, Btheta_a):
    return (a*Bz)/(R*Btheta_a)

R, a, Bz = 1.0, 0.3, 2.0
for Btheta_a in [0.3, 0.7]:
    q = safety_factor(a, R, Bz, Btheta_a)
    verdict = "kink-stable" if q > 1 else "KINK-UNSTABLE"
    print(f"Btheta(a)={Btheta_a} T   q(a)={q:.3f}   -> {verdict}")

# --- actual output ---
# Btheta(a)=0.3 T   q(a)=2.000   -> kink-stable
# Btheta(a)=0.7 T   q(a)=0.857   -> KINK-UNSTABLE

🔗 Where this is heading

Kink and sausage instabilities are the simplest members of a much larger stability theory — the same energy-principle logic, applied rigorously (via the MHD energy functional, beyond this course's scope), governs pressure-driven instabilities, β-limits, and disruption physics in real tokamaks. Lecture 9 steps back from analytic special cases and asks a more general question: given any 1-D MHD configuration, how do you solve the equations numerically on a computer — the tool needed once a problem is too complicated for a clean closed-form equilibrium or dispersion relation.

Q1. Why does satisfying ∇p = J×B exactly (Lecture 4) not guarantee an equilibrium is physically safe to operate?
A. Because the equation is only approximately true
B. Because force balance says the net force is zero at that instant, but says nothing about whether a small perturbation grows or decays
C. Because real plasmas never satisfy this equation
D. Because the equation only applies to vacuum
Show answer

B. Stability is a separate question from force balance — a pencil balanced on its tip and a ball at the bottom of a bowl can both have zero net force, but respond completely differently to a nudge. (A, C, D) misstate the equation's validity or applicability.

Q2. In the sausage (m=0) instability of a pure Z-pinch, a small constriction grows because…
A. B_θ decreases at the constriction, releasing the pinch
B. B_θ increases at the constriction (smaller radius, same enclosed current), increasing the local pinching force and squeezing it further
C. The axial field B_z always dominates in a Z-pinch
D. The plasma has no current in a Z-pinch
Show answer

B. B_θ = μ₀I/(2πr), so a smaller local radius r at fixed enclosed current I means a larger B_θ there, pinching the constriction further — a self-reinforcing runaway. (A) is the opposite of what Ampère's law gives. (C) a pure Z-pinch has no B_z at all. (D) the pinch's entire confining field comes from its axial current.

Q3. What physically stabilizes the kink (m=1) instability as an axial field B_z is added?
A. B_z has no effect on kink stability
B. Magnetic tension in the bent B_z field lines resists the helical bending, opposing the displacement
C. B_z eliminates the plasma's current entirely
D. B_z increases B_θ, which is itself destabilizing
Show answer

B. Bending the column also bends the frozen-in B_z field lines, and magnetic tension resists that bending, providing the restoring force the kink mode otherwise lacks. (A) B_z is precisely the stabilizing ingredient. (C) B_z and the plasma current are independent. (D) B_z does not increase B_θ; they are separate field components.

Q4. A screw pinch has a=0.3 m, R=1.0 m, B_z=2.0 T, and B_θ(a)=0.7 T. The safety factor q(a) = aB_z/(RB_θ(a)) ≈ 0.857. This configuration is…
A. Kink-stable, with a large margin
B. Kink-unstable, since q < 1
C. Sausage-unstable only, with no implication for kink modes
D. Impossible to evaluate without knowing the plasma pressure
Show answer

B. q ≈ 0.857 falls below the Kruskal–Shafranov threshold of 1, so this configuration is kink-unstable. (A) q < 1 is the unstable regime. (C) q specifically addresses kink (not sausage) stability. (D) q(a) as defined depends only on the field geometry, not directly on pressure.

Q5. Why do tokamak operators keep the edge safety factor q(a) comfortably above 1 (typically ≳ 2–3) rather than operating right at q=1?
A. Because q has no connection to any real instability
B. Because q=1 is the marginal kink-stability threshold, and operating with margin guards against disruptions from kink-related instabilities
C. Because higher q always means a hotter plasma
D. Because q determines the fusion reaction rate directly
Show answer

B. q=1 is the Kruskal–Shafranov marginal-stability point; real devices keep q(a) safely above this (and other related) threshold(s) to avoid triggering kink-driven disruptions. (A) q is directly tied to kink stability. (C, D) q is a magnetic-geometry quantity, not a direct measure of temperature or fusion rate.
