Week 5 · Lecture 5

Magnetic Pressure, Tension & Plasma Beta

The J×B force is secretly two different forces wearing one symbol — a pressure that pushes field lines apart, and a tension that pulls curved ones straight, both scaling as the same B²/μ₀. A separate number, plasma beta, instead tells you whether thermal pressure or the magnetic field itself is dynamically in charge.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Decompose the Lorentz force density J×B into a magnetic-pressure term and a magnetic-tension term.
  • Explain magnetic pressure B²/2μ₀ as an isotropic push, and magnetic tension as a restoring force on curved field lines.
  • Define plasma beta β = 2μ₀p/B² and state what small/large β imply about a plasma.
  • Compute β for a tokamak core, the solar wind, and the solar corona, and rank them.
  • Explain that β compares thermal pressure to magnetic pressure specifically — and that whether magnetic pressure or magnetic tension dominates the field's own force balance is a separate, geometry-dependent question, since both scale as B²/μ₀.

5.1Splitting the Lorentz Force

Lecture 4's force-balance equation buried a lot of physics inside the single term J×B. Using Ampère's law to write J = ∇×B/μ₀, and then applying the standard vector identity (∇×A)×A = (A·∇)A − ∇(|A|²/2) with A = B, the Lorentz force density splits cleanly into two physically distinct pieces:

J×B = (1/μ₀)(B·∇)B − ∇( B²/(2μ₀) )Magnetic tension (first term) plus minus the gradient of magnetic pressure (second term). Exactly analogous to how a stretched elastic membrane exerts both an isotropic pressure and a tension along its surface.

This single algebraic rearrangement is one of the most useful results in all of MHD, because the two pieces behave completely differently and have completely different physical origins.

5.2Magnetic Pressure: An Isotropic Push

The second term, −∇(B²/2μ₀), has exactly the mathematical form of a pressure-gradient force, with an effective magnetic pressure:

P_B = B²/(2μ₀)Units of Pa, identical to thermal pressure. Depends only on the local field strength |B|, not on its direction or curvature.

Just like thermal pressure, magnetic pressure pushes isotropically from regions of strong field toward regions of weak field — it has no preferred direction tied to the field lines themselves. This is exactly the mechanism behind the θ-pinch of Lecture 4: field lines piled up densely at the edge (high B, high P_B) push inward against the plasma's thermal pressure piled up at the center.

5.3Magnetic Tension: A Restoring Force on Curved Lines

The first term, (B·∇)B/μ₀, is identically zero wherever field lines are perfectly straight — it is purely a curvature effect. For a field line with local radius of curvature Rc, this term points from the field line toward its local center of curvature with magnitude B²/(μ₀Rc), exactly like the restoring tension in a stretched string or rubber band that has been bent: the bigger the curvature (smaller Rc) or the stronger the field, the harder the line "wants" to snap straight.

tension force density = (B²/μ₀) × (curvature vector)Nonzero only where the field line bends. The quantity B²/μ₀ plays the role of a tension-per-unit-area, exactly analogous to the tension in a stretched elastic string.

This is precisely the origin of the extra −Bθ²/(μ₀r) term in Lecture 4's screw-pinch equation: an azimuthal field Bθ(r) is intrinsically curved (it wraps around the axis with radius of curvature r), so it always carries a tension contribution pointing radially inward — the "hoop stress" that pinches a Z-pinch together. A purely axial field Bz(r) in a straight cylinder, by contrast, has no curvature and contributes tension zero, which is exactly why the θ-pinch equation reduced to pure pressure balance.

Common mistakeDo not assume a strong field automatically means strong tension. Tension depends on curvature, not just magnitude — a very strong but perfectly straight field (like the θ-pinch's B_z) contributes zero tension, while a weaker but tightly curved field can contribute significant tension. Always ask "how bent is this field line?" before invoking tension.

5.4Plasma Beta: Who's Doing the Confining?

With magnetic pressure now defined on equal footing with thermal pressure, their ratio is a single, powerfully diagnostic dimensionless number: plasma beta.

β = p / (B²/2μ₀) = 2μ₀p/B²The ratio of thermal to magnetic pressure. β ≪ 1: the field dominates and is dynamically "stiff." β ≫ 1: thermal pressure dominates and the field is a comparatively weak, almost passive tracer of the flow (still frozen-in, per Lecture 3, just not dynamically important).

β is not just a bookkeeping ratio — it tells you whether thermal pressure or the magnetic field itself is dynamically in charge of a given equilibrium, and it is one of the single most-quoted numbers in fusion and space-plasma physics, because it directly measures how efficiently a magnetic field confines thermal energy. (Note β is silent on whether magnetic pressure or magnetic tension dominates the field's own force balance in a given geometry — both scale as the same B²/μ₀, and §5.2–5.3's distinction between them is a separate question from β entirely.) A fusion reactor wants β as high as practically possible (more thermal pressure — meaning more fusion reactions — for the same expensive magnetic field), but there is a β limit above which the configuration becomes unstable (Lecture 8 previews why).

✦ Worked Example — plasma beta in a tokamak core

A tokamak core has electron and ion densities ne = ni = 1×10²⁰ m⁻³, with Te = Ti = 10 keV, in a field B = 5 T. Using μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷, find the thermal pressure, the magnetic pressure, and β.

  1. Thermal pressure (electrons + ions, both at 10 keV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁵ J): p = nek_BTe + nik_BTi = 2 × (10²⁰ × 1.602×10⁻¹⁵) ≈ 3.20×10⁵ Pa.
  2. Magnetic pressure: P_B = B²/(2μ₀) = 25/(2 × 4π×10⁻⁷) ≈ 9.95×10⁶ Pa.
  3. Plasma beta: β = p/P_B = 3.20×10⁵ / 9.95×10⁶ ≈ 0.032 (3.2%).
  4. Interpretation: the magnetic field's confining pressure outweighs the thermal pressure it is confining by a factor of about 31 — a typical, healthy, magnetically dominated tokamak operating point. (Pushing β much higher is exactly what triggers the β-limiting instabilities previewed in Lecture 8.)

🛠️ Hands-On: compute and compare plasma beta

# plasma_beta.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np

mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7
e = 1.602176634e-19

def plasma_beta(n, T_eV, B, species=2):
    # species=2: electrons + ions both at T_eV (quasineutral hydrogenic plasma)
    p = species * n * T_eV * e
    P_B = B**2/(2*mu0)
    return p, P_B, p/P_B

cases = {
    "tokamak core": (1e20, 10e3, 5.0),
    "solar corona": (1e15, 100.0, 0.01),
    "solar wind":   (5e6,  10.0, 5e-9),
}
for name, (n, T, B) in cases.items():
    p, P_B, beta = plasma_beta(n, T, B)
    print(f"{name:14s}  p={p:.3e} Pa  P_B={P_B:.3e} Pa  beta={beta:.3e}")

# --- actual output ---
# tokamak core    p=3.204e+05 Pa  P_B=9.947e+06 Pa  beta=3.222e-02
# solar corona    p=3.204e-02 Pa  P_B=3.979e+01 Pa  beta=8.053e-04
# solar wind      p=1.602e-11 Pa  P_B=9.947e-12 Pa  beta=1.611e+00

Try this: hold the tokamak's n and T fixed and lower B to 1 T. Beta scales as 1/B², so even a modest field reduction pushes β up sharply — watch it approach (and in this toy case, exceed) typical operational limits.

🔗 Where this is heading

Magnetic tension is the restoring force behind every oscillation this course studies next: Lecture 6's Alfvén wave is literally a transverse wave on a "string" of tension B²/μ₀ and mass density ρ, exactly like a wave on a guitar string. Magnetic pressure, meanwhile, supplies the compressional restoring force behind the magnetosonic waves of Lecture 7. Beta reappears directly in Lecture 7's wave speeds (through the sound speed, which depends on p) and again in Lecture 8, where beta limits set hard bounds on how much fusion power a given magnetic field can usefully confine.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The Lorentz force density J×B splits into which two pieces?
A. Gravity and friction
B. A magnetic-pressure gradient term and a magnetic-tension term
C. Two identical copies of the thermal pressure force
D. Electric field and current alone, with no magnetic contribution
Show answer

B. Using Ampère's law and a vector identity, J×B = (B·∇)B/μ₀ − ∇(B²/2μ₀): tension plus minus the gradient of magnetic pressure. (A, C, D) are not what this decomposition produces.

Q2. Magnetic tension, (B·∇)B/μ₀, is nonzero only when…
A. The field lines are curved — it vanishes for a perfectly straight field, however strong
B. The field strength |B| is large, regardless of direction
C. The plasma has zero pressure
D. The plasma is not quasineutral
Show answer

A. Tension is a pure curvature effect; a strong but straight field (like a θ-pinch's B_z) contributes zero tension. (B) a strong straight field still has zero tension. (C, D) are unrelated to whether tension is present.

Q3. Plasma beta is defined as β = 2μ₀p/B². A plasma with β ≪ 1 is best described as…
A. Thermally dominated — the magnetic field is a weak, nearly passive tracer
B. Magnetically dominated — the field's pressure dwarfs the thermal pressure it confines
C. Having zero magnetic field
D. Having zero thermal pressure
Show answer

B. β ≪ 1 means B²/2μ₀ ≫ p — magnetic pressure far exceeds thermal pressure, so the field dominates the dynamics. (A) describes β ≫ 1, the opposite case. (C, D) are not implied — both p and B can be nonzero with β ≪ 1.

Q4. A tokamak core (n=10²⁰ m⁻³, T=10 keV, B=5 T) has β ≈ 0.032, while the solar wind at 1 AU has β ≈ 1.6. What does this comparison tell you?
A. The tokamak has no magnetic field
B. The solar wind has no thermal pressure
C. The tokamak is strongly magnetically dominated, while the solar wind's thermal and magnetic pressures are comparable (thermal slightly ahead)
D. Both plasmas behave identically
Show answer

C. β ≈ 0.032 ≪ 1 for the tokamak means the field dominates by a factor of ~30; β ≈ 1.6 for the solar wind means thermal and magnetic pressures are of the same order, with thermal slightly larger. (A, B) both plasmas have both pressures nonzero. (D) their β values differ by nearly two orders of magnitude.

Q5. Why do fusion reactor designers want β as high as practically possible, subject to stability limits?
A. Higher β means less thermal pressure, which is always safer
B. Beta has no connection to reactor economics or performance
C. Higher β eliminates the need for any magnetic field
D. Higher β means more thermal (fusion-relevant) pressure confined per unit of (expensive) magnetic field pressure — better economic use of the field
Show answer

D. Since β measures thermal pressure (and hence fusion reactivity) relative to the costly magnetic field, a higher β extracts more fusion performance from the same field strength — subject to the stability limits Lecture 8 previews. (A) higher β means more thermal pressure, not less. (C) the field is still required to confine anything. (B) β is precisely the number that connects to reactor economics.
