Ideal MHD & the Frozen-In-Flux Theorem

When resistivity is negligible, magnetic field lines stop being abstract bookkeeping and start behaving like threads painted onto the fluid — stretched, twisted, and dragged along, but never cut.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the ideal-MHD induction equation, ∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B), as the η → 0 limit of Lecture 2's result.
  • Derive the frozen-in-flux theorem: the magnetic flux through any surface moving with the fluid is constant in time.
  • Explain the physical sense in which field lines "move with" the fluid in ideal MHD.
  • State Alfvén's theorem: two fluid elements on a common field line stay on a common field line forever, under ideal MHD.
  • Identify when frozen-in breaks down (finite resistivity, magnetic reconnection) and why that matters.

3.1Taking the Ideal Limit

Lecture 2 derived the resistive induction equation, ∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) + (η/μ₀)∇²B, and showed that the Lundquist number S = μ₀Lv/η compares the two terms on the right. The worked examples found S of order 10⁵–10¹⁴ for lab and astrophysical plasmas — in every practically interesting case, the diffusive term is negligible next to the advective one over the relevant dynamical timescale. Dropping it gives the ideal MHD induction equation:

∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B)The η → 0 (or equivalently S → ∞) limit. The plasma behaves as a perfect electrical conductor.

This single equation — purely kinematic, no resistivity in sight — turns out to have a remarkable geometric consequence for how the magnetic field is carried around by the fluid. That consequence is the subject of this lecture.

3.2Deriving the Frozen-In-Flux Theorem

Consider any closed loop of fluid elements C(t), each one simply drifting with the local fluid velocity v(x,t), and let S(t) be a surface bounded by that loop, moving and deforming along with it. Define the magnetic flux through that comoving surface:

Φ(t) = ∫_S(t) B · dA

Φ(t) can change for two distinct reasons: the field B itself may be changing in time at each fixed point (captured by ∂B/∂t), and the boundary loop is physically moving, so it sweeps through regions where B differs from before. Standard vector calculus for a surface advected by a velocity field v gives the exact kinematic identity:

dΦ/dt = ∫_S ∂B/∂t · dA − ∮_C (v×B) · dl

Apply Stokes' theorem to convert the loop integral into a surface integral, ∮_C(v×B)·dl = ∫_S ∇×(v×B)·dA, and combine:

dΦ/dt = ∫_S [ ∂B/∂t − ∇×(v×B) ] · dA

The bracket is exactly the ideal induction equation from §3.1, which says ∂B/∂t − ∇×(v×B) = 0 everywhere. Therefore:

dΦ/dt = 0    (the frozen-in-flux theorem)The magnetic flux through any surface that moves with the ideal-MHD fluid never changes — no matter how the loop stretches, twists, or deforms as it is carried along.
AnalogyImagine a wire loop drawn in permanent ink on the surface of a lake, and count how many fish swim through that loop as the lake's surface currents stretch and distort it. The frozen-in theorem says: however wildly the loop deforms, the magnetic "flux" through it — the fish count, so to speak — never changes, as long as nothing can swim across the ink line itself. That is exactly the role resistivity would play if it were not zero: a finite η lets field lines "leak" across fluid boundaries; ideal MHD forbids it entirely.

3.3What "Frozen In" Really Means

Flux conservation through every possible comoving loop is a strong enough statement to justify a vivid, and physically exact, picture: in ideal MHD, you may think of individual field lines as attached to the fluid, like threads painted onto a flowing liquid. This is Alfvén's theorem: if two fluid elements start out connected by a common field line, they remain connected by a common field line for all time, however the fluid is stirred, stretched, or compressed.

This is a statement about topology, not just magnitude. Field lines can be stretched (increasing |B| locally, since flux through a shrinking cross-section must be conserved), bent, and braided by the flow — all of that is allowed and is exactly how MHD waves and instabilities move energy around. What ideal MHD forbids is a change in which fluid elements are connected to which — field lines cannot be cut and reconnected to new partners. That reconnection of magnetic topology is precisely what finite resistivity (however small) eventually permits, and it is the mechanism behind solar flares, tokamak disruptions, and magnetospheric substorms.

💬 Discussion 1 — If field lines can't be cut, how do solar flares happen?

The frozen-in theorem says ideal MHD forbids any change in which fluid elements share a field line. Yet solar flares and tokamak disruptions release enormous stored magnetic energy in minutes by exactly this kind of field-line "reconnection." Reconcile the two: what has to be true locally, in some small region, for reconnection to happen at all — and why can that region be tiny (even microscopic) while still releasing energy stored over a huge volume? (Hint: look back at the Lundquist number — reconnection physics lives in the thin layers where the local S drops low enough for the diffusive term in the induction equation to matter again.)

Model response

Locally, the term ideal MHD drops — the resistive diffusion piece (η/μ₀)∇²B in the induction equation — has to become dynamically important again. That only happens where the local Lundquist number S = μ₀Lv/η falls to order 1, and since S scales as L¹ (τ_η∝L² but τ_A∝L, so the extra power of L cancels), you don't need η itself to change: shrinking the local lengthscale L (a thin current sheet instead of the whole loop) is enough to collapse S by many orders of magnitude even while the bulk plasma, on its own much larger scale, keeps an enormous S and stays essentially frozen-in.

The region can be tiny because the thin layer only has to act as the gatekeeper for a topology change, not as the energy source. The magnetic energy actually released was stored over the entire large-scale volume, built up slowly as stresses accumulated in the frozen-in field; the reconnection site just lets that large-scale field relax to a lower-energy topology once it's permitted to "cut and reconnect" locally. A small, localized breakdown of the frozen-in approximation can therefore unlock energy from a volume many orders of magnitude larger than the breakdown region itself.

✦ Worked Example — how "frozen" is the solar corona, quantitatively?

A coronal loop has length scale L = 1×10⁷ m (10,000 km), Alfvén speed v_A ≈ 6.9×10⁶ m/s (from n ≈ 10¹⁵ m⁻³, B ≈ 0.01 T), and Spitzer resistivity η ≈ 3.0×10⁻⁶ Ω·m at Te ≈ 100 eV. Compare the Alfvén-crossing time to the resistive-diffusion time.

  1. Alfvén-crossing (ideal dynamical) time: τ_A = L/v_A = 10⁷/6.9×10⁶ ≈ 1.45 s — the time for a disturbance to cross the loop at the Alfvén speed.
  2. Resistive diffusion time: τ_η = μ₀L²/η = (4π×10⁻⁷ × (10⁷)²)/3.0×10⁻⁶ ≈ 4.2×10¹³ s, i.e. about 1.3 million years.
  3. Ratio: τ_η/τ_A = S ≈ 2.9×10¹³ — confirming the Lundquist number from Lecture 2's scaling.
  4. Verdict: on any timescale a human (or a flare) cares about — seconds to days — resistive diffusion is utterly irrelevant, and the field is, to extremely high precision, frozen into the coronal plasma. Yet flares happen in minutes. The resolution (Discussion 1) is that reconnection occurs in highly localized thin current sheets where the local length scale shrinks enough to make the local Lundquist number small — the bulk corona stays frozen while a sliver of it does not.

🛠️ Hands-On: Alfvén-crossing vs. resistive-diffusion time

# frozen_in_timescales.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7

def timescales(L, vA, eta):
    tau_A   = L/vA
    tau_eta = mu0*L**2/eta
    return tau_A, tau_eta, tau_eta/tau_A

L, vA, eta = 1e7, 6.898e6, 2.985e-6
tau_A, tau_eta, S = timescales(L, vA, eta)
print(f"tau_A   = {tau_A:.3f} s")
print(f"tau_eta = {tau_eta:.4e} s  ({tau_eta/3.156e7:.3e} years)")
print(f"S = tau_eta/tau_A = {S:.4e}")

# --- actual output ---
# tau_A   = 1.450 s
# tau_eta = 4.2098e+13 s  (1.334e+06 years)
# S = tau_eta/tau_A = 2.9039e+13

Try this: shrink L to 10 m (a thin reconnection current sheet instead of a whole loop), keeping η the same. Watch S collapse by six orders of magnitude, from ~2.9×10¹³ down to ~2.9×10⁷ (since τ_η ∝ L² while τ_A ∝ L, so S = τ_η/τ_A ∝ L¹ — the shrink in L itself is six orders, 10⁷/10 = 10⁶) — a concrete illustration of why reconnection physics lives in thin layers, even though S stays far above 1.

🔗 Where this is heading

Frozen-in flux is the geometric heart of ideal MHD and sets up everything that follows: Lecture 4's equilibria are static configurations where the frozen field and the fluid pressure balance each other exactly; Lectures 6–7's waves are small perturbations of that frozen field snapping back toward equilibrium; Lecture 8's instabilities are cases where the frozen field's own tension and pressure work against restoring the equilibrium. The one thing ideal MHD can never do on its own is explain how a confined plasma's energy or topology changes irreversibly — real devices always have some resistivity, however small, waiting in the wings.

Q1. The ideal-MHD induction equation ∂B/∂t = ∇×(v×B) is obtained from the full resistive equation by…
A. Setting v = 0 everywhere
B. Taking the η → 0 (equivalently S → ∞) limit, dropping the resistive diffusion term
C. Setting B = 0 everywhere
D. Assuming the fluid has no pressure
Show answer

B. Dropping the (η/μ₀)∇²B term — justified when S ≫ 1 — leaves the purely advective ideal induction equation. (A) would remove the advective term too, leaving nothing. (C, D) are unrelated to this limit.

Q2. The frozen-in-flux theorem states that…
A. The magnetic flux through any surface moving with the ideal-MHD fluid is constant in time
B. The magnetic field is always uniform in space
C. The fluid velocity is always zero
D. The magnetic field can never change in strength anywhere
Show answer

A. dΦ/dt = 0 for any comoving surface, as derived from the ideal induction equation plus Stokes' theorem. (B) B can vary in space; only the flux through a comoving surface is conserved. (C) the fluid is generally moving — that's the whole point. (D) B's magnitude can change locally (e.g. by stretching field lines) even while total flux through a comoving loop is fixed.

Q3. Alfvén's theorem, built on frozen-in flux, says that two fluid elements starting on the same field line…
A. Always end up on different field lines after any motion
B. Remain on a common field line for all time, under ideal MHD, however the line is stretched or bent
C. Must have the same velocity forever
D. Must be the same distance apart forever
Show answer

B. This is the precise topological statement: connectivity is preserved, even though the field line's length, shape, and the elements' separation can all change. (A) is the opposite. (C, D) overstate the theorem — only the topological connection, not the detailed kinematics, is fixed.

Q4. A coronal loop has τ_A ≈ 1.5 s and τ_η ≈ 1.3 million years. What does this enormous ratio (the Lundquist number) tell you?
A. The field diffuses through the plasma almost instantly
B. The corona has no magnetic field
C. On any timescale relevant to flares or loop dynamics, resistive diffusion is utterly negligible and the field is effectively frozen in
D. Ideal MHD is a bad approximation for the corona
Show answer

C. τ_η ≫ τ_A by thirteen orders of magnitude means diffusion is irrelevant on dynamical timescales — exactly the regime where frozen-in flux is an excellent approximation almost everywhere in the volume. (A) is backwards. (B) the corona's field is strong and essential. (D) the opposite conclusion follows from this ratio.

Q5. Magnetic reconnection — the process behind solar flares — is possible despite the frozen-in theorem because…
A. The frozen-in theorem is simply wrong
B. Reconnection does not actually change which fluid elements share a field line
C. The Sun has no resistivity at all
D. Reconnection happens in thin current layers where the local length scale is small enough that resistive diffusion (the term dropped to get ideal MHD) becomes important again, even though S is huge in the bulk plasma
Show answer

D. Since τ_η ∝ L² while τ_A ∝ L, shrinking the relevant length scale (a thin current sheet) collapses the local Lundquist number by many orders of magnitude, letting the diffusive term matter locally even while the bulk volume remains essentially ideal. (A) the theorem is correct wherever ideal MHD genuinely holds. (C) all real plasmas have some finite resistivity. (B) reconnection is defined by exactly this topological change.
