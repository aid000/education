Week 1 · Lecture 1

From Particles to Fluids: The MHD Approximation

Ten billion trillion particles, each gyrating on its own field line — and yet a plasma often moves as one obedient conducting fluid. Knowing exactly when that simplification is legal is the whole foundation of this course.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Contrast the kinetic description of a plasma (a distribution function in position-velocity space) with the fluid description (a handful of fields in position space).
  • Explain how adding the electron and ion momentum equations and dropping electron inertia produces a single-fluid model.
  • State the two scale-separation conditions — L ≫ ρi and ω ≪ Ωci — that justify treating a plasma as a magnetohydrodynamic (MHD) fluid.
  • Compute an ion Larmor radius and cyclotron frequency and check scale separation for a real plasma.
  • Name what MHD keeps (macroscopic equilibria, large-scale waves and instabilities) and what it necessarily discards (kinetic, velocity-space effects).

1.1Two Ways to Describe the Same Plasma

In Course 1 you met a plasma one particle at a time: each electron and ion obeys the Lorentz force law, gyrates around field lines, and drifts under E×B and grad-B forces. That picture is exact but unmanageable — a cubic meter of fusion plasma holds on the order of 1020 particles (n ~ 1020 m⁻³ is this course's standard density convention), and no computer will ever track that many trajectories directly. The kinetic theory you'll meet in Course 2 compresses this into a distribution function f(x, v, t) — still a function of six variables (three position, three velocity) plus time, and still formidable to solve in general.

This course takes a different compression. Instead of asking "how many particles are at this position with this velocity," a fluid description asks only: at this point in space, what is the local density, the local bulk velocity, and the local pressure? That throws away everything about the shape of the velocity distribution and keeps only its first few moments (density = the zeroth moment, mean velocity = the first, pressure = related to the second). The payoff is enormous: instead of a function of six variables, you are solving for a handful of fields — ρ(x,t), v(x,t), p(x,t), B(x,t) — that obey ordinary fluid-dynamics-like partial differential equations. That theory is magnetohydrodynamics, MHD: "magneto" because the fluid carries current and feels Lorentz forces, "hydrodynamics" because everything else looks like the Navier–Stokes equations you'd write for water or air.

AnalogyDescribing a crowd leaving a stadium kinetically means tracking every person's position and velocity. Describing it as a fluid means drawing a few arrows for "the crowd here is moving this fast, this densely, in this direction" — you lose the information about any one person's path, but you gain a model simple enough to actually use for predicting how the crowd as a whole will flow through the exits.

1.2From Two Fluids to One

A plasma genuinely has (at least) two interpenetrating fluids — electrons and ions — each with its own density, velocity, and pressure, each obeying its own momentum equation with the Lorentz force q(E + v×B) and inter-species friction from collisions. MHD is the further step of combining those two into a single conducting fluid. Define the total mass density, the center-of-mass (bulk) velocity, and the net current density:

ρ = n_i m_i + n_e m_e ≈ n_i m_i    v = (n_i m_i v_i + n_e m_e v_e)/ρ    J = e(Z n_i v_i − n_e v_e)Electrons carry essentially no mass (m_e/m_i ~ 1/1836 for hydrogen), so the bulk flow v is set almost entirely by the ions, while the much faster electrons are what carry most of the current J.

Adding the electron and ion momentum equations together, the inter-species friction terms cancel (one species' drag on the other is the exact opposite of the reverse), and because the electron mass is negligible the electron inertia term can be dropped. What survives is a single momentum equation for the whole fluid, with the two species' pressures combined into one scalar p = pe + pi, and the two species' Lorentz forces combined into one net force density J×B (the electric-field terms cancel in the sum because the plasma is quasineutral). We will write that equation out in full in Lecture 2 — the point here is where it comes from: one fluid is two fluids added together, with the small, fast electron physics absorbed into effective quantities like the current and the resistivity.

1.3When Is a Fluid Description Legal? Separation of Scales

Replacing every gyrating particle with a smooth fluid element is only valid if the particle's own orbit is small and fast compared to whatever macroscopic phenomenon you're trying to describe. Concretely, MHD requires two scale separations, one in space and one in time:

(1) L ≫ ρ_i = m_i v_th / (eB)     (2) ω ≪ Ω_ci = eB / m_iL = the length scale of the phenomenon of interest; ρ_i = ion Larmor (gyro) radius; ω = the frequency of interest; Ω_ci = ion cyclotron frequency.

Condition (1) says the structures you care about — an equilibrium profile, a wave, an instability — vary over distances much larger than the tight little circle an ion gyrates on. Then a "fluid element" containing many gyro-orbits has a well-defined bulk velocity and pressure, and you can forget that it's secretly made of particles spiraling around field lines. Condition (2) says the same thing in time: if the phenomenon evolves slowly compared to one gyration, each ion completes many orbits before anything macroscopic changes, so the plasma stays locally isotropic in the plane perpendicular to B — exactly what lets us use a single scalar pressure p instead of tracking separate parallel and perpendicular pressures.

Strongly magnetized plasmas — exactly the fusion-relevant regime this course targets — satisfy both conditions almost effortlessly: the magnetic field is so strong that ρi shrinks to millimeters while the device is meters across, and Ωci is tens of megahertz while the equilibria and instabilities we care about evolve on microsecond-to-millisecond timescales. That is precisely why MHD, despite being a drastic simplification, is the workhorse theory for fusion confinement.

Common mistakeDo not confuse this approximation (treating the plasma as a fluid at all) with the separate approximation of ideal MHD (treating that fluid as a perfect conductor with zero resistivity) that we meet in Lecture 3. A plasma can satisfy L ≫ ρi and ω ≪ Ωci — making the fluid description valid — while still having enough resistivity that ideal MHD is a poor approximation. The two approximations are independent layers, stacked on top of each other.

1.4What You Gain, and What You Give Up

The payoff of the MHD approximation is a theory you can actually compute with: four coupled field equations (continuity, momentum, induction, and a pressure closure — all coming in Lecture 2) replace a six-dimensional kinetic equation. Equilibria become algebra, waves become dispersion relations, and instabilities become eigenvalue problems — all solvable by hand or on a laptop, which is exactly this course's promise.

The cost is that MHD is blind to velocity space. Anything that depends on the detailed shape of the particle distribution — Landau damping, resonant wave-particle interactions, runaway electrons, anisotropic pressure from a non-Maxwellian population — is invisible to a fluid model that only tracks density, bulk velocity, and a scalar pressure. Those are exactly the phenomena Course 2's kinetic theory is built to capture. MHD and kinetic theory are not competitors; they are complementary descriptions of the same plasma, each accurate in the regime its assumptions hold. MHD wins for the big, slow, macroscopic question this course is built around: will the magnetic field actually hold the plasma together?

✦ Worked Example — checking scale separation in a fusion-relevant plasma

A tokamak-like deuterium plasma has B = 5 T, ion temperature Ti = 10 keV, and a macroscopic scale L = 0.5 m (roughly the minor radius). Using μ₀ = 4π×10⁻⁷ and the deuteron mass mi = 2mp = 3.344×10⁻²⁷ kg, check whether the MHD length-scale condition holds.

  1. Thermal speed: v_th = √(2Ti/mi) with Ti = 10 keV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁵ J: v_th = √(2 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁵ / 3.344×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 9.79×10⁵ m/s.
  2. Ion cyclotron frequency: Ωci = eB/mi = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 5)/(3.344×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 2.40×10⁸ rad/s (fci ≈ 38.1 MHz).
  3. Ion Larmor radius: ρi = v_th/Ωci = 9.79×10⁵ / 2.40×10⁸ ≈ 4.09×10⁻³ m (about 4.1 mm).
  4. Scale-separation ratio: L/ρi = 0.5 / 4.09×10⁻³ ≈ 122 — comfortably ≫ 1, so the spatial condition for treating this plasma as an MHD fluid holds well.
  5. Interpretation: any equilibrium or wave with a wavelength or gradient scale of order centimeters or larger is safely in the MHD regime; only phenomena on the millimeter scale of ρi itself (such as finite-Larmor-radius corrections) would require stepping outside MHD.

🛠️ Hands-On: check MHD validity in Python

Week 1 setup: this course uses plain Python + NumPy + SciPy — no specialized plasma package required (PlasmaPy is optional if you want unit-aware formulas, but every formula here is short enough to write directly). Install once:

⬇ Install Python + NumPy/SciPy (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed, create an environment and install the scientific stack:

python3 -m venv mhd-env
source mhd-env/bin/activate        # macOS / Linux
mhd-env\Scripts\activate           # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib

Verify: run python -c "import numpy, scipy; print('ok')".

Now reproduce the worked example and check scale separation for any (B, T, L):

# mhd_validity.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

e   = 1.602176634e-19   # C
m_p = 1.67262192369e-27 # kg

def check_mhd_validity(B, T_i_eV, L, m_i=2*m_p):
    T_J   = T_i_eV * e
    v_th  = np.sqrt(2*T_J/m_i)
    Omega_ci = e*B/m_i
    rho_i = v_th/Omega_ci
    return v_th, Omega_ci, rho_i, L/rho_i

v_th, Omega_ci, rho_i, ratio = check_mhd_validity(B=5.0, T_i_eV=10e3, L=0.5)
print(f"v_th      = {v_th:.3e} m/s")
print(f"Omega_ci  = {Omega_ci:.3e} rad/s  (f_ci = {Omega_ci/(2*np.pi):.3e} Hz)")
print(f"rho_i     = {rho_i*1e3:.3f} mm")
print(f"L / rho_i = {ratio:.1f}   (need >> 1 for MHD to apply)")

# --- actual output ---
# v_th      = 9.787e+05 m/s
# Omega_ci  = 2.395e+08 rad/s  (f_ci = 3.811e+07 Hz)
# rho_i     = 4.087 mm
# L / rho_i = 122.3   (need >> 1 for MHD to apply)

Try this: drop B to 0.1 T (a lab plasma, not a tokamak) and keep everything else fixed. ρi scales as 1/B, so it grows 50×; re-run and see how much it eats into the scale-separation margin.

Q1. What is the key simplification a fluid (MHD) description makes relative to a kinetic description?
A. It ignores the magnetic field entirely
B. It requires more variables than the kinetic description
C. It assumes the plasma has no particles at all
D. It tracks only a few moments of the particle distribution (density, bulk velocity, pressure) instead of the full distribution function
Show answer

D. MHD replaces f(x,v,t) with a handful of position-space fields — density, bulk velocity, pressure, and B — discarding the detailed shape of the velocity distribution. (A) MHD is built entirely around the magnetic field. (B) MHD uses dramatically fewer variables, which is exactly its appeal. (C) MHD still describes a real particle fluid, just via moments.

Q2. In going from two fluids (electrons + ions) to one MHD fluid, why can electron inertia be dropped from the combined momentum equation?
A. Electrons carry no charge
B. Electrons don't move
C. The electron mass is thousands of times smaller than the ion mass, so its inertial contribution is negligible
D. Electrons are not part of a plasma
Show answer

C. m_e/m_i ~ 1/1836 for hydrogen (and smaller still relative to heavier ions), so the electron's contribution to the combined momentum (mass × velocity) is negligible even though electrons move fast and carry most of the current. (A) electrons are the carriers of negative charge. (B) electrons move fast — that's why they carry the current. (D) electrons are one of the two fluids being combined.

Q3. Which pair of conditions justifies treating a plasma as an MHD fluid?
A. L ≪ ρ_i and ω ≫ Ω_ci
B. L ≫ ρ_i and ω ≪ Ω_ci
C. The plasma must be exactly neutral
D. The magnetic field must be exactly zero
Show answer

B. The phenomenon of interest must vary over length scales much larger than the ion Larmor radius and evolve much slower than the ion cyclotron frequency, so individual gyro-orbits average out into a smooth fluid. (A) is backwards — it would put you deep in single-particle, not fluid, territory. (C) exact neutrality is neither required nor physical (recall quasineutrality from C1). (D) MHD requires a magnetic field; it is central to the whole theory.

Q4. A tokamak plasma has ρ_i ≈ 4 mm and a minor radius L ≈ 0.5 m. What does L/ρ_i ≈ 122 tell you?
A. The plasma is too small to exist
B. MHD is a poor approximation here
C. The spatial scale-separation condition for MHD is well satisfied
D. The ion cyclotron frequency is zero
Show answer

C. L/ρ_i ≫ 1 (here ≈ 122) means macroscopic structures are far larger than a single gyro-orbit, exactly the regime where the fluid approximation is legitimate. (B) is the opposite conclusion. (A, D) are not supported by this ratio at all.

Q5. What does the MHD approximation necessarily give up compared to a full kinetic treatment?
A. The ability to describe any waves at all
B. Velocity-space (kinetic) effects such as Landau damping and resonant wave-particle interactions
C. The magnetic field
D. Conservation of mass
Show answer

B. By tracking only moments (density, velocity, pressure), MHD cannot see effects that depend on the detailed shape of the velocity distribution — that is Course 2's kinetic territory. (A) MHD describes several important wave types, as Lectures 6–7 show. (C) B is central to MHD. (D) mass conservation (continuity) is one of the four MHD equations.
