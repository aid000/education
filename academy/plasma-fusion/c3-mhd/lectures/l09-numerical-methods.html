Numerical Methods for 1-D MHD

Analytic equilibria and clean dispersion relations only go so far. The last ingredient this course needs is how to put the MHD equations on a grid — and the one rule that decides whether a simulation runs cleanly or explodes into nonsense.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Discretize a 1-D MHD-type wave equation using finite differences in space and time.
  • Explain, physically, why explicit time-stepping schemes are only conditionally stable.
  • State the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition, Δt ≤ C·Δx/v_max, with v_max the fastest wave speed present.
  • Compute a stable timestep for a given grid spacing and fast-magnetosonic speed.
  • Demonstrate numerically that violating the CFL condition causes an explicit scheme to blow up.

9.1Putting the Equations on a Grid

Every analytic result in this course — the θ-pinch profile, the Alfvén and magnetosonic dispersion relations, the kink criterion — came from a special geometry or a linearization simple enough to solve by hand. Most real problems are not that cooperative: an arbitrary equilibrium profile, a nonlinear perturbation, or a wave propagating through a non-uniform plasma generally has no closed-form solution at all. The remaining tool is to solve the MHD equations numerically, and this lecture builds the minimal machinery for doing so in one spatial dimension.

The idea is straightforward: replace the continuous domain with a discrete grid of points xi = i·Δx, and replace each derivative with a finite difference between neighboring grid values. For a wave-type equation, the simplest energy-conserving choice is a central difference in space and a central (leapfrog) difference in time:

∂²f/∂x² ≈ ( f_{i+1} − 2f_i + f_{i−1} ) / Δx²     ∂²f/∂t² ≈ ( f_i^{n+1} − 2f_i^n + f_i^{n−1} ) / Δt²Both approximations become exact as Δx, Δt → 0; for finite step sizes they introduce a controllable, small error.

Substituting these into the 1-D wave equation ∂²f/∂t² = v²∂²f/∂x² (the same equation that governs the Alfvén wave of Lecture 6, or any single magnetosonic branch of Lecture 7 in its linear, 1-D form) gives an explicit update rule: knowing the field at time steps n and n−1 everywhere on the grid, compute it directly at step n+1, point by point, with no equations to invert.

9.2Why "Explicit" Does Not Mean "Always Stable"

An explicit scheme is appealingly cheap — each new value is computed directly from old ones, with no linear system to solve. But cheap is not the same as safe. The physical wave described by the equation carries information at a finite speed v: a disturbance at grid point i cannot physically reach point i+2 any faster than it takes to cross two grid spacings at speed v. The numerical scheme, by contrast, only looks one grid spacing away at each timestep. If the timestep Δt is so large that the true physical signal would have crossed more than one grid cell in that time, the numerical scheme is being asked to predict something it structurally cannot "see" — and the result is not just inaccurate, it is catastrophically, exponentially unstable, with any tiny numerical error doubling, redoubling, and eventually overflowing within a few dozen steps.

AnalogyImagine trying to track a fast-moving car by only ever looking at two photographs: your current position and your neighbor's position, one driveway away. If the car can cross three driveways between photographs, no amount of cleverness with just those two neighboring snapshots can correctly predict where it went — your estimate necessarily overshoots, and overshooting compounds on every subsequent frame. Shrink the time between photographs until the car moves less than one driveway per frame, and tracking becomes reliable again. That "less than one driveway per frame" rule is exactly the CFL condition.

9.3The CFL Condition

Formalizing that picture gives the Courant–Friedrichs–Lewy (CFL) condition: for an explicit scheme to remain numerically stable, the timestep must be small enough that no physical signal crosses more than about one grid cell per step:

Δt ≤ C · Δx / v_maxC is the Courant number, an O(1) constant set by the specific numerical scheme (often C ≤ 1 for simple explicit schemes); v_max is the fastest physical signal speed present in the problem.

For 1-D MHD, vmax is the fast magnetosonic speed — by Lecture 7's ordering, the fast mode is always the fastest signal in the system, exceeding both v_A and c_s at every propagation angle. A code that satisfies the CFL condition using only, say, the sound speed while ignoring a much larger Alfvén speed will blow up the moment the magnetic field becomes dynamically important — a classic, easy-to-make mistake in a first MHD code.

Common mistakeDo not pick Δt based on the slowest interesting speed in the problem (e.g. the flow speed, or the sound speed alone) just because it is the physically "important" one for your science question. Numerical stability cares only about the fastest signal the discretized equations can carry — in MHD, that is almost always the fast magnetosonic speed (often dominated by v_A), even if the phenomenon you actually care about evolves much more slowly.
✦ Worked Example — a stable timestep for a tokamak-scale grid

A 1-D MHD code uses grid spacing Δx = 0.01 m (1 cm) to resolve a tokamak-core plasma whose fast magnetosonic speed (perpendicular propagation, from Lecture 7) is v_fast ≈ 7.81×10⁶ m/s. Find the maximum stable timestep (C=1) and a safer, commonly used working value (C=0.5).

  1. Maximum stable timestep (C=1): Δtmax = Δx/v_fast = 0.01/7.81×10⁶ ≈ 1.28×10⁻⁹ s (about 1.28 nanoseconds).
  2. Recommended working timestep (C=0.5): Δt = 0.5 × 1.28×10⁻⁹ ≈ 6.40×10⁻¹⁰ s — a safety margin below the hard limit, standard practice since real schemes have less margin than the idealized C=1 bound suggests.
  3. Interpretation: resolving a tokamak-scale device (~meters) at centimeter resolution with this timestep requires roughly a billion timesteps to simulate even one second of physical time — a concrete illustration of why the fast magnetosonic speed's CFL limit is often the single biggest computational cost in a full MHD simulation, and why many production codes use implicit or semi-implicit time-stepping specifically to sidestep it.

🛠️ Hands-On: watch the CFL condition fail in Python

# cfl_demo.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

v, dx = 7.8145e6, 0.01
dt_cfl = dx / v
print(f"dt_max (CFL=1) = {dt_cfl:.4e} s")

def run_wave(dt, nsteps, N=200):
    x = np.arange(N)*dx
    f_curr = np.exp(-(x-x[N//2])**2/(2*(3*dx)**2))   # Gaussian pulse
    f_prev = f_curr.copy()
    c2 = (v*dt/dx)**2
    for n in range(nsteps):
        f_next = np.zeros_like(f_curr)
        f_next[1:-1] = (2*f_curr[1:-1] - f_prev[1:-1]
                        + c2*(f_curr[2:] - 2*f_curr[1:-1] + f_curr[:-2]))
        f_prev, f_curr = f_curr, f_next
        if not np.all(np.isfinite(f_curr)):
            return np.inf
    return np.nanmax(np.abs(f_curr))

print("Stable   (dt = 0.5 * dt_cfl):", run_wave(0.5*dt_cfl, 300))
print("Unstable (dt = 1.5 * dt_cfl):", run_wave(1.5*dt_cfl, 300))

# --- actual output ---
# dt_max (CFL=1) = 1.2797e-09 s
# Stable   (dt = 0.5 * dt_cfl): 0.4761       (started at amplitude 1.0 -- pulse spread, still finite)
# Unstable (dt = 1.5 * dt_cfl): 1.929e+234   (started at amplitude 1.0 -- huge but still a finite float by step 300, not actually inf)

A timestep just 50% over the CFL limit is enough to turn a smooth, physically sensible Gaussian pulse into a ~10²³⁴-amplitude runaway within 300 steps — not literally infinite yet (double-precision floats only overflow past ~1.8×10³⁰⁸), but a few hundred more steps of the same exponential growth would get it there. There is no "a little bit unstable."

💬 Discussion 3 — explicit vs. implicit: is the CFL limit a law of physics, or a choice?

The CFL condition as derived here is a property of explicit time-stepping schemes specifically — it is not a fundamental limit on simulating MHD itself. Implicit schemes (which solve a linear system coupling the whole grid at the new time step, rather than updating point-by-point from old values) can remain stable at far larger Δt, sometimes unconditionally. Given that CFL-limited timesteps forced a billion-plus steps to simulate one second of a tokamak in the worked example, why doesn't every MHD code simply switch to an implicit scheme and sidestep the problem entirely? What would you guess is the catch — what does an implicit scheme have to sacrifice in exchange for removing the CFL constraint on Δt?

Model response

The catch is per-step cost and complexity, not accuracy for free. An explicit scheme's update rule is purely local — each grid point's new value depends only on its old neighbors, so one step is a handful of cheap array operations. An implicit scheme instead couples every point on the grid together into one large (sparse) linear system that has to be solved — by matrix factorization or an iterative solver — at every single timestep. That per-step solve is far more expensive and harder to implement and parallelize efficiently than the explicit update, so removing the CFL limit trades "many cheap steps" for "fewer, much more expensive steps."

There's a second catch beyond raw cost: a larger Δt that's merely stable is not automatically accurate. If the physics itself is evolving on a fast timescale (e.g. a real fast-magnetosonic transient you actually care about resolving), you still want a small Δt for accuracy regardless of what stability allows — so the apparent advantage shrinks for problems where the fast dynamics matter physically, not just numerically. This is exactly why many production MHD codes use semi-implicit schemes: treat only the stiff, CFL-limiting piece (the fast wave) implicitly, and the rest explicitly, getting most of the stability benefit without paying full price for a fully implicit solve.

Q1. Why is an explicit finite-difference scheme for a wave equation only conditionally stable?
A. Because finite differences are always inaccurate
B. Because if the timestep is too large, the true physical signal would cross more than one grid cell per step — information the one-cell-wide update rule cannot account for, causing errors to grow exponentially
C. Because computers cannot represent small numbers accurately
D. Explicit schemes are always unconditionally stable; this is a myth
Show answer

B. The scheme's domain of dependence (one grid cell per step) must contain the true physical domain of dependence (v·Δt); violating this is the root cause of the instability. (A, C) are not the mechanism. (D) explicit schemes for hyperbolic equations are specifically and famously conditionally stable — that's the entire content of the CFL condition.

Q2. The CFL condition for 1-D MHD is Δt ≤ C·Δx/v_max. What should v_max be?
A. The fast magnetosonic speed — the fastest signal the discretized MHD equations can carry
B. The slowest speed in the problem, since that's what you care about physically
C. Always exactly the speed of light
D. Zero
Show answer

A. Numerical stability is set by the fastest signal present, which in ideal MHD is always the fast magnetosonic speed (Lecture 7). (B) using a slower speed would under-constrain Δt and the scheme would blow up. (C) MHD uses the non-relativistic, pre-Maxwell ordering (Lecture 2); light speed is irrelevant here. (D) would make the scheme useless.

Q3. A grid has Δx = 0.01 m and the fast magnetosonic speed is 7.81×10⁶ m/s. The maximum stable timestep (C=1) is approximately…
A. 1.28 seconds
B. 1.28×10⁻⁹ s
C. 1.28×10⁹ s
D. Exactly zero
Show answer

B. Δt_max = Δx/v = 0.01/7.81×10⁶ ≈ 1.28×10⁻⁹ s, matching both the hand calculation and the Python demo. (A, C) are off by many orders of magnitude. (D) a finite, nonzero stable timestep exists.

Q4. In the Python demo, a timestep 50% above the CFL limit caused the simulated field to…
A. Remain exactly the same as the stable case
B. Decay smoothly to zero
C. Grow exponentially to an enormous (though still finite) value within a few hundred steps
D. Oscillate with a slightly longer period but stay bounded
Show answer

C. The demo showed the amplitude reaching ~1.9×10²³⁴ by step 300 — a huge but still-finite double-precision float, not literally "inf" yet, though a few hundred more steps of the same exponential growth would get it there — the hallmark exponential blow-up of a CFL-violating explicit scheme. (A, B, D) all understate how dramatically and quickly CFL violation destroys a simulation.

Q5. Why do many production MHD codes use implicit or semi-implicit time-stepping specifically for the fast magnetosonic wave?
A. Because the fast wave is scientifically unimportant and can be ignored
B. Because explicit schemes cannot represent magnetic fields at all
C. Because implicit schemes are always less accurate and used only for convenience
D. Because the fast speed (often dominated by v_A) sets an extremely restrictive CFL limit under explicit schemes, forcing impractically many timesteps to simulate realistic physical times
Show answer

D. As the worked example showed, the fast speed's CFL limit can force well over a billion timesteps to reach one second of physical time — implicit methods trade per-step cost for freedom from that restriction. (A) the fast wave is physically essential, not ignorable. (C) implicit methods are a legitimate, widely used approach, not a lesser one. (B) explicit schemes handle B perfectly well; the issue is purely about timestep size.
