Week 7 · Lecture 7

Magnetosonic Waves

Let the plasma compress again, and the Alfvén wave's quiet tension gets joined by thermal pressure in two new ways — sometimes cooperating into a fast wave, sometimes fighting into a slow one.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the general ideal-MHD dispersion relation for a plane wave propagating at angle θ to a uniform B₀, allowing compression.
  • Identify the three wave branches it contains: shear Alfvén, fast magnetosonic, slow magnetosonic.
  • Evaluate the fast and slow magnetosonic speeds in the parallel (θ=0) and perpendicular (θ=90°) limits.
  • Explain physically why the fast mode always exceeds or equals both v_A and c_s (equaling the larger of the two exactly at θ=0), while the slow mode never exceeds either.
  • Compute fast/slow magnetosonic speeds for a tokamak-core plasma at several propagation angles.

7.1Restoring Compression

Lecture 6 switched off compression entirely — the shear Alfvén wave carries no density or pressure perturbation. Switch it back on: allow a general plane-wave perturbation with wavevector k at an arbitrary angle θ to the uniform background field B₀, and let density, pressure, and all three velocity and field components perturb together, linking the momentum equation, the induction equation, and the adiabatic pressure closure (p₁ = (γp₀/ρ₀)ρ₁, using Lecture 2's closure). Carrying this out (the full linear algebra was checked symbolically for this course) produces a sixth-order polynomial in ω that factors cleanly into two pieces:

( ω² − k²v_A²cos²θ ) · [ ω⁴ − ω²k²(v_A²+c_s²) + k⁴v_A²c_s²cos²θ ] = 0c_s = √(γp₀/ρ₀) is the ordinary sound speed (γ = 5/3). The first factor is exactly Lecture 6's shear Alfvén relation.

The first factor reproduces the shear Alfvén wave exactly, unchanged by the presence of compression — it remains a distinct, purely transverse, incompressible branch at every angle. The second factor, a quadratic in ω², is new: it describes two additional branches, the fast and slow magnetosonic waves, both genuinely compressional.

7.2Solving for the Fast and Slow Speeds

The bracketed quadratic in ω² solves directly by the quadratic formula, giving the two magnetosonic phase speeds v = ω/k as functions of the propagation angle θ:

v_{fast,slow}² = (1/2)[ (v_A²+c_s²) ± √( (v_A²+c_s²)² − 4v_A²c_s²cos²θ ) ]"+" gives the fast speed, "−" gives the slow speed. Both depend continuously on θ, interpolating between the two limiting cases below.

At θ = 0 (propagation exactly along B₀), the discriminant simplifies to (v_A² − c_s²)², so the square root is just |v_A² − c_s²|, and the two roots cleanly separate into the larger and smaller of v_A and c_s individually — propagation along the field decouples into a pure sound wave and a pure Alfvén wave, with no mixing. At θ = 90° (propagation exactly perpendicular to B₀), cos θ = 0 and the discriminant becomes simply (v_A²+c_s²)², giving:

v_fast(90°) = √(v_A² + c_s²)     v_slow(90°) = 0Perpendicular to the field, magnetic and thermal pressure compress together into one combined fast wave; the slow wave does not propagate at all.

7.3Why "Fast" Is Always Fastest, and "Slow" Is Always Slowest

The physical distinction between the two branches is whether magnetic pressure and thermal pressure compress in phase or out of phase. In the fast wave, a compression squeezes both the plasma (raising p) and the field lines together (raising B, hence B²/2μ₀) at the same time — the two restoring pressures add constructively, which is why the fast speed always exceeds or equals both v_A and c_s individually (as the θ=90° formula √(v_A²+c_s²) makes explicit — a sum under the square root, not a difference; the fast speed only drops to equal the larger of v_A and c_s, rather than strictly exceeding it, at θ=0°, where it reduces exactly to max(v_A, c_s)). In the slow wave, the two compressions work against each other — where the plasma is compressed, the field is locally rarefied, and vice versa — so the restoring effect partially cancels, and the slow speed never exceeds the smaller of v_A and c_s.

AnalogyThink of two people pushing a swing. If they push in sync (fast mode), the swing goes higher and faster than either push alone could manage. If they push against each other (slow mode), their efforts partly cancel and the swing barely moves. Magnetic and thermal pressure "pushing" together make the fast wave outrun both; "pushing" against each other make the slow wave underrun both.
Common mistakeDo not assume the slow magnetosonic wave is simply "the sound wave" and the fast one is simply "the Alfvén wave" in general. That identification is only exact at θ = 0 (parallel propagation); at any other angle, both magnetosonic branches are genuine mixtures of magnetic and thermal compression, and the shear Alfvén wave remains a third, separate branch throughout.
✦ Worked Example — fast and slow speeds in a tokamak core

Using the tokamak-core values from Lectures 5–6 (n = 1×10²⁰ m⁻³ deuterium, Te=Ti=10 keV, B = 5 T), find v_A, c_s, and the fast/slow speeds at θ = 0° and θ = 90°.

  1. Alfvén speed (from Lecture 6): v_A ≈ 7.71×10⁶ m/s.
  2. Sound speed: c_s = √(γp/ρ) with γ=5/3, p = 3.204×10⁵ Pa (Lecture 5), ρ = n(2m_p) = 3.345×10⁻⁷ kg/m³: c_s = √(5/3 × 3.204×10⁵/3.345×10⁻⁷) ≈ 1.26×10⁶ m/s.
  3. θ=0° (parallel): since v_A > c_s here, v_fast = v_A ≈ 7.71×10⁶ m/s, v_slow = c_s ≈ 1.26×10⁶ m/s — pure Alfvén and pure sound, decoupled.
  4. θ=90° (perpendicular): v_fast = √(v_A²+c_s²) = √(7.71²+1.26²)×10⁶ ≈ 7.81×10⁶ m/s; v_slow = 0 — no slow-mode propagation at all perpendicular to B.
  5. Interpretation: in this low-β (β≈3%) plasma, v_A ≫ c_s, so the fast speed barely changes with angle (7.71 → 7.81×10⁶ m/s) while the slow speed drops from c_s all the way to zero — exactly the qualitative picture of the figure above.

🛠️ Hands-On: magnetosonic speeds vs. propagation angle

# magnetosonic_speeds.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np

def magnetosonic_speeds(theta_deg, vA, cs):
    theta = np.radians(theta_deg)
    s    = vA**2 + cs**2
    disc = np.sqrt(s**2 - 4*vA**2*cs**2*np.cos(theta)**2)
    v_fast = np.sqrt((s + disc)/2)
    v_slow = np.sqrt(max((s - disc)/2, 0.0))
    return v_fast, v_slow

vA, cs = 7.7117e6, 1.2635e6   # tokamak-core values, m/s
for theta in [0, 30, 60, 90]:
    vf, vs = magnetosonic_speeds(theta, vA, cs)
    print(f"theta={theta:3d} deg   v_fast={vf:.4e} m/s   v_slow={vs:.4e} m/s")

# --- actual output ---
# theta=  0 deg   v_fast=7.7117e+06 m/s   v_slow=1.2635e+06 m/s
# theta= 30 deg   v_fast=7.7381e+06 m/s   v_slow=1.0905e+06 m/s
# theta= 60 deg   v_fast=7.7895e+06 m/s   v_slow=6.2544e+05 m/s
# theta= 90 deg   v_fast=7.8145e+06 m/s   v_slow=0.0000e+00 m/s

Try this: set cs > vA (a higher-β case) and rerun — at θ=0 the fast/slow identification with Alfvén/sound flips (fast=sound, slow=Alfvén), exactly as the "larger/smaller of the two" rule in §7.2 predicts.

🔗 Where this is heading

You now have the complete linear wave spectrum of ideal MHD: three branches (Alfvén, fast, slow), each a small, well-behaved perturbation of a stable equilibrium. Lecture 8 asks the opposite question: when does a perturbation not stay small — when does the equilibrium itself actively amplify a disturbance instead of merely propagating it? That marks the transition from waves to instabilities, the chief practical obstacle in confining a real fusion plasma.

Q1. Restoring pressure perturbations to the Alfvén-wave analysis of Lecture 6 reveals how many distinct ideal-MHD wave branches in total?
A. One
B. Two
C. Three — shear Alfvén, fast magnetosonic, slow magnetosonic
D. Five
Show answer

C. The dispersion relation factors into the Alfvén branch plus a quadratic in ω² giving two more branches: fast and slow magnetosonic. (A, B, D) do not match the factored dispersion relation derived in §7.1.

Q2. At θ = 90° (propagation exactly perpendicular to B₀), the fast magnetosonic speed is…
A. Zero
B. √(v_A² + c_s²) — magnetic and thermal pressure compress together
C. Exactly v_A
D. Exactly c_s
Show answer

B. At θ=90° the discriminant reduces exactly to (v_A²+c_s²)², giving v_fast=√(v_A²+c_s²). (A) that is the slow-mode value at this angle, not the fast. (C, D) only hold exactly at θ=0, not θ=90°.

Q3. Why is the fast magnetosonic speed always greater than or equal to both v_A and c_s?
A. Because in the fast mode, magnetic and thermal pressure compress in phase, adding their restoring effects constructively
B. Because the fast mode has no physical restoring force
C. Because the slow mode is faster in reality
D. Because v_A and c_s are always equal
Show answer

A. Constructive ("in-phase") compression of magnetic and thermal pressure makes the combined restoring force — and hence the wave speed — exceed either pressure's wave speed alone. (B) it has a real, combined restoring force. (C) is the opposite of the correct ordering. (D) v_A and c_s are generally different, as the worked example shows.

Q4. At θ = 0° (propagation exactly along B₀) in a plasma where v_A > c_s, the fast and slow speeds become…
A. Both exactly zero
B. v_fast = c_s and v_slow = v_A, regardless of which is larger
C. Both exactly equal to √(v_A²+c_s²)
D. v_fast = v_A and v_slow = c_s — pure Alfvén and pure sound waves, fully decoupled
Show answer

D. At θ=0 the discriminant becomes |v_A²−c_s²|, splitting the two roots into exactly the larger and smaller of v_A and c_s — here v_A > c_s, so fast=v_A, slow=c_s. (A, C) do not match the θ=0 limit. (B) gets the fast/slow assignment backwards for this ordering of v_A and c_s.

Q5. At what propagation angle does the shear Alfvén wave (ω = kv_A cos θ) fail to propagate at all?
A. θ = 0° (parallel to B₀)
B. θ = 90° (perpendicular to B₀)
C. It always propagates at every angle with the same speed
D. θ = 45°
Show answer

B. cos(90°) = 0 makes ω = 0 for any k, so the shear Alfvén wave vanishes exactly perpendicular to the field. (A) θ=0 gives the maximum Alfvén speed, ω=kv_A. (C) the speed depends explicitly on cos θ. (D) θ=45° gives an intermediate, nonzero speed.
