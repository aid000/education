Reactivity ⟨σv⟩ & Fusion Power Density

A real plasma isn't one energy — it's a whole spread of thermal speeds. Average the cross-section over that spread and you get the single number every power-balance calculation in this course actually uses.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the reactivity ⟨σv⟩(T) as a Maxwellian average of σ(v)v, and explain why it depends only on temperature.
  • Use the Bosch-Hale fit to evaluate ⟨σv⟩ for D-T, D-D, and D-He3 at reactor-relevant temperatures.
  • Explain the Gamow peak — the typical relative energy at which fusion reactions actually occur, well above the mean thermal energy.
  • Write and apply the fusion power density formula P = n₁n₂⟨σv⟩E to a representative plasma.

3.1From σ(E) to ⟨σv⟩(T)

Lecture 2 gave you σ(E), the cross-section at one specific relative energy. A real thermal plasma has particles spread across a Maxwellian distribution — recall from kinetic theory that this is just the equilibrium spread of particle speeds at temperature T, with most particles near the thermal energy but a long tail reaching to much higher energies. The quantity that actually controls the fusion rate is σ(v)v averaged over that whole distribution of relative velocities between the two reacting species:

⟨σv⟩(T) = ∫ σ(v)·v·fMaxwellian(v; T) d³vf is normalized so ⟨σv⟩ depends on temperature alone — not on density, not on the particles' individual speeds.

This is the reactivity: a single function of T (units m³/s) that packages away everything about the cross-section and the thermal distribution. Every power-balance calculation from here through the capstone works directly with ⟨σv⟩(T) rather than the underlying σ(E) — exactly the kind of averaging that turns a messy microscopic cross-section into a usable macroscopic quantity.

3.2The Bosch-Hale Fit & Reactivity Values

The integral above has no closed form once you plug in a real σ(E), so fusion physics uses a standard empirical fit — the Bosch-Hale (1992) parameterization — that reproduces the full Maxwellian-averaged reactivity to a few percent accuracy from 0.2 to 100 keV. Evaluating it for the three reactions from Lectures 1-2 gives:

⟨σv⟩ at T = 10 keV:  D-T = 1.14×10⁻²² m³/s   D-D = 5.8×10⁻²⁵ m³/s   D-He3 = 2.1×10⁻²⁵ m³/sD-T beats D-D by ~200× and D-He3 by ~530× at this temperature — the quantitative payoff of Lecture 2's Gamow-factor argument.

As temperature rises, every reactivity increases, but at very different rates. D-T's reactivity peaks (≈8.6×10⁻²² m³/s) around 50-70 keV and then gently falls; D-He3's keeps climbing and only overtakes D-T's somewhere above ~150-200 keV — temperatures no confinement scheme has sustained in a working reactor. This is the quantitative version of why D-T, not D-He3, is the near-term fuel.

3.3The Gamow Peak: Where Fusion Actually Happens

Two competing effects shape the integrand of ⟨σv⟩: the Maxwellian weight falls exponentially as e−E/T (fewer particles at high energy), while the Gamow tunneling factor rises steeply with E. Their product is peaked at an intermediate energy — the Gamow peak — found by maximizing −E/T − √(EG/E):

E0 = (EGT²/4)1/3For D-T at T = 10 keV, E_0 ≈ 31 keV — about 3× the thermal temperature itself.

This is a genuinely important, slightly counterintuitive result: the "typical" fusion reaction does not happen near the average thermal energy T — it happens at several times T, in the high-energy tail of the Maxwellian, where there are few particles but each one tunnels far more easily. Reactor performance is therefore controlled by the tail of the distribution, not by the bulk — a fact that becomes important when non-Maxwellian effects (fast ions from auxiliary heating, alpha particles) are considered in more advanced treatments.

Common mistakeDon't assume most fusion reactions occur between particles moving at "the" thermal speed corresponding to T. The Gamow peak sits well above T (E₀/T ≈ 3 at 10 keV, falling slowly toward ≈1.8 by 50 keV) — it is the product of a falling Maxwellian weight and a rising tunneling probability, not either one alone.

3.4Fusion Power Density

With ⟨σv⟩(T) in hand, the volumetric fusion power is immediate: the reaction rate per unit volume is the product of the two reactant densities and the reactivity, and each reaction releases its Q-value as kinetic energy:

Pfusion = n1n2⟨σv⟩Efusn_1, n_2 are the densities of the two reacting species (e.g. n_D and n_T); E_fus is the reaction's Q-value.

For a 50-50 D-T mix with total ion density n (so nD = nT = n/2), this becomes:

Pfusion = (1/4)n²⟨σv⟩EfusThe factor of 1/4 comes directly from n_D·n_T = (n/2)·(n/2) = n²/4.
✦ Worked Example — fusion power density of a representative core plasma

A D-T plasma has total ion density n = 1×10²⁰ m⁻³ at T = 10 keV. Find the fusion power density.

  1. Reactivity: from the Bosch-Hale fit, ⟨σv⟩(10 keV) = 1.136×10⁻²² m³/s.
  2. Reaction rate density: (1/4)n² = 0.25 × (1×10²⁰)² = 2.5×10³⁹ m⁻⁶; rate = 2.5×10³⁹ × 1.136×10⁻²² = 2.84×10¹⁷ reactions/(m³·s).
  3. Energy per reaction: E_fus = 17.6 MeV = 17600 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁶ J = 2.82×10⁻¹² J.
  4. Power density: P = 2.84×10¹⁷ × 2.82×10⁻¹² ≈ 8.0×10⁵ W/m³ = 0.80 MW/m³.
  5. Sanity check: ITER's design fusion power (~500 MW) over its plasma volume (~830 m³) averages to ~0.6 MW/m³ — in the same ballpark as this single-temperature estimate, with the difference reflecting real density/temperature profiles peaked above the volume average.

🛠️ Hands-On: the Bosch-Hale reactivity fit in Python

Still the same free Python/NumPy stack. This implements the Bosch-Hale D-T fit directly and reproduces the worked example's power density.

# reactivity.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

def sigmav_DT(T_keV):
    """Bosch-Hale (1992) D-T reactivity fit. Returns <sigma v> in m^3/s."""
    BG, mrc2 = 34.3827, 1124656.0
    C1,C2,C3,C4,C5,C6,C7 = 1.17302e-9,1.51361e-2,7.51886e-2,4.60643e-3,1.35e-2,-1.0675e-4,1.366e-5
    T = T_keV
    theta = T/(1 - (T*(C2+T*(C4+T*C6)))/(1+T*(C3+T*(C5+T*C7))))
    xi = (BG**2/(4*theta))**(1/3)
    sv_cm3 = C1*theta*np.sqrt(xi/(mrc2*T**3))*np.exp(-3*xi)
    return sv_cm3*1e-6   # cm^3/s -> m^3/s

E_FUS_J = 17.6e6*1.602176634e-19    # D-T Q-value in joules

def P_fusion(n, T_keV):
    # 50-50 D-T mix, n = total ion density
    return 0.25*n**2*sigmav_DT(T_keV)*E_FUS_J

n = 1e20
for T in [5, 10, 15, 20]:
    sv = sigmav_DT(T)
    P  = P_fusion(n, T)
    print(f"T={T:4.1f} keV  <sv>={sv:.3e} m3/s  P_fusion={P/1e3:7.1f} kW/m3")

# Verified output:
# T= 5.0 keV  <sv>=1.366e-23 m3/s  P_fusion=   12.0 kW/m3
# T=10.0 keV  <sv>=1.136e-22 m3/s  P_fusion=  800.9 kW/m3
# T=15.0 keV  <sv>=2.740e-22 m3/s  P_fusion= 1930.6 kW/m3
# T=20.0 keV  <sv>=4.330e-22 m3/s  P_fusion= 3052.6 kW/m3

The T=10 keV row reproduces the worked example exactly. Notice how fast P_fusion climbs with T between 5 and 10 keV — reactivity near the Gamow-suppressed low-T end of the fit is extremely temperature-sensitive, which is exactly why reaching ~10 keV is the make-or-break threshold for a reactor.

💬 Discussion 1 — is chasing D-He3 ever rational?

D-He3's reactivity trails D-T's by roughly 500× at 10 keV, yet D-He3 is routinely proposed as an "advanced" fusion fuel because its dominant products are charged (a proton and an alpha), avoiding the 14.1 MeV neutron that activates reactor structures. Q3. The Gamow peak energy E₀ for D-T at T = 10 keV (≈31 keV) being well above T itself tells you that…

Q4. For a 50-50 D-T mixture with total ion density n, the fusion power density P = n₁n₂⟨σv⟩E_fus becomes P = (1/4)n²⟨σv⟩E_fus because…
