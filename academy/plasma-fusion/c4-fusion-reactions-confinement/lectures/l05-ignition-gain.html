Week 5 · Lecture 5

Ignition & Fusion Gain Q

Breakeven is a milestone, not the finish line. The fusion gain Q tracks exactly how close a plasma is to needing no outside help at all.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the fusion gain Q as the ratio of fusion power to external heating power.
  • Distinguish scientific breakeven (Q=1) from engineering breakeven (plant electrical output = plant electrical input).
  • Derive the relationship between Q and the alpha self-heating fraction f_α, and show Q→∞ as f_α→1 at ignition.
  • Place real devices (JET, ITER's target) on the Q scale using this relationship.

5.1Defining Fusion Gain Q

Lecture 1 flagged the naming collision: a reaction's Q-value (MeV released per reaction) is not the fusion gain Q used from here on. The fusion gain is a dimensionless performance number — the ratio of fusion power produced to the external heating power you had to supply to sustain the plasma:

Q = Pfusion / PauxP_aux is the power delivered to the plasma by external heating systems (neutral beams, RF heating, etc.) — not counting the alpha self-heating, which is already "inside" P_fusion's bookkeeping.

Scientific breakeven is Q = 1: the fusion power produced exactly equals the heating power delivered to the plasma. JET (the Joint European Torus) reached Q≈0.67 in its record D-T shots — close to, but short of, scientific breakeven.

5.2Scientific vs Engineering Breakeven

Q as defined above compares power delivered to the plasma against power the plasma releases. It says nothing about the efficiency of the equipment that generated that heating power in the first place. Engineering (or "net") breakeven is the far harder condition that total electrical output of the whole plant exceeds total electrical input to the whole plant — including the inefficient heating systems, magnets, cryogenics, and cooling:

Qeng = (plant electrical output) / (plant electrical input)Because heating systems are typically only 30-50% efficient at converting wall-plug power into power delivered to the plasma, Q_eng ≈ 1 typically requires scientific Q well above 1 — commonly cited estimates put the requirement around Q ≳ 5-10 depending on the heating and conversion efficiencies assumed.
Common mistakeA headline claiming "fusion net energy gain" almost always refers to a scientific comparison (fusion energy released vs. energy delivered to the fuel/plasma), not engineering breakeven (whole-plant electricity in vs. out). The two are very different bars, and conflating them has caused real public confusion about how close commercial fusion power actually is.

5.3Alpha Self-Heating and the Path to Ignition

As a D-T plasma heats up, an increasing share of its energy losses are made up not by external heating but by the alpha particles produced in fusion reactions themselves (recall: 3.54 of the 17.6 MeV per reaction stays in the plasma as the alpha's kinetic energy). Define the alpha self-heating fraction fα as the share of total heating power supplied by alphas, with the rest supplied externally — assuming steady state, so total heating power equals the loss power Ploss:

fα = Pα/Ploss     Paux = Ploss(1 − fα)At f_α = 1, alphas alone balance every loss — no external heating is needed. That is exactly the ignition condition from Lecture 4.

Since Pfusion = (Efus/Eα)·Pα ≈ 5.03·Pα (the fixed energy-split ratio from Lecture 1), substituting gives Q directly in terms of fα:

Q = (Efus/Eα)·fα/(1 − fα) ≈ 5.03·fα/(1 − fα)A single, clean relationship: pushing f_α toward 1 (self-heating dominates) drives Q to infinity — ignition is the f_α→1 limit of the fusion-gain curve.

5.4Placing Real Devices on the Q Scale

JET's best D-T result, Q≈0.67, corresponds to a modest alpha fraction — most of its heating was still external. ITER's design target is Q=10 (not full ignition): solving the fα-Q relationship shows this requires fα≈0.665, meaning alpha particles already supply roughly two-thirds of the heating power needed to sustain ITER's plasma at its target operating point, with only the remaining third delivered by external heating systems. Pushing further toward fα→1 (true ignition, Q→∞) is the explicit goal of next-generation devices beyond ITER.

✦ Worked Example — Q at three alpha fractions

Using Q = 5.03·f_α/(1−f_α), find Q at f_α = 0.5, f_α = 0.8, and solve for f_α at Q = 10.

  1. f_α = 0.5: Q = 5.03 × 0.5/0.5 = 5.03.
  2. f_α = 0.8: Q = 5.03 × 0.8/0.2 = 5.03 × 4 = 20.1.
  3. Solve for f_α at Q = 10: 10 = 5.03·f_α/(1−f_α) ⟹ 10(1−f_α) = 5.03f_α ⟹ 10 = 15.03f_α ⟹ f_α = 10/15.03 ≈ 0.665.
  4. Check: Q(0.665) = 5.03 × 0.665/0.335 ≈ 5.03 × 1.985 ≈ 9.98 ≈ 10 ✓ — matches, confirming ITER's Q=10 target corresponds to f_α≈66.5%.

🛠️ Hands-On: Q vs alpha self-heating fraction in Python

# fusion_gain.py — Lecture 5
from scipy.optimize import brentq

RATIO = 17.6/3.5   # E_fus/E_alpha

def Q_of_falpha(f_alpha):
    return RATIO*f_alpha/(1-f_alpha)

for f in [0.2, 0.5, 0.665, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95]:
    print(f"f_alpha={f:.3f}  ->  Q={Q_of_falpha(f):6.2f}")

# solve for f_alpha at a target Q
target_Q = 10.0
f_at_Q10 = brentq(lambda f: Q_of_falpha(f) - target_Q, 1e-6, 1-1e-6)
print(f"f_alpha at Q={target_Q}: {f_at_Q10:.4f}")

# Verified output:
# f_alpha=0.200  ->  Q=  1.26
# f_alpha=0.500  ->  Q=  5.03
# f_alpha=0.665  ->  Q= 10.07
# f_alpha=0.800  ->  Q= 20.11
# f_alpha=0.900  ->  Q= 45.26
# f_alpha=0.950  ->  Q= 95.54
# f_alpha at Q=10: 0.6654

Notice how Q accelerates: moving f_α from 0.9 to 0.95 more than doubles Q, while moving it from 0.2 to 0.5 only quadruples it. Chasing the last few percent of f_α toward true ignition is disproportionately hard and disproportionately valuable.

Q1. The fusion gain Q = P_fusion/P_aux equal to 1 defines…
A. ignition
B. engineering breakeven
C. scientific breakeven
D. the Lawson criterion
Show answer

C. Q=1 means fusion power equals the power delivered to the plasma — scientific breakeven. (A) ignition is the f_α→1, Q→∞ limit, far beyond Q=1. (B) engineering breakeven compares whole-plant electricity, a much harder bar. (D) the Lawson criterion is a density-temperature-confinement threshold, not a power ratio.

Q2. A news report that a fusion experiment achieved "net energy gain" most likely refers to…
A. engineering breakeven — the whole plant produced more electricity than it consumed
B. scientific breakeven or similar — fusion energy released compared to energy delivered to the fuel/plasma, not whole-plant electricity
C. the plant is now commercially viable
D. the Lawson criterion has been exceeded by 100×
Show answer

B. As flagged in Section 5.2's common mistake, "net energy gain" headlines virtually always mean the scientific comparison, which is a far lower bar than whole-plant engineering breakeven. (A) is the much harder, rarely-yet-achieved condition. (C) does not follow from scientific gain alone. (D) is an invented, unsupported multiple.

Q3. As the alpha self-heating fraction f_α approaches 1, the fusion gain Q = 5.03f_α/(1−f_α)…
A. approaches 5.03
B. approaches 1
C. diverges toward infinity
D. approaches 0
Show answer

C. The denominator (1−f_α) → 0 while the numerator → 5.03, so Q → ∞ — the mathematical signature of ignition, where no external heating (P_aux→0) is needed at all. (A) would be the f_α=0.5 case, not the limit. (B)/(D) don't match the diverging denominator.

Q4. ITER's design target of Q=10 corresponds to an alpha self-heating fraction f_α of approximately…
A. 0.1 (10%)
B. 0.5 (50%)
C. 0.665 (66.5%)
D. 1.0 (100%, full ignition)
Show answer

C. Solving 10 = 5.03f_α/(1−f_α) gives f_α≈0.665 (Section 5.4 and the worked example) — ITER's Q=10 target already relies on alphas for about two-thirds of the needed heating. (A)/(B) are too low to reach Q=10. (D) is full ignition (Q=∞), a step beyond ITER's design target.

Q5. Going from f_α=0.9 to f_α=0.95 increases Q by more than doubling it, while going from f_α=0.2 to f_α=0.5 only roughly quadruples a much smaller Q. This illustrates that…
A. Q is a linear function of f_α
B. the Q-vs-f_α relationship accelerates sharply as f_α approaches 1 — the last few percent toward ignition are disproportionately hard to win and disproportionately valuable
C. Q is independent of f_α at high f_α
D. there is a data error in the hands-on script
Show answer

B. The 1/(1−f_α) factor blows up as f_α→1, so equal increments in f_α near 1 produce much larger jumps in Q than the same increments at moderate f_α — exactly what the Section 5's hands-on numbers show. (A) Q is manifestly nonlinear (it diverges). (C) the opposite is true — Q is most sensitive to f_α near 1. (D) the numbers are correctly computed and verified.
