Fusion Cross-Sections: the Coulomb Barrier & the Gamow Factor

Two nuclei must get close enough for the strong force to grab them — but they repel each other the whole way in. The physics of "how often" starts with that fight.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Estimate the classical Coulomb barrier height for a fusion reaction and compare it to a reactor-relevant thermal energy.
  • Explain why quantum tunneling, not classical crossing, is responsible for essentially all terrestrial and stellar fusion.
  • Define the Gamow energy and compute it for D-T, D-D, and D-He3.
  • Write the cross-section σ(E) in astrophysical S-factor form and explain each piece.
  • Explain quantitatively why D-T's cross-section so thoroughly dominates D-D and D-He3 at reactor temperatures.

2.1The Coulomb Barrier

Deuterium and tritium nuclei are both positively charged, so as they approach each other their electrostatic potential energy climbs as 1/r — a repulsive wall that must be crossed before the short-range (~1-2 fm) strong force can bind them together:

U(r) = k·Z1Z2e²/r     with k·e² ≈ 1.440 MeV·fmUsing a nuclear-radius estimate r ≈ 1.2(A11/3+A21/3) fm for where the strong force takes over.

Plugging in nuclear radii for D (A=2) and T (A=3) gives a barrier peak around 440 keV for D-T — and the barrier only gets taller for reactions with more charge, like D-He3 (Z2=2):

Upeak: D-T ≈ 444 keV    D-D ≈ 476 keV    D-He3 ≈ 888 keVApproximate, from the A^(1/3) nuclear-radius scaling above. D-He3's doubled charge product roughly doubles its barrier relative to D-T/D-D.

Compare that to the temperatures a reactor can realistically sustain: 10-20 keV (100-200 million kelvin). The thermal energy is 20-40× smaller than the barrier peak. Classically, almost no particle in a 10-20 keV Maxwellian distribution — recall from kinetic theory that a Maxwellian is just the thermal-equilibrium spread of particle speeds at a given temperature — has anywhere near enough energy to climb over the barrier. By that logic, fusion reactors (and the Sun) shouldn't work at all.

2.2Quantum Tunneling & the Gamow Factor

The resolution is quantum tunneling: a particle has a nonzero probability of appearing on the far side of a barrier it could never classically surmount, decaying exponentially with how "thick" the barrier looks relative to the particle's energy. For two nuclei of charge Z1, Z2 and reduced mass μ approaching with relative energy E, the WKB tunneling probability through the full 1/r Coulomb tail is:

Ptunnel(E) ∝ exp(−√(EG/E))The Gamow factor. E_G is the Gamow energy — a single number per reaction that sets how fast tunneling is suppressed as E falls.
EG = 2μc²(παZ1Z2)²μc² is the reduced-mass rest energy, α ≈ 1/137.036 the fine-structure constant. Independently verified against the standard Bosch-Hale reactivity-fit constants below.

Computing EG for the three reactions from Lecture 1 — and cross-checking each against the Gamow-energy constant used in the standard Bosch-Hale reactivity fits (Lecture 3) — gives:

EG: D-T ≈ 1182 keV    D-D ≈ 986 keV    D-He3 ≈ 4727 keVD-He3's doubled charge enters E_G squared, nearly quadrupling it relative to D-T/D-D — a much harder tunneling problem.
AnalogyRolling a ball at a steep hill with a pit on the far side: throw it gently and classically it just rolls back — it never reaches the top, let alone falls in. But the ball is also a wave, and that wave has a small amplitude already present on the far side of the hill even though the peak was never classically crossed. Occasionally the ball "is" there. Make the hill steeper (bigger E_G) or throw more gently (smaller E) and that amplitude shrinks fast — exactly the exp(−√(E_G/E)) suppression.

2.3The Cross-Section σ(E): Separating Nuclear Physics from Tunneling

The fusion cross-section σ(E) — the effective "target area" two nuclei present to each other at relative energy E — is conventionally factored into two very different pieces: everything about tunneling through the Coulomb barrier (which we now understand), and everything about what happens once the nuclei are close enough for the strong force to act (genuinely nuclear physics, no simple formula):

σ(E) = S(E)/E · exp(−√(EG/E))S(E), the astrophysical S-factor, is slowly varying with E (only weakly energy-dependent nuclear-reaction physics) — all the violent energy dependence is isolated in the exponential.

This factorization is the whole point: without it, σ(E) would span many orders of magnitude over the energy range of interest, making it nearly impossible to tabulate or fit. Dividing out the Gamow exponential leaves S(E) nearly flat (or slowly varying), which is why experimentalists report S(E) rather than σ(E) directly, and why Lecture 3's reactivity fits can use smooth, well-behaved functions of temperature.

Common mistakeDon't read "S(E) is slowly varying" as "σ(E) is slowly varying." σ(E) itself falls by many orders of magnitude as E drops from 100 keV to 1 keV — almost entirely because of the exp(−√(E_G/E)) factor, not because the underlying nuclear physics is changing quickly.

2.4Why D-T Wins at Reactor Temperatures

Put the pieces together and D-T's advantage becomes obvious: it has both the lowest Gamow energy (1182 keV, vs 986 keV for D-D — actually slightly higher than D-D's — and 4727 keV for D-He3) and a comparatively large S-factor from a nuclear resonance near 100 keV that boosts its cross-section further. D-D's Gamow energy is marginally lower than D-T's, but its nuclear S-factor is intrinsically smaller (no equivalent resonance), so D-T still wins decisively once you account for the full cross-section — which Lecture 3's Maxwellian-averaged reactivity numbers will make numerically explicit. D-He3's much larger Gamow energy (nearly 4× D-T's) means its tunneling suppression is severe at reactor-relevant energies, even though its Q-value (18.35 MeV) is actually the highest of the four reactions.

The lesson: Q-value tells you the payoff, the Gamow factor tells you the odds, and at 10-20 keV the odds dominate completely. This is exactly why, despite tritium's practical downsides (radioactive, must be bred from lithium, not naturally abundant), essentially every near-term fusion reactor design burns D-T.

✦ Worked Example — tunneling suppression across the three fuels at T = 15 keV

Compare the Gamow tunneling factor exp(−√(E_G/E)) at E = 15 keV for D-T (E_G = 1182 keV), D-D (986 keV), and D-He3 (4727 keV).

  1. D-T: √(1182/15) = √78.8 = 8.88 → exp(−8.88) ≈ 1.39×10⁻⁴.
  2. D-D: √(986/15) = √65.7 = 8.11 → exp(−8.11) ≈ 3.01×10⁻⁴.
  3. D-He3: √(4727/15) = √315.1 = 17.75 → exp(−17.75) ≈ 1.95×10⁻⁸.
  4. Ratio D-D/D-T: 3.01×10⁻⁴ / 1.39×10⁻⁴ ≈ 2.2 — D-D's tunneling factor is actually slightly larger than D-T's at this energy (lower E_G), consistent with Section 2.4's point that the full cross-section story needs S(E) too.
  5. Ratio D-T/D-He3: 1.39×10⁻⁴ / 1.95×10⁻⁸ ≈ 7100 — D-He3's much larger Gamow energy crushes its tunneling probability by over three orders of magnitude relative to D-T at this one energy, before any Maxwellian averaging.

🛠️ Hands-On: Gamow energies and tunneling factors in Python

Still no new installs — plain Python + NumPy. This script computes E_G from first principles and reproduces the worked example above.

# gamow_factors.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

alpha = 1/137.035999
u_keV = 931494.1  # keV per atomic mass unit

def E_gamow_keV(Z1, Z2, mu_amu):
    # E_G = 2 * mu*c^2 * (pi*alpha*Z1*Z2)^2
    return 2*mu_amu*u_keV*(np.pi*alpha*Z1*Z2)**2

def tunneling_factor(E_G_keV, E_keV):
    return np.exp(-np.sqrt(E_G_keV/E_keV))

m = {"D": 2.014, "T": 3.016, "He3": 3.015}
mu_DT   = m["D"]*m["T"]  /(m["D"]+m["T"])
mu_DD   = m["D"]*m["D"]  /(m["D"]+m["D"])
mu_DHe3 = m["D"]*m["He3"]/(m["D"]+m["He3"])

EG_DT   = E_gamow_keV(1, 1, mu_DT)
EG_DD   = E_gamow_keV(1, 1, mu_DD)
EG_DHe3 = E_gamow_keV(1, 2, mu_DHe3)
print(f"E_G:  D-T={EG_DT:.0f} keV   D-D={EG_DD:.0f} keV   D-He3={EG_DHe3:.0f} keV")

for label, EG in [("D-T", EG_DT), ("D-D", EG_DD), ("D-He3", EG_DHe3)]:
    P = tunneling_factor(EG, 15.0)
    print(f"  tunneling factor at 15 keV, {label}: {P:.3e}")

# Verified output:
# E_G:  D-T=1182 keV   D-D=986 keV   D-He3=4727 keV
#   tunneling factor at 15 keV, D-T: 1.389e-04
#   tunneling factor at 15 keV, D-D: 3.012e-04
#   tunneling factor at 15 keV, D-He3: 1.951e-08

Sweep E from 1 to 100 keV for all three reactions and plot log(tunneling factor) vs 1/√E — each should trace out a straight line (since the exponent is linear in 1/√E), a good way to sanity-check your own implementation.

🔗 Where this is heading

σ(E) tells you the reaction rate for two particles meeting at one specific relative energy E. A real plasma has particles spread across a whole Maxwellian distribution of energies, not a single E. Lecture 3 averages σ(E)v over that distribution to get the reactivity ⟨σv⟩(T) — the single number, as a function of temperature alone, that every power-balance calculation from here through the capstone actually uses.

Q1. The classical Coulomb barrier for D-T fusion (~440 keV) compared to a reactor-relevant thermal energy of 10-20 keV means…
A. fusion is classically impossible at reactor temperatures, and only quantum tunneling makes it happen
B. the reactor must be heated past 440 keV before any fusion can occur
C. the barrier is irrelevant because the strong force is long-range
D. D-T fusion cannot occur below the Curie temperature
Show answer

A. Thermal energies of 10-20 keV sit 20-40× below the barrier peak, so essentially no particle classically clears it — tunneling is the entire mechanism. (B) reactors run far below the barrier peak and still fuse, via tunneling. (C) the strong force is short-range (~1-2 fm); the barrier matters precisely because it must be tunneled through first. (D) unrelated concept.

Q2. The Gamow factor exp(−√(E_G/E)) describes…
A. the probability of classically climbing over the Coulomb barrier
B. the quantum-mechanical probability of tunneling through the Coulomb barrier at relative energy E
C. the probability that a fusion product decays
D. the strength of the strong nuclear force
Show answer

B. It is the WKB tunneling probability through the 1/r Coulomb tail, falling off as E decreases or E_G (set by charges and reduced mass) increases. (A) tunneling is explicitly non-classical. (C)/(D) unrelated to this factor.

Q3. D-He3's Gamow energy (≈4727 keV) is roughly 4× D-T's (≈1182 keV) mainly because…
A. helium-3 is radioactive
B. E_G scales with (Z₁Z₂)², and He-3's charge Z=2 doubles the charge product relative to D-T's Z=1
C. deuterium and helium-3 have different reduced masses that happen to differ by 4×
D. the strong force is weaker for helium
Show answer

B. E_G = 2μc²(παZ₁Z₂)², so doubling Z₂ (1→2) roughly quadruples E_G through the squared charge product; the reduced-mass change between D-He3 and D-T is comparatively small. (A) irrelevant to the barrier physics. (C) the reduced masses are nearly equal, not 4× different. (D) the strong force isn't what sets E_G — the Coulomb repulsion does.

Q4. In σ(E) = S(E)/E · exp(−√(E_G/E)), the astrophysical S-factor S(E) is useful because…
A. it equals the full cross-section σ(E)
B. it isolates the slowly-varying nuclear physics after dividing out the violently energy-dependent tunneling exponential
C. it is independent of which reaction you are studying
D. it replaces the need for a Gamow factor entirely
Show answer

B. Factoring out exp(−√(E_G/E)) leaves S(E) nearly flat, making it tabulable/fittable — σ(E) itself spans many orders of magnitude over the relevant energy range. (A) S(E) ≠ σ(E); the exponential and 1/E factor are separate. (C) S(E) differs reaction to reaction (it encodes the specific nuclear physics). (D) S(E) and the Gamow factor are both needed, multiplicatively.

Q5. D-T dominates D-D and D-He3 at reactor-relevant temperatures mainly because…
A. D-T has the highest Q-value of the three reactions
B. D-T combines a low Gamow energy with a comparatively large nuclear S-factor (a near-100 keV resonance), beating D-D's lower S-factor and D-He3's much higher Gamow energy
C. deuterium and tritium are the only fusionable isotopes
D. tritium is not radioactive
Show answer

B. Q-value sets the payoff, not the rate; D-He3 actually has the highest Q-value (18.35 MeV) but loses badly on tunneling suppression. D-T's combination of modest E_G and a boosting resonance in S(E) wins on cross-section, which is what matters for reaction rate. (A) is the Q-value trap flagged in Lecture 1. (C) D-D and D-He3 are both real, usable reactions. (D) tritium is in fact radioactive (half-life ~12.3 years) — a real engineering downside despite D-T's physics advantage.
