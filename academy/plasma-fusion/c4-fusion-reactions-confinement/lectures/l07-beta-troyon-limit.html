Plasma Beta & the Troyon Limit

Magnets are the most expensive part of a tokamak. Beta measures how much fusion-relevant pressure you squeeze out of every tesla you pay for — and a stability limit caps how far you can push it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recall the definition of plasma beta and compute it for a representative plasma.
  • Explain why beta is an economic figure of merit: fusion power density scales as β²B⁴.
  • State the Troyon limit and the normalized beta β_N, and apply them to an ITER-like device.
  • Explain why beta is a stability-limited, not just an engineering, quantity.

7.1Recap: Plasma Beta

Recall from C3's MHD treatment that plasma beta compares the plasma's kinetic (thermal) pressure to the magnetic pressure confining it — a dimensionless ratio that tells you how "efficiently" a given magnetic field is being used to hold back plasma pressure:

β = p / (B²/2μ0) = 2μ0p/B²p is the total kinetic pressure, p = n_eT_e + n_iT_i = 2nT for n_e=n_i=n, T_e=T_i=T. B²/2μ_0 is the magnetic pressure.

A typical tokamak core runs at β of a few percent: large enough to be economically meaningful, small enough to respect the stability limit this lecture derives.

7.2Why Beta Is an Economic Figure of Merit

Superconducting magnets are, by far, the most expensive component of a tokamak, and the achievable field strength B is set by magnet technology, not plasma physics. For fixed T, Lecture 3's power-density formula combined with the beta definition shows fusion power density scales with beta and field in a very specific, steep way. Writing n in terms of p = 2nT and p in terms of β and B:

Pfusion ∝ n² ∝ p² ∝ β²B⁴  (at fixed T)Independently verified: doubling β at fixed B and T quadruples P_fusion; doubling B at fixed β and T multiplies P_fusion by 16.

This is the central economic argument for pushing beta as high as possible: at a fixed, expensive magnet field, doubling beta quadruples the fusion power you get out of the same machine. A reactor design that can run stably at higher beta extracts dramatically more power per dollar of magnet.

AnalogyThink of the magnetic field as the walls of a pressure vessel you already paid to build. Beta measures how much pressure you're actually putting inside those walls relative to what they could in principle hold. A vessel run at low internal pressure is "wasting" the strength you paid for; running it near its rated limit gets the most output per dollar of steel — exactly the logic that makes high beta operation so valuable, and exactly why there's a real limit on how far you can push it.

7.3The Troyon Limit

Beta cannot be pushed arbitrarily high: beyond a threshold, MHD instabilities (kink and ballooning modes — C3 territory) set in and confinement collapses. The empirical Troyon limit, derived from stability studies across many tokamaks, caps the achievable beta in terms of plasma current, minor radius, and field:

βmax[%] = g · Ip[MA] / (a[m]·B[T])g, the Troyon coefficient, is empirically ≈2.8-3.5 for conventional ("no-wall") tokamak operation; advanced scenarios with stabilizing conducting walls can push g higher.

It is often more convenient to invert this into the normalized beta βN, which packages the current/size/field dependence into the Troyon coefficient itself:

βN = β[%]·a[m]·B[T]/Ip[MA]    Troyon limit: βN ≲ g ≈ 2.8-3.5β_N is a single number you can compare directly across tokamaks of very different size, current, and field.

7.4ITER's Position Relative to the Limit

For ITER-like parameters (Ip=15 MA, a=2.0 m, B=5.3 T), the Troyon limit with g=2.8 gives βmax≈3.96%. ITER's baseline Q=10 scenario targets β around 2.5%, corresponding to βN≈1.77 — comfortably below the Troyon no-wall limit, leaving margin for stability and control. This is a deliberate design choice: operating with margin below the stability boundary trades some fusion power (recall Pfusion∝β²) for a much more robust, controllable plasma.

Common mistakeDon't treat the Troyon limit as a hard engineering ceiling set by magnet strength. It is an MHD stability limit — exceed it and the plasma itself becomes unstable and disrupts, regardless of how strong the magnets are. This is a plasma-physics constraint, distinct from (and usually more restrictive than) any purely engineering limit on achievable field or current.
✦ Worked Example — beta and the Troyon margin for a candidate plasma

A plasma has n=1×10²⁰ m⁻³, T=10 keV, B=5.3 T. The device has I_p=15 MA, a=2.0 m, g=2.8. Find β, the Troyon limit, and the margin.

  1. Pressure: p = 2nT = 2×10²⁰×10 keV×1.602×10⁻¹⁶ J/keV = 3.204×10⁻⁵×10²⁰... compute directly: p = 2×1×10²⁰×1.602×10⁻¹⁵ J = 3.204×10⁵ Pa.
  2. Magnetic pressure: B²/2μ₀ = 5.3²/(2×4π×10⁻⁷) = 28.09/(2.513×10⁻⁶) ≈ 1.118×10⁷ Pa.
  3. Beta: β = p/(B²/2μ₀) = 3.204×10⁵/1.118×10⁷ ≈ 2.87%.
  4. Troyon limit: β_max = 2.8×15/(2.0×5.3) = 42/10.6 ≈ 3.96%.
  5. Margin: 2.87%/3.96% ≈ 0.72 — this plasma runs at about 72% of the Troyon limit, with β_N = 2.87×2.0×5.3/15 ≈ 2.03, comfortably under g≈2.8.

🛠️ Hands-On: beta, Troyon limit, and the margin, in Python

# beta_troyon.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
mu0 = 4*np.pi*1e-7
keV_to_J = 1.602176634e-16

def beta(n, T_keV, B):
    p = 2*n*T_keV*keV_to_J
    return 2*mu0*p/B**2

def troyon_beta_max(g, Ip_MA, a_m, B_T):
    return g*Ip_MA/(a_m*B_T)/100   # returns as a fraction

n, T, B = 1e20, 10.0, 5.3
Ip, a, g = 15.0, 2.0, 2.8

b = beta(n, T, B)
b_max = troyon_beta_max(g, Ip, a, B)
beta_N = b*100*a*B/Ip

print(f"beta        = {b*100:.2f} %")
print(f"Troyon max  = {b_max*100:.2f} %")
print(f"margin      = {b/b_max*100:.1f} % of limit")
print(f"beta_N      = {beta_N:.2f}  (limit g = {g})")

# Verified output:
# beta        = 2.87 %
# Troyon max  = 3.96 %
# margin      = 72.4 % of limit
# beta_N      = 2.03  (limit g = 2.8)

Try raising n until β hits the Troyon limit exactly, then recompute P_fusion (reusing Lecture 3's formula) — you'll see how much fusion power is "left on the table" by operating with stability margin rather than right at the boundary.

Q1. Plasma beta β = 2μ₀p/B² measures…
A. the plasma's electrical conductivity
B. the ratio of kinetic (thermal) pressure to magnetic pressure
C. the confinement time
D. the fusion gain Q
Show answer

B. Beta directly compares plasma pressure p to magnetic pressure B²/2μ₀ — a dimensionless efficiency measure for how the field is being used. (A)/(C)/(D) are all unrelated quantities.

Q2. At fixed temperature and magnetic field, doubling beta changes the fusion power density by roughly…
A. 2×
B. 4×
C. 8×
D. no change
Show answer

B. P_fusion ∝ β² at fixed B and T (Section 7.2, independently verified numerically), so doubling β quadruples P_fusion. (A) ignores the squared dependence. (C) overstates it. (D) ignores the n²-dependence of fusion power entirely.

Q3. The Troyon limit on beta is fundamentally a…
A. magnet-engineering limit on achievable field strength
B. MHD stability limit — exceeding it triggers instabilities (kink/ballooning modes) and disruption
C. limit on how hot the plasma can get
D. a limit on tritium breeding ratio
Show answer

B. The Troyon limit is a plasma-stability boundary, not an engineering ceiling on the magnets themselves — a device can have magnets capable of a higher field while the plasma still disrupts if beta exceeds the stability limit. (A) is a distinct, usually less restrictive constraint. (C)/(D) are unrelated quantities.

Q4. ITER's baseline design targets β_N≈1.8, while the Troyon no-wall limit is around g≈2.8-3.5. This gap means…
A. ITER's magnets are too weak to reach the limit
B. ITER deliberately operates with stability margin below the limit, trading some fusion power for robustness
C. ITER cannot achieve fusion at all at this beta
D. the Troyon limit does not apply to ITER's geometry
Show answer

B. Operating below the Troyon limit is a deliberate design choice for control and reliability, at the cost of the extra fusion power (∝β²) that running closer to the limit would provide. (A) is not implied by the margin — ITER could run at higher β_N and still be below the limit. (C) ITER's Q=10 target is defined at this very β. (D) the Troyon limit is a general tokamak stability result.

Q5. The normalized beta β_N is useful because…
A. it is always equal to 1 for a stable plasma
B. it packages beta's dependence on current, size, and field into one number, letting you compare stability margins across tokamaks of very different scale
C. it replaces the need to measure beta directly
D. it is only defined for D-He3 plasmas
Show answer

B. β_N = β·a·B/I_p absorbs the device-specific scaling so the Troyon limit becomes a single comparable number (g≈2.8-3.5) across machines of very different size and current. (A) β_N varies by device and operating point; it is not fixed at 1. (C) β_N is computed from β, not a substitute for measuring it. (D) β_N applies to any tokamak plasma, any fuel.
