Energy Confinement Time & Scaling Laws

τ_E has been a placeholder since Lecture 4. Time to define it properly — and meet the empirical formulas that let you predict it before a machine is ever built.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define τ_E rigorously via global power balance and describe how it is measured.
  • Explain qualitatively why confinement tends to improve with size, current, and density, and degrade with heating power.
  • Describe how empirical confinement scaling laws are built from multi-device regression.
  • Apply a reduced, illustrative scaling law to estimate τ_E for an ITER-like device.

6.1Defining τ_E Rigorously

Lecture 4 introduced τ_E informally. Formally, the energy confinement time is defined by the global power balance of the plasma, including the rate of change of its stored energy:

dW/dt = Pheat − Ploss     τE ≡ W/Ploss (steady state: Ploss=Pheat)In steady state (dW/dt=0), τ_E is simply the stored energy divided by the total heating power sustaining it — the same quantity used throughout Lectures 4-5.

Experimentally, W is measured from the plasma's diamagnetic response or kinetic profile reconstructions (density and temperature profiles from Thomson scattering, interferometry, and related diagnostics — recall from C1/C2 that these measure the particle and energy content directly), and Pheat is the known injected heating power (plus alpha heating, once relevant) minus any power explicitly radiated before reaching the confined core. Dividing the two gives a measured τ_E for every discharge — the raw data behind every scaling law in this lecture.

6.2Why Confinement Improves with Size and Degrades with Heating

Three qualitative trends recur across every magnetic-confinement device studied to date:

Bigger is better. Heat and particles escape mainly by diffusing across field lines to the wall — a transport process governed by the plasma's surface-to-volume ratio. A larger plasma has proportionally less surface relative to its volume, so, all else equal, τ_E grows with size (empirically, strongly — close to the square of a linear dimension).

More current and field help. Higher plasma current and stronger magnetic field tighten the particle orbits (smaller Larmor radius — recall from C1 that gyroradius shrinks as B grows) and improve MHD stability margins (C3's territory), both of which reduce transport.

More heating power hurts, proportionally. This one is counterintuitive: pumping in more auxiliary heating power to raise the temperature simultaneously drives more turbulent transport, so τ_E empirically degrades with increasing Pheat (a negative power-law exponent). This is why simply cranking up the heaters is not a free way to reach ignition — it fights itself.

Common mistakeDon't assume that adding more heating power always helps reach ignition faster. Because τ_E falls as Pheat rises (empirically, as roughly P−0.7 in H-mode scalings), overdriving the heating can shrink the triple product nTτ_E even as temperature climbs — real devices must balance heating against this confinement-degradation penalty.

6.3The Empirical Scaling-Law Approach

No first-principles theory predicts τ_E to engineering accuracy — turbulent transport in a magnetized plasma is simply too complex (this remains an active research frontier). Instead, the field uses empirical scaling laws: power-law regressions fit to a multi-device database of thousands of discharges across many different tokamaks, of the general form

τE = C · Ipa · nb · Bc · Pheatd · Re · (shape terms)I_p: plasma current; n: density; B: toroidal field; P_heat: total heating power; R: major radius; shape terms include aspect ratio, elongation, isotope mass.

The best-known family is the ITER H-mode scaling, IPB98(y,2), fit across the international multi-tokamak database with exponents of roughly 0.93 (Ip), 0.41 (density), 0.15 (B), −0.69 (Pheat), and 1.97 (R), plus weaker terms for aspect ratio, elongation, and isotope mass. The signs confirm Section 6.2's qualitative story exactly: positive exponents on size/current/field/density, a negative exponent on heating power.

6.4A Reduced Scaling Law for This Course

The full IPB98(y,2)-style regression uses eight fitted parameters and a specific prefactor from the international database. For hand and code calculations in this course, we use a deliberately reduced, 5-parameter version that keeps the same qualitative exponents on current, density, field, heating power, and major radius, but drops the weaker shape/isotope terms and uses an illustrative prefactor calibrated to land near a representative ITER-like discharge — not the literature's exact published fit:

τE,sim = C · Ip[MA]0.93 · n̄e,190.41 · B[T]0.15 · Pheat[MW]−0.69 · R[m]1.97    C ≈ 0.037n̄_e,19 is the line-averaged electron density in units of 10¹⁹ m⁻³. Flagged explicitly as a simplified, illustrative scaling law — not the published IPB98(y,2) regression.

For ITER-like parameters (Ip=15 MA, n̄e,19=10, B=5.3 T, Pheat=50 MW, R=6.2 m), this gives τE≈3.7 s — the right order of magnitude for ITER's actual confinement target, which is the point of calibrating the prefactor this way.

✦ Worked Example — confinement degradation with heating power

Using the reduced scaling law with I_p=15 MA, n̄_e,19=10, B=5.3 T, R=6.2 m, C=0.037, find τ_E at P_heat = 30 MW and at P_heat = 80 MW.

  1. Fixed-parameter product: I_p^0.93 · n^0.41 · B^0.15 · R^1.97 = 15^0.93 × 10^0.41 × 5.3^0.15 × 6.2^1.97 ≈ 12.41 × 2.57 × 1.284 × 36.39 ≈ 1491.
  2. At P_heat=30 MW: τ_E = 0.037 × 1491 × 30^−0.69 ≈ 5.28 s.
  3. At P_heat=80 MW: τ_E = 0.037 × 1491 × 80^−0.69 ≈ 2.68 s.
  4. Interpretation: nearly tripling the heating power (30→80 MW) shrinks τ_E by about half — exactly the confinement-degradation trend from Section 6.2's common mistake. More heating raises T but actively works against the triple product through τ_E.

🛠️ Hands-On: the reduced confinement scaling law in Python

# confinement_scaling.py — Lecture 6
C_SCALE = 0.037

def tau_E_scaling(Ip_MA, n19, B_T, P_MW, R_m):
    return (C_SCALE * Ip_MA**0.93 * n19**0.41 * B_T**0.15
            * P_MW**-0.69 * R_m**1.97)

# ITER-like baseline
Ip, n19, B, R = 15.0, 10.0, 5.3, 6.2
for P in [30, 50, 80, 100]:
    tau = tau_E_scaling(Ip, n19, B, P, R)
    print(f"P_heat={P:5.1f} MW  ->  tau_E = {tau:.2f} s")

# Verified output:
# P_heat= 30.0 MW  ->  tau_E = 5.28 s
# P_heat= 50.0 MW  ->  tau_E = 3.71 s
# P_heat= 80.0 MW  ->  tau_E = 2.68 s
# P_heat=100.0 MW  ->  tau_E = 2.30 s

Try sweeping R instead (holding P fixed) — you should find τ_E grows much faster with size than it degrades with heating power (exponent 1.97 vs −0.69), which is exactly why every generation of tokamak has been built bigger than the last.

Model response

A power-law fit like τ_E ∝ R^1.97 is only guaranteed to hold inside the (I_p, n, B, P_heat, R) ranges of the machines used to fit it — it is a regression, not a derivation from first-principles transport physics. Extrapolating to 2× ITER's radius risks several real failure modes: the H-mode pedestal and edge-localized-mode (ELM) physics that governs the edge transport barrier may not scale the same way at larger size; turbulence can transition between regimes (e.g., ion- vs electron-scale dominated transport) as dimensionless parameters like ρ* (gyroradius/minor-radius) shrink at larger size, changing which physics sets τ_E; divertor heat-flux handling and the Greenwald density limit don't scale as favorably as confinement time, so the "4× τ_E" prediction could be unachievable in practice even if the core-transport scaling held exactly; and aspect ratio, elongation, and current-drive efficiency constraints could force design compromises the fit never saw.

Before committing billions to a 2×-ITER-scale device, you would want: (1) a multi-machine database check — does the fit already span a wide enough range in the dimensionless parameters (ρ*, ν*, β) that the new device isn't a true extrapolation in the physics that matters, even if it looks like one in raw size? (2) cross-validation against independent scaling-law families (empirical IPB98(y,2)-style vs. gyro-Bohm/Bohm theory-based scalings) — if they agree well inside the database but diverge sharply when extrapolated to the new size, that disagreement itself is the warning sign. (3) first-principles gyrokinetic turbulence simulations benchmarked against present devices, then run at the new device's parameters, to catch a regime change the empirical fit can't see. (4) staged construction — an intermediate-size device (or dedicated experimental campaigns on an existing large tokamak run at matched dimensionless parameters) to test the extrapolation's assumptions before the full-scale build.

Self-check rubric

  • Identifies that the scaling law is an empirical regression, valid only inside its fitted database, not a first-principles law
  • Names at least one concrete physical mechanism that could change at larger size (pedestal/ELM physics, turbulence regime change via ρ*, divertor heat-flux/density-limit scaling, or similar)
  • Proposes a concrete verification strategy before full-scale construction (dimensionless-parameter database check, cross-validating multiple scaling laws, first-principles simulation, or staged/intermediate experiments)

🔗 Where this is heading

Confinement scaling laws tell you how long you can hold energy in — but they say nothing about how much pressure the magnetic field can actually contain before the plasma becomes unstable and confinement collapses entirely. Lecture 7 introduces plasma beta, the ratio that answers exactly that question, and the Troyon limit that caps it.

Q1. In steady state, τ_E is defined as…
A. the plasma's total lifetime
B. W/P_loss — stored thermal energy divided by the loss power sustaining the steady state
C. the time for one fusion reaction to occur
D. the inverse of the plasma frequency
Show answer

B. τ_E ≡ W/P_loss, with P_loss=P_heat in steady state — the formal version of the "leaky bucket" picture from Lecture 4. (A)/(C)/(D) are all different timescales entirely.

Q2. Why does τ_E tend to improve with the size of a tokamak?
A. Bigger plasmas have a more favorable surface-to-volume ratio for cross-field transport losses
B. Bigger plasmas have a weaker magnetic field
C. Bigger plasmas automatically run at lower temperature
D. Size has no effect on confinement — only current and field matter
Show answer

A. A larger plasma has proportionally less surface area per unit volume through which energy can leak, improving confinement — the physical basis for the strong positive exponent on R in scaling laws. (B) field strength is independent of size. (C) temperature is set by heating, not size. (D) scaling laws show a strong R-dependence (exponent ≈1.97), among the strongest in the formula.

Q3. The negative exponent on heating power P_heat in confinement scaling laws (τ_E ∝ P_heat^−0.69) means…
A. heating power has no real effect on the plasma
B. heating power and τ_E are unrelated quantities
C. the scaling law is non-physical and should be discarded
D. adding more heating power actively degrades confinement time, partially offsetting the benefit of higher temperature
Show answer

D. More injected power drives more turbulent transport, empirically shrinking τ_E even as it raises temperature — the Section 6.2 "more heating hurts, proportionally" trend, confirmed numerically in the worked example (30→80 MW roughly halves τ_E). (A)/(B) contradict the explicit P_heat dependence. (C) the negative exponent is a robust, well-established empirical feature of H-mode confinement, not an error.

A. it uses entirely different, unrelated exponents

C. it only applies to inertial confinement devices
D. it has no dependence on heating power at all
Show answer

B. The reduced law keeps the same qualitative exponents on I_p, n, B, P_heat, and R as real H-mode scalings, but is explicitly flagged (Section 6.4) as simplified — not a claim to reproduce the literature's exact 8-parameter fit or prefactor. (A) the core exponents are real, IPB98(y,2)-style values. (C) the law describes magnetic (tokamak) confinement, not inertial. (D) P_heat appears explicitly with exponent −0.69.

Q5. For the ITER-like baseline in this lecture, increasing major radius R while holding all other parameters fixed increases τ_E…
A. more strongly than decreasing heating power by the same relative amount, because the R exponent (≈1.97) is much larger in magnitude than the P_heat exponent (≈−0.69)
B. less strongly, because size has a smaller exponent
C. not at all — R does not appear in the scaling law
D. only if current is also increased
Show answer

A. The R exponent (≈1.97) is nearly 3× the magnitude of the P_heat exponent (≈−0.69), so proportional changes in size move τ_E much more than the same proportional change in heating power — consistent with every generation of tokamak being built larger. (B) understates R's outsized exponent. (C) R explicitly appears with exponent 1.97. (D) the scaling law treats each parameter's effect independently in this power-law form.
