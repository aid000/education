Which Reactions Fuse? Binding Energy & Q-Values

Not all fusion reactions are created equal. Before asking how hot or how dense, ask the simplest question: which reaction, and how much energy does it actually give back?

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recall why fusion of light nuclei releases energy, in terms of binding energy per nucleon.
  • Write the D-T, D-D (both branches), and D-He3 fusion reactions and state their Q-values.
  • Compute a reaction's Q-value from atomic mass defects and split it between the two products by momentum conservation.
  • Explain, at the level of "which reaction has the lowest barrier," why D-T is the fuel every near-term reactor chooses — setting up the cross-section story in Lecture 2.

1.1Recap: the Binding-Energy Curve

Plot binding energy per nucleon, B/A, against mass number A and you get one of the most important curves in physics: a steep rise from hydrogen, a broad peak near iron-56 at about 8.8 MeV/nucleon, then a slow decline out to uranium. A nucleus is more tightly bound — lower in energy — the higher it sits on that curve. Both fission (heavy nuclei splitting) and fusion (light nuclei merging) release energy because both move their products up toward the peak; they simply start from opposite ends.

This course picks up exactly where that curve is steepest: the far left edge, where hydrogen's isotopes sit at B/A of only 1-3 MeV/nucleon while helium-4 sits at a comparatively enormous 7.07 MeV/nucleon. That steep climb from hydrogen to helium is why fusing the lightest elements releases far more energy, gram for gram, than fusing or fissioning anything else.

B/A: D = 1.11    T = 2.83    He-3 = 2.57    He-4 = 7.07  MeV/nucleonComputed from atomic mass defects (Section 1.3's method). Helium-4's jump reflects its extra "doubly magic" stability — two protons and two neutrons in a fully closed shell.

1.2The Candidate Reactions: D-T, D-D, D-He3

Four light-nuclei reactions dominate any discussion of terrestrial fusion. Each releases its energy as kinetic energy split between two product particles — momentum conservation forces the lighter product to carry the larger share:

D + T → He-4 (3.54 MeV) + n (14.05 MeV)   Q = 17.59 MeVThe workhorse reaction: lowest Coulomb barrier of the four (Lecture 2), carried by a fast neutron.
D + D → T (1.01 MeV) + p (3.02 MeV)   Q = 4.03 MeVBranch A of the D-D reaction — self-fueling: the tritium produced can go on to fuse with more deuterium.
D + D → He-3 (0.82 MeV) + n (2.45 MeV)   Q = 3.27 MeVBranch B of D-D, occurring at roughly comparable probability to branch A at reactor temperatures — the two branches split close to 50/50.
D + He-3 → He-4 (3.69 MeV) + p (14.66 MeV)   Q = 18.35 MeV"Aneutronic" in the sense that the dominant product is a charged proton, not a neutron — but it needs a much higher Coulomb barrier (Lecture 2) and He-3 is scarce on Earth.

Every one of these numbers above was independently recomputed from atomic mass defects for this lecture (Section 1.3 shows the method) — they are not quoted from memory alone. Notice D-T releases the most energy per reaction of the common options and, critically, deposits most of it (14.05 of 17.59 MeV) in a neutron that escapes the plasma to be captured as heat in a surrounding blanket, while the charged alpha's 3.54 MeV stays behind to reheat the fuel — the seed of the "self-heating" story in Lecture 5.

Common mistakeDon't confuse the reaction's Q-value (energy released per reaction, here in MeV) with the fusion gain Q you will meet in Lecture 5 (a dimensionless ratio of output power to input power). Same letter, two completely different physics quantities — this course uses "Q-value" explicitly whenever the MeV number is meant.

1.3Computing Q from Mass Defect

Every Q-value above comes from the same method: add up the atomic masses going in, subtract the atomic masses coming out, and convert the missing mass to energy via E = mc². Using atomic masses (nucleus + its full complement of electrons) rather than bare nuclear masses is a convenient shortcut — the electron count is identical on both sides of a fusion reaction, so the electron masses cancel and you can work entirely from tabulated atomic-mass values:

Q = (Σmin − Σmout) · 931.494 MeV/uΣm are atomic masses in atomic mass units (u). 931.494 MeV is the energy equivalent of 1 u.

Once you have Q, split it between the two products using nonrelativistic momentum conservation (the products recoil back-to-back from a nearly-at-rest center of mass, since thermal reactant energies of tens of keV are negligible next to Q in MeV):

Elight = Q · mheavy/(mlight+mheavy)   Eheavy = Q · mlight/(mlight+mheavy)The lighter product always carries the larger energy share — it has to move faster to match the heavier product's momentum.
✦ Worked Example — the D-D → He-3 + n branch

Verify Q and the energy split for D + D → He-3 + n. Atomic masses (u): m(D) = 2.0141018, m(He-3) = 3.0160293, m(n) = 1.0086649.

  1. Mass in: 2 × m(D) = 2 × 2.0141018 = 4.0282036 u.
  2. Mass out: m(He-3) + m(n) = 3.0160293 + 1.0086649 = 4.0246942 u.
  3. Mass defect: Δm = 4.0282036 − 4.0246942 = 0.0035094 u.
  4. Q-value: Q = 0.0035094 × 931.494 = 3.27 MeV — matches Section 1.2.
  5. Split: En = Q·m(He-3)/(m(He-3)+m(n)) = 3.27 × 3.0160293/4.0246942 ≈ 2.45 MeV; EHe-3 = 3.27 × 1.0086649/4.0246942 ≈ 0.82 MeV. Both match the quoted values — the lighter neutron carries the larger share, as expected.

🛠️ Hands-On: compute every Q-value in Python

No new installs — this course reuses the Python/NumPy environment from C1–C3. The script below reproduces all four reactions from Section 1.2 from a single table of atomic masses.

# fusion_qvalues.py — Lecture 1
u = 931.494  # MeV per atomic mass unit
m = {"H1": 1.0078250, "D": 2.0141018, "T": 3.0160493,
     "He3": 3.0160293, "He4": 4.0026032, "n": 1.0086649}

def Q(mass_in, mass_out):
    return (sum(mass_in) - sum(mass_out)) * u

def split(Qval, m_light, m_heavy):
    return Qval*m_heavy/(m_light+m_heavy), Qval*m_light/(m_light+m_heavy)

reactions = {
    "D+T  -> He4+n ": ([m["D"], m["T"]], [m["He4"], m["n"]],  m["n"],   m["He4"]),
    "D+D  -> T+p   ": ([m["D"], m["D"]], [m["T"], m["H1"]],   m["H1"],  m["T"]),
    "D+D  -> He3+n ": ([m["D"], m["D"]], [m["He3"], m["n"]],  m["n"],   m["He3"]),
    "D+He3-> He4+p ": ([m["D"], m["He3"]], [m["He4"], m["H1"]], m["H1"], m["He4"]),
}
for label, (mi, mo, m_light, m_heavy) in reactions.items():
    q = Q(mi, mo)
    e_light, e_heavy = split(q, m_light, m_heavy)
    print(f"{label}  Q = {q:5.2f} MeV   split = {e_light:5.2f} / {e_heavy:5.2f} MeV")

# Verified output:
# D+T  -> He4+n    Q = 17.59 MeV   split = 14.05 / 3.54 MeV
# D+D  -> T+p      Q =  4.03 MeV   split =  3.02 / 1.01 MeV
# D+D  -> He3+n    Q =  3.27 MeV   split =  2.45 / 0.82 MeV
# D+He3-> He4+p    Q = 18.35 MeV   split = 14.66 / 3.69 MeV

Every number printed above matches Section 1.2 exactly — this script is how those numbers were produced. Keep it; the capstone (Lecture 10) imports this exact Q-value table.

🔗 Where this is heading

Q-values tell you the payoff of a reaction once it happens — but they say nothing about how often it happens. Two positively charged nuclei have to overcome mutual electrostatic repulsion before the strong force can bind them, and that barrier is different for D-T, D-D, and D-He3 (bigger charge product, bigger barrier). Lecture 2 quantifies that barrier with the Gamow factor and shows numerically why D-T's modest advantage in Q-value becomes an overwhelming advantage in reaction rate at reactor temperatures.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Fusing light nuclei releases energy because the products…
A. have higher binding energy per nucleon than the reactants
B. have more total mass than the reactants
C. contain more nucleons than the reactants
D. are always radioactive
Show answer

A. Moving up the steep left side of the B/A curve toward helium-4 (7.07 MeV/nucleon) means more tightly bound products; the freed binding energy becomes kinetic energy. (B) is backwards — the products are lighter (mass defect). (C) nucleons are conserved. (D) irrelevant to the energy released.

Q2. In D + T → He-4 + n (Q = 17.59 MeV), the neutron carries about 14.05 MeV and the alpha about 3.54 MeV because…
A. the neutron is uncharged, so it gets extra energy
B. momentum conservation splits energy inversely with mass, favoring the lighter product
C. the split is arbitrary and varies reaction to reaction
D. the alpha particle absorbs energy rather than releasing it
Show answer

B. Equal and opposite momenta with unequal masses mean the lighter particle (the neutron, mass ≈1 u) moves faster and thus carries the larger kinetic-energy share than the heavier alpha (mass ≈4 u). (A) charge is irrelevant to the kinematics. (C) the split is fixed by the mass ratio, not arbitrary. (D) the alpha gains energy too, just less of it.

Q3. The two branches of the D-D reaction (→ T+p and → He-3+n) differ from each other in that…
A. one releases energy and the other absorbs it
B. only one of them is physically possible
C. they have different Q-values (4.03 MeV vs 3.27 MeV) and occur at roughly comparable rates
D. they both produce identical products
Show answer

C. Both branches are real, both release energy, with distinct Q-values (4.03 and 3.27 MeV) and roughly 50/50 branching at reactor-relevant temperatures. (A) both release energy. (B) both occur. (D) the products (T+p vs He-3+n) are different.

Q4. To compute a fusion reaction's Q-value from a table of atomic masses (nucleus + electrons), you can ignore the electron masses because…
A. electrons have zero mass
B. atomic masses already exclude electrons
C. electrons are not involved in nuclear reactions at all
D. the same number of electrons appears on both sides, so they cancel in the mass difference
Show answer

D. D, T, He-3, He-4, and H-1 atomic masses each include their full electron complement, and the total electron count balances on both sides of a fusion reaction, so the small electron-mass contributions cancel in Σmin − Σmout. (A) electrons have small but nonzero mass. (C) true that nucleons react, but that's not why the trick works. (B) atomic masses include electrons by definition.

Q5. "Fusion gain Q" (Lecture 5) and a reaction's "Q-value" (this lecture) are…
A. the same quantity, just in different units
B. different physics quantities that happen to share the letter Q — one is energy per reaction in MeV, the other a dimensionless power ratio
C. always numerically equal for D-T
D. both measured in keV·s/m³
Show answer

B. The reaction Q-value (MeV per reaction, e.g. 17.59 MeV for D-T) and the fusion gain Q (output power ÷ input power, dimensionless, defined in Lecture 5) are unrelated quantities that happen to share a letter — this course flags "Q-value" explicitly to avoid the mix-up. (A)/(C)/(D) all conflate the two.
