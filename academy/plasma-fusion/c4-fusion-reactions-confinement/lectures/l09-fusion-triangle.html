9.1Assembling the Lawson Diagram

Every physics tool built in Lectures 1-8 — Q-values, reactivity, power density, the triple product, fusion gain, confinement scaling, beta — ultimately feeds one standard chart: the Lawson diagram, plotting the achieved nτ_E (or equivalently the triple product nTτ_E) against operating temperature T, with the ignition curve from Lecture 4 drawn across it as the target line devices are judged against.

Ignition curve: nτE(T) = 12T/(⟨σv⟩(T)Eα)The same curve plotted in Lecture 4's figure — every device's measured (or projected) operating point is compared against this line, not against a single fixed number, since the threshold itself depends on T.

9.3Why the Triangle Is Hard

Call it the fusion triangle: n, T, and τ_E are not independent dials you can turn up one at a time to reach ignition. Raising density runs into the Troyon beta limit (Lecture 7) at fixed field and temperature. Raising temperature pushes ⟨σv⟩ favorably but also tends to degrade τ_E through the heating-power penalty (Lecture 6). Raising τ_E usually means building a physically larger (and far more expensive) device, or achieving a much more symmetric, harder implosion in the ICF case. Progress has been incremental for decades precisely because every gain on one vertex tends to cost something on another — there is no free vertex to push.

Common mistakeDon't read "ITER's design target is Q=10" as "ITER's target is above the ignition curve" — it isn't, and the point of retuning the number above is to make that clean: at ITER-target's representative (n,T,τ_E), the triple product (≈1.9×10²¹) sits below the 3×10²¹ ignition benchmark, margin ×0.62. ITER's Q=10 target (Lecture 5) corresponds to f_α≈0.665 — strong alpha self-heating and a high fusion gain, but still well short of full ignition (f_α=1, Q=∞, margin≥1×). High Q and clearing the ignition triple-product threshold are related but distinct milestones; a device can have an impressively high Q while still sitting below the ignition curve.
✦ Worked Example — ranking three device classes by ignition margin

Using the three triple-product values from Section 9.2, rank the device classes by margin over each device's own ignition-curve value at its operating temperature — not a single flat number, since the curve dips (Section 9.2's figure).

  1. JET-class: threshold at T=15 keV ≈2.82×10²¹; 7.5×10²⁰ / 2.82×10²¹ ≈ 0.27× (sub-ignition, consistent with Q<1 historically).
  2. ITER-target: threshold at T=13 keV ≈2.79×10²¹; 1.86×10²¹ / 2.79×10²¹ ≈ 0.67× (sub-ignition — matches the Lecture 10 capstone calculation at the retuned τ_E≈1.4 s).
  3. NIF-class ICF: threshold at T=5 keV ≈6.28×10²¹ (far larger than the flat ≈3×10²¹ benchmark, since 5 keV is well below the curve's ≈13-14 keV minimum); 8.2×10²¹ / 6.28×10²¹ ≈ 1.31× (above threshold, the only one of the three that is — but by a much tighter margin than the misleading ×2.74 you'd get from the flat benchmark).
  4. Ranking: NIF-class ICF > ITER-target > JET-class. Note margin over the triple-product threshold is not the same as fusion gain Q (Lecture 5) — ITER-target's margin (0.67×) is higher than JET-class's (0.27×) yet both are sub-ignition, while ITER-target's Q=10 is far higher than JET's real Q≈0.67. (In fact margin-at-the-same-T and f_α are the same number algebraically — both reduce to n·τ_E·⟨σv⟩·E_α/(12T) — so ITER-target's 0.67× margin is exactly its f_α≈0.665, not a coincidence.) A high triple-product margin does not automatically mean a high Q, and vice versa, since Q also depends on how much external heating is actually required versus delivered in a specific device's real operating scheme.

🛠️ Hands-On: building the Lawson-diagram comparison table

# lawson_diagram.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

# T-dependent ignition threshold, not a flat number (Lecture 4)
def sigmav_DT(T_keV):
    BG, mrc2 = 34.3827, 1124656.0
    C1,C2,C3,C4,C5,C6,C7 = 1.17302e-9,1.51361e-2,7.51886e-2,4.60643e-3,1.35e-2,-1.0675e-4,1.366e-5
    T = T_keV
    theta = T/(1 - (T*(C2+T*(C4+T*C6)))/(1+T*(C3+T*(C5+T*C7))))
    xi = (BG**2/(4*theta))**(1/3)
    return C1*theta*np.sqrt(xi/(mrc2*T**3))*np.exp(-3*xi)*1e-6

def ignition_threshold(T_keV, E_ALPHA_KEV=3500.0):
    return 12*T_keV**2/(sigmav_DT(T_keV)*E_ALPHA_KEV)

devices = {
    "JET-class":    dict(n=5e19,    T=15.0, tau=1.0),
    "ITER-target":  dict(n=1e20,    T=13.0, tau=1.43),
    "NIF-class ICF":dict(n=2.41e31, T=5.0,  tau=6.83e-11),
}

results = []
for name, d in devices.items():
    triple = d["n"]*d["T"]*d["tau"]
    threshold = ignition_threshold(d["T"])
    margin = triple/threshold
    results.append((name, triple, threshold, margin))

for name, triple, threshold, margin in sorted(results, key=lambda r: -r[3]):
    verdict = "IGNITION" if margin >= 1 else "sub-ignition"
    print(f"{name:14s}  triple={triple:.2e}  threshold@T={threshold:.2e}  margin=x{margin:.2f}  {verdict}")

# Verified output:
# NIF-class ICF   triple=8.23e+21  threshold@T=6.28e+21  margin=x1.31  IGNITION
# ITER-target     triple=1.86e+21  threshold@T=2.79e+21  margin=x0.67  sub-ignition
# JET-class       triple=7.50e+20  threshold@T=2.82e+21  margin=x0.27  sub-ignition

This exact table structure — a dictionary of device parameters, each scored against its own T-dependent threshold rather than one flat number — is what the capstone (Lecture 10) generalizes into a full standalone calculator.

Model response

The triple product itself is not a fundamental constant of nature in the way, say, the fine-structure constant is — it's a derived bookkeeping number that falls out of setting P_fusion (or P_α) equal to P_loss=3nT/τ_E for a specific reaction (D-T here). What is fundamental underneath it is the reactivity ⟨σv⟩(T), which encodes the real quantum tunneling and nuclear physics from Lectures 2-3. The triple product threshold is simply the multiplicative combination of n, T, τ_E that makes the self-heating power balance work out for that underlying physics — and because the balance is genuinely multiplicative (fusion power ∝ n², loss power ∝ n/τ_E, one n cancels leaving the product nTτ_E), any combination of the three variables that reaches the same product satisfies the same physics, regardless of how wildly different the individual n, T, τ_E values are.

That MCF and ICF can both reach it from opposite ends is really a statement about the structure of the power-balance equation (it only constrains a product, not the individual factors) rather than evidence that the specific "≈3×10²¹ keV·s/m³" number is some deep universal constant. Change the reaction (e.g. D-He3 instead of D-T) and the required triple product changes because ⟨σv⟩(T) and E_α change — so the threshold is reaction-specific, not universal. In that sense the Lawson criterion is "fundamental" in its functional form (a direct, derivable consequence of power balance and reactivity) but the particular numeric value quoted for D-T is incidental to that reaction's specific cross-section physics, not some law of nature fusion itself must obey. The real takeaway: engineering freedom exists precisely because the physics only cares about one combined number, which is exactly why two such differently-engineered paths (bottling a dilute plasma vs. imploding a dense pellet) could both plausibly reach ignition despite sharing almost no engineering details.

  • Notes that the specific numeric threshold (~3×10²¹ keV·s/m³) is reaction-specific (tied to D-T's ⟨σv⟩(T) and E_α), not a universal constant — it would differ for D-D or D-He3
  • Connects the multiplicative structure of the criterion to why it admits multiple engineering solutions (MCF vs ICF) rather than requiring one specific (n, T, τ_E) combination
