The Fusion Triangle: Where Real Devices Sit

Nine lectures of physics collapse onto one map: density, temperature, and confinement time, with the ignition curve cutting across it and every real device plotted relative to that line.

9.1Assembling the Lawson Diagram

Every physics tool built in Lectures 1-8 — Q-values, reactivity, power density, the triple product, fusion gain, confinement scaling, beta — ultimately feeds one standard chart: the Lawson diagram, plotting the achieved nτ_E (or equivalently the triple product nTτ_E) against operating temperature T, with the ignition curve from Lecture 4 drawn across it as the target line devices are judged against.

Ignition curve: nτE(T) = 12T/(⟨σv⟩(T)Eα)The same curve plotted in Lecture 4's figure — every device's measured (or projected) operating point is compared against this line, not against a single fixed number, since the threshold itself depends on T.

9.2Placing Representative Device Classes

Using representative, order-of-magnitude parameters for each device class (not literal shot-by-shot records), the picture looks like this:

JET-class MCF: n~5×10¹⁹ m⁻³, T~15 keV, τ_E~1 s  →  triple ~7.5×10²⁰ keV·s/m³ (below ignition)Consistent with JET's historical best D-T results approaching, but not reaching, scientific breakeven (Q<1) — see Lecture 5.
ITER-target MCF: n~1×10²⁰ m⁻³, T~13 keV, τ_E~3 s  →  triple ~3.9×10²¹ keV·s/m³ (above ignition, Q~10 design target)Matches the capstone's verified calculation in Lecture 4/10 (margin ×1.30 over the 3×10²¹ benchmark).
NIF-class ICF: n~2.4×10³¹ m⁻³, T~5 keV, τ_E~7×10⁻¹¹ s  →  triple ~8.2×10²¹ keV·s/m³ (above ignition)Matches Lecture 8's worked hot-spot estimate (margin ×2.74).

9.3Why the Triangle Is Hard

Call it the fusion triangle: n, T, and τ_E are not independent dials you can turn up one at a time to reach ignition. Raising density runs into the Troyon beta limit (Lecture 7) at fixed field and temperature. Raising temperature pushes ⟨σv⟩ favorably but also tends to degrade τ_E through the heating-power penalty (Lecture 6). Raising τ_E usually means building a physically larger (and far more expensive) device, or achieving a much more symmetric, harder implosion in the ICF case. Progress has been incremental for decades precisely because every gain on one vertex tends to cost something on another — there is no free vertex to push.

Common mistakeDon't read "ITER's target is above the ignition curve" as "ITER will ignite." ITER's Q=10 target (Lecture 5) corresponds to f_α≈0.665 — strong alpha self-heating, well above scientific breakeven, but still short of full ignition (f_α=1, Q=∞). Clearing the Lawson triple-product threshold is necessary for ignition but the device must also actually sustain that state in practice, including real transport, impurities, and control margins this course's reduced models do not capture.
✦ Worked Example — ranking three device classes by ignition margin

Using the three triple-product values from Section 9.2, rank the device classes by margin over the 3×10²¹ keV·s/m³ ignition benchmark.

  1. JET-class: 7.5×10²⁰ / 3×10²¹ ≈ 0.25× (sub-ignition, consistent with Q<1 historically).
  2. ITER-target: 3.9×10²¹ / 3×10²¹ ≈ 1.30× (above threshold — matches the Lecture 4/10 capstone calculation exactly).
  3. NIF-class ICF: 8.2×10²¹ / 3×10²¹ ≈ 2.74× (above threshold, highest margin of the three — matches Lecture 8 exactly).
  4. Ranking: NIF-class ICF > ITER-target > JET-class. Note margin over the triple-product threshold is not the same as fusion gain Q (Lecture 5) — a high triple-product margin does not automatically mean a high Q, since Q also depends on how much external heating is actually required versus delivered in a specific device's real operating scheme.

🛠️ Hands-On: building the Lawson-diagram comparison table

# lawson_diagram.py — Lecture 9
IGNITION_TRIPLE = 3.0e21  # keV s/m^3

devices = {
    "JET-class":    dict(n=5e19,    T=15.0, tau=1.0),
    "ITER-target":  dict(n=1e20,    T=13.0, tau=3.0),
    "NIF-class ICF":dict(n=2.41e31, T=5.0,  tau=6.83e-11),
}

results = []
for name, d in devices.items():
    triple = d["n"]*d["T"]*d["tau"]
    margin = triple/IGNITION_TRIPLE
    results.append((name, triple, margin))

for name, triple, margin in sorted(results, key=lambda r: -r[2]):
    verdict = "IGNITION" if margin >= 1 else "sub-ignition"
    print(f"{name:14s}  triple={triple:.2e} keV s/m^3  margin=x{margin:.2f}  {verdict}")

# Verified output:
# NIF-class ICF   triple=8.22e+21 keV s/m^3  margin=x2.74  IGNITION
# ITER-target     triple=3.90e+21 keV s/m^3  margin=x1.30  IGNITION
# JET-class       triple=7.50e+20 keV s/m^3  margin=x0.25  sub-ignition

This exact table structure — a dictionary of device parameters scored against one threshold — is what the capstone (Lecture 10) generalizes into a full standalone calculator.

💬 Discussion 3 — one criterion, two physically distinct paths

MCF and ICF sit at opposite corners of the n-τ_E plane yet both target the same triple product. Given how differently their limiting physics works (MHD stability and heat exhaust for MCF; implosion symmetry and hydrodynamic instabilities for ICF), what does the existence of two such different viable paths to the same threshold tell you about how fundamental — or how incidental — the specific numbers in the Lawson criterion really are to the underlying physics of fusion?

Q2. Being above the ignition curve on the Lawson diagram means a device's design target…
A. has definitely already achieved commercial fusion power

Q3. The "fusion triangle" (n, T, τ_E) is hard to climb because…
A. the three quantities are completely independent and can each be maximized freely
B. pushing any one vertex tends to cost something on another — e.g. higher n runs into the beta limit, higher T tends to degrade τ_E through the heating-power penalty
C. only temperature actually matters for ignition
D. the triangle has no connection to any of the physics covered earlier in the course
Show answer

B. The vertices are physically coupled through the beta limit, confinement-scaling degradation with heating power, and device size/cost tradeoffs — exactly the mechanisms from Lectures 6-7. (A) ignores those real couplings. (C) all three quantities matter multiplicatively in the triple product. (D) the triangle is a direct synthesis of Lectures 1-8.
