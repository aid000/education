Week 2 · Lecture 2

The Tokamak Concept

Run a current through the plasma itself and it builds its own poloidal field for free — at the price of needing that current in the first place.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the tokamak's three field-producing coil sets: toroidal field coils, the central solenoid, and poloidal field (shaping) coils.
  • Explain how a transformer action drives a toroidal plasma current, and how that current generates the poloidal field needed for confinement.
  • Define the safety factor q(r) and compute it for a simple circular cross-section.
  • Explain, at a conceptual level, why q must stay above roughly 1 for basic stability and why operators target an edge value around 3.
  • Sketch the resulting helical field line and nested flux surfaces that make up the tokamak's basic equilibrium picture.

2.1Three Coil Sets, One Confining Field

Lecture 1 established the requirement: a torus needs both a toroidal field component and a poloidal field component, wound together into a helix. The tokamak (a transliteration of a Russian acronym for "toroidal chamber with magnetic coils") produces these with three distinct magnet systems:

The toroidal field (TF) coils

A ring of coils encircling the torus the short way, each carrying current in the same sense, produces the strong toroidal field B_φ — the same 1/R-falling field from Lecture 1.

The central solenoid

A large coil threading through the hole in the middle of the torus (like the axis of the donut) acts as the primary winding of a transformer. Ramping its current up induces a toroidal electric field around the torus — exactly a transformer's secondary loop — and that electric field drives a toroidal plasma current I_p through the plasma itself, which behaves as the secondary winding.

The poloidal field (PF) coils

A separate set of coils, arranged around the outside of the torus, shapes and positions the plasma (controlling its vertical position and cross-sectional shape) but is not the main source of the poloidal field used for confinement — that job belongs to the plasma current itself.

Common mistakeDon't conflate the poloidal field coils (external shaping/position-control magnets) with the poloidal field that confines the plasma (generated by the plasma current I_p via Ampere's law). They are different things that happen to share the word "poloidal." The confining poloidal field comes from the current flowing inside the plasma, not from the external PF coil set.

2.2The Plasma Current Makes Its Own Poloidal Field

A straight wire carrying current I produces a circulating magnetic field around it — ordinary Ampere's law. The toroidal plasma current I_p is just a (very large, very hot) current loop, so it produces exactly this circulating field, except now the "circulating" direction wraps the short way around the torus: it is the poloidal field.

B_θ(r) = μ₀ I_enc(r) / (2πr)Ampere's law in the simple cylindrical (large-aspect-ratio) approximation: the poloidal field at minor radius r is set by the plasma current enclosed inside r.

Combine this with the toroidal field and every field line now has both components — it winds helically around the torus as it also circles the long way, tracing out a surface (a flux surface). Nested flux surfaces, one inside another like Russian dolls, make up the tokamak's basic magnetic equilibrium: the full force-balance problem (pressure pushing outward, magnetic tension and pressure pushing back) is solved by the Grad-Shafranov equation, which we won't derive here — it's enough to know that it exists and that it is what MHD equilibrium codes solve to design a tokamak's field shape.

2.3The Safety Factor q

How tightly does the field line wind? That's measured by the safety factor q: the number of times a field line goes around the torus the long way (toroidally) for each single trip the short way (poloidally). In the simple large-aspect-ratio, circular cross-section approximation:

q(r) = r B_φ / (R B_θ(r))R is the major radius, r the minor radius of the flux surface, B_φ the toroidal field, B_θ(r) the poloidal field from the enclosed plasma current.

The name comes from stability: low-q surfaces (meaning the field line almost closes on itself after one poloidal turn) are prone to current-driven kink and tearing instabilities. The widely used rule of thumb is that the edge value q_a must stay comfortably above 1 to avoid a gross kink instability, and real devices operate with an edge safety factor (often written q_95, evaluated near the plasma edge) typically around 3 for stable operation. Pushing I_p too high at fixed B_φ and size drives q down and risks a disruption — a sudden, violent loss of the plasma current and stored energy. This single number, more than almost anything else, sets how much current a given tokamak can safely carry.

AnalogyThink of q like the pitch of a screw thread. A very "loose" thread (high q, field line barely twists) behaves gently. A thread wound so tight it's almost a straight groove around the circumference (q near 1) is close to unwinding catastrophically under load — that's the kink instability waiting to happen.
✦ Worked Example — edge safety factor for a representative tokamak

Major radius R = 6 m, minor radius a = 2 m, toroidal field B_φ = 5 T, plasma current I_p = 5.56 MA (assume uniform current density so the current enclosed at r = a is the full I_p). Find q at the edge.

  1. Poloidal field at the edge: B_θ(a) = μ₀I_p/(2πa) = (4π×10⁻⁷)(5.56×10⁶)/(2π×2) ≈ 0.556 T.
  2. Safety factor: q(a) = a B_φ/(R B_θ(a)) = (2×5)/(6×0.556) ≈ 3.00.
  3. Verdict: q(a) ≈ 3 sits right in the typical stable operating range (comfortably above the q ≈ 1 kink threshold). If I_p were doubled at fixed B_φ and size, q would roughly halve to ≈1.5 — getting uncomfortably close to the disruption boundary.

🛠️ Hands-On: a safety-factor calculator

Still no new install — plain Python. This script reproduces the worked example and lets you explore how q responds to I_p, B_φ, and machine size.

# safety_factor.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

mu0 = 4 * np.pi * 1e-7

def edge_q(R, a, B_phi, Ip):
    B_theta = mu0 * Ip / (2 * np.pi * a)   # poloidal field at r=a
    return (a * B_phi) / (R * B_theta)

R, a, B_phi = 6.0, 2.0, 5.0
for Ip_MA in [3.0, 5.56, 8.0, 11.1]:
    q = edge_q(R, a, B_phi, Ip_MA * 1e6)
    flag = "OK (q>2)" if q > 2 else ("marginal" if q > 1 else "DISRUPTION RISK (q<1)")
    print(f"Ip = {Ip_MA:5.2f} MA  ->  q(a) = {q:5.2f}   [{flag}]")
# Ip=5.56 MA reproduces q=3.00; doubling Ip to 11.1 MA roughly halves q to ~1.5

Notice q scales as 1/I_p at fixed B_φ and geometry — this is exactly why raising plasma current to get more fusion power is not free: it trades directly against stability margin.

🔗 Where this is heading

The tokamak gets its poloidal field "for free" from the plasma current — but that current has to be driven somehow, and a transformer (the central solenoid) can only ramp its flux in one direction before it saturates, which fundamentally limits how long a classic tokamak discharge can run inductively. Lecture 4 covers the non-inductive alternatives (neutral beams, RF waves) that let a tokamak sustain its current indefinitely. First, though, Lecture 3 looks at the stellarator's completely different answer to Lecture 1's problem: what if you just don't use a plasma current at all?

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. What generates the confining poloidal field in a tokamak?
A. The external poloidal field (shaping) coils
B. The toroidal field coils
C. The toroidal plasma current, via Ampere's law
D. The central solenoid's static field
Show answer

C. The plasma current I_p circulating toroidally generates a poloidal field around itself, exactly like any current-carrying wire. The PF coils (A) shape/position the plasma but are not the main confining-field source; the TF coils (B) make the toroidal field, not poloidal; the solenoid (D) drives the current rather than directly supplying the confining field.

Q2. The central solenoid drives the plasma current by acting as…
A. a permanent magnet
B. a radio antenna
C. a simple resistor dissipating heat
D. the primary winding of a transformer, with the plasma as the secondary
Show answer

D. Ramping the solenoid's current induces a toroidal electric field (transformer action) that drives I_p in the plasma, which acts as a single-turn secondary circuit.

Q3. In the simple circular-cross-section approximation, the safety factor q(r) is defined as…
A. the ratio of plasma temperature to magnetic field
B. r B_φ / (R B_θ(r)) — toroidal turns per poloidal turn of a field line
C. the plasma density divided by the Debye length
D. the ratio of major to minor radius (aspect ratio)
Show answer

B. q(r) = r B_φ/(R B_θ(r)) counts how many times a field line circles toroidally for each poloidal circuit — a direct measure of the helix's pitch.

Q4. Doubling the plasma current I_p while holding B_φ and the machine's size fixed will…
A. roughly double the edge safety factor q(a)
B. leave q(a) unchanged
C. roughly halve the edge safety factor q(a), pushing it toward the disruption boundary
D. have no effect on stability
Show answer

C. B_θ(a) ∝ I_p, and q(a) ∝ 1/B_θ(a), so q(a) ∝ 1/I_p. Doubling I_p roughly halves q — exactly the trade-off in the worked example (q: 3.00 → ≈1.5).

Q5. Why is a low edge safety factor (q approaching 1) dangerous for a tokamak?
A. It means the plasma is too cold to fuse
B. It increases the risk of kink/tearing instabilities and disruptions
C. It means the toroidal field coils have failed
D. It has no physical significance — q is purely a bookkeeping number
Show answer

B. A field line that nearly closes on itself after one poloidal turn (q near 1) is especially susceptible to current-driven kink and tearing instabilities, which can trigger a disruption — a sudden loss of plasma current and stored energy.

✓ What you learned

  • The tokamak uses three coil systems: toroidal field coils (B_φ), a central solenoid (drives I_p via transformer action), and poloidal field coils (shaping/position).
  • The toroidal plasma current I_p generates the confining poloidal field directly, via Ampere's law — distinct from the external PF coils.
  • The combined field traces helical nested flux surfaces; the full equilibrium is governed by the Grad-Shafranov equation (not derived here).
  • The safety factor q(r) = rB_φ/(RB_θ) measures the helix's pitch; q ≈ 1 risks kink instability, operational designs typically target an edge value around q ≈ 3.
  • Computed q = 3.00 for a representative R=6m, a=2m, B_φ=5T, I_p=5.56 MA tokamak, and saw q ∝ 1/I_p.
