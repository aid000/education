Week 5 · Lecture 5

Divertors & Plasma-Wall Interaction

Every watt you put into the plasma has to come back out somewhere. The divertor is the engineered answer to "where."

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the power-exhaust problem: in steady state, all heating power must leave through the plasma boundary.
  • Define the scrape-off layer (SOL), separatrix, and X-point.
  • Explain the divertor concept — redirecting the SOL onto a dedicated target away from the main chamber wall — and contrast it with a simple limiter.
  • Explain why the divertor also helps remove impurities and helium "ash" from the core plasma.
  • Estimate peak heat flux for a simple divertor geometry and see why flux spreading over a larger wetted area matters.

5.1The Exhaust Problem

Run a tokamak or stellarator in steady state and every watt you put in — ohmic heating, NBI, RF, and (once ignition is approached) the alpha-particle self-heating from Lecture 6 — has to come back out somewhere, since the plasma isn't storing energy indefinitely. That energy leaves the confined plasma mostly as particles and heat crossing the outermost confined flux surface into a thin layer just outside it. Concentrate tens or hundreds of megawatts of exhaust power onto any small, unprepared surface and you melt it. Managing where and how that power lands is one of the central engineering problems a magnetic-confinement reactor has to solve — arguably as hard as the confinement physics itself.

5.2The Scrape-Off Layer and the Separatrix

The outermost closed flux surface that still bounds confined plasma is the separatrix. Just outside it, field lines are no longer closed — they run into a material surface somewhere. The thin region of plasma that straddles this boundary, carrying heat and particles along open field lines toward a wall, is the scrape-off layer (SOL).

The simplest way to handle the SOL is a limiter: a physical surface positioned to intersect the plasma edge directly, defining the outermost boundary by simple contact. Limiters are straightforward but put plasma-wall interaction right at the edge of the main confined volume, where contamination and heat deposition are hardest to control and most likely to cool or pollute the confined core.

5.3The Divertor Concept

The divertor takes a different approach: shape the poloidal field (using extra coils) so that, instead of smoothly closing, field lines near the edge pinch down to a magnetic null point — the X-point — where the poloidal field vanishes. Just past the X-point, the field lines (and the SOL plasma riding along them) are steered away from the main chamber entirely and guided down into a separate structure, the divertor, specifically engineered to absorb the exhaust. Where the SOL field lines finally land is the strike point.

This buys two things at once. First, the exhaust power lands on a dedicated, engineered surface far from the main chamber wall — one that can be designed specifically to survive extreme heat flux (shaped at a shallow angle to spread the load, actively cooled, made of a high-melting-point material). Second, as impurities and the helium "ash" produced by fusion reactions drift into the SOL, they get carried to the divertor and pumped away rather than accumulating in and diluting the confined core — the divertor doubles as the plasma's exhaust-and-cleanup system.

Common mistakeA divertor does not reduce the total exhaust power — it only relocates where that power lands. The engineering challenge (spreading megawatts per square meter over a survivable area) doesn't go away; the divertor just concentrates the problem onto a surface built to handle it, instead of letting it hit the unprepared main wall.
✦ Worked Example — why you need a divertor at all

150 MW crosses the separatrix into the SOL. Compare the peak heat flux if it's concentrated onto a 5 m² divertor "wetted area" versus spread uniformly over an entire first-wall surface of ≈474 m² (torus with R=6m, a=2m).

  1. Onto the divertor: q = P/A = 150×10⁶ W / 5 m² = 30 MW/m².
  2. Spread over the full first wall: q = 150×10⁶ / 474 m² ≈ 0.32 MW/m².
  3. Verdict: even the divertor's engineered, concentrated value (30 MW/m²) is an extreme heat flux by any industrial standard — and it is nearly 100× the unconcentrated first-wall average. This is exactly why real divertor designs spread the strike line further still (shallow incidence angles, active cooling, sometimes partial detachment of the plasma before it reaches the plate) rather than accepting 30 MW/m² outright.

🛠️ Hands-On: heat-flux estimator

No new install. This reproduces the worked example and lets you explore how wetted area and incidence angle change the peak flux.

# divertor_heatflux.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np

def peak_flux(P, A_perp, angle_deg=90.0):
    # A_perp: footprint area perpendicular to field flow; a shallow
    # incidence angle spreads the same power over a larger wetted area
    A_wetted = A_perp / np.sin(np.radians(angle_deg))
    return P / A_wetted

P = 150e6          # W crossing the separatrix
A_perp = 5.0        # m^2, perpendicular footprint

for angle in [90, 30, 10, 3]:
    q = peak_flux(P, A_perp, angle)
    print(f"incidence angle {angle:3d} deg  ->  peak flux {q/1e6:6.2f} MW/m^2")
# 90 deg -> 30.0 MW/m^2; shallower angles spread the same power over
# more wetted area, lowering peak flux -- the real reason divertor
# targets are angled almost edge-on to the incoming field lines

Notice the big lever here isn't changing the physics power flow — it's geometry: a shallower strike angle spreads the same megawatts over a much larger physical surface, which is the single biggest tool divertor designers have.

Q1. Why must a steady-state fusion plasma continuously exhaust power through its boundary?
A. Because the plasma stores all input power indefinitely otherwise
B. Because in steady state, energy in must equal energy out — all heating and alpha power must eventually leave through the boundary
C. Because the magnetic field forces all power out instantly
D. It doesn't need to — power simply disappears
Show answer

B. Energy balance in steady state requires exhaust to match input; the plasma is not an infinite energy sink.

Q2. The separatrix is…
A. the physical wall of the vacuum vessel
B. the outermost closed flux surface bounding the confined plasma
C. the central solenoid
D. the strike point on the divertor plate
Show answer

B. Inside the separatrix, flux surfaces are closed and confine plasma; just outside, field lines open up and run to a material surface, forming the SOL.

Q3. Compared to a simple limiter, a divertor's main advantage is…
A. it eliminates all exhaust power so no target is needed
B. it redirects the scrape-off-layer plasma to a dedicated, engineered target away from the main chamber wall, instead of letting plasma touch an edge surface directly
C. it requires no magnetic field shaping at all
D. it increases the plasma current
Show answer

B. By creating an X-point and steering SOL field lines to a separate structure, the divertor moves plasma-wall contact away from the main chamber to a surface specifically designed to survive it.

Q4. Besides handling heat exhaust, a divertor also helps by…
A. increasing the plasma's electrical resistivity
B. removing impurities and helium "ash" that would otherwise dilute/contaminate the confined core
C. generating the toroidal field
D. replacing the need for a central solenoid
Show answer

B. Impurities and fusion-produced helium drift into the SOL and get carried to the divertor for pumping/removal, rather than accumulating in the core.

Q5. In the worked example, why does a shallower divertor strike angle reduce the peak heat flux for the same total power?
A. It reduces the total power crossing the separatrix
B. It spreads the same power over a larger physical wetted area on the target
C. It increases the plasma temperature
D. Shallower angles have no effect on flux
Show answer

B. q = P/A_wetted, and A_wetted = A_perp/sin(angle) grows as the incidence angle shrinks — same power, bigger footprint, lower peak flux. This is exactly what the hands-on script demonstrates.

✓ What you learned

  • In steady state, all input/alpha heating power must exit through the plasma boundary — this is the power-exhaust problem.
  • The separatrix bounds the confined plasma; the scrape-off layer (SOL) carries exhaust along open field lines beyond it.
  • A divertor uses an X-point to steer the SOL onto a dedicated target (the strike point), away from the main chamber wall — unlike a simple limiter.
  • The divertor also removes impurities and helium ash from the core, doubling as the exhaust-and-cleanup system.
  • Computed peak heat flux (30 MW/m² concentrated vs ≈0.32 MW/m² spread) and saw how a shallow strike angle lowers peak flux by spreading the wetted area.
