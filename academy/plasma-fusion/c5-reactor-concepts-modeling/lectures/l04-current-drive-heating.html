Current Drive & Heating Methods

Ohmic heating runs out of steam exactly when you need it most. Everything else in this lecture exists to pick up where it fails.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why ohmic heating alone cannot reach fusion-relevant temperatures, using the falling Spitzer resistivity.
  • Describe neutral beam injection (NBI): why beams must be neutral to cross the field, and how they heat and drive current once ionized.
  • Describe RF heating (ion-cyclotron and electron-cyclotron resonance heating) and the resonance condition it relies on.
  • Compute an ion or electron cyclotron frequency for a given field, and explain why that sets the required RF source frequency.
  • Explain why non-inductive current drive matters for sustained (non-pulsed) tokamak operation.

4.1Ohmic Heating Hits a Wall

The simplest way to heat a tokamak plasma is also the one it gets automatically: the plasma current I_p flowing against the plasma's own electrical resistance dissipates power, just like a toaster coil — this is ohmic heating, P_ohmic = I_p² R_plasma. It needs no extra hardware and is how early tokamaks heated their plasmas.

The catch is one you already met as a C1 discussion question: plasma resistivity (the Spitzer resistivity) falls with temperature, roughly as T^(−3/2). As ohmic heating raises T, the plasma becomes a better conductor, so the same current dissipates less power — ohmic heating is strongly self-limiting and simply cannot push a reactor-scale plasma up to the ten-keV-class temperatures fusion needs. Something else has to do the rest of the work.

Common mistakeDon't assume more current fixes this. Raising I_p to compensate for falling resistivity runs straight into Lecture 2's safety-factor limit — more current drives q down toward the disruption boundary. Ohmic heating's ceiling is a genuine physics wall, not just an engineering inconvenience to push past with more current.

4.2Neutral Beam Injection (NBI)

To add heat (and, usefully, momentum) directly to a magnetized plasma, you'd like to fire a beam of fast particles into it. But any charged beam gets bent by the confining field before it ever reaches the core. The trick: accelerate ions to high energy, then pass them through a neutralizer cell that captures an electron onto each ion, converting them to fast neutral atoms just before injection. Neutral atoms sail straight through the magnetic field — undeflected — and only become charged again once they collide and ionize deep inside the plasma, depositing their energy (and momentum) right where it's wanted via collisions with the background plasma. Because NBI deposits net momentum along a particular direction, it can also drive a net toroidal current non-inductively — not just heat.

4.3RF Heating: Resonant Absorption

The second major heating family launches electromagnetic waves into the plasma at a frequency tuned to resonate with a natural particle motion, so the wave's energy is absorbed efficiently at a specific, controllable location. Two common targets:

Ion Cyclotron Resonance Heating (ICRH)

Tuned to the ion cyclotron frequency ω_ci = qB/m_i — the rate at which an ion gyrates around the field. A wave at this frequency resonantly accelerates the ion's gyration, heating it directly.

Electron Cyclotron Resonance Heating (ECRH)

Same idea, tuned instead to the much faster electron cyclotron frequency ω_ce = qB/m_e (roughly 3,670× higher than ω_ci for the same B — ≈2×1836 for a deuteron, since m_e « m_i). ECRH's shorter wavelength lets it be launched and focused with high directional precision, including for highly localized, non-inductive current drive at a chosen location in the plasma.

f_c = qB / (2πm)Cyclotron frequency for a particle of charge q, mass m, in field B. Same formula for ions (ICRH) and electrons (ECRH); only the mass differs.

Because the resonance condition pins the frequency to the local field strength, and B varies with major radius (Lecture 1's 1/R falloff), tuning the source frequency lets operators choose where in the plasma the wave deposits its energy — a genuine advantage over NBI, whose deposition profile is harder to localize as precisely.

✦ Worked Example — cyclotron frequencies at B = 5 T

Find the deuteron cyclotron frequency (for ICRH) and the electron cyclotron frequency (for ECRH) at B = 5 T, and compare.

  1. Deuteron (ICRH): f_ci = qB/(2πm_d) = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹×5)/(2π×3.344×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 38.1 MHz — radio-frequency range, consistent with real ICRH systems (tens of MHz).
  2. Electron (ECRH): f_ce = qB/(2πm_e) = (1.602×10⁻¹⁹×5)/(2π×9.109×10⁻³¹) ≈ 140.0 GHz — microwave/millimeter-wave range, consistent with real ECRH gyrotron sources.
  3. Ratio: f_ce/f_ci = m_d/m_e ≈ 3,670 — the electron is thousands of times lighter than the deuteron, so it gyrates thousands of times faster at the same field, demanding a correspondingly higher-frequency (and more technologically demanding) source for ECRH.

🛠️ Hands-On: cyclotron-frequency calculator

No new install. This reproduces the worked example and lets you check what source frequency a given B field demands.

# cyclotron_freq.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np

q = 1.602176634e-19
m_e = 9.1093837015e-31
m_d = 2.014102 * 1.66053906660e-27   # deuteron

def f_cyclotron(B, m):
    return q * B / (2 * np.pi * m)

B = 5.0
f_ci = f_cyclotron(B, m_d)
f_ce = f_cyclotron(B, m_e)
print(f"ICRH (deuteron) frequency : {f_ci/1e6:7.2f} MHz")
print(f"ECRH (electron)  frequency: {f_ce/1e9:7.2f} GHz")
print(f"ratio f_ce/f_ci            : {f_ce/f_ci:8.1f}  (~= m_d/m_e)")
# ICRH ~38.1 MHz, ECRH ~140.0 GHz, ratio ~3670 -- matches m_d/m_e

Q1. Why can't ohmic heating alone reach fusion-relevant plasma temperatures?
A. The plasma current cannot be increased at all
B. Plasma resistivity falls as temperature rises, so ohmic heating becomes self-limiting
C. Ohmic heating only works in stellarators
D. Ohmic heating cools the plasma instead of heating it
Show answer

B. Spitzer resistivity falls roughly as T^(−3/2), so as the plasma heats up, the same current dissipates less power — a built-in ceiling, not a fixable engineering limit.

Q2. Why must neutral beam injection use neutral atoms rather than charged ions for the beam itself?
A. Neutral atoms travel faster than ions
B. Neutral atoms are easier to accelerate from rest
C. A charged beam would be deflected by the confining magnetic field before reaching the plasma core
D. Ions cannot carry energy
Show answer

C. A charged particle entering the confining field would gyrate/deflect immediately; only a neutral particle crosses the field in a straight line, ionizing (and depositing its energy) once it collides inside the plasma.

Q3. ICRH and ECRH both work by…
A. cooling the plasma edge to reduce losses
B. increasing the plasma current directly
C. injecting cold neutral gas
D. launching waves tuned to resonate with a particle's cyclotron gyration frequency, so energy is absorbed efficiently
Show answer

D. Both rely on the resonance condition ω = qB/m for the relevant species (ion for ICRH, electron for ECRH) to deposit wave energy efficiently at a chosen location.

Q4. At the same magnetic field strength, why is the electron cyclotron frequency so much higher than the ion cyclotron frequency?
A. Electrons carry more charge than ions
B. f_c = qB/(2πm), and the electron mass is thousands of times smaller than an ion's, so 1/m is thousands of times larger
C. Electrons experience a different magnetic field than ions
D. It isn't — they're actually equal
Show answer

B. Both species have the same charge magnitude, so the entire difference comes from mass: m_d/m_e ≈ 3,670, giving f_ce/f_ci the same ratio (verified in the worked example: ≈140 GHz vs ≈38 MHz).

Q5. Why does a tokamak need non-inductive current-drive methods (like NBI or RF) in addition to the central solenoid?
A. The solenoid can only ramp its flux so far before it's exhausted, fundamentally limiting inductive-only pulse length
B. The solenoid cannot produce any current at all
C. Non-inductive methods are required by law
D. Stellarators require it, and tokamaks copy the requirement unnecessarily
Show answer

A. Transformer (inductive) drive is intrinsically pulsed — the solenoid's flux swing is finite. Sustaining current beyond that swing (toward steady-state operation) requires non-inductive drive from NBI or RF systems, which can also heat simultaneously.

✓ What you learned

  • Ohmic heating is self-limiting because plasma resistivity falls with temperature — it cannot reach fusion-relevant temperatures alone.
  • Neutral beam injection crosses the confining field as neutral atoms, then ionizes and deposits energy/momentum inside the plasma — and can drive current.
  • ICRH and ECRH resonantly heat ions or electrons at their respective cyclotron frequencies ω_c = qB/m, with ECRH offering highly localized, tunable deposition.
  • Computed representative cyclotron frequencies (≈38 MHz ICRH, ≈140 GHz ECRH at B=5T) and confirmed their ratio matches m_d/m_e.
  • Non-inductive current drive (NBI, RF) is what lets a tokamak move beyond the solenoid's pulsed limit toward sustained operation.
