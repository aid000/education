Week 1 · Lecture 1

Why Toroidal? Magnetic Confinement Concepts

A magnetic bottle with open ends always leaks. Bend the field lines into a closed loop and the ends disappear — but a new problem shows up immediately.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a linear magnetic mirror always has end losses, and why closing the field lines into a torus removes them.
  • Show that a purely toroidal field is not uniform — it is stronger on the inboard side — and explain the curvature and grad-B drifts this produces.
  • Explain why those single-particle drifts cause ions and electrons to separate vertically, building an electric field that would expel the whole plasma on a short timescale.
  • State why the cure requires adding a poloidal field component, so the total field line winds around the torus — the rotational-transform idea that both the tokamak and the stellarator are built to deliver.
  • Estimate the uncorrected drift-loss timescale for a representative plasma and compare it to a workable confinement time.

1.1The Trouble With Open Ends

C1 introduced the magnetic mirror: a straight field line that pinches down at both ends, reflecting particles whose pitch angle puts them outside the loss cone. It works, but imperfectly — every particle born inside the loss cone streams straight out one end, and the mirror leaks continuously. No amount of clever coil shaping removes this: a linear device, by definition, has two ends, and both are holes in the bottle.

The fix is almost embarrassingly direct: get rid of the ends. Bend the straight field line around until it joins itself — a torus (a donut shape) instead of a cylinder. A particle gyrating around a field line that closes on itself never reaches an "end," because there isn't one. This single topological move is the starting point for every magnetic-confinement fusion concept covered in this course.

AnalogyA race car on a straight drag strip eventually runs out of track. Bend that same strip into a closed oval and the car can circle forever without ever reaching an edge. Toroidal confinement is the plasma-physics version of closing the track into a loop.

1.2But a Simple Torus Isn't Uniform

Wrap a set of circular coils around a donut-shaped tube and you get a purely toroidal field: field lines that run the long way around the torus, parallel to each other, with no twist. There's an immediate catch. Each coil's field at a point falls off like 1/R from the torus's central axis, so the field is stronger on the inboard side (small major radius) and weaker on the outboard side (large major radius):

B_φ(R) ∝ 1/RThe toroidal field strength falls off with major radius R — stronger near the machine's central column, weaker toward the outer wall.

A field that varies in strength and is curved is exactly the setup that produces the single-particle drifts from C1: the grad-B drift (from the field-strength gradient) and the curvature drift (from the curved field lines). In a torus the two drifts point the same direction and add together, and — critically — that direction is vertical, straight up for one sign of charge and straight down for the other.

v_D ∼ W / (q B R)Order-of-magnitude combined curvature + grad-B drift speed for a particle of kinetic energy W, charge q, in toroidal field B at major radius R.

1.3Charge Separation and the Short-Circuit You Need

Because the drift direction depends on the sign of the charge, ions drift vertically one way and electrons drift the other way. In a purely toroidal field, with no way for charge to move along field lines to cancel this, the plasma rapidly builds up a vertical electric field as positive charge piles up on one side and negative on the other. That field then drives an E×B drift — and unlike the curvature/grad-B drift, E×B drift is charge-independent, so it pushes both species radially outward together, straight into the outer wall. A purely toroidal field is therefore not just imperfect — it is unconfining almost immediately.

The cure is to give field lines a poloidal component — a twist the short way around the torus — so that as a particle circles the long way (toroidally) it also migrates the short way (poloidally), spending equal time on the top and the bottom of the cross-section. Averaged over a poloidal circuit, the vertical drift cancels: up on one half, down on the other. A field line with both components traces a helix on a nested set of surfaces (flux surfaces), and the amount of twist per toroidal circuit is called the rotational transform. Lectures 2 and 3 are two different engineering answers to the same question: how do you add that poloidal component? The tokamak drives a plasma current to generate it; the stellarator shapes external coils to generate it without any plasma current at all.

✦ Worked Example — how fast does an uncorrected torus leak?

Take a deuteron at T = 10 keV in a toroidal field B = 5 T at major radius R = 6 m, minor radius a = 2 m. Estimate the vertical drift speed and how long it takes to drift the width of the plasma if nothing corrects it.

  1. Thermal energy: W ≈ T = 10 keV = 1.0×10⁴ eV × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ J/eV = 1.602×10⁻¹⁵ J.
  2. Drift speed: v_D ∼ W/(qBR) = (1.602×10⁻¹⁵)/(1.602×10⁻¹⁹×5×6) ≈ 333 m/s.
  3. Crossing time: t ≈ a / v_D = 2 m / 333 m/s ≈ 6 ms.
  4. Verdict: a workable fusion plasma needs an energy confinement time of order a second or more. Six milliseconds is roughly 100–1000× too fast — a purely toroidal field loses the plasma almost as soon as it forms. Adding the poloidal field component is not an optional refinement; it is the difference between a confinement concept and a leak.

🛠️ Hands-On: order-of-magnitude drift-loss estimator

No new install this week — reuse the Python/NumPy stack from C1–C4. This short script reproduces the worked example and lets you sweep B, R, and T.

# toroidal_drift.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

e = 1.602176634e-19   # C

def vertical_drift(T_keV, B, R):
    W = T_keV * 1e3 * e               # characteristic kinetic energy, J
    return W / (e * B * R)             # v_D ~ W / (q B R), m/s

B, R, a, T_keV = 5.0, 6.0, 2.0, 10.0
v_D = vertical_drift(T_keV, B, R)
t_loss = a / v_D
print(f"v_D    = {v_D:8.1f} m/s")
print(f"t_loss = {t_loss*1e3:8.3f} ms   (across minor radius a={a} m)")
# v_D ~ 333 m/s, t_loss ~ 6 ms -- far too fast without a poloidal field

Try raising B to 10 T, or R to 10 m. Both lengthen t_loss (higher field or larger machine slows the drift) — but neither comes close to turning a leaky torus into a confining one on its own. That's the job of the poloidal field, next lecture.

🔗 Where this is heading

Every magnetic-confinement fusion concept is, at bottom, an answer to "how do I give the field a poloidal component?" Lecture 2 covers the tokamak's answer — drive a toroidal plasma current, which generates its own poloidal field — and introduces the safety factor q that measures how tightly the resulting helix winds. Lecture 3 covers the stellarator's answer: shape the external coils in three dimensions so no plasma current is needed at all. By the end of Week 3 you'll be able to compare both concepts' trade-offs directly.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Closing a linear magnetic mirror into a torus primarily solves which problem?
A. It increases the plasma density
B. It removes the open ends that particles inside the loss cone stream out of
C. It eliminates the need for any magnetic field at all
D. It cools the plasma to fusion-relevant temperatures
Show answer

B. A torus has no ends, so there is no loss-cone leak the way a linear mirror has. It does not by itself address density, temperature, or field strength.

Q2. In a purely toroidal field, why is the field stronger on the inboard side than the outboard side?
A. The field from the toroidal coils falls off as 1/R with major radius
B. The plasma itself blocks field on the outboard side
C. Gravity pulls field lines toward the floor
D. The field is actually uniform; this is a common misconception
Show answer

A. B_φ ∝ 1/R is a direct consequence of Ampere's law for toroidal field coils — closer to the central column (smaller R) the field is stronger. It is a real, unavoidable feature of toroidal geometry, not a misconception.

Q3. In a purely toroidal field, the combined curvature + grad-B drift causes…
A. ions and electrons to drift vertically in opposite directions, separating charge
B. all particles to drift in exactly the same direction regardless of charge
C. no drift at all, since the field magnitude is constant
D. particles to drift purely toroidally, with no vertical component
Show answer

A. The curvature/grad-B drift direction depends on the sign of the charge, so ions and electrons separate vertically — exactly like a magnetic mirror's grad-B drift, but now unavoidable because of the 1/R toroidal field variation.

Q4. Once vertical charge separation builds an electric field, the resulting E×B drift…
A. cancels the original charge separation automatically
B. pushes ions and electrons radially outward together, regardless of charge sign
C. only affects electrons
D. only affects ions
Show answer

B. E×B drift velocity is (E×B)/B², with no charge dependence — so once the vertical E field forms, it drives the whole plasma, ions and electrons together, radially outward into the wall.

Q5. The fix for the vertical-drift/charge-separation problem is to…
A. remove the toroidal field entirely
B. add a poloidal field component so field lines trace a helix, averaging the vertical drift over top and bottom
C. increase the plasma density only
D. cool the plasma so drifts stop
Show answer

B. A poloidal field component makes field lines spiral around the cross-section (rotational transform), so a particle samples both the top and bottom of the plasma as it circulates — the vertical drift averages to roughly zero over a poloidal circuit. This is exactly what the tokamak (via plasma current) and stellarator (via 3-D coils) each supply.

✓ What you learned

  • A torus removes the open-end losses of a linear magnetic mirror by closing the field line on itself.
  • A purely toroidal field is not uniform (B_φ ∝ 1/R), which drives curvature and grad-B drifts that separate ions from electrons vertically.
  • The resulting vertical electric field then drives an E×B drift that pushes the whole plasma radially outward — a purely toroidal field cannot confine on its own.
  • The fix is a poloidal field component giving field lines a helical twist (rotational transform), so the vertical drift averages out over a poloidal circuit.
  • Estimated the uncorrected loss timescale (∼6 ms for a representative plasma) against the ∼1 s+ needed for real confinement — a factor of 100–1000 shortfall that motivates everything in Lectures 2–3.
