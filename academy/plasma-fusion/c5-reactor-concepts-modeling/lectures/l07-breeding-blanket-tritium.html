The Breeding Blanket & Tritium Breeding

A D-T fusion plant has to manufacture its own fuel, in real time, from lithium — because tritium essentially doesn't exist in nature.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why tritium is not available in useful natural quantities and must be bred on-site.
  • Write and verify the Q-values of the two lithium breeding reactions, Li-6(n,α)T and Li-7(n,n'α)T.
  • Define the tritium breeding ratio (TBR) and explain why a real plant targets TBR modestly above 1.
  • Describe, conceptually, the two broad blanket design families: solid breeder and liquid breeder.
  • Compute a plant's tritium consumption and breeding surplus rate from its fusion power and TBR.

7.1Tritium Doesn't Grow on Trees

Deuterium is abundant and easily extracted from seawater. Tritium, the other half of the D-T fuel, is a different story: it's radioactive, with a half-life of about 12.3 years, and only trace amounts exist naturally (produced occasionally by cosmic-ray interactions in the upper atmosphere). There is no mine, well, or ocean reservoir of tritium a power plant could draw from. A D-T fusion plant therefore has to manufacture its own tritium, continuously, on-site — and it has to produce at least as much as it consumes, or it simply runs out of fuel.

Common mistakeDon't assume a fusion plant is "given" its tritium supply the way a fission plant is given mined/enriched uranium. Tritium must be bred during operation from lithium — this is a hard design constraint baked into the blanket, not a logistics afterthought.

7.2Breeding Tritium From Lithium

The 14.1 MeV neutron that escapes every D-T reaction (Lecture 6) is the tool: fire it into lithium, and it can produce exactly the tritium the plant needs. Natural lithium is a mix of two isotopes, Li-6 (≈7.5%) and Li-7 (≈92.5%), and each reacts differently:

Li-6 + n → He-4 + T    Q = +4.79 MeVExothermic. Dominant breeding reaction; its cross-section is largest for slow (thermalized) neutrons.
Li-7 + n → He-4 + T + n    Q = −2.47 MeVEndothermic threshold reaction. Needs a fast neutron (kinetic energy above ~2.47 MeV) — exactly what the 14.1 MeV fusion neutron supplies — and it regenerates a neutron that can go on to breed again or be captured elsewhere.

Blanket designers can enrich the lithium in Li-6 beyond its natural 7.5% abundance to favor the energetically "free" exothermic reaction and raise the overall breeding rate, at an engineering and cost trade-off. Many blanket concepts also add a neutron multiplier — beryllium or lead, which undergo (n,2n)-type reactions that produce an extra neutron per incoming fast neutron — specifically to boost the neutron population available for Li-7 breeding and offset losses elsewhere in the blanket structure.

7.3The Tritium Breeding Ratio (TBR)

The key design metric is the tritium breeding ratio: the number of new tritium atoms produced per tritium atom consumed in fusion reactions.

TBR = (tritium atoms bred) / (tritium atoms consumed)TBR must exceed 1 for the plant to be self-sufficient in its own fuel.

A bare TBR of exactly 1.00 sounds sufficient but isn't, in practice: tritium decays (12.3-year half-life, so some fraction is always lost to decay before use), some permeates into structural materials and is never recovered, and a new plant needs to build up a starting inventory. Real blanket designs therefore target a breeding margin — a TBR modestly above 1, commonly discussed in the range of roughly 1.05–1.15 depending on the specific design's loss mechanisms — to guarantee tritium self-sufficiency with margin to spare.

7.4Two Blanket Design Families (Conceptual)

Solid breeder

A lithium-containing ceramic (such as a lithium silicate or lithium titanate, in pebble or pellet form) is packed into the blanket alongside a separate neutron multiplier (beryllium or a beryllium compound) and a separate coolant (helium gas or water) that carries heat away. Tritium produced in the ceramic diffuses out and is continuously extracted by a purge gas flow.

Liquid breeder

A liquid metal containing lithium — either molten lithium itself or a lithium-lead eutectic (often written Pb-Li) — serves simultaneously as the breeding material, the neutron multiplier (lead itself contributes some multiplication), and the coolant that removes heat, all in one flowing medium. This combines several functions but introduces its own engineering challenges (handling a flowing liquid metal at high temperature, extracting dissolved tritium from it continuously).

Both families remain active areas of ongoing fusion blanket research; this course describes them conceptually rather than endorsing either as "the" solution — that choice is still an open engineering question across the field.

✦ Worked Example — Q-values and a plant's tritium mass balance

Verify the Li-6 and Li-7 reaction Q-values from atomic masses, then find the tritium consumption and breeding surplus rate for an illustrative plant with P_fusion = 2500 MW and TBR = 1.05. Use m(Li-6)=6.015123 u, m(Li-7)=7.016004 u, m(n)=1.008665 u, m(He-4)=4.002602 u, m(T)=3.016049 u, 1 u = 931.494 MeV/c².

  1. Li-6 + n → He-4 + T: Δm = (6.015123+1.008665) − (4.002602+3.016049) = 0.005137 u → Q = 0.005137×931.494 ≈ +4.79 MeV ✓.
  2. Li-7 + n → He-4 + T + n: Δm = 7.016004 − (4.002602+3.016049) = −0.002647 u → Q = −0.002647×931.494 ≈ −2.47 MeV ✓ (endothermic, as expected for a threshold reaction).
  3. Reaction rate: each D-T fusion consumes exactly one tritium atom. Reactions/s = P_fusion/(Q_total in joules) = 2500×10⁶ / (17.6×1.602×10⁻¹³) ≈ 8.87×10²⁰ reactions/s.
  4. Consumption rate: 8.87×10²⁰ atoms/s × mass of T (3.016049 u = 5.009×10⁻²⁷ kg) × 86,400 s/day ≈ 383.6 g/day of tritium consumed.
  5. With TBR = 1.05: breeding produces 1.05× what's consumed, so net surplus = 0.05 × 383.6 ≈ 19.2 g/day — the margin that builds up startup inventory for the next plant and covers losses.

🛠️ Hands-On: tritium mass-balance calculator

No new install. This reproduces the worked example and feeds directly into the Lecture 10 capstone's tritium module.

# tritium_balance.py — Lecture 7
MEV_TO_J = 1.602176634e-13
U_TO_KG = 1.66053906660e-27

Q_TOTAL_MEV = 17.6
m_T_u = 3.016049

def tritium_rates(P_fusion_W, TBR):
    reactions_per_s = P_fusion_W / (Q_TOTAL_MEV * MEV_TO_J)
    m_T_kg = m_T_u * U_TO_KG
    consumed_kg_per_day = reactions_per_s * m_T_kg * 86400
    surplus_kg_per_day = (TBR - 1) * consumed_kg_per_day
    return reactions_per_s, consumed_kg_per_day, surplus_kg_per_day

P_fusion = 2500e6
TBR = 1.05
rxn_s, consumed, surplus = tritium_rates(P_fusion, TBR)
print(f"reactions/s     : {rxn_s:.3e}")
print(f"T consumed      : {consumed*1000:7.2f} g/day")
print(f"T net surplus   : {surplus*1000:7.2f} g/day  (TBR={TBR})")
# reactions/s ~ 8.87e20; consumed ~ 383.6 g/day; surplus ~ 19.2 g/day

Try TBR = 1.00 (zero surplus — exactly breakeven, no margin for decay/losses) and TBR = 0.95 (negative surplus — the plant would be running down its tritium inventory and could not sustain operation indefinitely).

🔗 Where this is heading

Breeding tritium is only half of what the blanket does with the neutron's energy — it also captures essentially all 14.1 MeV as useful heat (plus the extra energy released by the exothermic Li-6 reaction itself, a small energy-multiplication bonus). But absorbing that much high-energy neutron flux, continuously, for years, does serious damage to the materials doing the absorbing. Lecture 8 covers exactly that materials challenge.

Q1. Why must a D-T fusion plant breed its own tritium rather than simply mining or purchasing a stockpile?
A. Tritium is too cheap to be worth mining
B. Tritium is radioactive with a ~12.3-year half-life and exists in nature only in trace amounts, so there is no usable natural reservoir
C. Tritium is identical to deuterium and already abundant in seawater
D. It is illegal to mine tritium
Show answer

B. Tritium's radioactivity and short half-life (relative to a plant's lifetime) mean any natural reservoir decays away; it must be manufactured continuously from lithium instead.

Q2. Which lithium reaction is exothermic and works best with slow (thermalized) neutrons?
A. Li-7 + n → He-4 + T + n
B. Li-6 + n → He-4 + T
C. Both are equally exothermic
D. Neither reaction releases energy
Show answer

B. Li-6 + n → He-4 + T releases +4.79 MeV and has its largest cross-section for slow neutrons, making it the dominant breeding reaction. Li-7's reaction (A) is endothermic and needs a fast (~14.1 MeV) neutron.

Q3. The tritium breeding ratio (TBR) is defined as…
A. tritium atoms consumed divided by tritium atoms produced
B. tritium atoms produced divided by tritium atoms consumed
C. the fraction of lithium that is Li-6
D. the neutron multiplication factor only
Show answer

B. TBR = bred / consumed; it must exceed 1 for self-sufficiency, and real designs target a modest margin above 1 (commonly discussed as roughly 1.05–1.15) to cover decay and losses.

Q4. Why do real blanket designs target TBR modestly above 1.00 rather than exactly 1.00?
A. Because TBR values below 2.00 are physically impossible
B. To cover tritium losses to radioactive decay, permeation into structures, and to build up startup inventory for future plants
C. Because exactly 1.00 would produce too much tritium
D. TBR has no relationship to self-sufficiency
Show answer

B. A bare TBR=1 leaves no margin for the real-world losses (decay, permeation) and no surplus for seeding new plants, so designs target a breeding margin above 1.

Q5. In a liquid-breeder blanket concept (e.g., a lithium-lead eutectic), the liquid metal typically serves as…
A. only a coolant, with breeding handled entirely by a separate solid ceramic
B. breeding material, neutron multiplier, and coolant simultaneously
C. purely a structural material with no neutron interactions
D. a replacement for the toroidal field coils
Show answer

B. A flowing liquid-metal breeder (Li or Pb-Li) can combine breeding, multiplication (lead contributes some), and heat removal in one medium — the main appeal of the liquid-breeder concept, at the cost of liquid-metal handling challenges.
