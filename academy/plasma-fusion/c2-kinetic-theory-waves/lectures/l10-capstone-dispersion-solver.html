10.1The Capstone Question

Nine lectures built two parallel toolkits for the same underlying physics. The fluid shortcut (Lectures 4–7) took velocity moments of the Vlasov equation with a pressure closure — fast, intuitive, and it correctly reproduces real parts of dispersion relations like Bohm-Gross, but it structurally cannot see resonant particles, so it has no way to produce damping or growth. The full kinetic treatment (Lectures 3, 8–9) kept the resonance pole at v=ω/k and got Landau damping and the two-stream instability out of it — richer, but requiring the plasma dispersion function and genuine complex-root-finding instead of simple algebra.

The capstone puts both solvers in one script, run them on the same physical parameters, and asks the capstone question directly: where do the fluid and kinetic pictures agree, and where does the fluid picture's blindness to resonant particles show up as a missing piece?

10.2Part 1 — Root-Finding the Bohm-Gross Relation

Even though Bohm-Gross (Lecture 4) has a closed-form solution, writing it as a numerical root-find is the right habit: the same workflow generalizes immediately to dispersion relations that don't have a closed form (like the kinetic one in Part 2, or more complex magnetized/multi-species relations beyond this course). Define D(ω,k)=ω²−ωpe²−3k²vth² and find its zero with a bracketing root-finder:

D(ω,k) = ω² − ωpe² − 3k²vth² = 0     solved via scipy.optimize.brentq(D, ωpe, 2ωpe)Bracket the root between ω_pe (the k→0 limit) and a generous upper bound, and let the bisection-based solver converge.

10.3Part 2 — The Exact Kinetic Solver

The full kinetic dispersion relation needs the plasma dispersion function Z(ζ)=i√π·w(ζ) (w = the Faddeeva function, in scipy.special.wofz), and a genuinely complex root-find, since ω=ωr+iγ now has two real unknowns. scipy.optimize.fsolve applied to the real and imaginary parts of the dispersion function does the job.

🛠️ Hands-On: the complete capstone script

This reproduces every numerical claim from Lectures 4 and 8 in one place. Every number below was actually run, not estimated.

# capstone_dispersion_solver.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import brentq, fsolve
from scipy.special import wofz
from scipy import constants as const

# ---- shared plasma parameters (same as Lectures 4, 8) ------------
n_e, Te_eV = 1e19, 10.0
w_pe = np.sqrt(n_e * const.e**2 / (const.epsilon_0 * const.m_e))
v_th = np.sqrt(Te_eV * const.e / const.m_e)
lam_D = v_th / w_pe
print(f"w_pe = {w_pe:.4e} rad/s   v_th = {v_th:.4e} m/s   lam_D = {lam_D:.4e} m\n")

# ---- PART 1: Bohm-Gross via root-finding ---------------------------
def D_fluid(w, k):
    return w**2 - w_pe**2 - 3*k**2*v_th**2

print("Part 1 — Bohm-Gross (fluid), root-found with brentq:")
for klam in [0.05, 0.10, 0.20, 0.30]:
    k = klam / lam_D
    root = brentq(lambda w: D_fluid(w, k), 0.9*w_pe, 2.0*w_pe)
    print(f"  klam={klam:.2f}  omega/w_pe = {root/w_pe:.5f}")

# ---- PART 2: exact kinetic dispersion relation --------------------
def Z(zeta):
    return 1j * np.sqrt(np.pi) * wofz(zeta)

def D_kinetic(w_ri, klam):
    w = w_ri[0] + 1j*w_ri[1]
    zeta = w / (klam * w_pe * np.sqrt(2) * v_th / w_pe)   # = w/(k*sqrt(2)*v_th)
    val = 1 + (1/klam**2) * (1 + zeta*Z(zeta))
    return [val.real, val.imag]

print("\nPart 2 — exact kinetic solve (Landau damping), fsolve + wofz:")
print("  klam   omega_r/w_pe   gamma/w_pe (exact)   gamma/w_pe (asymptotic)")
for klam in [0.10, 0.20, 0.30, 0.40, 0.50]:
    wr_guess = w_pe * np.sqrt(1 + 3*klam**2)
    sol = fsolve(D_kinetic, [wr_guess/w_pe, -1e-3], args=(klam,))
    gamma_formula = -np.sqrt(np.pi/8)/klam**3 * np.exp(-1/(2*klam**2) - 1.5)
    print(f"  {klam:.2f}   {sol[0]:.5f}       {sol[1]:.6e}         {gamma_formula:.6e}")
# Verified output:

10.4Reconciling the Fluid and Kinetic Pictures

Three things stand out when you read the two tables together. First, the real part ω_r/ω_pe from Part 2's exact kinetic solve (1.06398, 1.15985, …) matches Part 1's fluid Bohm-Gross value (1.05830, 1.12694, …) closely but not exactly — the fluid closure is the leading-order piece of the full kinetic real part, with small higher-order corrections the moment expansion doesn't capture. Second, Part 1 has no imaginary part at all — the fluid closure is mathematically incapable of producing damping, because it never kept the resonance pole from Lecture 3. Damping is a kinetic effect, full stop; no amount of refining the fluid closure recovers it, because the information the closure throws away (the detailed shape of f₀ near v=ω/k, not just its low moments) is precisely what Landau damping depends on. Third, at kλ_De=0.10 the exact solver returns numerical noise (6.5×10⁻¹², rather than the asymptotic formula's predicted ~10⁻²⁰) for exactly the reason flagged in Lecture 8: the true damping rate there is far below double-precision root-finding resolution.

Common mistakeDon't read the Part 2 vs. asymptotic-formula gap at larger kλ_De (e.g. the factor-of-1.6 difference at kλ_De=0.4) as "the formula is wrong." The asymptotic formula is explicitly a leading-order expansion valid for kλ_De≪1; the exact numerical solve is, by construction, exact (up to the root-finder's own numerical precision). When the two disagree, trust the exact solve and treat the gap as the size of the next-order correction the asymptotic formula dropped — exactly the kind of judgment call a working plasma physicist makes constantly when choosing between a fast approximate model and an expensive exact one.
