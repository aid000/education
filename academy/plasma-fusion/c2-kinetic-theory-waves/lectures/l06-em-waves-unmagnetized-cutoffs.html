EM Waves in Unmagnetized Plasma & the Plasma Cutoff

Every electrostatic wave so far needed a density ripple. A transverse electromagnetic wave doesn't — and the plasma's response to it is strikingly simple: perfectly transparent above one frequency, perfectly reflecting below it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a transverse EM wave drives no density perturbation and needs no pressure closure.
  • Derive the EM dispersion relation ω² = ω_pe² + k²c² from the cold electron fluid and Maxwell's equations.
  • Identify the plasma frequency as a hard cutoff and interpret the evanescent (non-propagating) regime below it.
  • Compute the critical density for a given wave frequency, and vice versa.
  • Connect the cutoff to ionospheric radio reflection and laser-plasma (ICF) critical-surface physics.

6.1A Wave With No Pressure Term

Langmuir and ion-acoustic waves are both electrostatic: the wave's electric field points along its direction of propagation, which means it compresses the fluid and needs a pressure closure to resist that compression. An electromagnetic wave is transverse — its E-field (and B-field) point perpendicular to k — so electrons oscillate sideways, never bunching up. No density perturbation, no pressure term, no closure needed. The electron response is pure cold-fluid momentum balance:

−iωmevy = −eEy  ⟹   Jy = −en₀vy = i·ε₀ωpe²·Ey/ωThe transverse current response. (The wave's own magnetic force on the electrons, v×B_wave, is a product of two first-order quantities — second order, dropped exactly as in every other linearization this course.)

6.2The Dispersion Relation & the Cutoff

Feed this current into Maxwell's equations (take the curl of Faraday's law, substitute Ampère's law, use ∇·E=0 for a purely transverse wave) and the algebra closes cleanly:

ω² = ωpe² + k²c²     N² ≡ (ck/ω)² = 1 − ωpe²/ω²N is the refractive index. In vacuum (n_e=0) this is just ω=kc, the ordinary light line; turning on density pulls the curve up and to the right.

Solve for k²: k² = (ω²−ωpe²)/c². For ω>ωpe, k² is positive — an ordinary propagating wave, just with N<1 (phase velocity ω/k > c, though the group velocity that actually carries energy stays below c). For ω<ωpe, k² is negative — k itself is imaginary, and the "wave" decays exponentially in space instead of oscillating:

ω < ωpe: E(x) ∝ exp(−x/δ)   δ = c/√(ωpe²−ω²)An evanescent wave: it carries no energy flux into the plasma on average and is reflected at the boundary. ω_pe is a hard cutoff frequency.
AnalogyThis is exactly the physics of total internal reflection in optics, or a wave hitting a waveguide below its cutoff frequency: ask for a propagating solution where none exists, and the math responds with an imaginary wavenumber — a decaying, non-propagating field instead. The plasma frequency plays the same role here that the waveguide's geometry plays there: a single number that cleanly splits "passes through" from "bounces off."
Common mistakeA cutoff is not absorption. In this lossless, collisionless model, a wave below ω_pe isn't dissipated into heat — it's reflected, cleanly and completely, at the boundary where ω=ω_pe. Real plasmas have some collisional absorption too, but the cutoff itself is a pure propagation effect, not a loss mechanism.
✦ Worked Example — why AM radio bounces off the sky

Earth's ionosphere has electron density n_e ≈ 1×10¹² m⁻³ in its denser layers. Find the critical (cutoff) frequency, and explain why shortwave radio reflects back to the ground while FM/TV does not.

  1. Plasma frequency: ω_pe = √(n_ee²/ε₀m_e) = √[(1×10¹²)(1.602×10⁻¹⁹)²/((8.854×10⁻¹²)(9.109×10⁻³¹))] ≈ 5.64×10⁷ rad/s.
  2. Cutoff frequency: f_pe = ω_pe/2π ≈ 8.98 MHz.
  3. Interpretation: any radio signal with f < 8.98 MHz (shortwave/AM bands) satisfies ω<ω_pe in the ionosphere — it's reflected and can travel around the curve of the Earth by bouncing between the ionosphere and ground. A signal with f > 8.98 MHz (FM radio ~100 MHz, broadcast TV, satellite links) satisfies ω>ω_pe — it passes straight through the ionosphere, which is exactly why those bands need line-of-sight or orbiting satellites instead.

🛠️ Hands-On: cutoff frequency and critical density

Compute the ionosphere result above, plus the critical density for a 1.064 µm Nd:glass laser — the frequency at which inertial-confinement-fusion (ICF) lasers are driven, and the density beyond which such a laser cannot penetrate a target plasma.

# plasma_cutoff.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from scipy import constants as const

def f_pe(n_e):
    return np.sqrt(n_e * const.e**2 / (const.epsilon_0 * const.m_e)) / (2*np.pi)

def critical_density(wavelength_m):
    w = 2*np.pi*const.c / wavelength_m
    return const.epsilon_0 * const.m_e * w**2 / const.e**2

print(f"Ionosphere f_pe (n_e=1e12 m^-3)  = {f_pe(1e12)/1e6:.2f} MHz")
print(f"Critical density, 1.064 um laser = {critical_density(1.064e-6):.3e} m^-3"
      f"  = {critical_density(1.064e-6)/1e6:.3e} cm^-3")
# Verified output:
# Ionosphere f_pe (n_e=1e12 m^-3)  = 8.98 MHz
# Critical density, 1.064 um laser = 9.848e+26 m^-3  = 9.848e+20 cm^-3

The laser result matches the standard ICF rule of thumb n_c[cm⁻³] ≈ 1.1×10²¹/λ[µm]² to within 1.3% — beyond this density, a 1.064 µm beam can no longer propagate and is absorbed/reflected at the critical surface, the central geometric fact behind how ICF capsules couple laser energy to their outer plasma.

Q1. Why does the transverse EM wave derivation need no pressure closure, unlike Langmuir or ion-acoustic waves?
A. Because the plasma has no electrons
B. Because the wave's E-field is perpendicular to k, so electrons oscillate sideways with no density compression
C. Because the wave is always in vacuum
D. Because the frequency is always below ω_pe
Show answer

B. A transverse wave produces purely sideways electron motion — no bunching, no density perturbation, hence no pressure gradient to close. This is the key structural difference from the electrostatic waves of Lectures 4–5.

Q2. For ω < ω_pe, the EM dispersion relation ω²=ω_pe²+k²c² implies…
A. k is real and the wave propagates normally
B. k² is negative — k is imaginary and the field decays exponentially (evanescent)
C. ω_pe itself becomes imaginary
D. the wave travels faster than light
Show answer

B. k²=(ω²−ω_pe²)/c² is negative when ω<ω_pe, so k is purely imaginary and E(x)∝exp(−x/δ) — an evanescent, non-propagating field that reflects at the boundary.

Q3. A "cutoff" at ω_pe means the wave below that frequency is…
A. absorbed and converted to heat
B. reflected, in this lossless collisionless model — not absorbed
C. converted into an ion-acoustic wave
D. unaffected by the plasma
Show answer

B. In the ideal collisionless model, sub-cutoff waves are perfectly reflected, not dissipated — a pure propagation effect. Real plasmas add some collisional absorption on top, but that's a separate mechanism from the cutoff itself.

Q4. The ionosphere's f_pe ≈ 8.98 MHz (for n_e=1×10¹² m⁻³) explains why…
A. FM radio (~100 MHz) reflects off the ionosphere
B. Shortwave/AM signals below ~9 MHz reflect, while FM/TV above it passes through
C. all radio waves are absorbed by the ionosphere
D. the ionosphere has no effect on any radio frequency
Show answer

B. Frequencies below f_pe satisfy ω<ω_pe and reflect; frequencies above it (like FM's ~100 MHz) satisfy ω>ω_pe and pass straight through — which is exactly why FM/TV/satellite links need line-of-sight or orbiting relays.

Q5. The "critical density" for a given laser wavelength is the density at which…
A. the plasma becomes fully ionized
B. the local plasma frequency equals the laser's frequency, ω_pe=ω_laser
C. the Debye length equals the laser wavelength
D. the plasma becomes magnetized
Show answer

B. Critical density is defined by ω_pe(n_c)=ω_laser — exactly the cutoff condition of this lecture, applied to a specific laser frequency. Beyond this density the laser cannot propagate and is reflected/absorbed at the critical surface.
