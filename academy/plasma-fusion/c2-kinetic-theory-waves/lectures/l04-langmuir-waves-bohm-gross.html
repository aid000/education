Langmuir Waves & the Bohm-Gross Relation

C1's plasma frequency was the ω=ω_pe, k-independent limit of something richer. Give the electrons a temperature and the oscillation turns into a genuine wave — one that carries energy and depends on wavelength.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up the linearized two-fluid (warm electron / fixed ion) model for electrostatic oscillations.
  • Derive the Bohm-Gross dispersion relation ω² = ω_pe² + 3k²v_th² from continuity, momentum, and Poisson's equation.
  • Compute phase and group velocity for a Langmuir wave and show v_phase·v_group = 3v_th².
  • Explain physically why electron pressure raises the oscillation frequency above ω_pe.
  • Evaluate the Bohm-Gross relation numerically in Python for realistic lab-plasma parameters.

4.1The Fluid Shortcut: Warm Electrons, Fixed Ions

Lecture 3 built the exact kinetic dispersion relation ε(k,ω)=0, valid for any f₀ — but evaluating its integral for a Maxwellian means confronting the resonance pole, which we're deferring to Lecture 8. For now there's a shortcut: take the first two velocity moments of the linearized Vlasov equation (Lecture 1's n and u) instead of solving for f₁ itself, close the system with a pressure law, and you get the same leading-order physics without ever touching the pole.

The model: electrons form a fluid with density n₀+n₁, velocity u₁, and pressure p₁; ions are far too heavy to respond on this timescale, so they sit as a fixed, uniform neutralizing background. Linearize continuity and momentum, and close the system with a 1-D adiabatic pressure law (γ=3, since compressing a gas along one direction only — no transverse motion here — gives γ=(f+2)/f with f=1 degree of freedom):

∂n₁/∂t + n₀∂u₁/∂x = 0    men₀∂u₁/∂t = −∂p₁/∂x − en₀E₁    p₁ = 3k_BT_e·n₁Continuity, linearized momentum (electron charge −e), and the adiabatic closure that stands in for the full kinetic pressure response.

4.2Deriving the Bohm-Gross Relation

Apply the plane-wave ansatz ∂/∂t→−iω, ∂/∂x→ik to all three equations, plus Poisson's equation ikE₁ = −en₁/ε₀, and solve the resulting 4×4 linear system for the condition that a nonzero solution exists (the determinant of the coefficient matrix vanishes). Carried out symbolically, the algebra collapses to one clean result:

ω² = ωpe² + 3k²vth²     vth² ≡ k_BT_e/m_eThe Bohm-Gross dispersion relation. ω_pe² = n₀e²/(ε₀m_e) is C1's plasma frequency; the 3k²v_th² term is new — it's the electron pressure correction, and it's exactly what the cold-beam limit of Lecture 3 was missing.

Two limits check against what you already know. As k→0 (very long wavelength, no spatial gradient for pressure to act against), ω→ωpe — C1's result, recovered exactly. As Te→0 (a perfectly cold plasma), vth→0 and again ω→ωpe — matching Lecture 3's cold-beam worked example. Temperature and wavenumber only matter together, through the combination k²vth², and the whole correction is governed by one dimensionless number: kλDe, where λDe=vth/ωpe is C1's Debye length.

AnalogyA cold plasma oscillates like a mass on a spring with no friction and no stiffness beyond the electric restoring force — the frequency is fixed, independent of how far you displace it. A warm plasma is the same spring with an added internal pressure, like compressing a real gas: squeeze it over a shorter distance (larger k = shorter wavelength) and the gas pushes back harder, stiffening the effective spring and raising the frequency above ω_pe. That's the whole content of the +3k²v_th² term.
✦ Worked Example — a Langmuir wave in a 10 eV lab plasma

n_e = 1×10¹⁹ m⁻³, T_e = 10 eV. Find ω_pe, v_th, λ_De, and ω for a wave with kλ_De = 0.2, then find its phase and group velocity.

  1. Plasma frequency: ω_pe = √(n_ee²/ε₀m_e) = 1.784×10¹¹ rad/s.
  2. Thermal speed: T_e is given in eV, so it must be converted to joules first: v_th = √(e·T_e/m_e) = 1.326×10⁶ m/s; Debye length λ_De = v_th/ω_pe = 7.43 µm (matches C1's formula exactly).
  3. Wavenumber: kλ_De=0.2 ⟹ k = 0.2/λ_De = 2.690×10⁴ m⁻¹ (wavelength 2π/k ≈ 0.23 mm).
  4. Bohm-Gross frequency: ω = ω_pe√(1+3(0.2)²) = ω_pe√1.12 ≈ 1.0583 ω_pe = 1.888×10¹¹ rad/s — a 5.8% upward shift from the cold value.
  5. Phase & group velocity: v_phase = ω/k ≈ 7.02×10⁶ m/s (5.29 v_th); v_group = dω/dk = 3kv_th²/ω ≈ 7.52×10⁵ m/s (0.567 v_th). Check: v_phase·v_group = 3v_th² exactly, as the dispersion relation requires.
Common mistakeThe Bohm-Gross (Langmuir) wave and the ion-acoustic wave (next lecture) are both "plasma sound waves," but they're driven by completely different physics: Langmuir waves are an electron-pressure effect with ions inert in the background, while ion-acoustic waves need the ions to actually move and rely on the much faster electrons merely screening (Boltzmann relation) rather than oscillating. Don't mix up which species supplies the inertia and which supplies the restoring pressure in each case.

🛠️ Hands-On: Bohm-Gross in Python

Reproduce the worked example and sweep across k to generate the dispersion curve above.

# bohm_gross.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np
from scipy import constants as const

n_e, Te_eV = 1e19, 10.0
w_pe = np.sqrt(n_e * const.e**2 / (const.epsilon_0 * const.m_e))
v_th = np.sqrt(Te_eV * const.e / const.m_e)
lam_D = v_th / w_pe

klam = 0.2
k = klam / lam_D
w = np.sqrt(w_pe**2 + 3 * k**2 * v_th**2)
v_phase = w / k
v_group = 3 * k * v_th**2 / w

print(f"w_pe    = {w_pe:.4e} rad/s")
print(f"v_th    = {v_th:.4e} m/s")
print(f"lam_D   = {lam_D:.4e} m")
print(f"omega   = {w:.4e} rad/s  (omega/w_pe = {w/w_pe:.4f})")
print(f"v_phase = {v_phase:.4e} m/s   v_group = {v_group:.4e} m/s")
print(f"v_phase*v_group / (3*v_th**2) = {v_phase*v_group/(3*v_th**2):.6f}  (should be 1)")
# Verified output:
# w_pe    = 1.7840e+11 rad/s
# v_th    = 1.3262e+06 m/s
# lam_D   = 7.4339e-06 m
# omega   = 1.8880e+11 rad/s  (omega/w_pe = 1.0583)
# v_phase = 7.0176e+06 m/s   v_group = 7.5189e+05 m/s
# v_phase*v_group / (3*v_th**2) = 1.000000  (should be 1)

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In deriving the Bohm-Gross relation, the ions were treated as…
A. a fast, inertialess fluid providing screening
B. completely absent from the plasma
C. a fixed, uniform neutralizing background — too heavy to respond
D. moving at the same velocity as the electrons
Show answer

C. On the fast electron-plasma-oscillation timescale, ions are far too massive to move — they just maintain the background charge neutrality. (A) describes their role in the ion-acoustic wave (Lecture 5), not here.

Q2. As k→0, the Bohm-Gross relation ω²=ω_pe²+3k²v_th² reduces to…
A. ω=0
B. ω=ω_pe, matching C1's plasma frequency
C. ω=kv_th
D. ω=3ω_pe
Show answer

B. At long wavelength (k→0) the pressure correction vanishes and the wave reduces to the dispersionless cold plasma oscillation, exactly as required for consistency with C1 and Lecture 3's cold-beam limit.

Q3. Why does ω increase above ω_pe as kλ_De grows?
A. Electron pressure resists compression over shorter wavelengths, stiffening the restoring force
B. The ions start oscillating faster
C. ω_pe itself increases with k
D. It's a numerical artifact of the adiabatic closure
Show answer

A. Shorter wavelengths (larger k) compress the electron fluid over a shorter distance, so the pressure gradient — and hence the restoring force — grows, raising the oscillation frequency. ω_pe is a constant set by n_e and m_e; it doesn't depend on k.

Q4. For the worked example's Langmuir wave (kλ_De=0.2), the product v_phase·v_group equals…
A. ω_pe²
B. c² (the speed of light squared)
C. 3v_th², directly from differentiating the dispersion relation
D. zero
Show answer

C. From ω²=ω_pe²+3k²v_th², differentiating gives 2ω dω/dk = 6kv_th², so (ω/k)(dω/dk) = v_phase·v_group = 3v_th² — confirmed numerically in the hands-on code to 6 decimal places.

Q5. The pressure closure p₁=3k_BT_e·n₁ used γ=3 because…
A. electrons have 3 spatial dimensions of motion
B. it's a 1-D adiabatic compression with one effective degree of freedom, γ=(f+2)/f with f=1
C. 3 is the number of velocity moments taken
D. γ=3 is required by charge conservation
Show answer

B. The wave compresses the electron fluid along one direction only (the direction of k); the adiabatic index for one effective degree of freedom is γ=(f+2)/f=3. It's a closure choice standing in for the true kinetic pressure response, not a dimensional-counting coincidence.
