Landau Damping

A perfectly reversible, collisionless equation somehow makes waves decay. The resolution is one of the most celebrated results in plasma physics — and it's hiding in the pole we set aside back in Lecture 3.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the apparent paradox: a time-reversible Vlasov-Poisson system producing apparently irreversible wave damping.
  • Explain the resonant-particle mechanism physically, including the role of ∂f₀/∂v at the phase velocity.
  • State Landau's prescription for the pole and the resulting asymptotic damping-rate formula.
  • Compute the Landau damping rate for specific kλ_De and interpret the weak- vs. strong-damping regimes.
  • Numerically solve the exact kinetic dispersion relation and compare to the asymptotic formula, including where it breaks down.

8.1The Paradox

The Vlasov equation (Lecture 2) is exactly time-reversible — run it backward and it's still a valid solution, with no entropy, no friction, no collisions anywhere in it. Yet in 1946, Lev Landau showed that a small electrostatic perturbation in a Maxwellian plasma decays — the electric field amplitude shrinks exponentially in time, just like a damped oscillator with friction. How can a perfectly reversible equation produce something that looks exactly like damping?

The resolution is that nothing is actually lost — it's hidden. Recall Lecture 2's free-streaming example: different velocity groups shear phase space into ever-finer filaments (phase mixing). The macroscopic, observable field — built from a velocity integral over f — genuinely decays, because the fine-scale structure in f that still "remembers" the original wave becomes too tangled to contribute coherently to that integral. The information isn't destroyed (and famously, waiting long enough and perturbing the plasma a second time can trigger a "Landau echo" that partially reconstructs it) — it's just phase-mixed into a form the macroscopic field can no longer see.

8.2Resonant Particles: the Physical Mechanism

Recall the resonance denominator from Lecture 3: particles with velocity v=ω/k move at exactly the wave's phase velocity, so they feel something closer to a steady force than an oscillating one, and they exchange energy with the wave far more effectively than the bulk. The direction of that exchange depends on the local slope of f₀ right at the resonance:

∂f₀/∂v < 0 at v=ω/k  ⟹  more slow resonant particles than fast ones  ⟹  net energy flows wave → particles  ⟹  dampingA Maxwellian's tail (v>0) always has ∂f₀/∂v<0 — slightly more particles just below the phase velocity (gaining energy, accelerated toward it) than just above (losing energy, decelerated). That tiny imbalance is the entire effect.
AnalogyPicture surfers paddling near a wave's speed. Surfers moving just slower than the wave catch it and accelerate, stealing energy from the wave. Surfers moving just faster than the wave get pushed back by it, giving energy to the wave. If there are more slow surfers than fast ones nearby — exactly the Maxwellian's negative slope — more energy is stolen than given back, and the wave loses amplitude. Flip that imbalance (more fast surfers than slow, a positive slope) and the wave would grow instead — precisely what happens in Lecture 9.

8.3Landau's Prescription & the Damping-Rate Formula

Landau's technical contribution was showing how to handle the pole correctly: treat ω as the limit of a complex frequency approaching the real axis from above (consistent with the problem being posed as an initial-value problem, which is causal), which fixes a specific way to deform the velocity-integration contour around v=ω/k. Carrying this through for a Maxwellian and expanding for weak damping (kλ_De≪1) gives the frequency shift back to the Bohm-Gross relation (Lecture 4) at leading order, plus a small imaginary part:

ω = ω_r + iγ    ω_r ≈ ω_pe√(1+3k²λDe²) (Bohm-Gross)    γ/ωpe = −√(π/8)·(kλDe)−3·exp[ −1/(2(kλDe)²) − 3/2 ]γ is the imaginary part of ω in the exp(i(kx−ωt)) convention: γ<0 means the amplitude ∝ exp(γt) decays — damping. The exponential makes γ extraordinarily sensitive to kλ_De: double kλ_De and the exponent's magnitude drops by a factor of 4.
Common mistakeLandau damping is not collisional dissipation. No entropy-generating collision appears anywhere in this derivation — the damping is a purely reversible phase-mixing effect at the macroscopic (field) level, even though the underlying six-dimensional Vlasov dynamics never lose information. That's also why it's genuinely surprising, and why it took until 1946 (and considerable mathematical care with the contour) to get right.
✦ Worked Example — weak vs. strong Landau damping

Evaluate γ/ω_pe for kλ_De=0.2 (weak-damping regime) and kλ_De=0.5 (approaching the edge of validity).

  1. kλ_De=0.2: γ/ω_pe = −√(π/8)·(0.2)⁻³·exp[−1/(2·0.04)−1.5] = −0.6267·125·exp(−14) ≈ −6.51×10⁻⁵. The wave loses only 0.0065% of its amplitude per radian of oscillation — essentially undampened on any experimentally relevant timescale.
  2. kλ_De=0.5: γ/ω_pe = −√(π/8)·(0.5)⁻³·exp[−1/(2·0.25)−1.5] = −0.6267·8·exp(−3.5) ≈ −0.1514. This wave loses about 15% of its amplitude per radian — it damps out within roughly one oscillation period.
  3. Lesson: the exponential in γ means the transition from "essentially undamped" to "gone within a cycle" happens over a surprisingly narrow range of kλ_De — Langmuir waves are either robust or short-lived, with very little gradual middle ground.

🛠️ Hands-On: solving the exact kinetic dispersion relation

The asymptotic formula above is a leading-order expansion. We can check it against the exact kinetic dispersion relation by using the plasma dispersion function Z(ζ) = i√π·w(ζ), where w is the Faddeeva function (available in SciPy), and root-finding for complex ω at fixed k.

# landau_exact.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np
from scipy.special import wofz
from scipy.optimize import fsolve

def Z(zeta):
    return 1j * np.sqrt(np.pi) * wofz(zeta)

def dispersion(w_ri, klam):      # normalized: w_pe=1, v_th=1, lambda_De=1
    w = w_ri[0] + 1j*w_ri[1]
    zeta = w / (klam * np.sqrt(2))
    val = 1 + (1/klam**2) * (1 + zeta*Z(zeta))
    return [val.real, val.imag]

print("klam   omega_r/wpe   gamma/wpe (exact)   gamma/wpe (asymptotic formula)")
for klam in [0.2, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5]:
    wr_guess = np.sqrt(1 + 3*klam**2)
    sol = fsolve(dispersion, [wr_guess, -1e-3], args=(klam,))
    gamma_formula = -np.sqrt(np.pi/8)/klam**3 * np.exp(-1/(2*klam**2) - 1.5)
    print(f"{klam:.1f}    {sol[0]:.5f}      {sol[1]:.6e}        {gamma_formula:.6e}")
# Verified output:
# klam   omega_r/wpe   gamma/wpe (exact)   gamma/wpe (asymptotic formula)
# 0.2    1.06398      -5.510748e-05        -6.513542e-05
# 0.3    1.15985      -1.262037e-02        -2.002061e-02
# 0.4    1.28506      -6.612796e-02        -9.599265e-02
# 0.5    1.41566      -1.533595e-01        -1.513872e-01

Agreement is good (same order of magnitude) across this whole range and especially tight at kλ_De=0.5 (within 1.3%) — somewhat better than the formula's "leading order only" label might suggest. At kλ_De=0.1, γ/ω_pe is of order 10⁻²⁰ — far below the ~10⁻⁶ relative precision a general-purpose root-finder like fsolve can resolve, so it isn't shown here. That's not a physics failure, it's a genuine numerical-methods lesson revisited in the Lecture 10 capstone: exponentially small quantities need either extended precision or the analytic asymptotic formula, not brute-force root-finding.

🔗 Where this is heading

Landau damping is really a special case of a much more general statement: the sign of ∂f₀/∂v at the resonant velocity controls whether a wave grows or decays, for any f₀, not just a Maxwellian. Lecture 9 flips that sign on purpose — two counter-streaming beams create a region of positive slope — and the exact same resonance mechanism turns into exponential growth: the two-stream instability.

Q1. The "paradox" of Landau damping is that…
A. a time-reversible, collisionless equation produces an apparently irreversible macroscopic decay
B. the Vlasov equation has no solutions
C. collisions are required but were left out
D. the plasma frequency becomes negative
Show answer

A. The Vlasov equation has no friction or entropy production anywhere in it, yet the macroscopic field amplitude genuinely decays — resolved by recognizing the "lost" information is phase-mixed into fine velocity-space structure, not actually destroyed.

Q2. For a Maxwellian, the sign of ∂f₀/∂v at the resonant velocity v=ω/k (for ω/k>0) is…
A. always positive, causing damping
B. always zero
C. always negative, causing damping
D. undefined
Show answer

C. A Maxwellian is single-peaked and monotonically decreasing for v>0, so ∂f₀/∂v<0 there — more slow resonant particles gain energy from the wave than fast ones give back, draining the wave (damping).

Q3. In the exp(i(kx−ωt)) convention, a negative imaginary part of ω (γ<0) corresponds to…
A. exponential growth
B. a purely real frequency
C. no change in amplitude
D. exponential decay (damping) of the wave amplitude
Show answer

D. The amplitude factor is exp(−iωt)=exp(−iω_rt)exp(γt); γ<0 makes exp(γt) decay with time — damping. γ>0 would instead describe growth (Lecture 9's two-stream instability).

Q4. From the worked example, going from kλ_De=0.2 to kλ_De=0.5 changed γ/ω_pe from about −6.5×10⁻⁵ to about −0.151. This illustrates that…
A. the exponential sensitivity of γ means damping strength changes very rapidly over a narrow range of kλ_De
B. γ depends linearly on kλ_De
C. damping is independent of k
D. the formula is wrong at kλ_De=0.5
Show answer

A. The exp[−1/(2(kλ_De)²)] factor is extraordinarily sensitive to kλ_De, so the damping rate swings by more than three orders of magnitude over this modest range — essentially undamped to damped-within-a-cycle.

Q5. At kλ_De=0.1, the hands-on root-finder failed to resolve a nonzero γ. This is because…
A. Landau damping doesn't exist at small kλ_De
B. fsolve cannot handle complex numbers
C. the true γ/ω_pe is of order 10⁻²⁰ — far below the numerical precision a general root-finder can resolve
D. the plasma dispersion function is undefined there
Show answer

C. The asymptotic formula predicts an exponentially tiny γ at small kλ_De — real, but far smaller than double-precision root-finding can distinguish from zero. This is a numerical-methods limitation, not evidence that the damping vanishes.
