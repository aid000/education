Week 5 · Lecture 5

Ion-Acoustic Waves

Flip Lecture 4's roles: let the ions carry the inertia and the electrons supply nothing but pressure, and a plasma grows a genuine sound wave — one that exists in ordinary air too, just with electrons standing in for the restoring force.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why electrons respond via the Boltzmann relation while ions provide inertia in this wave.
  • Derive the ion-acoustic dispersion relation ω² ≈ k²c_s²/(1+k²λ_De²) and the sound speed c_s² = (T_e+3T_i)/M.
  • Identify the long-wavelength limit ω=kc_s as a true, non-dispersive sound wave.
  • Compute c_s numerically for hydrogen, deuterium, and tritium plasmas and explain the scaling with ion mass.
  • State why T_e ≫ T_i is required for the wave to survive (foreshadowing Landau damping).

5.1Why the Ions Get to Move This Time

Lecture 4 built a wave entirely out of electron dynamics, with ions as an inert backdrop — valid because ions are thousands of times heavier and simply can't keep up with the fast electron-plasma-oscillation timescale. Ion-acoustic waves live at the opposite end of the spectrum: frequencies low enough that ions have time to respond, but still much faster than ion-ion collisions (so the collisionless Vlasov framework still applies).

At these lower frequencies the electrons are the fast species: they stream back and forth along the wave many times before the ions move appreciably, which keeps them in local thermal (Boltzmann) equilibrium with the slowly-varying potential at every instant:

n_e1/n₀ = eφ₁/(k_BT_e)The linearized Boltzmann relation. Electrons are effectively inertialess here — they adjust instantly to screen the potential, exactly like the Debye shielding of C1, just now time-dependent.

Ions, by contrast, are the slow, massive species — they provide the inertia that makes this a genuine propagating wave rather than instantaneous screening. Their dynamics are governed by linearized continuity and momentum (with an adiabatic 1-D pressure closure, γᵢ=3, exactly as in Lecture 4 but now for ions), coupled to the electrons only through Poisson's equation.

5.2Deriving the Ion-Acoustic Dispersion Relation

Combine the ion continuity and momentum equations, the electron Boltzmann relation, and Poisson's equation ikE₁=(e/ε₀)(n_i1−n_e1), apply the plane-wave ansatz, and solve the resulting linear system for ω(k) exactly as in Lecture 4. The leading-order (long-wavelength) result is:

ω² ≈ k²cs² / (1 + k²λDe²)     cs² = (k_BT_e + 3k_BT_i) / MThe ion-acoustic dispersion relation. M is the ion mass; λ_De is the electron Debye length from C1. The 3T_i term is the ion-pressure correction, structurally identical to Lecture 4's electron pressure term.

At long wavelength (kλDe≪1, the regime of nearly every lab and space-plasma application), the denominator is ≈1 and the relation collapses to a genuine, non-dispersive sound wave:

ω ≈ k·cs     (kλDe ≪ 1)A constant-speed sound wave: the ion-acoustic analog of ordinary sound in a gas. Every k below this limit propagates at the same speed c_s, unlike the Langmuir wave of Lecture 4, which disperses at essentially all k.

At shorter wavelengths (kλDe approaching and exceeding 1) the k²λDe² term in the denominator grows and the curve bends over — ω rises more slowly than linearly in k and saturates, in the simplest cold-ion (T_i→0) version of the formula, toward the ion plasma frequency ωpi=√(n₀e²/ε₀M). Physically: once the wavelength gets comparable to the electron Debye length, electrons can no longer perfectly screen the ion charge bunching, and the wave stops being a simple pressure-driven sound wave.

AnalogyIn ordinary air, sound propagates because molecules bump into each other, building up pressure that pushes neighboring molecules, and so on. In the ion-acoustic wave, the ions play the role of the air molecules (mass, inertia, the thing that's actually bunching up and moving), while the electrons play the role of the gas pressure itself — a fast-responding background that resists compression of the ion charge bunches through Debye screening, rather than through direct ion-ion collisions.
✦ Worked Example — sound speed in a hydrogen plasma

A hydrogen plasma has T_e = 10 eV, T_i = 1 eV. Find the ion-acoustic speed c_s, and compare to the simpler T_e-only estimate often quoted.

  1. Full formula: c_s = √[(T_e+3T_i)e/M] = √[(10+3)(1.602×10⁻¹⁹)/(1.673×10⁻²⁷)] ≈ 3.529×10⁴ m/s (35.3 km/s).
  2. T_e-only estimate: c_s,simple = √(T_ee/M) = √[(10)(1.602×10⁻¹⁹)/(1.673×10⁻²⁷)] ≈ 3.095×10⁴ m/s (30.9 km/s).
  3. Correction factor: 35.29/30.95 ≈ 1.140 — the ion-pressure term (3T_i, here 3 eV against 10 eV of T_e) is a non-negligible 14% correction even at a healthy T_e/T_i=10 ratio.
  4. Mass scaling: c_s ∝ 1/√M, so deuterium (M≈2m_p) gives c_s≈25.0 km/s and tritium (M≈3m_p) gives c_s≈20.4 km/s, each exactly √2 and √3 times slower than hydrogen — a scaling directly relevant to fusion-edge plasma turbulence (C4/C5), where D-T mixtures are the fuel of choice.
Common mistakeThis whole derivation assumed T_e≫T_i. If T_i approaches T_e, a large fraction of ions move at speeds comparable to c_s itself — the resonant-particle mechanism of Lecture 8 kicks in and strongly Landau-damps the wave, often within a single oscillation. Real ion-acoustic experiments and edge-plasma turbulence studies work hard to stay in the T_e≫T_i regime specifically so this wave survives long enough to observe.

🛠️ Hands-On: ion-acoustic speed across isotopes

# ion_acoustic.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
from scipy import constants as const

Te_eV, Ti_eV = 10.0, 1.0
masses = {"H": const.m_p, "D": 2*const.m_p, "T": 3*const.m_p}  # approx, ignoring binding energy

for name, M in masses.items():
    cs = np.sqrt((Te_eV*const.e + 3*Ti_eV*const.e) / M)
    cs_simple = np.sqrt(Te_eV*const.e / M)
    print(f"{name}: c_s = {cs:,.0f} m/s   (T_e-only: {cs_simple:,.0f} m/s, ratio {cs/cs_simple:.4f})")
# Verified output:
# H: c_s = 35,288 m/s   (T_e-only: 30,950 m/s, ratio 1.1402)
# D: c_s = 24,952 m/s   (T_e-only: 21,885 m/s, ratio 1.1402)
# T: c_s = 20,374 m/s   (T_e-only: 17,869 m/s, ratio 1.1402)

The correction ratio (1.1402) is identical across isotopes — it depends only on T_e/T_i, not on M, since M cancels between c_s and c_s,simple. Only the absolute speed scales with 1/√M.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the ion-acoustic wave, which species provides the wave's inertia?
A. Ions — their mass is what resists acceleration and sustains the oscillation
B. Electrons, via their thermal pressure
C. Neutrals
D. Photons
Show answer

A. Electrons respond essentially instantaneously (Boltzmann relation) and contribute pressure/screening, not inertia; the ion mass M is what actually resists the wave's acceleration and sets c_s ∝ 1/√M.

Q2. The electron Boltzmann relation n_e1/n₀ = eφ₁/k_BT_e is valid here because…
A. electrons are too heavy to move
B. electrons carry no charge in this model
C. electrons move fast enough to stay in local thermal equilibrium with the slowly-varying potential
D. the wave frequency exceeds ω_pe
Show answer

C. At ion-acoustic frequencies (far below ω_pe), electrons complete many oscillations per ion-acoustic period, so they continuously re-equilibrate — exactly the assumption behind using the instantaneous Boltzmann relation instead of full electron dynamics.

Q3. At long wavelength (kλ_De≪1), the ion-acoustic dispersion relation reduces to…
A. ω ≈ kc_s — a non-dispersive sound wave
B. ω = ω_pe
C. ω = 0
D. ω² = ω_pe²+3k²v_th²
Show answer

A. When k²λ_De²≪1, the denominator (1+k²λ_De²)≈1, giving a constant-speed sound wave ω≈kc_s. (D) is the Bohm-Gross relation from Lecture 4, a different wave entirely.

Q4. In the worked example (H plasma, T_e=10 eV, T_i=1 eV), the full c_s exceeded the T_e-only estimate by about…
A. 0.1%
B. they were exactly equal
C. 300%
D. 14%
Show answer

D. 35.29/30.95 ≈ 1.140 — a 14% correction from the 3T_i ion-pressure term, even at a comfortable T_e/T_i=10. The correction grows as T_i approaches T_e.

Q5. Why do real ion-acoustic experiments require T_e≫T_i?
A. Otherwise the wave would be electromagnetic instead of electrostatic
B. Cold ions are required to conserve charge
C. If T_i is comparable to T_e, many ions move near the wave's phase velocity and strongly Landau-damp it
D. It has no physical consequence; it's just a convention
Show answer

C. When T_i approaches T_e, the ion thermal spread overlaps the wave's phase velocity c_s, and resonant ions (Lecture 8's mechanism) damp the wave rapidly — the fluid derivation here implicitly assumed this damping is negligible.
