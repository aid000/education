Week 1 · Lecture 1

Distribution Functions & Velocity-Space Moments

C1 treated every particle as a point following its own orbit. Here we zoom out to a cloud of particles in velocity space too — and discover that density, flow, and pressure are all just slices of one function.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the distribution function f(r,v,t) and explain what it counts in six-dimensional phase space.
  • Derive density, mean flow velocity, and pressure as the zeroth, first, and second velocity moments of f.
  • State the Maxwellian distribution and identify it as the equilibrium, maximum-entropy state.
  • Compute moments of a non-Maxwellian distribution by hand and check them against a numerical integration.
  • Explain why a kinetic (f-based) description is sometimes unavoidable, and why fluid variables are never lost — only coarser.

1.1From Particles to a Distribution Function

In C1, every plasma result came from tracking individual particles: an electron drifting with E×B, a proton gyrating in a mirror field. That works for one particle, or a few. A real plasma has 10¹⁸–10²⁰ particles per cubic meter, each with its own position and velocity. Tracking all of them is both impossible and the wrong question — what we actually want is how many particles are moving roughly this fast, roughly here, right now.

That question is answered by the distribution function f(r, v, t). It lives in six-dimensional phase space — three position coordinates plus three velocity coordinates — and is defined so that

f(r,v,t) d³r d³v = number of particles in the small box d³r around r, with velocities in the small box d³v around vf is a number density per unit phase-space volume — units of particles · m⁻³ · (m/s)⁻³.

This is strictly more information than a fluid description carries. A fluid gives you the density n(r,t) and flow velocity u(r,t) at every point — one number and one vector per location. The distribution function gives you the entire speed and direction spread of particles at every location: are they all moving together, or is there a hot tail, or two counter-streaming beams hiding inside the same average velocity? Every wave and instability in this course traces back to structure in f that a fluid description cannot see.

AnalogyA fluid description is a traffic report that says "40 cars/km, average speed 60 mph." The distribution function is the full speed histogram: maybe it's a tight cluster near 60, or maybe it's bimodal — half doing 40 in the slow lane, half doing 80 in the fast lane — with the same average speed either way. Fluid theory cannot tell those two traffic jams apart. Kinetic theory, carrying the whole histogram, always can — and as you'll see in Lectures 8–9, that difference is exactly what produces Landau damping and the two-stream instability.

1.2Moments: Density, Flow Velocity, Pressure

You never have to abandon the familiar fluid quantities — you recover every one of them by integrating f over all velocities, i.e. by taking its velocity moments. Each moment is the previous one weighted by one more power of v.

Zeroth moment — number density

n(r,t) = ∫ f(r,v,t) d³vIntegrate out velocity entirely and you're left with ordinary particles-per-volume.

First moment — mean flow velocity

u(r,t) = (1/n) ∫ v · f(r,v,t) d³vThe density-weighted average velocity — what a fluid description calls "the flow."

Second moment — pressure

Pressure measures the spread of velocities around the mean flow, not the mean flow itself — so you must subtract u first:

P_ij(r,t) = m ∫ (v_i − u_i)(v_j − u_j) · f(r,v,t) d³vThe pressure tensor. For an isotropic (equal in all directions) distribution it reduces to a scalar pressure p = (1/3)·trace(P) = n·k_BT.

Nothing stops you from continuing: the third moment is heat flux, and so on forever. This is the chain that eventually drowns you if you try to turn kinetic theory into fluid theory exactly (closing it at any finite moment always leaves one equation short — the famous closure problem). That's precisely why C3 (MHD) has to assume a closure, while this course works with f directly and sidesteps the problem entirely.

Common mistakeDon't confuse the ordinary density n(r) with the distribution function f(r,v). n(r) is a 3-D field — one number per point in space. f(r,v) is a 6-D field — one number per point in phase space. Moments are the bridge: they integrate velocity out, converting a 6-D object into the familiar 3-D fluid fields.

1.3The Maxwellian Distribution

Left alone and in thermal equilibrium (via collisions, however rare), a species settles into the Maxwellian distribution — the unique f that maximizes entropy for a given density, flow, and temperature:

f_M(v) = n · (m / 2πk_BT)^(3/2) · exp( −m|v−u|² / 2k_BT )A Gaussian in velocity, centered on the flow velocity u, with width set by the temperature T.

Plug f_M into the moment integrals of §1.2 and you get back exactly n, u, and p = nk_BT by construction — the Maxwellian is "the distribution that fluid theory is secretly always assuming." Whenever this course writes "the plasma is Maxwellian," it means: no hidden structure, no beams, no suprathermal tail — just a single bell curve in speed. Lectures 8 and 9 are entirely about what breaks (damping, instability) when that assumption fails.

✦ Worked Example — moments of a non-Maxwellian distribution

A 1-D toy distribution has a flat "top-hat" shape: f(v) = C for |v| ≤ v₀ = 1×10⁶ m/s, and f(v) = 0 otherwise, for electrons with density n₀ = 1×10¹⁹ m⁻³. Find C, the mean velocity, the pressure, and the effective temperature.

  1. Normalization: n₀ = ∫f dv = C·(2v₀) ⟹ C = n₀/(2v₀) = (1×10¹⁹)/(2×10⁶) = 5×10¹² (particles·s/m⁴).
  2. Mean velocity: the top-hat is symmetric about v=0, so u = (1/n₀)∫v·f dv = 0 by symmetry — no net flow.
  3. Pressure: p = mₑ∫(v−u)²f dv = mₑ·C·∫−v₀v₀v² dv = mₑ·C·(2v₀³/3) = mₑ·n₀·v₀²/3 = (9.109×10⁻³¹)(1×10¹⁹)(1×10⁶)²/3 ≈ 3.04 Pa.
  4. Effective temperature: matching p = n₀k_BT_eff gives T_eff = mₑv₀²/3k_B ≈ 1.90 eV — a well-defined "temperature" even though the distribution isn't Gaussian at all.
  5. Lesson: moments exist and are perfectly well-defined for any f, Maxwellian or not. A fluid description would hand you n₀, u=0, and T_eff≈1.90 eV and call it a day — with zero way to tell this flat-top distribution apart from a true Maxwellian of the same temperature. Only f itself remembers the difference.

🛠️ Hands-On: integrate a Maxwellian numerically

Reuse your Python + PlasmaPy environment from C1 Lecture 1 (pip install plasmapy matplotlib jupyter if you're starting fresh — see that lecture for the full install). This week we work mostly in plain NumPy: build a Maxwellian on a velocity grid and confirm its moments reproduce n, u, and p by direct numerical integration — the same check you'd run on any f you're handed.

# moments_demo.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import trapezoid
from scipy import constants as const

n0, Te_eV = 1e19, 10.0                      # m^-3, eV
v_th = np.sqrt(Te_eV * const.e / const.m_e) # thermal speed sqrt(kT/m)

v = np.linspace(-8*v_th, 8*v_th, 400_001)
f_M = n0 / (np.sqrt(2*np.pi)*v_th) * np.exp(-v**2 / (2*v_th**2))   # 1-D Maxwellian

n_num = trapezoid(f_M, v)
u_num = trapezoid(v*f_M, v) / n_num
p_num = const.m_e * trapezoid((v-u_num)**2 * f_M, v)

print(f"n_num = {n_num:.4e} m^-3   (target {n0:.0e})")
print(f"u_num = {u_num:.2e} m/s    (target 0)")
print(f"p_num = {p_num:.4f} Pa     (n0*kT analytic = {n0*Te_eV*const.e:.4f} Pa)")
# Verified output:
# n_num = 1.0000e+19 m^-3   (target 1e+19)
# u_num = 4.51e-11 m/s      (target 0)
# p_num = 16.0218 Pa        (n0*kT analytic = 16.0218 Pa)

The numerical pressure matches n₀k_BT_e to 6 significant figures — the integral is the physics. Try replacing f_M with the top-hat from the worked example and confirm you recover p ≈ 3.04 Pa and T_eff ≈ 1.90 eV numerically too.

🔗 Where this is heading

Every lecture from here on asks: how does f(r,v,t) evolve, and what structure can it hide that a fluid can't? Lecture 2 derives the equation that governs f itself — the Vlasov equation — straight from the fact that collisionless particles simply carry their own phase-space value along their trajectories. By Lecture 4 you'll see the Maxwellian's velocity spread show up as a literal pressure term correcting the plasma frequency, and by Lectures 8–9 you'll see that a non-Maxwellian f — even one with the exact same n, u, and T — can make a wave damp or grow without a single collision. The distribution function is the one object patient enough to remember.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The distribution function f(r,v,t) is defined so that f d³r d³v equals…
A. the total number of particles in the plasma
B. the number of particles in a small volume of position-velocity (phase) space
C. the probability that a single particle exists
D. the electric field at position r
Show answer

B. f is a phase-space number density: f·d³r·d³v counts particles in a small 6-D box around (r,v). (A) ignores the spatial/velocity resolution; (C)/(D) describe unrelated quantities.

Q2. Which velocity moment of f gives the fluid flow velocity u(r,t)?
A. The first moment, (1/n)∫v·f d³v
B. The zeroth moment, ∫f d³v
C. The second moment (pressure tensor)
D. None — u cannot be recovered from f
Show answer

A. The density-weighted average of v is exactly the first moment. The zeroth moment (B) gives density n; the second (C) gives pressure; (D) is false — all fluid quantities are moments of f.

Q3. Two distributions have identical density, flow velocity, and temperature — one is a true Maxwellian, the other is a symmetric two-humped (bimodal) distribution with the same variance. A fluid description...
A. correctly distinguishes the two cases
B. reports a different density for each
C. only works for the Maxwellian case
D. cannot distinguish them — fluid variables are identical by construction
Show answer

D. n, u, and p (hence T) are moments — the same three numbers can come from infinitely many different f's. Only the full distribution function carries the extra structure (and, as later lectures show, that structure is exactly what drives Landau damping or instabilities).

Q4. In the worked-example top-hat distribution (flat, width 2v₀, density n₀), the pressure came out to p = mₑn₀v₀²/3. What produced the factor of 1/3?
A. An arbitrary normalization choice
B. The three spatial dimensions of real space
C. The result of ∫v² dv over the top-hat's support, divided by its width
D. A typo — it should be 1/2
Show answer

C. ∫₋v₀^v₀ v² dv = 2v₀³/3; dividing by the normalization C = n₀/(2v₀) and multiplying by mₑ leaves exactly mₑn₀v₀²/3. It's a genuine result of doing the integral, not a dimensional coincidence (this is a 1-D toy model).

Q5. The Maxwellian distribution is special because it is the…
A. only distribution with a well-defined density
B. maximum-entropy distribution for given n, u, and T
C. only distribution that satisfies the Vlasov equation
D. distribution with zero pressure
Show answer

B. Among all distributions with the same density, flow, and temperature, the Maxwellian uniquely maximizes entropy — it's what collisions relax a plasma toward. (A) any normalizable f has a density; (C) many f's solve Vlasov, including non-Maxwellians (Lectures 8–9 depend on exactly that); (D) a Maxwellian has pressure p=nk_BT>0.
