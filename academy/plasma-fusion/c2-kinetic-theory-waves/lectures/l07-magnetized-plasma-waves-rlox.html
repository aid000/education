Waves in Magnetized Plasma: R, L, O, X (Intro)

Switch on a background magnetic field and Lecture 6's single cutoff splits in two. This lecture is a qualitative map of the magnetized-plasma wave zoo — enough to navigate it, not the full cold-plasma tensor derivation.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a background B₀ makes wave propagation depend on direction (parallel vs. perpendicular).
  • State the R- and L-wave cutoff frequencies for propagation along B₀, and verify their algebraic identities.
  • Describe the O-mode and X-mode for propagation across B₀, and state the upper-hybrid resonance.
  • Compute and order ω_ce, ω_pe, ω_L, ω_UH, ω_R for a representative lab-plasma example.
  • Distinguish the R/L (polarization) naming from the O/X (geometry) naming.

7.1A Magnetic Field Breaks the Symmetry

Every wave so far lived in an unmagnetized plasma, where no direction is special — the dispersion relation only ever depended on |k|. Add a background field B₀ and that symmetry is gone: electrons now gyrate around B₀ at the cyclotron frequency ωce=eB₀/me (C1, Lecture 3), and a wave's behavior depends on whether it travels along B₀ or across it. This lecture surveys both cases at a qualitative level, introducing the standard names — R, L (parallel propagation) and O, X (perpendicular propagation) — without deriving the full cold-plasma dielectric tensor (that's a topic for a dedicated plasma-waves course beyond this introduction).

7.2Parallel Propagation: the R- and L-Waves

For a wave traveling exactly along B₀, the natural description is in terms of circular polarization. Right-hand (R) and left-hand (L) circularly polarized waves see different effective dielectric responses, because the gyrating electrons resonate differently with each rotation sense. Each has its own cutoff — the frequency where k→0 and the wave can no longer propagate:

ωR = [ ωce + √(ωce² + 4ωpe²) ] / 2     ωL = [ −ωce + √(ωce² + 4ωpe²) ] / 2R- and L-wave cutoffs for propagation along B₀. Both reduce to the unmagnetized cutoff ω_pe when ω_ce→0, recovering Lecture 6.

Two clean identities fall straight out of these formulas (and are a good self-check on any numbers you compute): ωR·ωL = ωpe² and ωR−ωL = ωce. Since ωce>0, the R cutoff always sits above ωpe and the L cutoff always sits below it — the magnetic field splits the single unmagnetized cutoff into a pair straddling ωpe.

7.3Perpendicular Propagation: O-Mode, X-Mode, and the Upper Hybrid

For a wave traveling across B₀, the relevant split is by linear polarization relative to B₀, not circular rotation sense. The ordinary (O) mode has its E-field parallel to B₀ — electrons oscillate along the field, where B₀ exerts no force on them at all (v is parallel to B, so v×B=0), so the O-mode doesn't even notice the magnetic field: it obeys exactly Lecture 6's unmagnetized relation, ω²=ωpe²+k²c².

The extraordinary (X) mode has E perpendicular to both B₀ and k, so electrons are pushed across field lines and the magnetic force matters directly. The X-mode's dispersion is more involved, but it has one especially important feature: a resonance (where k→∞, not a cutoff) at the upper-hybrid frequency:

ωUH² = ωpe² + ωce²The upper-hybrid resonance — the natural frequency of an electron that is simultaneously gyrating (ω_ce) and plasma-oscillating (ω_pe). It sits between ω_L and ω_R.
AnalogyA magnetized plasma acts like a birefringent crystal: the two circular polarizations (R and L) propagate at different speeds along B₀, exactly as the two polarizations of light propagate at different speeds through calcite. Send in a linearly polarized wave (an equal mix of R and L) and the two components accumulate different phase as they travel — the polarization axis rotates. This is Faraday rotation, used to measure electron density and magnetic field in everything from the solar wind to tokamak diagnostics.
Common mistakeDon't conflate the two naming schemes. R/L describe circular polarization for propagation along B₀. O/X describe linear polarization relative to B₀ for propagation across B₀. They answer different questions (rotation sense vs. field alignment) for different geometries — a wave isn't simultaneously "R" and "X," because those labels apply to different propagation directions.
✦ Worked Example — the magnetized-plasma frequency ladder

n_e = 1×10¹⁹ m⁻³, B₀ = 0.1 T (a fairly weak lab field). Find ω_ce, ω_pe, ω_L, ω_UH, ω_R.

  1. Cyclotron frequency: ω_ce = eB₀/m_e = 1.759×10¹⁰ rad/s (f_ce ≈ 2.80 GHz).
  2. Plasma frequency: ω_pe = 1.784×10¹¹ rad/s (f_pe ≈ 28.39 GHz) — ten times larger than ω_ce here, a "weakly magnetized" regime.
  3. L cutoff: ω_L = [−1.759×10¹⁰ + √((1.759×10¹⁰)²+4(1.784×10¹¹)²)]/2 ≈ 1.698×10¹¹ rad/s (27.03 GHz).
  4. Upper hybrid: ω_UH = √(ω_pe²+ω_ce²) ≈ 1.793×10¹¹ rad/s (28.53 GHz).
  5. R cutoff: ω_R = [1.759×10¹⁰ + √((1.759×10¹⁰)²+4(1.784×10¹¹)²)]/2 ≈ 1.874×10¹¹ rad/s (29.83 GHz).
  6. Check: ω_R·ω_L = 3.1826×10²² = ω_pe² exactly ✓; ω_R−ω_L = 1.759×10¹⁰ = ω_ce exactly ✓.

🛠️ Hands-On: the frequency ladder in Python

# magnetized_cutoffs.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
from scipy import constants as const

n_e, B0 = 1e19, 0.1
w_ce = const.e * B0 / const.m_e
w_pe = np.sqrt(n_e * const.e**2 / (const.epsilon_0 * const.m_e))
D = np.sqrt(w_ce**2 + 4*w_pe**2)
w_R, w_L = (w_ce + D)/2, (-w_ce + D)/2
w_UH = np.sqrt(w_pe**2 + w_ce**2)

for name, w in [("w_ce", w_ce), ("w_pe", w_pe), ("w_L", w_L), ("w_UH", w_UH), ("w_R", w_R)]:
    print(f"{name:5s} = {w:.4e} rad/s  (f = {w/2/np.pi/1e9:.3f} GHz)")

print("ordering w_L < w_pe < w_UH < w_R :", w_L < w_pe < w_UH < w_R)
print("w_R*w_L / w_pe**2 =", w_R*w_L/w_pe**2, " (should be 1)")
print("(w_R - w_L) / w_ce =", (w_R - w_L)/w_ce, " (should be 1)")
# Verified output:
# w_ce  = 1.7588e+10 rad/s  (f = 2.799 GHz)
# w_pe  = 1.7840e+11 rad/s  (f = 28.393 GHz)
# w_L   = 1.6982e+11 rad/s  (f = 27.028 GHz)
# w_UH  = 1.7926e+11 rad/s  (f = 28.531 GHz)
# w_R   = 1.8741e+11 rad/s  (f = 29.827 GHz)
# ordering w_L < w_pe < w_UH < w_R : True
# w_R*w_L / w_pe**2 = 1.0000000000000002  (should be 1)
# (w_R - w_L) / w_ce = 1.0  (should be 1)

🔗 Where this is heading

Magnetized cutoffs and resonances are the basis of microwave heating and diagnostics in real fusion devices — electron-cyclotron and upper-hybrid resonances are deliberately targeted to deposit RF power exactly where you want it in a tokamak, and reflectometry (bouncing microwaves off a cutoff layer) maps density profiles in real time. For the rest of this course, though, we return to unmagnetized electrostatic waves and go kinetic: Lecture 8 switches the resonance pole from Lecture 3 back on and asks what a Maxwellian's shape does to the Langmuir wave of Lecture 4.

Q1. Why does a background magnetic field make wave propagation direction-dependent?
A. It changes the electron charge
B. It introduces a preferred axis (B₀), so the plasma's response differs for propagation along vs. across that axis
C. It increases the plasma density
D. It has no effect on wave propagation
Show answer

B. Without B₀ no direction is special (Lecture 6's isotropic result). With B₀, electron gyration about that one axis breaks the symmetry, so waves along B₀ and across B₀ obey genuinely different dispersion relations.

Q2. As ω_ce → 0, the R- and L-wave cutoffs ω_R, ω_L both approach…
A. zero
B. ω_pe — recovering the unmagnetized cutoff of Lecture 6
C. infinity
D. ω_UH
Show answer

B. Setting ω_ce=0 in both formulas gives ω_R=ω_L=ω_pe — the magnetized results correctly reduce to Lecture 6's single unmagnetized cutoff when the field is switched off.

Q3. The O-mode obeys the same dispersion relation as the unmagnetized EM wave (ω²=ω_pe²+k²c²) because…
A. it travels along B₀
B. its E-field is parallel to B₀, so electrons move along field lines where the magnetic force v×B vanishes
C. O-mode waves have no electric field at all
D. the plasma is unmagnetized in that case
Show answer

B. Electron velocity parallel to B₀ makes v×B=0 — the Lorentz force never engages — so the O-mode simply doesn't feel the magnetic field, even though it propagates perpendicular to it.

Q4. In the worked example (n_e=1e19 m⁻³, B₀=0.1 T), the frequencies satisfy ω_R·ω_L = …
A. ω_ce²
B. ω_pe²
C. zero
D. ω_UH²
Show answer

B. This is an exact algebraic identity from the quadratic cutoff formulas, confirmed numerically to machine precision in the hands-on code: ω_R·ω_L=ω_pe² and separately ω_R−ω_L=ω_ce.

Q5. R/L and O/X are different naming schemes because…
A. they're interchangeable names for the same four waves
B. R/L describe circular polarization for propagation along B₀; O/X describe linear polarization relative to B₀ for propagation across it
C. R/L only apply to ions, O/X only to electrons
D. O/X describe unmagnetized plasma only
Show answer

B. The two schemes answer different questions for different geometries — rotation sense along B₀ (R/L) versus field-alignment across B₀ (O/X) — and shouldn't be mixed as if a wave carried both labels for the same propagation direction.
