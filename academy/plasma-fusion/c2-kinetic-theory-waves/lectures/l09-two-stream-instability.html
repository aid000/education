Kinetic Instabilities: the Two-Stream Instability

Flip the sign of Lecture 8's slope on purpose, and the exact same resonance that drained energy from a wave now pumps energy into it, without bound — until something nonlinear finally stops it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the general principle: a positive slope in f₀ at the resonant velocity drives growth instead of damping.
  • Derive the two-cold-beam dispersion relation and reduce it to a quadratic in ω².
  • Identify the instability condition kv₀<ω_pe and derive the maximum growth rate and the k at which it occurs.
  • Compute numerical growth rates and growth times for a representative counter-streaming beam configuration.
  • Connect the two-stream mechanism to the broader class of velocity-space-driven instabilities relevant to fusion plasmas.

9.1Flipping the Sign

Lecture 8's whole mechanism hinged on one sign: a Maxwellian's negative ∂f₀/∂v at the resonant velocity drains the wave. Nothing in the underlying physics requires that sign to be negative — it's simply what a single-humped, monotonically falling distribution gives you. Build an f₀ with a positive slope somewhere (a dip between two peaks, a beam riding on a bulk population, or — the cleanest case — two separate beams moving in opposite directions) and the identical resonance mechanism now pumps energy into the wave. The wave amplitude grows exponentially instead of decaying: a kinetic instability.

9.2The Two-Cold-Beam Dispersion Relation

The cleanest concrete case: two cold (zero-temperature) electron beams, each with density n₀/2, counter-streaming at ±v₀, with ions as a fixed neutralizing background (total electron density n₀, exactly as in Lectures 4–8). Each beam is a cold fluid, so each contributes a term to the dispersion relation structurally identical to the cold-beam limit of Lecture 3, just centered on ±v₀ instead of 0:

1 = (ωpe²/2) · [ 1/(ω−kv₀)² + 1/(ω+kv₀)² ]ω_pe² = n₀e²/ε₀m_e uses the total density n₀ (both beams combined). Each beam individually carries half that density, hence the factor of 1/2 on each resonance term.

Clearing denominators and writing X≡ω² (in units of ωpe², with q≡kv₀/ωpe), this reduces to an ordinary quadratic:

X² − (2q²+1)X + (q⁴−q²) = 0     X = ω²/ωpe², q = kv₀/ωpeSolvable by the quadratic formula. The discriminant 8q²+1 is always positive, so X is always real — but X can be negative, which makes ω itself purely imaginary (pure growth or pure decay, no oscillation).

The smaller root is X₋ = [(2q²+1) − √(8q²+1)]/2, and a short check shows X₋<0 exactly when q²<1, i.e.:

Instability condition:   kv₀ < ωpeWhenever the beam speed exceeds the wave's natural phase-matching scale, a band of unstable k exists — the two-stream instability.

9.3The Growth Rate and Its Maximum

Inside the unstable band, γ ≡ √(−X₋)·ωpe gives the exponential growth rate (ω=iγ, field ∝ eγt). Maximizing γ(q) over q gives a clean, exact result:

γmax = ωpe / (2√2) ≈ 0.3536·ωpe     at    kv₀ = ωpe√(3/8) ≈ 0.6124·ωpeThe fastest-growing mode sits well inside the unstable band 0<kv₀<ω_pe, not at its edge — and grows at over a third of the plasma frequency itself. This is an extremely fast instability by plasma-physics standards.
AnalogyThink of two streams of cars merging at an intersection from opposite directions, moving at different average speeds relative to some reference. A perturbation that slightly bunches cars gets reinforced: relative motion between the two streams converts their kinetic energy into bunching — exactly like how relative drift between the two beams here converts directed kinetic energy into a growing electrostatic wave. The "intersection" is velocity space; the "merge" is the resonance at v=ω/k landing between the two beams.
✦ Worked Example — growth rate and growth time

Two counter-streaming electron beams, total density n₀=1×10¹⁹ m⁻³, beam speed v₀=1×10⁶ m/s. Find ω_pe, the fastest-growing k, the maximum growth rate, and the e-folding time.

  1. Plasma frequency: ω_pe = √(n₀e²/ε₀m_e) ≈ 1.784×10¹¹ rad/s (same formula as every previous lecture).
  2. Fastest-growing wavenumber: k_max = ω_pe√(3/8)/v₀ ≈ 1.092×10⁵ m⁻¹ (wavelength 2π/k ≈ 57.5 µm).
  3. Maximum growth rate: γ_max = ω_pe/(2√2) ≈ 6.307×10¹⁰ rad/s.
  4. Growth (e-folding) time: 1/γ_max ≈ 1.585×10⁻¹¹ s (15.9 picoseconds) — the perturbation amplitude multiplies by e roughly every 16 ps, and by e¹⁰≈22,000 in well under a nanosecond.
  5. Takeaway: this is a violently fast instability. In real beams and counter-streaming plasmas, it saturates nonlinearly (particle trapping in the growing wave, flattening the unstable part of f₀) long before the linear exponential could run away forever — the subject of more advanced treatments beyond this course.
Common mistakeThe two-stream instability requires a genuinely non-monotonic (multi-humped) f₀ — a single Maxwellian, no matter how hot, never goes unstable this way, because its slope never changes sign (it's always negative for v>0 and always positive for v<0, consistent with pure damping at any resonant velocity). This qualitative fact — stability for single-humped f₀, possible instability for multi-humped f₀ — is the heart of the Penrose stability criterion, a general test (beyond this course's scope to derive) for whether any given f₀ supports an unstable mode.

🛠️ Hands-On: solving the quadratic and maximizing γ(k)

# two_stream.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize_scalar
from scipy import constants as const

n0, v0 = 1e19, 1e6
w_pe = np.sqrt(n0 * const.e**2 / (const.epsilon_0 * const.m_e))

def gamma_over_wpe(q):               # q = k*v0/w_pe
    Xminus = ((2*q**2+1) - np.sqrt(8*q**2+1)) / 2
    return np.sqrt(-Xminus) if Xminus < 0 else 0.0

res = minimize_scalar(lambda q: -gamma_over_wpe(q), bounds=(1e-3, 0.999), method="bounded")
q_max, g_max = res.x, -res.fun
k_max = q_max * w_pe / v0
gamma_max = g_max * w_pe

print(f"w_pe      = {w_pe:.4e} rad/s")
print(f"q_max     = {q_max:.4f}  (theory: sqrt(3/8) = {np.sqrt(3/8):.4f})")
print(f"gamma_max/w_pe = {g_max:.4f}  (theory: 1/(2*sqrt(2)) = {1/(2*np.sqrt(2)):.4f})")
print(f"k_max     = {k_max:.4e} m^-1")
print(f"gamma_max = {gamma_max:.4e} rad/s   growth time 1/gamma_max = {1/gamma_max:.4e} s")
# Verified output:
# w_pe      = 1.7840e+11 rad/s
# q_max     = 0.6124  (theory: sqrt(3/8) = 0.6124)
# gamma_max/w_pe = 0.3536  (theory: 1/(2*sqrt(2)) = 0.3536)
# k_max     = 1.0925e+05 m^-1
# gamma_max = 6.3073e+10 rad/s   growth time 1/gamma_max = 1.5855e-11 s

💬 Discussion 3 (Week 9)

Both Landau damping and the two-stream instability come from the exact same resonance mechanism in the exact same equation — the only difference is the local sign of ∂f₀/∂v at v=ω/k. Given that, is it fair to call Landau damping and the two-stream instability "two different phenomena," or are they better described as two outcomes of one underlying process depending on what shape f₀ happens to have? What practical difference does the labeling make for someone trying to predict whether a given plasma (say, a fusion device with a population of fast fusion-born ions, or a beam injected for heating) will be stable?

🔗 Where this is heading

Counter-streaming beams are a clean toy model, but the same physics governs real, consequential instabilities: a fast ion population (from fusion reactions or neutral-beam heating) riding on a thermal bulk can drive similar "bump-on-tail" modes in tokamaks; runaway electron beams in disruption events are a two-stream-adjacent concern; and astrophysical shocks and the solar wind are full of counter-streaming particle populations doing exactly this. Q1. The general principle linking Lectures 8 and 9 is that a wave grows or damps depending on…
A. the total density of the plasma
B. the sign of ∂f₀/∂v evaluated at the resonant velocity v=ω/k
C. whether the plasma is magnetized
D. the sign of the charge of the species
Show answer

B. Negative slope at resonance (Maxwellian) damps; positive slope at resonance (two-stream, bump-on-tail) grows. Same mechanism, opposite sign.

Q2. The two-cold-beam dispersion relation 1=(ω_pe²/2)[1/(ω−kv₀)²+1/(ω+kv₀)²] reduces to a quadratic in X=ω² because…
A. cold beams always give linear equations
B. clearing the two resonance denominators and simplifying leaves only even powers of ω
C. ω_pe is assumed to be zero
D. only one beam actually contributes
Show answer

B. The symmetric ±v₀ structure makes the cleared-denominator equation depend only on ω² (and k²), so substituting X=ω² gives an ordinary quadratic, solvable exactly.

Q3. The instability condition for the symmetric two-beam model is…
A. kv₀ > ω_pe
B. kv₀ < ω_pe
C. k > 0 always
D. v₀ = 0
Show answer

B. The smaller root X₋ of the quadratic is negative (giving a real, growing γ) exactly when q=kv₀/ω_pe<1, i.e. kv₀<ω_pe. Beyond that, both roots are positive and the modes are ordinary stable oscillations.

Q4. The maximum growth rate γ_max = ω_pe/(2√2) occurs at…
A. the edge of the unstable band, kv₀=ω_pe
B. k=0
C. kv₀ = ω_pe√(3/8), strictly inside the unstable band
D. there is no maximum; γ increases without bound
Show answer

C. Maximizing γ(q) over the unstable band 0<q<1 gives an interior maximum at q=√(3/8)≈0.612, not at either edge — confirmed both analytically and by numerical optimization in the hands-on code.

Q5. Why can't a single Maxwellian ever drive a two-stream-type instability?
A. Maxwellians have zero density
B. A single-humped Maxwellian's slope never changes sign, so it can only damp, never grow, at any resonant velocity
C. Maxwellians are always magnetized
D. Instabilities require exactly two species
Show answer

B. ∂f₀/∂v is negative for v>0 and positive for v<0 for a Maxwellian, but the physically relevant comparison (more particles on the slow side than the fast side of resonance) always favors damping for a single peak — you need a non-monotonic, multi-humped f₀ (like two beams) to get a region that favors growth.
