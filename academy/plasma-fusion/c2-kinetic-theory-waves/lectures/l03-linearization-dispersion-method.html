Linearization & the Dispersion-Relation Method

One trick generates every wave result in this course: perturb the distribution function and the field together, demand they stay self-consistent, and read off ω(k) from where the algebra only has a solution if a determinant vanishes.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up the standard perturbation f = f₀+f₁, E = E₁ about a homogeneous, field-free equilibrium.
  • Apply the Fourier-Laplace ansatz exp(i(kx−ωt)) to linearize the Vlasov equation.
  • Solve the linearized Vlasov equation for f₁(v) in terms of E₁, and identify the resonance denominator.
  • Close the system with Poisson's equation to derive the general kinetic dielectric function ε(k,ω).
  • Recover the cold-plasma result ω=ω_pe as a limiting case, connecting back to C1.

3.1Perturbing the Equilibrium

Start from the simplest possible background: a spatially uniform plasma with no equilibrium electric field, where each species sits in some steady, field-free distribution f₀(v) — a Maxwellian, for instance, though nothing below requires that yet. Nudge it slightly:

f(x,v,t) = f₀(v) + f₁(x,v,t)     E(x,t) = 0 + E₁(x,t)f₀ and E₀=0 solve the unperturbed Vlasov-Poisson system exactly. f₁, E₁ are small — first order in some bookkeeping parameter ε, so we keep terms up to O(ε) and drop O(ε²).

Substitute into the 1-D electrostatic Vlasov equation (electrons; ions form a fixed, uniform neutralizing background — the same simplification used for the Langmuir wave in Lecture 4) and throw away the product of two small quantities, (e/m)E₁ · ∂f₁/∂v:

∂f₁/∂t + v·∂f₁/∂x − (e/m)E₁·(∂f₀/∂v) = 0Linearized Vlasov equation. The only surviving force term couples E₁ to the equilibrium gradient ∂f₀/∂v — this is why the shape of f₀, not just its moments, controls every wave property from here on.

Already this is the key structural fact of the whole course: whatever f₀ looks like, its velocity-derivative ∂f₀/∂v is what couples to the field. A flat f₀ (no derivative) can't drive anything; a Maxwellian's negative slope (for v>0) will turn out to damp waves (Lecture 8); two humps with a positive slope somewhere will turn out to grow them (Lecture 9). Everything downstream is this one equation's consequence.

3.2The Fourier-Laplace Ansatz & the Resonance Denominator

Because the linearized equation has constant coefficients in x and t, plane-wave perturbations f₁, E₁ ∝ exp(i(kx−ωt)) are exact normal modes. Substituting ∂/∂t → −iω and ∂/∂x → ik turns the PDE into pure algebra:

−iω f₁ + ikv f₁ − (e/m)E₁(∂f₀/∂v) = 0   ⟹    f₁(v) = (ie/m)·E₁·(∂f₀/∂v) / (ω−kv)Solve directly for f₁ in terms of E₁. The denominator (ω−kv) vanishes at v = ω/k — the resonance velocity, where a particle moves at exactly the wave's phase velocity.

That pole is not a mistake — it is the single most important feature of kinetic plasma theory. Particles near v=ω/k ride along with the wave instead of streaming past it, so they experience something closer to a constant force rather than an oscillating one, and they exchange energy with the wave far more efficiently than the bulk. For now (building the fluid-flavored dispersion relations of Lectures 4–7) we'll keep ω/k comfortably outside the bulk of f₀ so the pole causes no trouble. Lecture 8 is entirely about what happens when it can't be avoided.

Common mistakeDon't integrate straight through v=ω/k as if 1/(ω−kv) were an ordinary, well-behaved function. For real ω and k it's a genuine singularity sitting on the path of integration you're about to take in §3.3. Landau's 1946 resolution — treat ω as the limit of a complex frequency approaching the real axis from above, which prescribes a specific contour deformation around the pole — is deferred to Lecture 8, where it's the whole point.

3.3Closing the Loop: the Kinetic Dielectric Function

f₁ alone isn't the answer — it has to be consistent with the field that supposedly produced it. Integrate f₁ over velocity to get the perturbed density, then feed that into Poisson's equation:

n₁ = ∫f₁ dv = (ie/m)E₁∫(∂f₀/∂v)/(ω−kv) dv     ikE₁ = −(e/ε₀)n₁Poisson's equation: the perturbed charge density is −e·n₁ (electrons only; the fixed ion background doesn't perturb).

Substitute n₁ into Poisson's equation. E₁ appears on both sides and cancels (any nonzero mode must satisfy the same self-consistency condition regardless of amplitude) — leaving a condition on ω and k alone. Rearranging with ωpe²=n₀e²/(mε₀) (C1's plasma frequency, now written with the full equilibrium density n₀=∫f₀dv) gives the general kinetic dispersion relation:

ε(k,ω) ≡ 1 − (ωpe²/(n₀k²)) ∫ (∂f₀/∂v)/(v−ω/k) dv = 0The plasma dielectric function. Any (k,ω) pair solving ε=0 is a normal mode the plasma can support — this single equation is the parent of Lectures 4 through 9.

This is the master formula. Every later dispersion relation in this course is ε(k,ω)=0 evaluated for a specific f₀: a cold beam, a warm Maxwellian expanded to leading order (Lecture 4's fluid shortcut), two counter-streaming beams (Lecture 9), or the full Maxwellian with its pole handled properly (Lecture 8).

✦ Worked Example — recovering ω = ω_pe from a cold beam

Take the coldest possible equilibrium: every electron at rest, f₀(v) = n₀δ(v). Show that ε(k,ω)=0 reduces to the familiar cold plasma oscillation from C1.

  1. Integrate by parts (boundary terms vanish since δ and its derivative vanish at v→±∞): ∫(∂f₀/∂v)/(v−ω/k) dv = ∫f₀(v)·(v−ω/k)⁻² dv, using d/dv[(v−ω/k)⁻¹] = −(v−ω/k)⁻².
  2. Apply the delta function: ∫n₀δ(v)/(v−ω/k)² dv = n₀/(0−ω/k)² = n₀k²/ω².
  3. Substitute into ε=0: 1 − (ωpe²/(n₀k²))·(n₀k²/ω²) = 1 − ωpe²/ω² = 0.
  4. Solve: ω² = ωpe² ⟹ ω = ωpe, independent of k — exactly the dispersionless plasma oscillation from C1, now derived as a special case of the general kinetic machinery.
  5. Check numerically: approximating δ(v) by a narrowing Gaussian and integrating directly confirms the integral → n₀k²/ω² as the width shrinks (verified below to 3 significant figures before the finite-difference step size itself becomes the limiting error) — the by-parts shortcut isn't just formal, it's the correct limit.

🛠️ Hands-On: confirm the cold-beam limit numerically

Approximate the delta function by a narrow Gaussian of width σ, numerically differentiate and integrate, and watch the resonance integral converge to the cold-plasma prediction n₀/a² (with a=ω/k) as σ→0.

# cold_beam_limit.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import quad

n0, a = 1.0, 2.0            # a = omega/k, kept away from the bulk (v~0)

for sigma in [0.5, 0.2, 0.1, 0.05]:
    f0  = lambda v: n0/(np.sqrt(2*np.pi)*sigma) * np.exp(-v**2/(2*sigma**2))
    f0p = lambda v, h=1e-6: (f0(v+h) - f0(v-h)) / (2*h)
    integrand = lambda v: f0p(v) / (v - a)
    val, _ = quad(integrand, -10, 10, limit=200)
    print(f"sigma={sigma:.2f}:  integral={val:.5f}   target n0/a^2={n0/a**2:.5f}")
# Verified output:
# sigma=0.50:  integral=0.32634   target n0/a^2=0.25000
# sigma=0.20:  integral=0.25790   target n0/a^2=0.25000
# sigma=0.10:  integral=0.25190   target n0/a^2=0.25000
# sigma=0.05:  integral=0.25047   target n0/a^2=0.25000   <- converging nicely

The integral tightens monotonically toward 0.25 as the beam narrows — numerical confirmation that the delta-function shortcut in the worked example is the genuine σ→0 limit, not just a symbolic convenience.

💬 Discussion 1 (Week 3)

The linearized Vlasov equation only kept terms where E₁ multiplies ∂f₀/∂v, and threw away (e/m)E₁·∂f₁/∂v as "second order." For a wave with a truly tiny amplitude this is clearly fine — but real plasma waves grow, and eventually their amplitude isn't tiny anymore. What physically has to happen to f₀ itself once a wave gets large enough that the dropped term can no longer be ignored? (This is the linear theory's built-in expiration date — nonlinear saturation, trapping, and wave-particle energy exchange beyond the linear regime are a natural extension once you've met Landau damping in Lecture 8.)

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the linearized Vlasov equation, which term is dropped as "second order small"?
A. ∂f₁/∂t
B. v·∂f₁/∂x
C. (e/m)E₁·∂f₁/∂v — the product of two perturbed quantities
D. (e/m)E₁·∂f₀/∂v
Show answer

C. E₁ and f₁ are both already small (first order); their product is second order and dropped. The surviving force term (D) couples E₁ to the equilibrium gradient, which is only first order overall.

Q2. The resonance denominator (ω−kv) in f₁(v) vanishes at…
A. v=0 always
B. v = ω/k — the wave's phase velocity
C. v = kω
D. only for complex ω
Show answer

B. Particles moving at exactly the phase velocity ω/k satisfy ω−kv=0 — these are the resonant particles central to Landau damping (Lecture 8) and the two-stream instability (Lecture 9).

Q3. The general kinetic dielectric function ε(k,ω)=0 was derived by combining the linearized Vlasov equation with…
A. the ideal gas law
B. Poisson's equation, requiring E₁ to be self-consistent with the charge density it creates
C. Newton's third law
D. the Maxwellian distribution specifically
Show answer

B. Closing the loop (§3.3) means demanding E₁ be consistent with the density perturbation it itself produces via Poisson's equation. (D) is wrong — ε(k,ω) is general; it holds for any f₀, with the Maxwellian only entering once you choose to evaluate it (Lecture 8).

Q4. For a cold beam f₀(v)=n₀δ(v), the worked example showed ε(k,ω)=0 reduces to…
A. ω² = ω_pe² + 3k²v_th²
B. ω = ω_pe, independent of k
C. ω = kc
D. ω = 0 always
Show answer

B. A perfectly cold (zero-temperature) beam gives back the k-independent plasma oscillation ω=ω_pe from C1 — the general kinetic formula correctly contains the simplest limiting case. (A) is the Bohm-Gross relation (Lecture 4), which needs a warm f₀.

Q5. Why does the shape of f₀(v) — not just its density, flow, and temperature — matter for wave physics?
A. It doesn't; only the moments of f₀ ever enter the dispersion relation
B. Because ∂f₀/∂v appears directly inside the dispersion-relation integral, so different-shaped f₀'s with identical moments give different ε(k,ω)
C. Because f₀ determines the magnetic field
D. Because only Maxwellian f₀'s can be perturbed
Show answer

B. ε(k,ω) contains ∫(∂f₀/∂v)/(v−ω/k)dv — a functional of the full shape of f₀, not just n, u, T. Two distributions with the same moments (Lecture 1's bimodal-vs-Maxwellian example) generally give different ε(k,ω) and different wave behavior.
