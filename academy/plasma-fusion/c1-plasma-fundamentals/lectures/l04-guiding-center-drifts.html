Guiding-Center Drifts

E×B drift moved every particle together. Bend the field lines or let the field vary in strength, and three new drifts appear — and this time ions and electrons go separate ways.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the guiding-center approximation and when it is valid (r_L ≪ field's length scale).
  • Derive the grad-B drift and explain why it depends on charge.
  • Derive the curvature drift for a particle streaming along a curved field line.
  • Derive the polarization drift for a time-varying E field.
  • Build the Lorentz-force integrator that is this week's project checkpoint, and confirm it reproduces E×B and grad-B drift.

4.1The Guiding-Center Approximation

Lecture 3's E×B drift assumed perfectly uniform E and B. Real confinement fields are never perfectly uniform — B typically gets stronger toward the center of a magnet and field lines curve. The guiding-center approximation handles this by assuming the fields change only gently over one gyro-orbit:

r_L / L_B ≪ 1L_B is the length scale over which B changes appreciably (e.g. |B|/|∇B|). When this holds, the particle's fast gyration and its slow guiding-center drift can be treated separately.

Under this assumption, the particle's motion splits cleanly into (1) fast gyration at ω_c around a (2) slowly drifting guiding center. Lecture 3's E×B drift was the first example of a guiding-center drift. Non-uniform fields add three more, each the time-average effect, over one gyro-orbit, of the field varying slightly across that orbit.

4.2The Grad-B Drift

Suppose B points along z but its magnitude grows in the y-direction (∇B ≠ 0, still no curvature). As the particle gyrates, it spends half its orbit where B is slightly stronger (tighter curvature, smaller instantaneous radius) and half where B is slightly weaker (looser curvature, larger radius). The orbit is no longer a closed circle — it's a path that curves a bit more on the strong-field side, and this asymmetry produces a net sideways drift of the guiding center:

v_∇B = ( m v_⊥² / (2qB³) ) B × ∇B  =  (1/2) v_⊥ r_L ( B × ∇B ) / B²Unlike E×B, this drift carries an explicit q in the denominator — ions and electrons drift in opposite directions.

That charge-dependence is the headline result: grad-B drift separates charge. Ions drift one way, electrons the other, and that charge separation drives an electric current across the field — exactly the kind of current that matters for tokamak equilibrium (Course 5).

4.3The Curvature Drift

Now let the field line itself bend, with local radius of curvature Rc (a vector pointing from the center of curvature out to the field line). A particle streaming along the curved line with parallel velocity v∥ feels a centrifugal-like effect in the guiding-center frame, which the Lorentz force must balance — producing a second charge-dependent drift:

v_R = ( m v_∥² / (qB²) ) ( R_c × B ) / R_c²Grows with v_∥² (the "centrifugal" energy along the field line), exactly analogous to a car needing more sideways force to round a curve at higher speed.

In almost any real confinement field, curvature and a field-strength gradient come together (a current-free vacuum field satisfies ∇×B = 0, which links the two), so v∇B and vR are usually quoted as a combined pair, and both scale the same way with charge — reinforcing rather than canceling.

AnalogyGrad-B and curvature drift are like riding a bicycle around a banked curve where the bank angle also changes with your height on the track. The grad-B piece is "the bank keeps changing as I wobble up and down"; the curvature piece is "I'm also going around a bend, so I need extra sideways lean just to keep turning." Both nudge you sideways, and both depend on which way you're facing (charge sign) — unlike a flat, straight E×B drift that pushes everyone the same way.

4.4The Polarization Drift

One more case: let E vary in time rather than space, with B uniform and static. The guiding center can't respond instantaneously — it needs a moment to "catch up" to the changing E×B drift, and that lag itself is a drift:

v_pol = ( m / (qB²) ) ( dE/dt )Also charge-dependent (note the q, not q²) — ions and electrons again separate, but this drift vanishes the instant E stops changing.

Polarization drift is typically much smaller than the others in a steady fusion plasma (it needs E to be changing fast), but it matters for wave physics and for transient events — and it's the mechanism that gives a plasma an effective "mass density" response to an oscillating field, analogous to a dielectric's polarization response in ordinary electromagnetism (hence the name).

Common mistakeDon't assume all guiding-center drifts affect the plasma the same way. E×B drift moves bulk plasma with zero net current. Grad-B, curvature, and polarization drifts all carry explicit charge-dependence and therefore do generate currents — the distinction isn't a technicality, it's the difference between "the whole plasma slides" and "the plasma starts separating charge."
✦ Worked Example — grad-B and curvature drift for a 1 keV proton

A proton with 1 keV of perpendicular kinetic energy (and, separately, 1 keV parallel) sits in B = 1 T with a field-gradient scale length L_B = |B/∇B| = 0.5 m and a field-line radius of curvature R_c = 1 m. Find r_L, v_∇B, and v_R.

  1. Perpendicular speed: v_⊥ = √(2·1000 eV/m_p) ≈ 4.38×10⁵ m/s.
  2. Gyrofrequency and Larmor radius: ω_ci = eB/m_p ≈ 9.58×10⁷ rad/s ⇒ r_L = v_⊥/ω_ci ≈ 4.57×10⁻³ m (4.6 mm).
  3. Grad-B drift: v_∇B = (1/2) v_⊥ r_L / L_B ≈ 0.5 × (4.38×10⁵) × (4.57×10⁻³) / 0.5 ≈ 2.0×10³ m/s — about 0.46% of v_⊥, confirming the guiding-center approximation (r_L/L_B ≈ 0.9%) is comfortably satisfied.
  4. Curvature drift: with v_∥ also giving 1 keV (v_∥ ≈ 4.38×10⁵ m/s), v_R = m_p v_∥² / (eB·R_c) ≈ 2.0×10³ m/s — comparable in magnitude to v_∇B here, as expected since both scale the same way with the field geometry.
  5. Direction: both drifts pick up an explicit q in their formula — flip the sign of the charge (switch to an electron) and both reverse direction, confirming these are charge-separating drifts.

🛠️ Hands-On: Project Checkpoint — Lorentz-force integrator reproduces grad-B drift

This is the Week 4 project milestone: build the numerical integrator you'll reuse through Week 10, and confirm it reproduces the analytic grad-B drift above. We extract the drift cleanly by subtracting off the fast gyration (the "guiding-center transform") rather than just averaging the raw velocity, which converges far faster.

# gradB_drift_check.py — Lecture 4 project checkpoint
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

q, m = 1.602176634e-19, 1.67262192369e-27   # proton
B0, L_B = 1.0, 0.5                            # Tesla, grad-B scale length (m)
KE = 100 * 1.602176634e-19                    # 100 eV perpendicular KE
v_perp0 = np.sqrt(2*KE/m)

def rhs(t, s):
    x, y, z, vx, vy, vz = s
    Bz = B0*(1 + y/L_B)                       # B grows linearly with y
    ax = (q/m)*(vy*Bz)
    ay = (q/m)*(-vx*Bz)
    return [vx, vy, vz, ax, ay, 0.0]

wc0 = q*B0/m
Tc0 = 2*np.pi/wc0
sol = solve_ivp(rhs, (0, 50*Tc0), [0,0,0, v_perp0,0,0],
                 t_eval=np.linspace(0, 50*Tc0, 20000), rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-14, method='DOP853')

x, y, vx, vy = sol.y[0], sol.y[1], sol.y[3], sol.y[4]
wc_local = q*B0*(1 + y/L_B)/m
X_gc = x + vy/wc_local                        # guiding-center transform removes gyration
slope = np.polyfit(sol.t, X_gc, 1)[0]          # linear fit => drift velocity

rL0 = v_perp0/wc0
analytic = -0.5*v_perp0*rL0/L_B
print(f"analytic v_gradB  = {analytic:.4e} m/s")
print(f"numeric (fit) v_gradB = {slope:.4e} m/s")
print(f"agreement: {100*(1-abs(slope-analytic)/abs(analytic)):.2f}%")

# analytic v_gradB  = -2.0000e+02 m/s
# numeric (fit) v_gradB = -2.0117e+02 m/s
# agreement: 99.42%

🔗 Where this is heading

Grad-B and curvature drift separating charge by sign is precisely the headache that shapes real toroidal confinement: a purely toroidal field alone (bent into a donut, no twist) drifts ions up and electrons down (or vice versa), charging the top and bottom of the device and driving the plasma straight out of confinement. Course 5's tokamaks fix this with a rotational transform — a helical field that averages the drift away over one trip around the torus. Next week's magnetic mirrors take a completely different route to confinement, trading the drift problem for a leak at both ends.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The guiding-center approximation is valid when…
A. the particle's Larmor radius is much smaller than the scale over which the field changes
B. the particle is at rest
C. the field is exactly uniform everywhere
D. the plasma has no electric field
Show answer

A. r_L ≪ L_B (the field's length scale) lets you separate fast gyration from a slow drift of the guiding center. (C) would make all the new drifts in this lecture vanish — they exist precisely because the field is *not* perfectly uniform.

Q2. Unlike the E×B drift, the grad-B drift…
A. depends explicitly on the particle's charge, so ions and electrons drift in opposite directions
B. is always zero in a tokamak
C. only affects electrons
D. does no work on the particle, unlike E×B
Show answer

A. The grad-B formula carries an explicit q, so the drift direction flips with charge sign — generating a current, unlike the charge-blind E×B drift. (D) is wrong; neither drift does work, since both arise from the (always non-work-doing) magnetic force.

Q3. The curvature drift v_R scales with the particle's velocity as…
A. v_⊥²
B. v_∥ (linear)
C. independent of velocity
D. v_∥²
Show answer

D. v_R ∝ m v_∥²/(qB²R_c) — it comes from the particle's motion *along* the curved field line, so it depends on v_∥², not the perpendicular speed that drives grad-B drift.

Q4. The polarization drift v_pol = (m/qB²)(dE/dt) is nonzero only when…
A. B is uniform
B. the electric field is changing in time
C. the particle has no charge
D. the magnetic field is changing in time
Show answer

B. v_pol is directly proportional to dE/dt — a static E field (dE/dt = 0) produces zero polarization drift, no matter how strong. It represents the guiding center's lag in catching up to a changing E×B drift.

Q5. A purely toroidal (donut-shaped, untwisted) magnetic field confines a plasma poorly because…
A. there is no field at all inside a torus
B. grad-B and curvature drifts separate ions and electrons vertically, building a charge imbalance that drives the plasma outward
C. E×B drift pushes all particles out of the device together
D. the plasma frequency exceeds the cyclotron frequency
Show answer

B. In a simple torus, grad-B and curvature drift both point vertically and depend on charge sign, so ions drift one way and electrons the other — the resulting vertical electric field then drives an outward E×B drift for the whole plasma. Tokamaks fix this with a helical (rotational-transform) field, covered in Course 5.
