Magnetic Mirrors & Adiabatic Invariants

Squeeze a field line and a gyrating particle feels a force pushing it back out — a magnetic "wall" with no material at all. One invariant explains why, and also explains why it only works some of the time.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the magnetic moment μ and show it is an adiabatic invariant along a slowly-varying field line.
  • Derive the mirror force from conservation of μ and total kinetic energy.
  • Define the mirror ratio R_m and derive the loss-cone angle.
  • Explain why particles inside the loss cone escape and compute the trapped/lost fraction for an isotropic population.
  • Connect the mirror's confinement limitation to the motivation for toroidal (donut-shaped) fields.

5.1The Magnetic Moment: an Adiabatic Invariant

A gyrating particle is a tiny current loop: charge q circling at frequency fc = ωc/2π encloses current I = qfc, and a current loop of area A = πrL² has magnetic moment μ = IA. Working this out in terms of the perpendicular kinetic energy gives:

μ = m v_⊥² / (2B)  =  (perpendicular KE) / Bμ behaves like the magnetic moment of a tiny bar magnet, oriented to oppose the field (diamagnetic) — the gyrating particle always "pushes back" against B.

The key fact — proved rigorously using the guiding-center ordering from Lecture 4 — is that μ is an adiabatic invariant: as the particle's guiding center drifts into regions of different field strength (provided B changes slowly compared to one gyro-orbit, the same condition from Lecture 4), μ stays constant to very good approximation, even though neither v⊥ nor B individually stays fixed.

AnalogyA figure skater spinning with arms out, then pulling them in, spins faster — angular momentum is conserved while the moment of inertia shrinks. μ conservation is similar in spirit: as B grows, the particle's "spin" (v_⊥) must grow too, to keep μ = mv_⊥²/(2B) fixed. The skater's arms are B; the spin rate is v_⊥.

5.2The Mirror Force

Combine μ conservation with conservation of total kinetic energy (B does no work, Lecture 3): as a particle's guiding center moves into stronger B, μ = mv⊥²/(2B) constant forces v⊥² to grow proportionally with B. But total KE = (1/2)m(v⊥² + v∥²) is fixed, so v∥² must shrink to compensate. The particle slows down along the field line as it climbs into stronger field — exactly as if a force were decelerating it:

F_∥ = −μ (dB/dl)A force along the field line, pointing away from the strong-field region, proportional to how fast B is increasing. This is the mirror force — purely a kinematic consequence of μ and energy conservation, no new physics required.

If B keeps increasing enough along the particle's path, v∥ can reach zero before the particle gets there — the particle reflects, turning around and heading back toward weaker field. A device built from two coils producing strong field at each end and weak field in the middle — a magnetic mirror — can trap particles that bounce back and forth between the two strong-field "throats," confining them along the field line without any physical wall.

5.3Mirror Ratio and the Loss Cone

Define the mirror ratio Rm = Bmax/Bmin (throat field over midplane field). Using μ conservation, a particle at the midplane with pitch angle θ (the angle between v and B, so v⊥ = v sinθ) reflects before reaching the throat exactly when sin²θ ≥ Bmin/Bmax = 1/Rm. Particles with pitch angle below a critical value never turn around — they reach the throat still moving forward and escape:

θ_loss = arcsin( √(1/R_m) ) = arcsin( √(B_min/B_max) )Any particle with midplane pitch angle θ < θ_loss (too much v_∥, too little v_⊥) falls in the "loss cone" and is not reflected — note this depends only on direction, not on speed or energy.

The loss-cone criterion is strikingly energy-independent: it's purely about the direction of a particle's velocity at the midplane, not its speed. A fast particle moving mostly along the field (small θ) escapes just as readily as a slow one with the same pitch angle.

Common mistakeThe loss cone is not about particles being "too energetic" to be trapped. A 10 keV particle aimed nearly straight down the field line (small θ) is lost, while a 10 eV particle aimed nearly perpendicular to B (θ near 90°, mostly gyration, little streaming) is trapped. What matters is the pitch angle, not the energy — confusing the two is the single most common mirror-physics error.
✦ Worked Example — loss cone angle and lost fraction

A lab mirror has B_min = 0.1 T at the midplane and B_max = 1.0 T at the throats (mirror ratio R_m = 10). Find the loss-cone half-angle, and — assuming an isotropic population of particles at the midplane — the fraction that fall inside the loss cone and escape.

  1. Mirror ratio: R_m = B_max/B_min = 1.0/0.1 = 10.
  2. Loss-cone angle: θ_loss = arcsin(√(1/10)) = arcsin(0.3162) ≈ 18.4°. Particles with pitch angle below 18.4° (measured from the field direction) at either end of their bounce escape.
  3. Lost fraction (isotropic distribution): for a unit sphere of velocity directions, the solid angle inside a half-angle θ is 2π(1−cosθ) out of 4π total, i.e. a fraction (1−cosθ)/2 per cone. With loss cones at both ends of the pitch-angle distribution (particles streaming toward either throat), the total lost fraction is 1−cosθ_loss ≈ 1 − cos(18.4°) ≈ 1 − 0.9487 ≈ 5.1%.
  4. Sanity check: a tighter mirror (larger R_m) shrinks θ_loss and the lost fraction — e.g. R_m = 5 gives θ_loss ≈ 26.6° and a lost fraction of about 10.6%, confirming looser mirrors (smaller R_m) leak more, as expected.

🛠️ Hands-On: loss-cone angle and lost fraction vs. mirror ratio

# mirror_loss_cone.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np

def loss_cone_deg(Rm):
    return np.degrees(np.arcsin(np.sqrt(1/Rm)))

def lost_fraction(Rm):
    theta = np.arcsin(np.sqrt(1/Rm))
    return 1 - np.cos(theta)          # both loss cones combined

for Rm in [2, 5, 10, 20, 100]:
    print(f"Rm={Rm:4d}: theta_loss={loss_cone_deg(Rm):6.2f} deg, lost fraction={lost_fraction(Rm)*100:5.2f}%")

# Rm=   2: theta_loss= 45.00 deg, lost fraction=29.29%
# Rm=   5: theta_loss= 26.57 deg, lost fraction=10.56%
# Rm=  10: theta_loss= 18.43 deg, lost fraction= 5.13%
# Rm=  20: theta_loss= 12.92 deg, lost fraction= 2.53%
# Rm= 100: theta_loss=  5.74 deg, lost fraction= 0.50%

Even a mirror ratio of 100 — difficult to engineer — still leaves a nonzero loss cone: about half a percent of an isotropic population starts inside it and escapes essentially on its first pass through the throat. That 0.5% is not a per-bounce attrition rate applied to particles that are already trapped — bouncing itself conserves μ and energy exactly, so a trapped particle (outside the loss cone) cannot bounce its way into the loss cone on its own. What does erode confinement over time is collisions (Lecture 6): any process that scatters a trapped particle's pitch angle can knock it into the loss cone, continuously refilling the cone and resupplying the escape channel. This collisional leak, not a sudden failure, is the magnetic mirror's fundamental limitation.

🔗 Where this is heading

No matter how large you make R_m, the loss cone never closes completely — mirrors always leak some fraction of particles every bounce time, and in a real plasma, collisions (Lecture 6) continuously scatter trapped particles into the loss cone, refilling it. This structural leak is exactly why magnetic-mirror fusion devices were largely abandoned in favor of closed, end-less geometries — tokamaks and stellarators, Course 5's subject — where there is no throat to escape through at all, at the cost of the charge-separating drifts from Lecture 4 that a closed torus must then correct for.

Q1. The magnetic moment μ = mv_⊥²/(2B) is called an adiabatic invariant because…
A. it is exactly conserved under any conditions whatsoever
B. it stays approximately constant as long as B varies slowly compared to one gyro-orbit
C. it only applies to electrons
D. it is conserved only in a uniform field
Show answer

B. "Adiabatic" here means slowly-varying-field conditions (the same guiding-center ordering from Lecture 4), under which μ is conserved to high accuracy even though v_⊥ and B individually change a great deal.

Q2. As a particle's guiding center moves from weak B into strong B (conserving μ and total KE), its parallel velocity v_∥…
A. increases
B. stays exactly constant
C. decreases
D. reverses sign immediately
Show answer

C. μ conservation forces v_⊥² to grow with B; since total KE is fixed, v_∥² must shrink to compensate — the particle decelerates along the field line, which is exactly the mirror force.

Q3. Whether a particle is trapped in a magnetic mirror depends primarily on…
A. its total kinetic energy
B. the plasma frequency
C. its charge sign only
D. its pitch angle at the midplane, compared to the loss-cone angle
Show answer

D. The loss-cone criterion sin²θ ≥ 1/R_m depends only on direction (pitch angle), not speed — a high-energy particle aimed mostly along B is lost just as readily as a low-energy one with the same pitch angle.

Q4. Increasing the mirror ratio R_m (making the throats relatively stronger)…
A. shrinks the loss-cone angle and reduces the lost fraction
B. has no effect on confinement
C. grows the loss-cone angle
D. closes the loss cone completely at any finite R_m
Show answer

A. θ_loss = arcsin(√(1/R_m)) shrinks as R_m grows, reducing — but, per (D), never eliminating — the lost fraction, since 1/R_m never reaches exactly zero for finite R_m.

Q5. The fundamental reason magnetic-mirror machines were largely abandoned in favor of toroidal devices is…
A. mirrors cannot reach any plasma density at all
B. mirrors have open field-line ends, so the loss cone creates an unavoidable, continuously-refilled particle leak
C. toroidal devices have no grad-B or curvature drift to worry about
D. mirrors cannot support any magnetic field strength
Show answer

B. Every mirror ratio leaves a nonzero loss cone, and collisions continuously scatter trapped particles into it — a structural leak. (C) is false; closed tori trade the mirror's end-leak for the charge-separating drift problem from Lecture 4, which they solve differently (via rotational transform), not by eliminating the drifts.
