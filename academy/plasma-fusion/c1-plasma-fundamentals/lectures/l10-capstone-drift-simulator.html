10.1One Integrator, Any Field

Every drift this course derived — E×B (Lecture 3), grad-B, curvature, polarization (Lecture 4) — comes from the exact same starting point: m dv/dt = q(E + v×B). Rather than solving that equation separately for each special case the way we did by hand, we can integrate it numerically for literally any E(r,t) and B(r,t), and the drifts will simply fall out of the resulting trajectory — no guiding-center approximation required on the simulator's part, only on ours when we go looking for the drift afterward.

dv/dt = (q/m)( E(r,t) + v × B(r,t) )    dr/dt = vA six-component first-order ODE system — exactly what solve_ivp (or a hand-rolled RK4) is built to integrate.

10.2The Trick: Extracting a Drift from a Gyrating Trajectory

A raw simulated trajectory is dominated by fast gyration (Lecture 3) — the drift itself is usually a small correction riding on top of a much larger circular motion. Simply averaging the raw velocity over a few gyroperiods works for E×B (its drift is often comparable in size to the gyration speed), but for the smaller grad-B and curvature drifts, naively averaging converges far too slowly to be useful.

The fix is the same guiding-center transform used to derive these drifts analytically in Lecture 4: subtract off the instantaneous gyro-radius vector from the particle's position to recover the guiding center directly, then fit its slope in time:

R_gc = r + ( ẑ × v ) / ω_c(r)For B along z. This removes the gyration to leading order using only the particle's own instantaneous velocity and local field — no separate knowledge of the drift is needed to compute it.
Common mistakeDon't just average vx(t) and vy(t) over "a few periods" and call it the drift. For a weak drift riding on strong gyration, that estimate is dominated by whatever fraction of a gyro-period happens to be left over at the end of your averaging window — the guiding-center transform above removes gyration almost exactly, rather than relying on it averaging away over enough cycles, and converges far faster and more reliably.

10.3Reproducing E×B Drift

Set up uniform E = E₀x̂, B = B₀ẑ, start the particle at rest, and integrate. The particle traces the classic cycloid from Lecture 3; averaging the velocity over the last several gyroperiods (E×B drift is large enough that simple averaging works fine here) should recover vE = E₀/B₀ in the −y direction.

10.4Reproducing Grad-B Drift

Now set E = 0 and let B = B₀(1 + y/LB)ẑ — a field that grows linearly in y, with no E field at all. Launch a particle with pure perpendicular velocity (v⊥ only, no v∥) and apply the guiding-center transform from Section 10.2 to its trajectory. The fitted slope should recover the analytic grad-B drift v∇B = −(1/2)v⊥rL/LB from Lecture 4 (negative, since B increases with y here).

🛠️ Hands-On: the full capstone simulator

This single script reproduces both drifts from Lectures 3 and 4, side by side, and reports the agreement with the analytic formulas — this is the deliverable for the final project report.

# drift_simulator.py — Lecture 10 capstone
import numpy as np
from scipy.integrate import solve_ivp

q, m = 1.602176634e-19, 1.67262192369e-27   # proton

def lorentz_rhs(t, s, E_func, B_func):
    x, y, z, vx, vy, vz = s
    Ex, Ey, Ez = E_func(x, y, z, t)
    Bx, By, Bz = B_func(x, y, z, t)
    ax = (q/m)*(Ex + vy*Bz - vz*By)
    ay = (q/m)*(Ey + vz*Bx - vx*Bz)
    az = (q/m)*(Ez + vx*By - vy*Bx)
    return [vx, vy, vz, ax, ay, az]

# ---- Case 1: ExB drift (uniform E and B) ----
E0, B0 = 1.0e4, 0.5                          # V/m, T
Efun = lambda x,y,z,t: (E0, 0, 0)
Bfun = lambda x,y,z,t: (0, 0, B0)
wc = q*B0/m
Tc = 2*np.pi/wc
sol = solve_ivp(lorentz_rhs, (0, 20*Tc), [0,0,0, 0,0,0], args=(Efun, Bfun),
                 t_eval=np.linspace(0, 20*Tc, 4000), rtol=1e-10, atol=1e-13)
vy_drift = np.mean(sol.y[4][sol.t > 15*Tc])     # average over the last 5 gyroperiods
print(f"ExB drift:    analytic = {-E0/B0:.4e} m/s,  numeric = {vy_drift:.4e} m/s")

# ---- Case 2: grad-B drift (B grows linearly with y, no E) ----
L_B = 0.5
Efun2 = lambda x,y,z,t: (0, 0, 0)
Bfun2 = lambda x,y,z,t: (0, 0, B0*(1 + y/L_B))
KE = 100 * 1.602176634e-19                    # 100 eV perpendicular KE
v_perp0 = np.sqrt(2*KE/m)
wc0 = q*B0/m
Tc0 = 2*np.pi/wc0
sol2 = solve_ivp(lorentz_rhs, (0, 50*Tc0), [0,0,0, v_perp0,0,0], args=(Efun2, Bfun2),
                  t_eval=np.linspace(0, 50*Tc0, 20000), rtol=1e-11, atol=1e-14, method='DOP853')
x2, y2, vx2, vy2 = sol2.y[0], sol2.y[1], sol2.y[3], sol2.y[4]
wc_local = q*B0*(1 + y2/L_B)/m
X_gc = x2 + vy2/wc_local                       # guiding-center transform (Section 10.2)
slope = np.polyfit(sol2.t, X_gc, 1)[0]
rL0 = v_perp0/wc0
analytic_gradB = -0.5*v_perp0*rL0/L_B
print(f"grad-B drift: analytic = {analytic_gradB:.4e} m/s,  numeric = {slope:.4e} m/s "
      f"({100*(1-abs(slope-analytic_gradB)/abs(analytic_gradB)):.2f}% agreement)")

# ExB drift:    analytic = -2.0000e+04 m/s,  numeric = -1.9995e+04 m/s
# grad-B drift: analytic = -4.0000e+02 m/s,  numeric = -4.0469e+02 m/s (98.83% agreement)

The grad-B case agrees to about 99% — the remaining ~1% is a genuine, physical higher-order correction (not a bug): r_L/L_B ≈ 0.58% here, and guiding-center theory is only the leading-order term in that small ratio. Shrinking r_L/L_B further (lower energy, or larger L_B) pushes the agreement even closer to 100%, exactly as the theory predicts.

Q1. Why does naive velocity-averaging work reasonably well for extracting the E×B drift but poorly for the grad-B drift?
A. E×B drift doesn't actually exist numerically
B. E×B drift is typically a much larger fraction of the gyration speed, so residual gyration contamination matters less relative to the signal
C. Grad-B drift requires a different integrator entirely
D. Averaging never works for any drift
Show answer

B. Grad-B drift is suppressed by the small ratio r_L/L_B relative to the gyration speed, so any leftover (incompletely-averaged) gyration signal is a much larger fraction of the small drift signal — making a cruder averaging method far noisier for grad-B than for the comparatively larger E×B drift.

Q2. The guiding-center transform R_gc = r + (ẑ×v)/ω_c works by…
A. ignoring the particle's velocity entirely
B. adding back the instantaneous gyro-radius vector to cancel the gyration motion, leaving only the slow drift
C. applying a low-pass filter in the frequency domain
D. averaging the magnetic field over all space
Show answer

B. Since the particle's displacement from its guiding center is exactly the gyro-radius vector (ẑ×v)/ω_c, adding it to the instantaneous position removes the gyration to leading order, revealing the smooth guiding-center drift directly.

Q3. In the capstone's grad-B test, the numeric result agreed with the analytic formula to about 99%, not exactly 100%. The most likely explanation is…
A. a bug in the integrator
B. a genuine higher-order physical correction, since the guiding-center drift formula is only the leading term in a small-r_L/L_B expansion
C. floating-point error in Python
D. the particle's charge was entered incorrectly
Show answer

B. The analytic grad-B drift formula is a leading-order result valid for r_L/L_B ≪ 1; a small, finite ratio (here ≈0.58%) leaves a correspondingly small, real correction that shrinks further as r_L/L_B is reduced — exactly what a direct numerical integration (which makes no such small-ratio approximation at all) should reveal.

Q4. If you doubled the perpendicular kinetic energy in the grad-B test (holding L_B and B0 fixed), you would expect the agreement between numeric and analytic drift to…
A. improve, since more energy means a more accurate simulation
B. get slightly worse, since r_L grows with v_⊥ and the guiding-center approximation is less accurate at larger r_L/L_B
C. stay exactly the same
D. become undefined
Show answer

B. r_L ∝ v_⊥, so a larger perpendicular energy increases r_L/L_B, pushing further from the guiding-center approximation's ideal (r_L/L_B ≪ 1) regime and producing a somewhat larger discrepancy with the leading-order analytic formula — consistent with the trend explored in Lecture 4's worked example.

Q5. What does this capstone demonstrate that the purely analytic derivations in Lectures 3 and 4 could not, on their own?
A. That the drift formulas are wrong
B. That the drift formulas are leading-order approximations whose accuracy and regime of validity can be checked directly against an independent, first-principles numerical solution of the same equation of motion
C. That magnetic fields do work on particles
D. Nothing new — the capstone is purely decorative
Show answer

B. Direct numerical integration of the exact Lorentz force makes no guiding-center approximation at all, so comparing its result to the analytic drift formulas is a genuine, independent check of both the formulas themselves and the specific regime (r_L/L_B, etc.) in which they hold.
