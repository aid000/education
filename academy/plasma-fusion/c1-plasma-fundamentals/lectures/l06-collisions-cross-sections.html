Collisions & Cross-Sections

Every "collisionless" derivation so far quietly assumed collisions don't matter. Time to check that assumption — and discover the strange rule that hotter plasmas collide less.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the 90° Coulomb-deflection impact parameter and cross-section from the impulse approximation.
  • Explain why many small-angle collisions dominate Coulomb scattering, and what the Coulomb logarithm lnΛ represents.
  • Compute collision frequency and mean free path from density, temperature, and lnΛ.
  • Derive Spitzer-style resistivity and explain why it falls as temperature rises.
  • State when the collisionless treatments of Lectures 2–5 are actually justified.

6.1The 90° Coulomb Collision

Two charged particles never truly "touch" — they interact through the long-range Coulomb force at every separation. To get a usable cross-section, start with the simplest possible encounter: how close must an electron pass an ion to be deflected by a full 90°?

Using the impulse approximation (assume the electron travels in a nearly straight line at speed v past an ion at impact parameter b, and compute the sideways momentum kick Δp⊥ it picks up), a 90° deflection happens when the sideways kick becomes comparable to the electron's own forward momentum, Δp⊥ ≈ mev. Working out Δp⊥ for a Coulomb field and setting it equal to mev gives the critical impact parameter:

b_90 = e² / (4π ε₀ m_e v²)Closer than b_90 ⇒ deflection greater than 90°; farther ⇒ a gentler nudge. This is the same combination of constants that set the Debye length in Lecture 1.

The cross-section for a 90°-or-sharper deflection is simply the disk area σ90 = πb90².

6.2Why Small-Angle Collisions Dominate: the Coulomb Logarithm

Here's the catch: single 90° encounters (b ≲ b90) are actually rare. Far more common are countless gentle, small-angle nudges from distant charges — and because the Coulomb force has infinite range, an electron is always being nudged by something. Summing the cumulative effect of all these small deflections (a calculation due to Chandrasekhar and Spitzer) shows that their combined effect on an electron's trajectory is actually larger than the rare large-angle encounters, by a factor called the Coulomb logarithm:

lnΛ = ln( λ_D / b_90 )The ratio of the largest relevant impact parameter (cut off at the Debye length — beyond λ_D, Lecture 1's shielding erases the field entirely) to the smallest (b_90). Typically lnΛ ≈ 10–20 for lab and fusion plasmas — large enough that the "sum of small nudges" genuinely dominates.

The practical upshot: the effective momentum-randomizing cross-section is enhanced over the naive 90°-collision estimate by this logarithmic factor:

σ_eff ≈ σ_90 · lnΛ = π b_90² lnΛA good order-of-magnitude working formula for an intro treatment. The exact numerical prefactor from a full Fokker–Planck (Spitzer) solution differs by a factor of order unity — the tabulated NRL Plasma Formulary values below use that more careful calculation.
AnalogyWalking through a crowded room, you rarely get shoved hard by any one person — but you're constantly jostled a little by everyone nearby, and over the length of the room those countless small jostles add up to more total deflection than the one person who actually bumped into you squarely. Coulomb collisions work the same way: distant, gentle nudges outweigh the rare close encounter, and lnΛ counts how much more they matter.

6.3Collision Frequency and Mean Free Path

With an effective cross-section in hand, the electron-ion collision frequency and mean free path follow exactly as for any gas:

ν_ei ≈ n_i σ_eff v_the     λ_mfp = v_the / ν_ei = 1/(n_i σ_eff)v_the is the electron thermal speed. ν_ei grows with density (more targets) and falls steeply with temperature (faster electrons, smaller σ ∝ 1/v⁴ once you track v through b_90² — collisions get rarer as the plasma heats up.)

The field-standard practical formula (NRL Plasma Formulary convention, which uses the more careful Fokker–Planck numerical prefactor) is:

ν_e ≈ 2.91×10⁻⁶ · n_e[cm⁻³] · lnΛ · T_e[eV]^(−3/2)  s⁻¹Same qualitative scaling as our simple σ_eff estimate (∝ n, ∝ T^−3/2), with a different O(1) numerical coefficient from the exact kinetic treatment.

6.4Spitzer Resistivity: Hotter Means Less Resistive

Resistivity in a plasma comes from exactly this same electron-ion momentum-randomizing collision rate, via the simple relation η = meνei/(nee²) (current carried by drifting electrons is resisted at rate νei). Since νei ∝ Te^(−3/2), so is η:

η_Spitzer ∝ lnΛ / T_e^(3/2)The practical NRL formula: η_⊥ ≈ 1.03×10⁻² · Z · lnΛ · T_e[eV]^(−3/2) Ω·cm (perpendicular Spitzer resistivity).

This is backwards from an ordinary metal, where resistivity rises with temperature (more lattice vibrations to scatter off). In a plasma, hotter electrons simply move too fast for the Coulomb field to deflect them much — there's no lattice, only the 1/v⁴ falloff of the Coulomb cross-section, so resistivity collapses as Te climbs.

Common mistakeDon't assume a hotter plasma is always "more collisional" because it has more energetic particles. The opposite is true for Coulomb collisions: higher speed means less time near any one charge and a smaller deflection cross-section, so νei and η both fall with Te. This is exactly the problem Discussion 2 asks you to confront: a fusion plasma gets harder to resistively heat the hotter it gets.
✦ Worked Example — collision frequency, mean free path, and resistivity

A lab plasma has n_e = 1×10¹⁹ m⁻³, T_e = 10 eV (and λ_D ≈ 7.43 µm from Lecture 1's formula). Estimate b_90, lnΛ, σ_eff, ν_ei, λ_mfp, and η using our simple first-principles σ_eff ≈ σ_90 lnΛ approach.

  1. Thermal speed: v_the = √(2T_e/m_e) ≈ 1.88×10⁶ m/s.
  2. 90° impact parameter: b_90 = e²/(4πε₀m_e v_the²) ≈ 7.20×10⁻¹¹ m.
  3. Coulomb logarithm: lnΛ = ln(λ_D/b_90) = ln(7.43×10⁻⁶/7.20×10⁻¹¹) ≈ ln(1.03×10⁵) ≈ 11.5.
  4. Effective cross-section: σ_eff = πb_90² · lnΛ ≈ (1.63×10⁻²⁰)(11.5) ≈ 1.88×10⁻¹⁹ m².
  5. Collision frequency: ν_ei = n_e σ_eff v_the ≈ (10¹⁹)(1.88×10⁻¹⁹)(1.88×10⁶) ≈ 3.5×10⁶ s⁻¹ (τ_coll ≈ 280 ns).
  6. Mean free path: λ_mfp = 1/(n_e σ_eff) ≈ 0.53 m — comparable to a 1 m lab device, meaning this particular plasma sits right at the collisional/collisionless boundary rather than cleanly on either side.
  7. Resistivity (our estimate): η = m_e ν_ei/(n_e e²) ≈ 1.3×10⁻⁵ Ω·m. The NRL practical formula gives η_⊥ ≈ 3.8×10⁻⁵ Ω·m for the same parameters — same order of magnitude, differing by the O(1) Fokker–Planck prefactor mentioned in Section 6.2.

🛠️ Hands-On: collisionality from first principles, with a PlasmaPy cross-check

# collisions.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np

e, eps0, me = 1.602176634e-19, 8.8541878128e-12, 9.1093837015e-31

def collision_estimate(n_e, Te_eV):
    v_the = np.sqrt(2*Te_eV*e/me)
    lam_D = 7430*np.sqrt(Te_eV/n_e)                 # Lecture 1 formula, meters
    b90 = e**2/(4*np.pi*eps0*me*v_the**2)
    lnLambda = np.log(lam_D/b90)
    sigma_eff = np.pi*b90**2*lnLambda
    nu_ei = n_e*sigma_eff*v_the
    mfp = 1/(n_e*sigma_eff)
    eta = me*nu_ei/(n_e*e**2)
    return dict(v_the=v_the, lam_D=lam_D, b90=b90, lnLambda=lnLambda,
                nu_ei=nu_ei, mfp=mfp, eta=eta)

r = collision_estimate(1e19, 10.0)
for k, v in r.items():
    print(f"{k:10s} = {v:.4e}")

# v_the      = 1.8755e+06
# lam_D      = 7.4300e-06
# b90        = 7.1998e-11
# lnLambda   = 1.1544e+01
# nu_ei      = 3.5261e+06
# mfp        = 5.3191e-01
# eta        = 1.2513e-05

Try doubling Te_eV and watch nu_ei and eta both fall — the "hotter is less collisional" signature. PlasmaPy's plasmapy.formulary.Coulomb_logarithm and fundamental_electron_collision_freq give the full Fokker–Planck-accurate versions if you want to compare against this simplified estimate.

💬 Discussion 2

Plasma resistivity falls as temperature rises — the opposite of a metal. Ohmic (resistive) heating, the simplest way to heat a plasma, works by driving a current through that resistivity and letting collisions convert current into heat. What problem does a falling η create for heating a fusion plasma all the way to ignition temperatures using Ohmic heating alone? What does a reactor need instead once η becomes too small to matter?

🔗 Where this is heading

The mean free path you just computed — and whether it's large or small compared to the device — is the single number that decides whether a fluid description (treating the plasma as a continuous, locally-thermalized gas, like ordinary fluid dynamics) or a kinetic description (tracking the full velocity distribution, since particles never collide enough to thermalize) is the right tool. Q1. The Coulomb logarithm lnΛ appears in collision-frequency formulas because…
A. it corrects for relativistic effects
B. the cumulative effect of many small-angle, long-range deflections dominates over rare single 90° collisions
C. it accounts for magnetic forces during a collision
D. it is a fitting parameter with no physical meaning
Show answer

B. lnΛ = ln(λ_D/b_90) sums the contribution of all the gentle distant encounters out to the Debye-shielding cutoff, and this sum exceeds the rare-close-encounter estimate by that logarithmic factor.

Q2. Raising the electron temperature T_e (holding density fixed) makes the electron-ion collision frequency ν_ei…
A. increase
B. decrease
C. stay exactly constant
D. become negative
Show answer

B. ν_ei ∝ T_e^(−3/2): faster electrons spend less time near any given ion and are deflected less, so collisions become rarer as the plasma heats up.

Q3. Why does Spitzer resistivity fall with increasing temperature, unlike resistivity in an ordinary metal?
A. Plasmas have no free charge carriers at high temperature
B. A metal's resistivity comes from lattice vibrations (which grow with T); a plasma's comes from Coulomb collisions (whose cross-section shrinks with T)
C. Plasma resistivity is actually independent of temperature
D. Hotter plasmas have fewer electrons
Show answer

B. The two systems' resistivity mechanisms are physically different: lattice scattering grows with T in a metal, while Coulomb collision cross-section shrinks with T (∝ v⁻⁴) in a plasma — opposite trends from different physics.

Q4. A plasma is well-described as "collisionless" on a given timescale when…
A. the mean free path is much shorter than the device size
B. the collision frequency is much slower than the process of interest (e.g. ω_pe τ_coll ≫ 1)
C. the density is exactly zero
D. the plasma parameter N_D is small
Show answer

B. "Collisionless" is always relative to a timescale: if the process of interest (gyration, an oscillation, a drift) completes many times before a collision randomizes a particle's velocity, collisions can be neglected for that process — which is exactly the implicit assumption behind Lectures 2–5.

Q5. In the worked example (n_e=10¹⁹ m⁻³, T_e=10 eV), the computed mean free path was about 0.53 m. What does this tell you about a 1 m lab device at these parameters?
A. The plasma is cleanly collisionless — ignore collisions entirely
B. The plasma is cleanly collisional — treat it as a fluid everywhere
C. The mean free path is comparable to the device size, so this plasma sits near the collisional/collisionless boundary and neither extreme approximation is automatically safe
D. The result is meaningless because mean free path doesn't apply to plasmas
Show answer

C. λ_mfp ≈ 0.53 m vs. L ≈ 1 m is an order-1 ratio — neither λ_mfp ≪ L nor λ_mfp ≫ L holds cleanly, so this plasma is a genuine edge case where the choice of fluid vs. kinetic treatment (Lecture 7) needs real justification.
