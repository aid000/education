Fluid vs Kinetic Descriptions (Preview)

Every plasma is secretly a swarm of individual particle trajectories. Most of the time you don't want to track them all — you want a handful of averages. Those averages are moments, and they're where fluid and kinetic descriptions meet.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the distribution function f(r, v, t) and interpret it as a density in phase space.
  • Compute the zeroth, first, and second velocity moments of f: density, flow velocity, and pressure/temperature.
  • State the fluid closure problem: why the moment hierarchy never terminates on its own.
  • Explain the trade-off between the kinetic (Vlasov) and fluid (MHD) pictures, and when each is appropriate.
  • See where this course sequence goes next: Vlasov theory in Course 2, MHD in Course 3.

7.1The Distribution Function

Every description in this course so far has either followed one particle exactly (Lectures 3–5) or treated the whole plasma as a smooth charge density (Lectures 1–2, 6). The distribution function fs(r, v, t) for species s unifies both views: it is the number density of particles of that species in six-dimensional phase space — three position coordinates and three velocity coordinates — at time t.

Concretely, fs(r, v, t) d³r d³v is the number of particles of species s found in a small box of real-space volume d³r centered at r, with velocities in a small box d³v centered at v. Knowing fs everywhere and for all time is equivalent to knowing the exact state of every particle — it is the most complete description a plasma admits without resorting to individual trajectories.

7.2Moments: Density, Flow, Pressure

A moment of fs is an average over the velocity coordinates alone, weighting by increasing powers of v. The first three moments are exactly the familiar fluid quantities:

n_s(r,t) = ∫ f_s d³vZeroth moment: integrate over all velocities, keep only position dependence. This is the ordinary number density.
u_s(r,t) = (1/n_s) ∫ v f_s d³vFirst moment: the density-weighted average velocity — the fluid flow velocity.
P_s(r,t) = m_s ∫ (v − u_s)(v − u_s) f_s d³vSecond moment: the spread of velocities around the mean flow — the pressure tensor. Its trace gives the familiar scalar pressure and, via p = nk_BT, the temperature.

These three moments are exactly the variables that appear in ordinary fluid dynamics — density, velocity, and pressure — so every fluid description of a plasma, no matter how it's eventually written, is really a statement about the low-order moments of f.

Analogyf(r,v,t) is like having every individual commuter's position and speed on a highway at every instant. The traffic report just says "density of 40 cars/km, average speed 60 km/h here" — that's the zeroth and first moment. It throws away exactly who is going faster or slower than average (that spread is the second moment, pressure), and definitely doesn't tell you any one driver's full history. Fluid variables are traffic reports; f is the full dashcam footage of every car.

7.3The Closure Problem

Here's the catch that makes fluid theory genuinely hard, not just a bookkeeping exercise. The exact evolution equation for fs (the Vlasov equation, Course 2's subject) implies an evolution equation for each moment — but the equation for the density ns involves the flow us; the equation for us involves the pressure Ps; the equation for Ps involves a third-order moment (the heat flux qs); and so on, forever. This infinite chain is the moment hierarchy, and it never terminates on its own:

∂n/∂t + ∇·(nu) = 0  ⟸ needs u    mn(∂u/∂t+...) = −∇·P+...  ⟸ needs P    ∂P/∂t + ... = −∇·q+...  ⟸ needs q, ...Each moment's evolution equation always needs the next-higher moment to close it. Exact for an infinite hierarchy; useless for a finite fluid model unless you cut it off somewhere.

To get a usable, finite fluid model, you must close the hierarchy by hand — assert some relationship (an equation of state, an adiabatic law, or a collisional transport law) that expresses the highest-kept moment in terms of lower ones, discarding everything above it. Different closures (isothermal, adiabatic, Spitzer-resistive, double-adiabatic) are appropriate in different regimes, and choosing the wrong one for your plasma's actual collisionality (Lecture 6!) is a classic way to get wrong physics out of a perfectly correctly-solved fluid model.

Common mistakeA fluid closure is not a mathematical derivation from first principles — it's a physical assumption smuggled in to stop the moment hierarchy. "Just add one more equation" always works algebraically but is only correct physics when the plasma's actual collisionality (Lecture 6) justifies that particular closure. A collisionless plasma (long mean free path) generally needs the full kinetic f, not a truncated fluid closure, to get the right answer for fast or fine-scale phenomena.

7.4When Each Picture Wins

The choice between kinetic (keep f, no closure needed, but six-dimensional and expensive) and fluid (keep only a few moments, cheap, but requires a closure) comes down to the ratio of the mean free path to the scale of interest — exactly Lecture 6's collisionality question:

Fluid (MHD) wins when collisions are frequent enough (λ_mfp ≪ L) that the velocity distribution relaxes to something close to a local Maxwellian everywhere — then a few moments really do capture almost everything that matters, and a simple closure (like an ideal-gas equation of state) is accurate. This is Course 3's regime.

Kinetic (Vlasov) wins when collisions are rare (λ_mfp ≳ L) or when you care about fine structure in velocity space itself — wave-particle resonances, runaway electrons, the exact shape of a non-Maxwellian tail — all things a few fluid moments simply cannot represent, no matter how clever the closure. This is Course 2's regime, and it's also precisely why Lecture 6's "edge case" worked example (λ_mfp comparable to device size) is uncomfortable: neither picture is automatically safe there.

✦ Worked Example — moments of a drifting Maxwellian

Take the simplest possible f: a 1D Maxwellian drifting at speed u₀, f(v) = n₀ √(m/(2πk_BT)) exp(−m(v−u₀)²/(2k_BT)). Compute its zeroth, first, and second moments directly from the definitions in Section 7.2.

  1. Zeroth moment (density): ∫f(v)dv = n₀ by construction — the Gaussian is normalized so its total area is the density n₀.
  2. First moment (flow velocity): (1/n₀)∫v f(v)dv = u₀ — a Gaussian's mean is its center, so the flow velocity recovers exactly the drift speed u₀ that was built into f.
  3. Second moment (pressure): m∫(v−u₀)²f(v)dv = n₀k_BT — the Gaussian's variance is k_BT/m by construction, so this moment recovers exactly the ideal-gas pressure p = n₀k_BT.
  4. The point: for a plasma that actually is locally Maxwellian (the frequent-collision regime), the fluid moments n, u, p capture 100% of the physics in f — nothing is lost. The closure problem only bites once f deviates from a Maxwellian, which is exactly when collisions are too rare to keep enforcing one.

🛠️ Hands-On: computing moments numerically from a sampled distribution

# moments.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
n0, u0, T_eV, m = 1e19, 3.0e4, 10.0, 9.1093837015e-31   # density, drift (m/s), temp (eV), electron mass
kBT = T_eV * 1.602176634e-19

v_thermal = np.sqrt(kBT/m)
N = 2_000_000
v_sample = rng.normal(loc=u0, scale=v_thermal, size=N)   # sampled 1D Maxwellian, drifting

n_moment  = N / N * n0                 # by construction (sample represents n0 particles)
u_moment  = np.mean(v_sample)
p_moment  = m * np.var(v_sample) * n0  # n0 * m * <(v-u)^2> = pressure

print(f"input u0 = {u0:.4e} m/s   | moment-recovered u = {u_moment:.4e} m/s")
print(f"input kBT = {kBT:.4e} J    | moment-recovered kBT = {p_moment/n0:.4e} J")

# input u0 = 3.0000e+04 m/s   | moment-recovered u = 3.0743e+04 m/s
# input kBT = 1.6022e-18 J    | moment-recovered kBT = 1.6017e-18 J

With 2 million samples, the recovered temperature lands within 0.04% of the exact input — the mean is noisier (off by about 2%) simply because a single thermal speed's worth of spread divided by √N leaves more sampling noise in the first moment than the (far more averaged) second moment. Either way, the residual is Monte Carlo sampling noise, not a flaw in the moment definitions themselves; it shrinks as 1/√N if you increase the sample size.

Q1. The distribution function f(r,v,t) is best described as…
A. the electric field inside a plasma
B. a density of particles in six-dimensional phase space (position and velocity)
C. the total number of particles in the whole plasma
D. a single particle's exact trajectory
Show answer

B. f(r,v,t)d³rd³v counts particles in a small box of both position and velocity space — it is a phase-space density, strictly more information than position alone (which would just give n) or a single trajectory.

Q2. Which fluid quantity is the first velocity moment of f?
A. density n
B. flow velocity u
C. pressure P
D. the magnetic field
Show answer

B. Density is the zeroth moment (∫f d³v), flow velocity is the first moment (∫v f d³v, normalized by n), and pressure is the second moment. The magnetic field isn't a moment of f at all.

Q3. The "closure problem" in fluid plasma theory refers to the fact that…
A. the moment hierarchy never stops needing the next-higher moment, so it must be truncated by an added physical assumption
B. plasmas cannot be described by fluid equations at all
C. the density equation is already closed and needs no assumptions
D. only electrons have a closure problem, not ions
Show answer

A. Every moment's evolution equation references the next moment up (density needs flow, flow needs pressure, pressure needs heat flux, ...), so a finite fluid model requires an extra assumption — the closure — to cut the chain off somewhere.

Q4. A fluid (MHD-style) description is most justified when…
A. the mean free path is much larger than the system size
B. collisions are frequent enough that the distribution stays close to a local Maxwellian (λ_mfp ≪ L)
C. the plasma has zero temperature
D. there is no magnetic field present
Show answer

B. Frequent collisions keep f close to Maxwellian everywhere, so the low-order moments (n, u, P) capture nearly all the relevant physics and a simple closure is accurate — exactly the opposite regime from (A), which favors a kinetic treatment instead.

Q5. For an exactly Maxwellian (unperturbed, thermalized) velocity distribution, how much physical information is lost by keeping only the fluid moments n, u, and P?
A. All of it — the fluid picture is always a severe approximation
B. None — a Maxwellian is fully specified by exactly those three moments (density, mean, variance)
C. Half, on average
D. It depends on the magnetic field strength
Show answer

B. A Gaussian (Maxwellian) distribution is completely determined by its mean and variance — so for a plasma that genuinely is locally Maxwellian, n, u, and P capture the distribution exactly, with nothing left over. The closure problem only becomes a real loss once f departs from Maxwellian.
