Diagnostics — Measuring a Plasma

Every quantity this course has derived — n_e, T_e, V_f, ω_pe — has to come from somewhere in a real experiment. Here's how you actually measure them, and what each method costs the plasma.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Interpret a Langmuir probe's current-voltage (I-V) characteristic in terms of the sheath physics from Lecture 8.
  • Extract T_e from the exponential (electron-retardation) region of the I-V curve.
  • Extract n_e from the ion-saturation current using the Bohm current formula.
  • Describe microwave interferometry as a line-integrated density measurement.
  • Describe basic emission spectroscopy (Doppler broadening) as a non-invasive ion-temperature measurement.

9.1The Langmuir Probe: a Deliberately Biased Wall

A Langmuir probe is nothing exotic — it's a small conducting electrode inserted directly into the plasma, with its voltage swept across a range and its current recorded. Every bit of physics from Lecture 8 applies directly: the probe is just a wall whose potential we control rather than let float.

Sweep the probe voltage V from very negative to very positive relative to the plasma, and the collected current I traces out the I-V characteristic, which has three recognizable regions:

Ion saturation (V ≪ plasma potential): the probe repels essentially all electrons, and collects only the Bohm ion flux — a nearly flat, negative current.

Electron-retardation (exponential) region (V near and below the plasma's potential): the probe collects the full ion current plus an exponentially-growing electron current, exactly the Boltzmann factor from the floating-potential derivation (Lecture 8.2) — just with V now swept rather than fixed.

Electron saturation (V ≫ plasma potential): the probe now attracts essentially all electrons reaching it, and the current flattens again at a much larger (electron) saturation value.

9.2Extracting T_e and n_e

In the exponential region, electron collection follows Ie(V) ∝ exp(e(V−Vp)/kBTe) (Vp is the plasma potential). Taking the natural log of the electron current linearizes it:

ln(I_e) = (e/k_BT_e) · V + const.  ⟹   T_e[eV] = 1 / slope of ln(I_e) vs. VPlot ln(I_e) against V in the exponential region; the inverse of the line's slope directly gives T_e in eV — one of the cleanest measurements in plasma physics.

Density comes from the ion-saturation current, using the same Bohm flux from Lecture 8.3. For a probe of collecting area A:

I_isat ≈ 0.6 · n_e e A c_s = 0.6 n_e e A √(k_BT_e/m_i)The 0.6 factor is an empirical/semi-analytic correction accounting for the presheath density drop between the bulk plasma and the sheath edge where ions reach c_s. Since c_s itself needs T_e, you need the exponential-region fit first.

So a single voltage sweep, properly analyzed, yields both Te and ne — which is why the Langmuir probe remains the workhorse diagnostic across all of low-temperature and fusion-edge plasma physics, over 100 years after Irving Langmuir introduced it.

Common mistakeYou cannot get n_e from I_isat alone without already knowing T_e, because c_s = √(k_BT_e/m_i) appears explicitly in the Bohm-current formula. The correct procedure is always: (1) fit the exponential region's slope for T_e first, (2) only then plug that T_e into the ion-saturation formula to get n_e. Reversing the order, or assuming n_e can be read off "directly," is the single most common Langmuir-probe analysis error.

9.3Interferometry: a Non-Invasive, Line-Integrated Density

A Langmuir probe is invasive — it physically perturbs the plasma it's measuring, draws current, and can only report a local value at its own location. Microwave (or laser) interferometry trades locality for being completely non-invasive: beam a wave of frequency ω (chosen well above the plasma's ωpe, Lecture 2's criterion for propagation) straight through the plasma and measure the phase shift it accumulates relative to a reference beam that never entered the plasma.

A plasma has refractive index nr ≈ 1 − ωpe²/(2ω²) for ω ≫ ωpe (slightly less than 1 — plasmas speed up light slightly, since ωpe depends on ne). The accumulated phase shift relative to vacuum is directly proportional to the line-integrated density along the beam path:

Δφ ∝ ∫ n_e dl  (along the beam's chord through the plasma)Interferometry never gives a local n_e directly — it gives the integral of n_e along the whole beam path. Multiple chords at different angles (tomography) are needed to reconstruct the full 2D or 3D density profile.

9.4Spectroscopy: Reading Temperature from Light

Emission spectroscopy measures the light a plasma emits naturally (from excited atoms and ions relaxing) — no probe, no injected beam, entirely passive. The line shape of a given spectral line carries diagnostic information: thermal motion Doppler-shifts each emitting ion's photon by an amount proportional to its velocity along the line of sight, and the resulting spread (Doppler broadening) of the whole line directly encodes the ion temperature, since a hotter population has a broader spread of velocities. Zeeman splitting (in a magnetic field) and Stark broadening (in a strong electric field, e.g. near a sheath) similarly let you read off B or local E fields from how a line splits or broadens.

AnalogyThink of three ways to find out how fast traffic is moving on a highway. A Langmuir probe is a speed bump with a sensor: accurate, local, but it physically slows down and samples only the cars that hit it. Interferometry is a toll-bridge camera measuring total travel-time delay across the whole bridge: never touches a single car, but only reports an average over the whole span. Spectroscopy is listening to the Doppler pitch-shift of every car's engine noise as it passes: completely passive, and the spread of pitches tells you the spread of speeds — temperature — without ever stopping a single car.
✦ Worked Example — reading T_e and n_e off a probe trace

A hydrogen-plasma Langmuir probe (area A = 1 mm² = 1×10⁻⁶ m²) in the exponential region yields a fitted slope of 1/3 per volt (i.e. d[ln I_e]/dV = 1/3 V⁻¹). Its ion-saturation current is measured as I_isat = 1.63×10⁻³ A. Find T_e and n_e.

  1. Electron temperature: T_e[eV] = 1/slope = 1/(1/3) = 3.0 eV.
  2. Ion sound speed: c_s = √(T_e·e/m_p) = √(3 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹/1.673×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 1.695×10⁴ m/s.
  3. Solve the Bohm-current formula for n_e: n_e = I_isat / (0.6 e A c_s) = (1.63×10⁻³) / (0.6 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ × 1×10⁻⁶ × 1.695×10⁴) ≈ 1.0×10¹⁸ m⁻³.
  4. Sanity check: this reconstructs exactly the input assumed in Lecture 1's worked example (n_e = 1×10¹⁸ m⁻³) with T_e now 3 eV instead of 1 eV — the Langmuir probe really does recover both quantities from one sweep.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate and fit a Langmuir probe trace

# langmuir_fit.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

e, mp = 1.602176634e-19, 1.67262192369e-27
n_e_true, Te_true, A = 1e18, 3.0, 1e-6             # m^-3, eV, m^2 (the "true" plasma we simulate)

def probe_current(V, Vp=0.0):
    cs = np.sqrt(Te_true*e/mp)
    I_isat = 0.6*n_e_true*e*A*cs
    I_esat = I_isat * 50                            # electrons saturate much higher (mass ratio effect)
    I_e = np.minimum(I_esat, I_esat*np.exp((V-Vp)/Te_true))  # I_esat is the prefactor: I_e(Vp)=I_esat exactly
    return I_e - I_isat                             # net collected current (ion current taken as constant -I_isat)

V = np.linspace(-15, 5, 400)
I = probe_current(V)

# fit the exponential region (well below Vp, well above ion-sat floor)
mask = (V > -8) & (V < -1)
slope, intercept = np.polyfit(V[mask], np.log(I[mask]+0.6*n_e_true*e*A*np.sqrt(Te_true*e/mp)), 1)
Te_fit = 1/slope
I_isat_measured = 0.6*n_e_true*e*A*np.sqrt(Te_true*e/mp)
cs_fit = np.sqrt(Te_fit*e/mp)
n_e_fit = I_isat_measured/(0.6*e*A*cs_fit)

print(f"true  Te = {Te_true} eV,  fitted Te = {Te_fit:.3f} eV")
print(f"true  n_e = {n_e_true:.2e} m^-3,  fitted n_e = {n_e_fit:.3e} m^-3")

# true  Te = 3.0 eV,  fitted Te = 3.000 eV
# true  n_e = 1.00e+18 m^-3,  fitted n_e = 1.000e+18 m^-3

Because this synthetic trace has no measurement noise, the fit recovers the input exactly — in a real lab trace, noise in I(V) is what ultimately limits how precisely T_e and n_e can be determined from a single sweep.

Model response

A Langmuir probe is invasive: a physical electrode sits inside the plasma, draws current, and perturbs the local sheath it's trying to measure. In exchange, it gives genuinely local information — n_e and T_e right at the probe tip, with spatial resolution set only by the probe's own size — and it's cheap and simple. Microwave interferometry is the opposite trade: it's non-invasive (the beam never touches the plasma, so it perturbs nothing), but it can only report the line integral ∫n_e dl along the entire beam chord — all local structure along that path is averaged away unless you add many chords and do tomography.

Regime where you have no choice: a hot, dense fusion-grade plasma (a tokamak core, or anywhere near the plasma-facing edge of a burning plasma) would simply vaporize a probe on contact, so interferometry (or another non-invasive method) is the only option. Conversely, in a cool, low-density lab discharge where a probe survives fine and you need the actual local profile (not just a line-averaged number), the probe gives richer information that interferometry alone cannot provide.

Q1. In a Langmuir probe's I-V characteristic, the ion-saturation region occurs when…
A. the probe voltage is far above the plasma potential
B. the probe voltage is far below the plasma potential, repelling nearly all electrons
C. the probe is disconnected
D. the plasma has no ions
Show answer

B. At strongly negative bias, the probe repels essentially all electrons and collects only the (comparatively small, nearly voltage-independent) Bohm ion flux — giving the flat "ion saturation" current.

Q2. To extract T_e from a Langmuir probe trace, you should…
A. read the ion-saturation current directly
B. measure the probe's physical temperature
C. take the inverse of the slope of ln(I_e) vs. V in the exponential region
D. use the electron-saturation current alone
Show answer

C. The electron current in the retardation region follows I_e ∝ exp(eV/k_BT_e), so plotting ln(I_e) vs. V and inverting the slope gives T_e directly in eV.

Q3. Why can't you compute n_e directly from the ion-saturation current without a separate T_e measurement?
A. I_isat doesn't depend on n_e at all
B. Ion-saturation current only depends on probe area
C. It actually can be computed directly, no T_e needed
D. The Bohm current formula I_isat = 0.6 n_e e A c_s contains c_s = √(k_BT_e/m_i), which requires T_e
Show answer

D. The Bohm speed c_s appears explicitly in the ion-saturation formula, so T_e must be known (typically from the exponential-region fit) before n_e can be solved for.

Q4. Microwave interferometry measures…
A. the density integrated along the entire beam's path through the plasma
B. the local electron density at one exact point
C. the ion temperature only
D. the magnetic field strength
Show answer

A. The phase shift Δφ ∝ ∫n_e dl is a line integral along the whole beam chord — interferometry is inherently a line-integrated, not local, density diagnostic (unlike a Langmuir probe).

Q5. Doppler-broadened emission spectroscopy is primarily used to measure…
A. the probe's physical temperature
B. ion temperature, via the spread of velocities encoded in the broadened spectral line
C. the plasma frequency directly
D. the floating potential
Show answer

B. Thermal motion Doppler-shifts each emitting ion's photon proportionally to its line-of-sight velocity; the resulting spread (width) of the emission line directly reflects the spread of ion velocities — i.e., the ion temperature — and requires no probe or injected beam.
