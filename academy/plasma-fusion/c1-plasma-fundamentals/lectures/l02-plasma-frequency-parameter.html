The Plasma Frequency & Plasma Parameter

Displace a cloud of electrons and the plasma rings — at one characteristic rate, set entirely by how many electrons there are. That rate is the fastest clock in the plasma.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Derive the electron plasma frequency ωpe from a simple slab displacement model.
  • Explain plasma oscillations physically: why the restoring force is linear and collisionless.
  • Connect ωpe to the third plasma criterion (ωpeτ > 1) introduced in Lecture 1.
  • Revisit the plasma parameter ND and show it controls how "collective" vs "discrete" the plasma's behavior is.
  • Compute ωpe and the plasma period with PlasmaPy and compare to the Debye length's spatial scale.

2.1Displace the Electrons, and They Ring

Last week we asked what happens to a single test charge sitting inside a plasma. This week we ask a different question: what happens if you displace an entire slab of electrons as a group, then let go?

Picture a uniform slab of plasma. The heavy ions barely move on fast timescales, so treat them as a fixed, uniform positive background. Now shift every electron in a thin layer by a small distance x. The layer leaves behind a region of net positive charge (bare ions) and piles up net negative charge where it lands — exactly like pulling the negative plate of a capacitor away from the positive one.

That charge separation creates a restoring electric field pointing back toward the electrons' original position. Release the slab, and the field pulls it back — but inertia carries it past equilibrium, charge separation reverses, and the field now pushes the other way. The electrons oscillate. This is a plasma oscillation, and unlike a sound wave in air, it needs no collisions at all — it runs on the long-range electric field alone, the same collective force that drove Debye shielding in Lecture 1.

2.2Deriving the Plasma Frequency

Model the displaced slab as an infinite sheet of charge. Displacing electrons of density ne by distance x creates a surface charge density σ = ne e x on each face, and — just like the field between capacitor plates — the uniform field this produces inside the slab is:

E = n_e e x / ε₀The restoring field grows linearly with displacement x — exactly the signature of a harmonic oscillator.

Newton's second law for one electron (charge −e, mass me) feeling this restoring field gives:

m_e (d²x/dt²) = −e E = −(n_e e² / ε₀) xA linear restoring force ⇒ simple harmonic motion, no collisions required.

This is exactly the equation of a mass on a spring, d²x/dt² = −ω²x, with the "spring constant" supplied entirely by electrostatics. Reading off ω² gives the electron plasma frequency:

ω_pe = √( n_e e² / (ε₀ m_e) )  ≈  56.4 · √( n_e[m⁻³] )  rad/sDepends only on electron density — not on temperature, not on the ions. Denser plasma ⇒ stiffer "spring" ⇒ faster oscillation.

Because me sits in the denominator, electrons — being roughly 1836× lighter than protons — oscillate far faster than ions ever could. If you instead displaced the ion background while holding electrons fixed, you'd get an analogous ion plasma frequency ωpi, smaller by √(me/mi). On the timescales this course cares about early on, ωpe is the relevant clock.

AnalogyThink of the electron fluid as a mattress with the ions as its rigid frame. Push down on the mattress (displace the electrons) and let go — it springs back and overshoots, oscillating at a rate fixed by the mattress's own stiffness and mass, utterly indifferent to how hard you pushed. The plasma frequency is that same kind of built-in, amplitude-independent "ring."

2.3The Plasma Parameter, Revisited

Lecture 1 defined the plasma parameter ND — the number of electrons inside a sphere of radius λD — and required ND ≫ 1. We can now say precisely why that matters for oscillations too.

If ND were small, only a handful of discrete electrons would populate the screening cloud, and the smooth "fluid slab" picture we just used to derive ωpe would break down into a few individual, noisy Coulomb collisions. A large ND is precisely what lets us treat the electron population as a smooth charge density ne(x) rather than a swarm of discrete particles — the same approximation underlying both Debye shielding and plasma oscillations. ND ≫ 1 is the single number that licenses treating a plasma as a continuous, collective medium.

ND also has a tidy algebraic relationship to ωpe and λD: since λD = vthe/ωpe (the thermal electron speed divided by the plasma frequency — a disturbance "outruns" the field response once it moves faster than λDωpe), you can picture λD as the distance an electron travels in roughly one radian of its own plasma oscillation.

Common mistakeωpe is not a collision rate — it has nothing to do with how often particles bump into each other. It is a collective restoring-force frequency that exists even in a perfectly collisionless plasma. Criterion 3 from Lecture 1 (ωpeτ > 1, where τ is the mean time between collisions with neutrals) compares this collective rate to the collision rate — it does not mean ωpe itself comes from collisions.
✦ Worked Example — the plasma frequency of the Lecture 1 discharge

Same lab discharge as Lecture 1: ne = 1×10¹⁸ m⁻³. Find ωpe, the plasma period, and compare the distance an electron travels in one period (at the Lecture 1 thermal speed) to λD.

  1. Plasma frequency: ω_pe ≈ 56.4·√(10¹⁸) = 56.4 × 10⁹ ≈ 5.64×10¹⁰ rad/s.
  2. Plasma period: T_pe = 2π/ω_pe = 2π / (5.64×10¹⁰) ≈ 1.11×10⁻¹⁰ s (about 111 picoseconds — extraordinarily fast).
  3. Frequency in Hz: f_pe = ω_pe/2π ≈ 8.98×10⁹ Hz (≈9 GHz — this is why plasmas reflect microwaves below f_pe, a fact used in Lecture 9's diagnostics).
  4. Cross-check against Lecture 1's λ_D: λ_D ≈ 7.4 µm for this plasma. A thermal electron (T_e = 1 eV ⇒ v ≈ 4.2×10⁵ m/s) crosses one λ_D in about 7.4×10⁻⁶ / 4.2×10⁵ ≈ 1.8×10⁻¹¹ s — comparable to, and shorter than, one plasma period, confirming λ_D and 1/ω_pe are the matched length and time scales of the same collective response.

🛠️ Hands-On: ω_pe across densities with PlasmaPy

Reuse the PlasmaPy install from Lecture 1. This week, sweep electron density and watch ωpe climb as the square root of density — then compare a lab discharge to a fusion-relevant core plasma.

# plasma_frequency.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import astropy.units as u
from plasmapy.formulary import plasma_frequency, Debye_length

densities = [1e18, 1e20, 1e22] * u.m**-3   # lab discharge -> fusion core -> dense lab Z-pinch
T = 1 * u.eV

for n in densities:
    wpe = plasma_frequency(n, "e-")
    lam_D = Debye_length(T, n)
    print(f"n={n:.0e}: omega_pe={wpe:.3e}, T_pe={ (2*np.pi/wpe.value)*u.s :.3e}, lambda_D={lam_D.to(u.um):.2f}")

# n=1e+18 1 / m3: omega_pe=5.642e+10 rad / s, T_pe=1.114e-10 s, lambda_D=7.43 um
# n=1e+20 1 / m3: omega_pe=5.642e+11 rad / s, T_pe=1.114e-11 s, lambda_D=0.74 um
# n=1e+22 1 / m3: omega_pe=5.642e+12 rad / s, T_pe=1.114e-12 s, lambda_D=0.07 um

Notice ωpe scales as √n while λD scales as 1/√n for fixed T — doubling the density speeds up the oscillation and shrinks the screening length by the same factor. Try holding n fixed and sweeping T instead: ωpe shouldn't move at all.

🔗 Where this is heading

ωpe is the first of several natural frequencies this course will meet — next week's cyclotron frequency ωc is the second. Whenever a plasma supports a wave (Langmuir waves, cyclotron waves, Alfvén waves in Course 3), its dispersion relation almost always involves ωpe somewhere. Practically, a wave with frequency below ωpe cannot propagate through the plasma at all — it reflects — which is exactly how an ionospheric radio bounce, and a Lecture 9 interferometry diagnostic, both work.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The restoring force in a plasma oscillation comes from…
A. collisions between electrons and neutral atoms
B. the long-range electric field created by charge separation when electrons are displaced
C. gravity acting on the electron slab
D. the magnetic field of the plasma
Show answer

B. Displacing the electron fluid exposes a net ion charge on one side and piles electrons on the other; the resulting field pulls the slab back. No collisions, gravity, or magnetic field are needed — it's a purely electrostatic, collective effect.

Q2. Doubling the electron density (holding everything else fixed) changes ω_pe by a factor of…
A. 2
B. 4
C. √2
D. no change
Show answer

C. ω_pe ∝ √n_e, so doubling n_e scales ω_pe by √2 ≈ 1.41.

Q3. Why does ω_pe depend on the electron mass m_e but not on temperature T_e?
A. The derivation used Newton's second law with the electrostatic restoring force only — it never introduced thermal motion
B. Electrons are always at absolute zero in a plasma
C. Temperature only matters for ions
D. It actually does depend on T_e, just weakly
Show answer

A. The cold-plasma oscillation derivation is a force balance (inertia vs. electrostatic restoring force); it contains m_e but never T_e. (A warm-plasma correction does introduce a weak T_e-dependent term, but that's beyond this course's scope — the leading-order ω_pe is T-independent.)

Q4. A large plasma parameter N_D ≫ 1 is required because…
A. it licenses treating the electrons as a smooth, continuous charge density rather than discrete particles
B. it sets the value of ω_pe directly
C. it guarantees the plasma has no electric field
D. it measures the collision rate
Show answer

A. Both the Debye-shielding derivation and the plasma-oscillation slab derivation implicitly treat charge as a smooth fluid density. That approximation is only valid when many particles populate the relevant volume — exactly what N_D ≫ 1 guarantees.

Q5. An electromagnetic wave with frequency below a plasma's ω_pe, incident on that plasma, will…
A. pass through completely unaffected
B. be reflected — it cannot propagate inside the plasma
C. be absorbed and converted entirely to heat
D. speed up inside the plasma
Show answer

B. Below ω_pe the plasma's electrons can respond fast enough to screen out the wave's oscillating field entirely, so the wave reflects rather than propagating — the same physics behind ionospheric radio bounce and plasma interferometry (Lecture 9).
