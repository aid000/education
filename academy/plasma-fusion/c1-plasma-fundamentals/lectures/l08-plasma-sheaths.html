Plasma Sheaths & Boundaries

Every wall a plasma touches charges up negative, all by itself — fast electrons escape, slow ions don't, and the result is a self-built electric fence just a few Debye lengths thick.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why an isolated surface in contact with a plasma charges up negative (the Debye sheath).
  • Derive the floating potential by balancing electron and ion fluxes.
  • State and interpret the Bohm sheath criterion on the ion entry speed.
  • Explain the sheath's thickness scaling in terms of the Debye length.
  • Finish the Week 4 project's plasma-parameter calculator as this week's checkpoint.

8.1Why Walls Charge Up

Drop any solid surface — a probe, a wall, a spacecraft — into a plasma and leave it electrically isolated (no current forced in or out). At the instant of contact, both species bombard the surface at their own thermal flux. But electrons, being roughly √(mi/me) ≈ 43× (for hydrogen) faster at the same temperature, arrive far more often than ions. The surface accumulates negative charge almost immediately, building up a negative potential relative to the bulk plasma.

That negative potential then does exactly what Lecture 1's Debye shielding predicts: it repels the slower-moving electrons (only the fastest few in the tail of the distribution have enough energy to climb the resulting potential hill and still reach the wall) while continuing to pull ions in. A thin, strongly non-neutral boundary layer forms — the Debye sheath — inside which quasineutrality (Lecture 1's bedrock assumption for the bulk plasma) is explicitly violated.

8.2The Floating Potential

An isolated ("floating") surface settles at whatever potential makes the electron and ion fluxes exactly equal — net current zero. The ion flux arriving at the sheath edge is set by the Bohm criterion (Section 8.3) at the ion sound speed cs = √(kBTe/mi); the electron flux is the thermal flux reduced by the Boltzmann factor for climbing the (negative) wall potential Vf. Setting these equal and solving for Vf gives the floating potential:

V_f = − (k_BT_e / 2e) · ln( m_i / (2π m_e) )Always negative (the wall sits below the plasma potential) and grows (more negative) with electron temperature and with the ion/electron mass ratio.

For a hydrogen plasma, mi/me ≈ 1836, giving Vf ≈ −2.8 Te[eV] volts — a useful rule of thumb worth memorizing: any floating surface in a hydrogen-isotope plasma sits roughly three electron-temperatures (in eV, as volts) below the plasma.

AnalogyThink of a bouncer at a club door who lets in whoever can jump a low wall. Electrons are fast but light — most clear the wall easily unless it's built high enough. Ions are slow but heavy — once they're committed to walking toward the door, nothing turns them back. The floating potential is exactly the wall height that lets through just enough fast electrons to match the steady trickle of ions — no more, no less.

8.3The Bohm Sheath Criterion

Ions don't simply drift into the sheath at their thermal speed — they must already be moving faster than a specific threshold by the time they reach the sheath edge, or the sheath structure itself becomes unstable (a result due to David Bohm). The requirement is the Bohm sheath criterion:

v_i ≥ c_s = √( k_B T_e / m_i )The ion sound speed — note it uses the *electron* temperature, since it's the electron pressure that ultimately accelerates ions through a "presheath" region just outside the sheath proper, up to at least c_s.

Physically, a weak electric field extends slightly into the bulk plasma just outside the sheath (the "presheath") specifically to accelerate ions up to cs before they enter the sheath proper — without that pre-acceleration, the sheath's own charge balance cannot be self-consistently maintained. The Bohm criterion sets the ion flux reaching any wall or probe, which is exactly the quantity Lecture 9's Langmuir-probe ion-saturation current depends on.

Common mistakeDon't confuse the Bohm criterion's cs = √(kBTe/mi) with an ion thermal speed √(kBTi/mi). The sound speed uses the electron temperature — because it's the far more mobile, far hotter (in most lab and fusion-edge plasmas) electrons whose pressure does the work of accelerating ions through the presheath, regardless of how cold the ions themselves are.

8.4Sheath Thickness: the Child–Langmuir Scaling

Because the sheath carries a large potential drop across a region with almost no electrons (they've mostly been repelled), the ions there behave like a space-charge-limited beam — exactly the situation solved by the Child–Langmuir law in vacuum-tube physics. The resulting sheath thickness s scales as:

s ~ λ_D · ( ΔV / T_e )^(3/4)ΔV is the total potential drop across the sheath (of order |V_f| for a floating wall). The sheath is always a few Debye lengths thick — set by the very screening length Lecture 1 derived — but grows somewhat faster than linearly with the potential drop.

This connects directly back to Lecture 1's first plasma criterion (λD ≪ L): the sheath is a boundary-layer phenomenon precisely because λD is so much smaller than the device size. A plasma that failed criterion 1 wouldn't have a sharp sheath at all — the whole device would behave like one big non-neutral boundary layer.

✦ Worked Example — floating potential and ion sound speed

A hydrogen plasma (m_i = m_p) has T_e = 5 eV. Find the ion sound speed c_s and the floating potential V_f.

  1. Ion sound speed: c_s = √(T_e·e/m_p) = √(5 × 1.602×10⁻¹⁹ / 1.673×10⁻²⁷) ≈ 2.19×10⁴ m/s (about 22 km/s).
  2. Mass ratio: m_p/(2π m_e) = 1836.15/(2π) ≈ 292.2.
  3. Floating potential: V_f = −(T_e/2)·ln(292.2) = −(5/2)(5.677) ≈ −14.19 V.
  4. Rule-of-thumb check: −14.19 V / (5 eV) ≈ −2.84 — matches the "about −2.8 T_e[eV] volts" rule of thumb from Section 8.2 almost exactly.

🛠️ Hands-On: Project Checkpoint — the plasma-parameter calculator

This week's checkpoint finishes the second half of the Week 1 project: a single function that returns λ_D, N_D, ω_pe, c_s, and V_f for any (n_e, T_e) pair — the toolkit you'll reuse in the Week 10 capstone report.

# plasma_calculator.py — Lecture 8 project checkpoint
import numpy as np

e, eps0, me, mp = 1.602176634e-19, 8.8541878128e-12, 9.1093837015e-31, 1.67262192369e-27

def plasma_parameters(n_e, Te_eV, mi=mp):
    Te_J = Te_eV * e
    lam_D = np.sqrt(eps0*Te_J/(n_e*e**2))
    N_D = (4/3)*np.pi*n_e*lam_D**3
    omega_pe = np.sqrt(n_e*e**2/(eps0*me))
    c_s = np.sqrt(Te_J/mi)
    V_f = -(Te_eV/2)*np.log(mi/(2*np.pi*me))
    return dict(lambda_D_m=lam_D, N_D=N_D, omega_pe=omega_pe, c_s=c_s, V_f=V_f)

r = plasma_parameters(n_e=1e18, Te_eV=5.0)
for k, v in r.items():
    print(f"{k:12s} = {v:.4e}")

# lambda_D_m   = 1.6623e-05
# N_D          = 1.9240e+04
# omega_pe     = 5.6415e+10
# c_s          = 2.1885e+04
# V_f          = -1.4194e+01

🔗 Where this is heading

Everything about a Langmuir probe — Lecture 9's diagnostic — is sheath physics: the probe is just a small, deliberately-biased wall, and its current-voltage curve directly traces out the floating-potential, Bohm-current, and electron-repulsion physics derived here. Sheaths also set the boundary condition for every edge-plasma and divertor calculation in a real fusion device (Course 4), where the ion flux the Bohm criterion predicts determines how fast the plasma-facing material erodes.

Q1. An electrically isolated surface in contact with a plasma charges up negative because…
A. ions are faster than electrons and escape first
B. electrons, being much lighter, arrive at the surface far more often than ions at first contact
C. the plasma has no electrons at all
D. the surface is always made of a negatively-charged material
Show answer

B. At equal temperature, electrons move roughly √(m_i/m_e) times faster than ions, so they initially bombard any surface far more frequently — charging it negative until the resulting repulsive field throttles the electron flux down to match the ion flux.

Q2. The floating potential V_f of a surface in a hydrogen plasma with T_e = 5 eV is approximately…
A. +14 V
B. 0 V
C. −14 V
D. −5 V
Show answer

C. V_f = −(T_e/2)ln(m_i/2πm_e) ≈ −2.84 × T_e[eV] ≈ −14.2 V for T_e = 5 eV — negative, and several times T_e in magnitude, not equal to it.

Q3. The Bohm sheath criterion requires ions to enter the sheath at a speed of at least…
A. the electron thermal speed
B. √(k_BT_i/m_i), the ion thermal speed
C. √(k_BT_e/m_i), the ion sound speed (using electron temperature)
D. the speed of light
Show answer

C. The Bohm speed c_s = √(k_BT_e/m_i) uses the electron temperature, because electron pressure drives the presheath field that accelerates ions up to this speed before they reach the sheath edge.

Q4. The Debye sheath is typically how thick, compared to the Debye length λ_D?
A. Thousands of λ_D
B. A few λ_D (scaling roughly as λ_D(ΔV/T_e)^(3/4))
C. Exactly 1 λ_D, always
D. It has no relationship to λ_D at all
Show answer

B. The Child–Langmuir scaling gives a thickness of a handful of Debye lengths, growing somewhat faster than linearly with the potential drop — but always anchored to λ_D, the same screening length from Lecture 1.

Q5. Why is quasineutrality (Lecture 1) explicitly violated inside the sheath, even though it holds everywhere in the bulk plasma?
A. Quasineutrality is never actually true anywhere
B. The sheath is, by definition, the thin boundary layer where the wall's repulsion of electrons creates a real, measurable charge imbalance over a length scale of order λ_D
C. The sheath has no ions in it
D. Magnetic fields cause the violation
Show answer

B. The sheath is exactly the region where the bulk-plasma approximation of near-perfect charge balance breaks down — a real, non-negligible excess of ion charge builds up over a few Debye lengths next to the (electron-repelling) negative wall.
