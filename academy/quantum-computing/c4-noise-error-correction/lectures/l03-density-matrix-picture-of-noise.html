The Density-Matrix Picture of Noise

You have the channels. Now: how do you measure how "wrong" a noisy state has gotten — and what happens when noise doesn't hit once, but over and over, the way it really does inside a circuit?

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define fidelity F(ρ,σ) informally as a similarity measure between two density matrices, with F=1 meaning identical states.
  • Compose multiple noise channels by applying them sequentially to the same density matrix.
  • Track how fidelity to an ideal state decays as the same channel is applied repeatedly.
  • Explain, quantitatively, why "just living with the noise" fails as circuit depth grows.
  • Simulate repeated depolarizing-channel application in PennyLane and plot the fidelity decay.

3.1Fidelity: How "Close" Are Two States?

Week 2 gave you exact Kraus operators for three channels. But once a channel has acted, you need a number that answers a very practical question: how much damage did that actually do? That number is fidelity, F(ρ,σ) — informally, a similarity score between two density matrices.

F(ρ,σ) ∈ [0,1]    F=1 ⟺ ρ=σ (identical states)Higher fidelity = more similar. F=1 means no distinguishable difference at all; F=0 means maximally different (e.g. orthogonal pure states).

For comparing a noisy state ρ to the ideal pure state |ψ⟩ you were trying to prepare, fidelity reduces to something you've already computed without naming it: F(ρ, |ψ⟩⟨ψ|) = ⟨ψ|ρ|ψ⟩ — literally "the probability that a measurement in the |ψ⟩ basis would still say yes." That's exactly what Lecture 1's PennyLane snippet printed as fidelity when it read off ρ[1,1].

AnalogyFidelity is like a photocopy-quality score. F=1 is a perfect copy, indistinguishable from the original. As a document gets photocopied from a photocopy from a photocopy (composing channels, next section), fidelity to the original page keeps dropping — not because any single copy step was catastrophic, but because the small degradations compound.

3.2Composing Channels: Applying Noise More Than Once

A real circuit doesn't apply one noise channel once — every gate, every idle moment, every measurement is another opportunity for the environment to act. Composing channels is simple in the Kraus picture: apply one channel's operator-sum, then feed the result straight into the next:

ρ → ℰ₂(ℰ₁(ρ)) → ℰ₃(ℰ₂(ℰ₁(ρ))) → …Each ℰᵢ can be the same channel repeated, or a different channel entirely — composition just means "apply the whole operator-sum, then do it again."

Composition is where the real danger of noise shows up. A single application of a weak channel (small p) might barely move the fidelity. But a moderately deep circuit might apply that same channel (or similar ones) dozens or hundreds of times — once per gate — and small per-step degradations compound multiplicatively, not additively.

3.3Why Repeated Noise Is the Real Threat

Here's the concrete shape of the problem. For this channel acting on this state, a single application with strength p leaves fidelity to the ideal state at exactly F₁ = 1 − p/2 (not merely a leading-order approximation — the next worked example confirms this precisely). Apply it n times in a row, and fidelity doesn't fall linearly — it falls roughly geometrically:

Fₙ ≈ (1 − p)ⁿEach application multiplies the surviving fidelity by roughly (1−p) again — so fidelity decays toward 0 exponentially in the number of applications n, not linearly.

This is precisely why NISQ-era circuits have a practical depth limit even when each individual gate's error rate looks tiny: 100 gates at 99.9% fidelity each still compounds to roughly 0.999¹⁰⁰ ≈ 90.5%, and a circuit with a few thousand gates — well within reach of real algorithms — can be driven to fidelity near zero. "The per-gate error is small" is not the same statement as "the circuit is reliable."

✦ Worked Example — fidelity decay under repeated depolarizing noise

Start with the ideal state |1⟩⟨1|, apply the depolarizing channel with p=0.2 once, twice, then three times, and track fidelity to the ideal |1⟩⟨1| after each application.

  1. Recall the channel: ℰ(ρ) = (1−p)ρ + p·I/2, with p=0.2, so ℰ(ρ) = 0.8ρ + 0.1·I.
  2. Apply once to ρ₀=|1⟩⟨1|=[[0,0],[0,1]]: ρ₁ = 0.8·[[0,0],[0,1]] + 0.1·[[1,0],[0,1]] = [[0.1, 0],[0, 0.9]]. Fidelity to |1⟩⟨1| is the (1,1) entry: F₁ = 0.9.
  3. Apply a second time to ρ₁: ρ₂ = 0.8·ρ₁ + 0.1·I = 0.8·[[0.1,0],[0,0.9]] + [[0.1,0],[0,0.1]] = [[0.18, 0],[0, 0.82]]. F₂ = 0.82.
  4. Apply a third time to ρ₂: ρ₃ = 0.8·ρ₂ + 0.1·I = [[0.244, 0],[0, 0.756]]. F₃ = 0.756.
  5. Compare to the geometric estimate: (1−p)ⁿ at p=0.2 gives (0.8)¹=0.8, (0.8)²=0.64, (0.8)³=0.512 — the exact populations (0.9, 0.82, 0.756) decay slower than this crude estimate because every application also mixes back in exactly 0.5 from I/2, but the qualitative trend is identical: each application erodes fidelity further, converging toward the fully-mixed F=0.5, never reaching it exactly in finite steps.
  6. The takeaway: Three applications of a 20%-strength channel already cost nearly 15 points of fidelity (0.9→0.756). A real circuit with hundreds of noisy operations faces exactly this compounding — which is precisely why Week 4 introduces an actual countermeasure instead of just a worse worked example.

🛠️ Hands-On: plotting fidelity decay in PennyLane

Reproduce the worked example numerically, then push it further — watch what happens at n=10, n=50. Note: PennyLane's built-in DepolarizingChannel(p) uses the Nielsen–Chuang convention (1−4p/3)ρ+(4p/3)(I/2), not this lecture's (1−p)ρ+p·I/2 — the code below rescales p by 3/4 before calling it so the printed numbers match the worked example exactly.

# fidelity_decay.py — Lecture 3
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np

dev = qml.device("default.mixed", wires=1)

@qml.qnode(dev)
def repeated_noise(p, n_apps):
    qml.PauliX(wires=0)                        # prepare |1>
    p_pl = 3 * p / 4                            # rescale: PennyLane's (1-4p_pl/3) = this lecture's (1-p)
    for _ in range(n_apps):
        qml.DepolarizingChannel(p_pl, wires=0)  # compose the (rescaled) channel n_apps times
    return qml.density_matrix(wires=0)

ideal_pop = 1.0   # |1><1|'s own population entry
for n in [0, 1, 2, 3, 10, 50]:
    rho = repeated_noise(0.2, n)
    fidelity = np.real(rho[1, 1])
    print(f"n={n:2d} applications   fidelity-to-|1>: {fidelity:.4f}")

n=1,2,3 should match the worked example's 0.9, 0.82, 0.756. By n=50 the fidelity should be sitting very close to 0.5 — the maximally mixed state has essentially been reached, and no trace of "which state it started in" remains.

🔗 Where this is heading

You've now seen, concretely, why noise is a compounding problem rather than a fixed cost — fidelity doesn't just drop, it drops multiplicatively with every additional noisy operation. That is the entire motivation for Week 4's repetition code: a way to spend extra qubits to actively detect and reverse errors mid-circuit, rather than simply accumulating fidelity loss and hoping the final answer is still usable.

Q1. Fidelity F(ρ,σ) = 1 means…
A. ρ and σ are orthogonal
B. ρ is pure and σ is mixed
C. ρ and σ are identical (indistinguishable) states
D. ρ has trace 1
Show answer

C. F=1 is the maximum possible fidelity, reached only when the two density matrices describe the exact same state.

Q2. Composing two noise channels ℰ₁ then ℰ₂ on a state ρ means computing…
A. ℰ₁(ρ) + ℰ₂(ρ)
B. ℰ₁(ρ) · ℰ₂(ρ)
C. ℰ₂(ℰ₁(ρ)) — feed the output of the first channel into the second
D. the average of ℰ₁ and ℰ₂'s Kraus operators
Show answer

C. Composition is sequential: apply the first channel's full operator-sum to ρ, then apply the second channel's operator-sum to that result.

Q3. In the worked example, applying the p=0.2 depolarizing channel to |1⟩⟨1| three times in a row leaves fidelity at approximately…
A. 0.98
B. 0.756
C. 0.512
D. 0.2
Show answer

B. ρ₃=[[0.244,0],[0,0.756]] after three applications, computed by repeatedly applying ℰ(ρ)=0.8ρ+0.1I — not the cruder (1−p)ⁿ=0.512 geometric estimate, which decays faster than the true population.

Q4. Why does "the per-gate error rate is tiny" not guarantee a reliable circuit?
A. Because fidelity decays roughly multiplicatively with the number of noisy operations, so many small factors compound into a large overall loss
B. Because per-gate error rates are always underestimated by a factor of 10
C. Because quantum gates have no error at all, only measurement does
D. Because fidelity only matters at the very last gate in a circuit
Show answer

A. Repeated applications of even a weak channel compound roughly geometrically (Fₙ ≈ (1−p)ⁿ), so a circuit with enough gates can drive fidelity toward zero even if each individual gate looks nearly perfect.

Q5. What does the "leaked, not destroyed" framing of decoherence (Lecture 1) imply about the possibility of error correction?
A. Nothing — leaked information can never be recovered, so error correction is impossible in principle
B. It implies errors can, in principle, be identified and reversed, since the underlying evolution is still unitary on the larger qubit+environment system
C. It implies only classical bits can ever be protected, never quantum superpositions
D. It implies decoherence and measurement collapse are the same process
Show answer

B. Because decoherence is information leaking rather than being erased, the full qubit+environment evolution stays unitary and reversible in principle — error correction exploits exactly this by catching and reversing the leak before it's gone for good.
