Hadamard, Phase Gates & the Bloch Sphere Revisited

The Pauli gates only move the Bloch point to antipodal landmarks. The Hadamard and phase gates are what actually turn the sphere into a dial you can tune to any point on it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write the Hadamard gate H as a 2×2 unitary matrix and compute H|0⟩ and H|1⟩.
  • Explain H as the gate that maps the Z-basis {|0⟩,|1⟩} to the X-basis {|+⟩,|−⟩} and back.
  • Write the S and T phase gates as diagonal matrices and describe their action as Bloch-sphere rotations about the z-axis.
  • Connect every single-qubit gate explicitly to a rotation of the Bloch vector from Course 1 §4.
  • Apply H then S to |0⟩ by hand and locate the resulting state on the Bloch sphere.
  • Build and evolve an H+S circuit in Qiskit and read off the resulting statevector.

2.1The Hadamard Gate: Creating Superposition

Every Pauli gate from Lecture 1 maps a basis state to another basis state (up to a phase) — X, Y, and Z never turn a definite |0⟩ or |1⟩ into a genuine superposition. The Hadamard gate H is the simplest gate that does:

H = (1/√2) [ 1 1 ; 1 −1 ]H is its own inverse (H² = I) and is both Hermitian and unitary, like the Pauli gates.

Applied to the two basis states, H produces the equal superpositions this course will use constantly from here on:

H|0⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 ≡ |+⟩     H|1⟩ = (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 ≡ |−⟩|+⟩ and |−⟩ are given their own names because they recur everywhere: they are the eigenstates of X, exactly as Course 1 Lecture 2 defined them.

Applying H a second time undoes the first application exactly — H(H|0⟩) = H|+⟩ = |0⟩ — which is a quick way to confirm H² = I without multiplying matrices.

2.2H Swaps Bases: Z-basis ↔ X-basis

There is a second, equally important way to describe H: it is the gate that trades the computational basis {|0⟩,|1⟩} (the Z-basis, i.e. the eigenbasis of Z) for the X-basis {|+⟩,|−⟩} (the eigenbasis of X), and vice versa:

H|0⟩ = |+⟩   H|1⟩ = |−⟩     H|+⟩ = |0⟩   H|−⟩ = |1⟩H is literally a basis-change operator between the two most commonly used single-qubit bases in this course.

This also explains a fact you may already suspect from Lecture 1: H conjugation turns X into Z and Z into X — HXH = Z and HZH = X (you'll verify this directly in Lecture 8, where it's one of the course's named circuit equivalences). A gate that performs a bit-flip in one basis performs a phase-flip in the basis H rotates you into — same physics, different coordinates.

AnalogyThink of the Z-basis and X-basis as two different coordinate grids drawn on the same Bloch sphere, rotated 90° from each other around the y-axis. H doesn't create anything out of nothing — it just re-expresses the same two poles-and-equator structure using a different pair of "poles." What looks like a definite state in one grid (|0⟩ in the Z-grid) looks like an equal superposition in the other (|+⟩ sits on the Z-grid's equator) — superposition is partly a matter of which basis you're asking the question in.

2.3Phase Gates S and T: Rotating About the Z-Axis

Where H swaps bases, the phase gates S and T leave the computational basis alone and instead rotate the azimuthal angle φ from Course 1 §4 — the relative phase coordinate. Both are diagonal, and both are special cases of a general phase gate P(λ) = diag(1, eiλ):

S = [ 1 0 ; 0 i ] = P(π/2)     T = [ 1 0 ; 0 eiπ/4 ] = P(π/4)S|0⟩=|0⟩, S|1⟩=i|1⟩; T|0⟩=|0⟩, T|1⟩=eiπ/4|1⟩ — both fix the two poles exactly, since a diagonal matrix with a 1 in the top-left never touches |0⟩, and never changes |1⟩'s magnitude.

Because they fix the poles and only ever multiply the |1⟩-amplitude by a unit-magnitude phase, S and T act purely on φ: applying S shifts φ by π/2 (a quarter-turn around the z-axis of the Bloch sphere), and applying T shifts φ by π/4 (an eighth-turn). Four applications of S, or eight of T, bring you all the way back around — hence S² = Z (a half-turn) and T² = S (two eighth-turns make a quarter-turn).

Common mistakeDon't expect S or T to move a state off the poles or off the equator — they rotate around the z-axis, which means a state's polar angle θ (how far from the poles it is) never changes under S or T. Only φ moves. If you start at a pole (θ=0 or θ=π), S and T do literally nothing to the state at all — there's no "around" to rotate through at a single point, exactly as Course 1 §4.3 noted for the poles.

2.4Every Single-Qubit Gate Is a Bloch-Sphere Rotation

Lecture 1's analogy box called the Pauli gates 180° spins about the three coordinate axes. Now the full picture snaps into place: every single-qubit unitary gate corresponds to some rotation of the Bloch vector, around some axis, by some angle. X, Y, Z are 180° rotations about x, y, z. H is a 180° rotation about the axis halfway between x and z (which is exactly why it swaps the roles of the Z-basis and X-basis — it swaps the axes those bases sit on). S and T are partial rotations about z alone.

any single-qubit gate U  ⟺  a rotation R(axis, angle) of the Bloch vectorThis is the deepest reason unitarity matters geometrically: rotations preserve the sphere's radius (the state's normalization) exactly the way unitary matrices preserve |α|²+|β|²=1.

Figure 2.1 shows this concretely for H acting on |0⟩: the north pole swings down onto the equator.

✦ Worked Example — applying H then S to |0⟩

A qubit starts in |0⟩. A circuit applies H first, then S. Find the final state and its Bloch coordinates (θ, φ).

  1. Apply H: H|0⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 = |+⟩. Bloch coordinates (Course 1 §4.4): θ = 2·arccos(1/√2) = π/2, φ = arg(1/√2) − arg(1/√2) = 0. This sits exactly on the equator at φ=0, matching Figure 2.1.
  2. Apply S: S multiplies the |1⟩-amplitude by i: S|+⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2.
  3. Final state: SH|0⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩)/√2.
  4. New Bloch coordinates: θ is unchanged at π/2 (S never moves θ, per §2.3). φ shifts by the π/2 that S always adds: φ = 0 + π/2 = π/2. Equivalently, reading directly: arg(β) − arg(α) = arg(i) − arg(1) = π/2 − 0 = π/2. ✓ matches.
  5. Continuity check: (|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2 is exactly the state used as the running example in Course 1 Lecture 1's Born-rule discussion and Course 2 Lecture 1's worked example — H then S from |0⟩ is one concrete recipe for preparing it from scratch.

🛠️ Hands-On: build the H+S circuit in Qiskit

Confirm the worked example's statevector, and see Qiskit's built-in gates for S and T.

# hadamard_phase_circuit.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

qc = QuantumCircuit(1)
qc.h(0)    # Hadamard: |0> -> |+> = (|0>+|1>)/sqrt(2)
qc.s(0)    # S phase gate: multiplies the |1> amplitude by i

final = Statevector.from_label('0').evolve(qc)
print(final)                      # Statevector([0.707+0.j, 0.+0.707j])  -- matches (|0>+i|1>)/sqrt(2)
print(final.probabilities())      # [0.5, 0.5] -- still an equal superposition, as expected

# swap qc.s(0) for qc.t(0) to see the pi/4 phase gate instead

Qiskit's statevector printer uses a convention where a global phase may differ from the worked example's by an overall sign or phase — always compare probabilities or relative phase, never the raw printed amplitudes alone, to check two states for physical equality.

🔗 Where this is heading

H, S, and T are now three of the building blocks a real quantum compiler uses to approximate any single-qubit rotation — Lecture 3 makes that precise with the idea of a universal gate set. But single-qubit gates alone can never create entanglement; every state a one-qubit circuit can reach is, trivially, a product state with whatever else exists in the lab. Week 4 introduces the first multi-qubit gate, CNOT, which is what finally lets one qubit's state depend on another's — the missing ingredient for the Bell states two weeks from now.

Q1. H|0⟩ equals…
A. (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2
B. |1⟩
C. (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2
D. i|1⟩
Show answer

A. H|0⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 = |+⟩, the equal superposition with a relative phase of 0. Option C, |−⟩, is H|1⟩ instead.

Q2. H is best described as the gate that…
A. flips a sign on the |1⟩ amplitude only
B. swaps the computational (Z) basis with the X-basis {|+⟩,|−⟩}
C. is never its own inverse
D. only acts on multi-qubit states
Show answer

B. H|0⟩=|+⟩, H|1⟩=|−⟩, and applying H again returns you to the Z-basis — H is a basis-change operator between the two most-used single-qubit bases.

Q3. On the Bloch sphere, the S gate…
A. moves a state's polar angle θ but not its azimuthal angle φ
B. swaps the north and south poles
C. rotates the azimuthal angle φ by π/2, leaving θ unchanged
D. has no effect on any state whatsoever
Show answer

C. S is diagonal and only multiplies the |1⟩ amplitude by i — a pure phase shift of π/2 to φ, with θ (and both poles) left exactly where they were.

Q4. A qubit is exactly at the north pole, θ=0 (the state |0⟩). Applying S to it…
A. moves it to the equator
B. moves it to the south pole
C. is undefined at the poles
D. does nothing at all, since there is no azimuthal angle to rotate at a pole
Show answer

D. S|0⟩ = |0⟩ exactly — a diagonal phase gate with a 1 in the top-left entry never touches the |0⟩ amplitude, and a single point has no "around" for φ to rotate through.

Q5. Applying H, then S, to |0⟩ produces the state…
A. (|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2
B. |1⟩
C. (|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2
D. (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, unchanged by S
Show answer

A. H|0⟩=(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, and S then multiplies the |1⟩ amplitude by i, giving (|0⟩+i|1⟩)/√2 — exactly the worked example, and the same state used as a running example since Course 1 Lecture 1.
