Week 1 · Lecture 1

The Circuit Model & the Pauli Gates

A state vector tells you what a qubit is. A circuit tells you what happens to it — and the simplest things that can happen are the three Pauli gates.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Read a quantum circuit diagram: wires as qubits through time, boxes as gates, left-to-right order of operations.
  • Write the identity I and the Pauli gates X, Y, Z as 2×2 unitary matrices.
  • Describe what each Pauli gate does physically: bit-flip, phase-flip, or both.
  • Apply a sequence of Pauli gates to a qubit state by hand, matrix by matrix.
  • Build a one-qubit Qiskit circuit, apply gates, and read off the resulting statevector.

1.1The Circuit Model

Course 1 described a qubit's state — a vector |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ sitting in a two-dimensional complex space. This course is about what you do to that state. A quantum circuit is a picture of exactly that: a horizontal line (a wire) represents one qubit as it exists across time, and boxes placed on the wire represent gates — operations applied to the qubit. Time flows left to right: the leftmost box acts first, the rightmost box acts last.

|ψ₀⟩ ──[ G₁ ]──[ G₂ ]──[ G₃ ]── |ψ₃⟩One qubit wire, three gates applied in order. The qubit enters on the left in state |ψ₀⟩ and leaves on the right in state |ψ₃⟩ = G₃G₂G₁|ψ₀⟩.

That last detail trips everyone up at least once: circuit order reads left to right, but matrix order reads right to left. If G₁ is applied first and G₂ second, the combined operation is the matrix product G₂G₁ — G₁ sits closest to the state vector it acts on first, so it goes on the right of the product.

Every gate in this course — Pauli gates today, Hadamard and controlled gates soon — is represented by a unitary matrix: a matrix U whose inverse equals its conjugate transpose, U†U = I. Unitarity is not a convenience, it is a requirement: it is exactly the matrix condition that preserves the normalization |α|² + |β|² = 1 from Course 1. A gate that wasn't unitary would turn valid quantum states into invalid ones.

1.2The Identity and Pauli-X: the Bit-Flip

The simplest gate does nothing: the identity I. The next-simplest gate is the quantum analogue of a classical NOT gate — the Pauli-X gate, which swaps the two basis states:

I = [ 1 0 ; 0 1 ]     X = [ 0 1 ; 1 0 ]I leaves every state alone. X swaps the rows: X|0⟩ = |1⟩ and X|1⟩ = |0⟩.

Acting on a general state, X swaps the amplitudes themselves:

X(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) = α|1⟩ + β|0⟩ = β|0⟩ + α|1⟩The coefficient that was on |0⟩ moves to |1⟩, and vice versa — a literal bit-flip, now acting on amplitudes instead of a single bit.

On a circuit diagram, X is usually drawn as a box labeled "X," though many references also draw it as a ⊕ symbol borrowed from classical logic — a visual reminder that it generalizes the NOT gate you already know.

1.3Pauli-Z: the Phase-Flip, and Pauli-Y: Both at Once

X flips which basis state you're in. Pauli-Z does something classical bits can't even express: it leaves both basis states where they are but flips the sign of the |1⟩ amplitude — a phase-flip:

Z = [ 1 0 ; 0 −1 ]     Z(α|0⟩ + β|1⟩) = α|0⟩ − β|1⟩Z|0⟩ = |0⟩ and Z|1⟩ = −|1⟩. Measuring before or after Z gives identical probabilities |α|² and |β|² — Z is invisible to a direct measurement.

That last point matters: a Z gate changes nothing you could learn from measuring in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis right away, because flipping a sign doesn't change a squared magnitude. But it is not a do-nothing gate — the sign flip is a real, physical change to the relative phase from Course 1 §1.4, and it will show up the instant that qubit is combined with another gate (Hadamard, next week) or another qubit (interference, Course 3).

Pauli-Y does both a bit-flip and a phase-flip at once, with a factor of i along for the ride:

Y = [ 0 −i ; i 0 ]     Y|0⟩ = i|1⟩    Y|1⟩ = −i|0⟩Y = iXZ (up to how you order the product) — it swaps amplitudes and flips a sign and multiplies by i, all in one matrix.

All three Pauli matrices, together with I, are Hermitian (equal to their own conjugate transpose) and unitary. I, X, Y, Z form the Pauli basis; any real combination of them with unit Euclidean norm (a²+b²+c²+d²=1 in aI+bX+cY+dZ) is also both Hermitian and unitary — the Hadamard gate, (X+Z)/√2, is a familiar example you'll use starting next lecture. Even so, I, X, Y, Z remain the natural basis for describing any single-qubit operation or error.

AnalogyThink of the Bloch sphere from Course 1 §1.2 as a globe with |0⟩ at the north pole and |1⟩ at the south pole. X, Y, and Z are each a 180° spin of that globe around one of the three coordinate axes (hence the names): X spins around the x-axis, Y around the y-axis, Z around the z-axis. A north-pole point spun 180° about the x- or y-axis lands exactly on the south pole — that's the bit-flip. A point spun 180° about the z-axis (the axis running through both poles) doesn't move at all in position, but Course 2 Week 2 will show that a general point on the equator does move under Z — because Z is rotating phase, something only visible once you're off the poles.

1.4Circuit Notation & Composing Gates

Stacking gates on one wire means matrix multiplication, and matrix multiplication for 2×2 matrices does not commute — order matters. XZ and ZX are different matrices (you'll compute both in the worked example). This is the single biggest conceptual shift circuits add on top of Course 1's static state vectors: a circuit is a sequence, and the sequence is part of the answer.

XZ ≠ ZX    (in fact XZ = −ZX for the Pauli matrices)Gates that don't commute is exactly the kind of structure circuit diagrams are built to make unambiguous — read strictly left to right.
Common mistakeDon't read a circuit diagram's gate order and then multiply the matrices in that same left-to-right order. The first gate drawn (leftmost) acts on the state first, so it sits rightmost in the matrix product: a circuit drawn as "X then Z" corresponds to the matrix ZX, not XZ. Mixing this up silently gives you the wrong final state — and since X and Z don't commute here, you won't get a lucky match.
✦ Worked Example — applying X then Z by hand

A qubit is prepared in |ψ⟩ = (|0⟩ + i|1⟩) / √2 (the same state from Course 1 §1.3's Born-rule example). A circuit applies X first, then Z. Find the final state.

  1. Read off the amplitudes: α = 1/√2, β = i/√2.
  2. Apply X (swap the amplitudes): X|ψ⟩ = β|0⟩ + α|1⟩ = (i/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩.
  3. Apply Z (flip the sign on |1⟩): Z(X|ψ⟩) = (i/√2)|0⟩ − (1/√2)|1⟩.
  4. Final state: ZX|ψ⟩ = (i|0⟩ − |1⟩) / √2.
  5. Sanity check — normalization: |i/√2|² + |−1/√2|² = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 ✓. Unitary gates always preserve this, by construction.
  6. Sanity check — order matters: applying Z first and X second instead gives XZ|ψ⟩ = X((1/√2)|0⟩ − (i/√2)|1⟩) = −(i/√2)|0⟩ + (1/√2)|1⟩ — a different state (an overall factor of −1 on the i-term), confirming XZ ≠ ZX.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a one-qubit circuit in Qiskit

Qiskit is IBM's open-source Python framework for building and simulating quantum circuits — the toolkit for this course and the next. Week 1 setup: install it once (below), then reuse it every lecture and for the project.

⬇ Install Python + Qiskit (one-time, Week 1 setup)

With Python 3 installed (from python.org or your package manager), create an environment and install Qiskit plus its local simulator:

python3 -m venv qc-env
source qc-env/bin/activate         # macOS / Linux
qc-env\Scripts\activate            # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install qiskit qiskit-aer

Verify: run python -c "import qiskit; print(qiskit.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. qiskit-aer is the local simulator backend; no quantum hardware or IBM account is required for anything in this course.

Now build a one-qubit circuit that applies X then Z — exactly the worked example above — and read off the resulting statevector.

# pauli_circuit.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

# start from |psi> = (|0> + i|1>)/sqrt(2)
psi0 = Statevector([1/np.sqrt(2), 1j/np.sqrt(2)])

qc = QuantumCircuit(1)
qc.x(0)    # apply X first (bit-flip)
qc.z(0)    # apply Z second (phase-flip)

final = psi0.evolve(qc)
print(final)                      # Statevector([0.+0.707j, -0.707+0.j])
print(final.probabilities())      # [0.5, 0.5] -- matches the worked example

Swap the two lines to qc.z(0) then qc.x(0) and rerun. The printed statevector changes sign on one term — the circuit-level confirmation that XZ ≠ ZX.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Pauli gates are deliberately the simplest possible single-qubit operations — pure bit-flips and phase-flips, nothing in between. Week 2 introduces the Hadamard gate, which does something the Pauli gates never do: it turns a basis state into a genuine superposition, and it's the gate that makes the Bloch-sphere rotation picture from the analogy box above fully general. By Week 4 you'll extend single-qubit gates to multi-qubit gates like CNOT, where one qubit controls whether a Pauli gate fires on another — the mechanism behind every entangling circuit in this course, including the Bell states and teleportation circuit you'll build by Week 7.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a quantum circuit diagram, if gate A is drawn to the left of gate B on the same wire, the combined operation as a matrix acting on the input state is…
A. BA
B. AB
C. A + B
D. either, since matrices commute
Show answer

A. Circuit order reads left-to-right (A first), but matrix composition reads right-to-left, so the combined matrix is BA — B applied to the result of A applied to the state.

Q2. The Pauli-X gate acting on α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ produces…
A. α|0⟩ − β|1⟩
B. β|0⟩ + α|1⟩
C. α|1⟩ − β|0⟩
D. the same state, unchanged
Show answer

B. X swaps the two amplitudes — the coefficient on |0⟩ moves to |1⟩ and vice versa, giving β|0⟩ + α|1⟩. Option A has the right terms but a spurious sign error, and option C swaps the wrong labels — careful reading of the amplitude swap is the whole question.

Q3. Why can't a direct measurement in the {|0⟩, |1⟩} basis immediately after a Z gate ever detect that Z was applied?
A. Z is the identity matrix, so it truly does nothing
B. measurement always destroys the effect of any gate applied before it
C. Z only flips a sign, and squared magnitudes are unaffected by an overall sign flip on one amplitude's phase
D. Z is not a valid quantum gate
Show answer

C. Z(α|0⟩+β|1⟩) = α|0⟩ − β|1⟩, and |−β|² = |β|², so the measurement probabilities are unchanged. Z's effect is a real relative-phase change, just not one visible to a same-basis measurement alone.

Q4. Every single-qubit gate in this course — X, Y, Z, and later H and phase gates — must be represented by a unitary matrix because…
A. unitary matrices are easier to multiply by hand
B. only unitary matrices can be drawn as circuit boxes
C. unitary matrices always have real entries
D. unitarity is exactly the condition that preserves the normalization |α|²+|β|²=1
Show answer

D. A gate must map every valid (normalized) state to another valid (normalized) state. The matrix condition that guarantees this is unitarity, U†U = I. (Pauli-Y shows unitary matrices can absolutely have complex entries, ruling out option C.)

Q5. The Pauli-Y gate's action, Y|0⟩ = i|1⟩ and Y|1⟩ = −i|0⟩, is best described as…
A. a phase-flip only, with no bit-flip
B. a bit-flip and a phase-flip combined, with an extra factor of i
C. the identity operation in disguise
D. only defined for two-qubit systems
Show answer

B. Y changes |0⟩ to |1⟩ and |1⟩ to |0⟩ (the bit-flip part of X) while also attaching a sign and a factor of i (the phase-flip part of Z) — consistent with Y being proportional to the product XZ.
