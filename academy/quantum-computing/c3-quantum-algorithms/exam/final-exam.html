Oracles, Deutsch-Jozsa & BV/Simon · Weeks 1–3
1. The Deutsch problem's classical query complexity is exactly 2 because…
Answer: B. A single query result is consistent with f being either constant or balanced; only comparing f(0) and f(1) settles the question with certainty.
2. The Deutsch-Jozsa algorithm's quantum query count, regardless of n, is…
Answer: A. One oracle query, followed by H⊗n and a measurement, suffices for any n — the whole point of the exponential classical-vs-quantum separation.
3. Bernstein-Vazirani recovers a hidden n-bit string s from f(x)=s·x mod 2 using how many quantum queries?
Answer: C. The same H⊗n-oracle-H⊗n circuit used for Deutsch-Jozsa decodes the entire hidden string s in a single query, versus n queries classically.
4. Simon's problem's hidden structure is characterized by…
Answer: B. Every output value of f is produced by exactly the pair {x, x⊕s} — finding s is the goal, solved in O(n) quantum queries versus exponentially many classically.
5. The unifying insight across Deutsch-Jozsa and Bernstein-Vazirani is that…
Answer: A. Same circuit skeleton, different oracle promise and interpretation of the measured output — a strong hint the pattern is a genuine primitive, not three unrelated tricks.
Grover's Algorithm · Weeks 4–5
6. Classically, searching N unstructured items for one marked item requires, worst case…
Answer: B. With no exploitable structure, a classical algorithm may need to check nearly every item — linear, O(N), worst case.
7. The Grover diffusion operator D = 2|s⟩⟨s| − I acts by…
Answer: C. D reflects each amplitude about the mean amplitude — amplitudes below average rise, those above fall, by equal amounts.
8. Geometrically, one Grover iteration G = D·U_f acts on the 2D marked/unmarked state vector as…
Answer: A. Both the oracle and the diffusion operator are reflections; composing two reflections yields a fixed-angle rotation — the geometric heart of amplitude amplification.
9. The BBBV theorem shows that for unstructured search…
Answer: B. BBBV proves Ω(√N) is a hard floor for any quantum algorithm — Grover's algorithm is provably optimal, not merely the best known.
10. For N=16 with M=4 marked items, the optimal Grover iteration count compared to M=1 is roughly…
Answer: B. The asymptotic estimate r ≈ (π/4)√(N/M) suggests roughly halving when M quadruples, but the exact geometry — success probability sin²((2k+1)θ₀) with θ₀=arcsin(√(M/N)) — shows the optimal count drops even further for N=16: r=3 at M=1, but just r=1 at M=4 (θ₀=30°, so k=1 already reaches 90° and certainty; a second iteration would overshoot past the optimum).
QFT & Phase Estimation · Weeks 6–7
11. The QFT circuit is built from…
Answer: C. Each qubit gets a Hadamard then controlled rotations from every later qubit, with angles halving each step — the circuit analog of the classical FFT's recursion.
12. Despite its O(n²)-gate efficiency, the QFT does NOT let you…
Answer: B. The transformed coefficients exist only as amplitudes; one measurement collapses to a single basis state — the QFT is not a shortcut for extracting a classical Fourier spectrum.
13. Quantum phase estimation estimates…
Answer: A. QPE's entire output is an estimate of the real number θ hidden in U's eigenvalue — invisible on |ψ⟩ alone until the ancilla trick exposes it.
14. With t ancilla (counting) qubits, QPE resolves θ to roughly what precision?
Answer: C. t counting qubits distinguish 2ᵗ outcomes, resolving θ to roughly 1/2ᵗ — exponentially finer resolution per added ancilla qubit.
15. The three stages of the QPE circuit, in order, are…
Answer: D. H⊗t spreads the counting register, controlled-U powers kick back θ-dependent phases, and the inverse QFT decodes those phases into a readable integer.
Shor's Algorithm & Complexity · Weeks 8–9
16. Factoring N reduces, classically, to finding…
Answer: D. Once r is known, a gcd computation usually yields a nontrivial factor of N — a purely classical number-theory reduction.
17. Within Shor's algorithm, quantum phase estimation is applied to…
Answer: C. This unitary's eigenphases are of the form k/r, so QPE applied to it yields an estimate from which the period r can be extracted.
18. After QPE gives a phase estimate close to k/r, the classical continued-fraction algorithm is used to…
Answer: D. Continued fractions find small-denominator fractions close to the estimate; the denominator found is (a divisor of) the sought period r.
19. Shor's algorithm threatens RSA-style cryptography because…
Answer: A. This is a forward-looking, motivational result — current hardware is nowhere near able to run it at cryptographically meaningful sizes, but the asymptotic threat is real and specific.
20. BQP (bounded-error quantum polynomial time) is informally defined as…
Answer: D. BQP is the quantum analog of BPP: polynomial time, error bounded (conventionally ≤1/3, amplifiable by repetition) — not zero-error, and not restricted to NP-complete problems.
21. Shor's algorithm efficiently solving factoring (which is in NP) shows…
Answer: B. Factoring being in BQP ∩ NP is narrow and specific; BQP's relationship to NP-complete problems remains a central open question with no known efficient quantum algorithm.
Capstone Synthesis · Week 10
22. The Week 10 capstone project combines…
Answer: D. The capstone is the synthesis the course project asked for from Week 1: both halves of the course, built and verified side by side.
23. For the capstone's Grover part (N=16, M=1), the optimal iteration count is…
Answer: C. r ≈ (π/4)√(16/1) = π ≈ 3.14, rounding to 3 iterations.
24. For the capstone's QPE part targeting θ=3/8 with t=3 ancilla qubits, the expected result is…
Answer: D. θ=3/8=0.011₂ is exactly 3-bit representable, so t=3 ancillas recover it exactly, with certainty.
25. Course 4 picks up directly from this course's honest BQP assessment by addressing…
Answer: A. Every circuit in this course ran on a perfect noiseless simulator; Course 4 (Noise, Error Correction & NISQ) addresses exactly the engineering gap between that and real hardware.
