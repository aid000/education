Grover's Algorithm II: Optimality & Multiple Solutions

√N isn't just what Grover's construction happens to achieve — it's the best any quantum algorithm can do. And iterating too far rotates right past the answer.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State that O(√N) is a proven quantum lower bound for unstructured search — no algorithm can beat it.
  • Derive how the optimal iteration count changes when M>1 items are marked: ~√(N/M).
  • Explain why over-iterating past the optimum rotates the state past the marked axis, hurting success probability.
  • Compute optimal iteration counts for N=16 at M=1 and M=4.
  • Simulate over-iteration in Qiskit and observe the success probability oscillate.

5.1O(√N) Is Provably Optimal

Lecture 4 showed a quantum algorithm achieving O(√N) for unstructured search. A natural question: is that just the best construction found so far, or is it the best possible? The BBBV theorem (Bennett, Bernstein, Brassard, Vazirani, 1997) answers this with a genuine quantum lower bound: no quantum algorithm, no matter how cleverly built, can solve unstructured search in fewer than Ω(√N) queries. The proof is a careful "hybrid" argument comparing a quantum algorithm's behavior on oracles differing in exactly one marked position — it's beyond today's scope to work through, but the conclusion is load-bearing for the whole field:

Any quantum algorithm needs Ω(√N) queries for unstructured searchThis is one of the few quantum speedup limits proven rigorously, not just "not yet beaten" — Grover's algorithm is asymptotically optimal, not merely good.

This matters because it tells you where to stop looking for improvements. Grover's quadratic speedup is the ceiling for generic unstructured search — any further speedup on a real problem has to come from exploiting extra structure in that specific problem (the way Weeks 1–3's promises enabled exponential speedups), not from a better generic search algorithm.

5.2Multiple Marked Solutions: M > 1

Suppose instead of exactly one marked item, there are M marked items out of N (M known in advance). The geometric picture from Lecture 4 still applies — a 2D rotation in a marked/unmarked plane — but now the marked axis represents the combined subspace of all M marked states, and the starting angle is larger: θ₀ = arcsin(√(M/N)) instead of arcsin(1/√N).

Optimal iteration count r ≈ (π/4)√(N/M)More marked items means the starting vector is already closer to the marked axis, so fewer iterations are needed to rotate the rest of the way.

This is intuitive: if a quarter of all items are marked (M = N/4), a uniformly random guess already succeeds 25% of the time, so amplitude amplification has less work left to do — correspondingly, it needs fewer iterations than the M=1 case. The asymptotic formula says roughly √(N/M) = √4 = 2 times fewer, but that's a ballpark: the exact count (from the geometry in §5.3) can drop even further once you check it against the rounding, as the N=16 worked example below shows.

5.3Over-Iterating: Grover Walks Past the Answer

Because each Grover iteration is a fixed-angle rotation, there is nothing that stops the vector at the marked axis — it keeps rotating. Iterate past the optimal count r, and the vector overshoots the marked axis, swinging back down toward (and eventually past) the unmarked axis again. Success probability, as a function of the number of iterations, oscillates periodically rather than monotonically increasing and then plateauing.

Common mistakeIt's tempting to assume "more iterations = more amplification, so just run extra iterations to be safe." That's backwards — because the rotation doesn't stop at 90°, extra iterations past the optimum can drive the success probability back down toward zero, not hold it near its peak. Grover's algorithm needs the iteration count computed in advance (from N and M) and run exactly that many times — it is not a "the more the better" algorithm.
✦ Worked Example — optimal iterations for N=16, M=1 vs. M=4

Compute r = round((π/4)√(N/M)) for both cases and compare.

  1. M=1 (one marked item out of 16): r ≈ (π/4)√(16/1) = (π/4)(4) = π ≈ 3.14 → round to r = 3 iterations.
  2. M=4 (four marked items out of 16): the asymptotic estimate gives r ≈ (π/4)√(16/4) = (π/4)(2) = π/2 ≈ 1.57, which naively rounds to r = 2 — but check the exact geometry before trusting that: θ₀ = arcsin(√(4/16)) = arcsin(1/2) = 30°, so after just k=1 iteration the rotation angle is (2k+1)θ₀ = 90°, giving success probability sin²(90°) = 1 — certainty, already. A second iteration (k=2) pushes the angle to 150°, dropping success probability to sin²(150°) = 25% — overshooting past the optimum. So the true optimal count here is r = 1, not 2: naive rounding of the asymptotic formula overshoots.
  3. Compare: quadrupling the number of marked items (M: 1→4) doesn't just roughly halve the needed iteration count — it drops r from 3 all the way to 1 (a 3× reduction), because rounding the asymptotic estimate (1.57→2) overshoots past the exact optimum. The lesson: use (π/4)√(N/M) for a ballpark, then check the EXACT angle (2k+1)θ₀ (and its neighbors) before rounding, since rounding the wrong way overshoots and success probability drops back down.
  4. Sanity check against §5.1's bound: even M=1's r=3 is comfortably O(√16)=O(4) — well within the proven lower-bound regime, not beating it.

🛠️ Hands-On: watch success probability oscillate with iteration count

Reuse Lecture 4's N=8, single-marked-item circuit, but sweep the iteration count from 0 to 6 and print the marked-state probability at each step — confirm it peaks near the optimal r=2 and then falls back down, exactly as the oscillation figure predicts.

# grover_overshoot.py — Lecture 5
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

n = 3
marked = "101"

def oracle(qc):
    for i, b in enumerate(reversed(marked)):
        if b == '0': qc.x(i)
    qc.h(n - 1); qc.mcx(list(range(n - 1)), n - 1); qc.h(n - 1)
    for i, b in enumerate(reversed(marked)):
        if b == '0': qc.x(i)

def diffusion(qc):
    qc.h(range(n)); qc.x(range(n))
    qc.h(n - 1); qc.mcx(list(range(n - 1)), n - 1); qc.h(n - 1)
    qc.x(range(n)); qc.h(range(n))

for iterations in range(0, 7):
    qc = QuantumCircuit(n)
    qc.h(range(n))
    for _ in range(iterations):
        oracle(qc)
        diffusion(qc)
    sv = Statevector.from_instruction(qc)
    p_marked = sv.probabilities_dict()[marked]
    print(f"iterations={iterations:2d}  P(marked)={p_marked:.3f}")

Expected output: P(marked) rises from 0.125 (iteration 0, uniform) to a peak near iteration 2 (close to 1.0), then falls again at iterations 3–4 before rising a second time near iteration 5–6 — a visible oscillation confirming that running Grover's algorithm "a little extra for safety" can actively hurt your odds.

Q1. The BBBV theorem establishes that for unstructured search…
A. classical algorithms are always faster than quantum ones
B. no quantum algorithm can do better than Ω(√N) queries — a proven lower bound
C. Grover's algorithm can be improved to O(log N)
D. the problem is undecidable
Show answer

B. BBBV proves Ω(√N) is a hard floor for any quantum algorithm on generic unstructured search — Grover's algorithm is asymptotically optimal, not just currently the best known.

Q2. With M marked items out of N, the optimal Grover iteration count scales as…
A. √(N/M)
B. N/M
C. M/N
D. independent of M
Show answer

A. More marked items means the starting angle θ₀ is already larger, so less rotation (fewer iterations, scaling as √(N/M)) is needed to reach the marked subspace.

Q3. Running Grover's algorithm for significantly more iterations than the computed optimum r will…
A. always increase success probability further
B. have no effect on success probability
C. rotate the state past the marked axis, which can lower success probability
D. make the oracle stop working
Show answer

C. Each iteration is a fixed rotation that doesn't stop at the marked axis — overshooting past it swings success probability back down, sometimes close to its starting value.

Q4. For N=16 with M=1 marked item, the optimal iteration count r ≈ (π/4)√(N/M) rounds to…
A. 16
B. 1
C. 3
D. 8
Show answer

C. (π/4)√(16/1) = (π/4)(4) = π ≈ 3.14, rounding to 3 iterations — matching the worked example.

Q5. For N=16 with M=4 marked items, compared to M=1, the optimal iteration count is roughly…
A. the same
B. four times larger
C. four times smaller
D. about half as large
Show answer

D. The asymptotic formula r ∝ √(N/M) suggests roughly half the iterations when M quadruples — but checking the exact geometry (§5.3) shows the true optimum drops even further for N=16: r=3 at M=1, down to r=1 at M=4 (not r=2, which overshoots). Of the choices given, "about half as large" is the closest — the exact drop is more than half, a reminder to always check the exact angle rather than trust the rounded asymptotic estimate.
