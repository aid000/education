The Complexity Zoo: BQP and What "Quantum Speedup" Actually Means

An honest accounting of eight weeks of speedups — what was actually shown, for what kind of problem, and what wasn't.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define BQP informally and place it relative to P, NP, and BPP.
  • Explain why BQP is believed to strictly contain BPP, but its relation to NP is open.
  • Characterize honestly what speedup each algorithm in this course actually delivers.
  • Distinguish "exponential speedup on an artificial oracle problem" from "exponential speedup on a practically important problem."
  • Articulate a grounded, non-hyped statement of what's been learned about quantum computational power.

9.1BQP, Informally

BQP (bounded-error quantum polynomial time) is the class of decision problems solvable by a quantum computer in polynomial time, with probability of error at most 1/3 on every input (the "bounded error" part — the threshold 1/3 is arbitrary and can be amplified close to 0 by repetition, a standard complexity-theory convention). Informally: BQP is "the problems a quantum computer can solve efficiently, with high confidence."

BQP = problems solvable in poly(n) time by a quantum circuit, error ≤ 1/3Compare to its classical analog BPP (bounded-error probabilistic polynomial time) — the exact same definition, with a classical randomized circuit instead of a quantum one.

Three classical classes for comparison: P (deterministic polynomial time — no randomness, no error allowed), BPP (polynomial time with a classical coin and bounded error — P with randomness added), and NP (problems whose solutions, once found, can be verified in polynomial time — not about how hard they are to find).

9.2Where BQP Sits

What's known, carefully stated: P ⊆ BPP ⊆ BQP — every deterministic-polynomial problem is also solvable with bounded-error randomness, and every such randomized algorithm can be simulated on a quantum computer (a quantum computer can always just... run the classical randomized algorithm). The first containment is where complexity theorists expect no real gap at all: P = BPP is the usual working conjecture — derandomization (removing the randomness at only a polynomial-time cost) is widely believed to always be possible. The second containment is where the interesting, quantum-specific belief lives: whether BQP equals BPP (i.e., whether quantum computers ever help at all) is not resolved, but they're believed not equal, and Shor's algorithm is the strongest evidence — if the usual conjectures about classical factoring hardness hold, Shor's algorithm gives a problem in BQP with no known BPP algorithm.

P = BPP (conjectured)     BPP ≠ BQP (believed, not proven)The honest state of affairs: randomness is conjectured to buy classical computers nothing asymptotic (P=BPP, via derandomization), but quantum does buy something — BPP≠BQP is the separation Shor's algorithm provides strong evidence for. Neither is an unconditional proof; complexity theory has very few of those between major classes.

BQP's relationship to NP is a genuinely open and important question: it is not known whether BQP contains NP, is contained in NP, or neither. Factoring (solved efficiently by Shor's algorithm, putting it in BQP) is in NP but not believed to be NP-complete — so Shor's result does not show BQP contains all of NP, and in particular gives no evidence that quantum computers can efficiently solve NP-complete problems like SAT. This single point corrects one of the most common misconceptions about quantum computing in popular coverage.

Common mistake"Quantum computers can solve NP-complete problems efficiently" does not follow from anything shown in this course, and is not believed to be true. Grover's algorithm gives a quadratic speedup for brute-force search over any NP problem's candidate solutions — useful, but a quadratic speedup on an exponential brute-force search is still exponential overall. No known quantum algorithm solves NP-complete problems in polynomial time, and most researchers do not expect one to exist.

Compressing eight weeks into one honest table — what kind of speedup, and for what kind of problem:

The pattern worth internalizing: the biggest speedups (Deutsch-Jozsa, Simon) came on the least practically important problems, while the most practically important result (Shor's) is exponential but required a specific, carefully-chosen real-world structure (modular exponentiation's periodicity) to exploit — it is not a generic method that speeds up "hard problems" broadly. Grover sits in between: modest (quadratic) but genuinely general-purpose, applying to any brute-force search problem without needing special structure.

✦ Worked Example — classifying a new problem's speedup honestly

Suppose someone claims: "I found a quantum algorithm that searches an unstructured database of N records in O(log N) time." Apply this lecture's tools to evaluate the claim.

  1. Identify the problem class: unstructured search over N items, with no additional promise or structure specified — exactly Grover's problem setting from Weeks 4–5.
  2. Recall the proven bound: Week 5's BBBV theorem established Ω(√N) as a hard quantum lower bound for exactly this problem — no quantum algorithm, however clever, can beat it.
  3. Compare to the claim: O(log N) is asymptotically far below Ω(√N) for large N (log N grows much slower than √N).
  4. Conclusion: the claim contradicts a proven theorem. Either the problem being solved isn't truly unstructured search (some hidden extra structure is being assumed), or the claimed algorithm is incorrect — "O(log N) unstructured search" cannot be real, exactly the kind of overclaim this lecture's honesty framing is meant to catch.

No new circuit this week — instead, build a small script that reproduces the scorecard numerically for a chosen problem size, reinforcing just how different "exponential," "quadratic," and "linear" look once you plug in actual numbers.

# speedup_scorecard.py — Lecture 9
import math

n = 20          # number of bits / qubits
N = 2**n        # problem size for search-type problems

rows = [
    ("Deutsch-Jozsa",      2**(n-1) + 1,          1),
    ("Bernstein-Vazirani", n,                      1),
    ("Grover search",      N,                      round((math.pi/4)*math.sqrt(N))),
    ("Shor (factoring)",   "super-poly in log N (believed)", "poly in log N"),
]

print(f"{'Algorithm':<22}{'Classical cost':<30}{'Quantum cost':<15}")
for name, classical, quantum in rows:
    print(f"{name:<22}{str(classical):<30}{str(quantum):<15}")

Expected output: at n=20, Deutsch-Jozsa's classical cost is 524,289 vs. 1 quantum query; Grover's search cost is 1,048,576 classically vs. about 804 quantum iterations — numbers large enough to make the asymptotic gaps from this lecture's scorecard concrete rather than abstract.

Q2. The known containment relationship among P, BPP, and BQP, and what's believed about it, is…

A. Every deterministic algorithm is a randomized one, and every randomized algorithm can run on a quantum computer — giving the containment P ⊆ BPP ⊆ BQP. But the believed-strict separation is specifically BPP ≠ BQP (with Shor's algorithm as the strongest evidence); P = BPP, not a strict separation, is the usual working conjecture for the first containment.
