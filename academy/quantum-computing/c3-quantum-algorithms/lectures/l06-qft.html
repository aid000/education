Week 6 · Lecture 6

The Quantum Fourier Transform (QFT)

The circuit that turns computational-basis states into Fourier-coefficient superpositions — in O(n²) gates, not O(n·2ⁿ).

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the QFT as the quantum analog of the classical discrete Fourier transform (DFT).
  • Describe the QFT circuit: Hadamards plus controlled phase rotations, in a divide-and-conquer structure.
  • Explain why the QFT circuit needs only O(n²) gates, versus O(n·2ⁿ) for a brute-force classical DFT.
  • State clearly why the QFT does NOT give classical read-out access to the Fourier coefficients.
  • Build and run a 3-qubit QFT circuit in Qiskit and inspect the resulting amplitudes.

6.1The QFT as a Quantum Analog of the DFT

The classical discrete Fourier transform (DFT) takes a vector of N complex numbers and produces another vector of N complex numbers — the original data re-expressed in terms of frequency components. The quantum Fourier transform (QFT) is the exact same linear transformation, applied instead to the amplitudes of an n-qubit state (N = 2n):

QFT : |j⟩ ↦ (1/√N) Σₖ e^(2πijk/N) |k⟩   (N = 2ⁿ, sum over k = 0..N−1)Each computational basis state |j⟩ maps to a superposition over all N basis states, weighted by the same complex exponential the classical DFT uses.

Applied to a general superposition Σⱼ aⱼ|j⟩, the QFT acts linearly, mapping the amplitude vector (a₀,…,a_{N-1}) to its DFT (ã₀,…,ã_{N-1}), exactly as the classical transform would — just carried out as amplitudes inside a quantum state rather than as numbers in classical memory.

6.2The QFT Circuit: Hadamards + Controlled Phase Rotations

The QFT factors into a product of single- and two-qubit gates with a structure that echoes the classical FFT's divide-and-conquer recursion. For each qubit (from most- to least-significant), apply a Hadamard, then a cascade of controlled phase rotations Rk = diag(1, e2πi/2k) controlled by every qubit that comes after it, with the rotation angle halving each time you move one qubit further away:

R_k = [[1, 0], [0, e^(2πi/2ᵏ)]]A phase gate that leaves |0⟩ alone and multiplies |1⟩ by a 2ᵏ-th root of unity — the building block for every controlled rotation in the circuit.

This cascading, halving-angle structure is the direct circuit analog of the classical fast Fourier transform's (FFT) divide-and-conquer recursion — splitting the problem by bit position rather than computing the full O(N) sum for each output directly.

6.3O(n²) Gates vs. O(n·2ⁿ) Classical — But Read a Careful Asterisk

The QFT circuit uses one Hadamard and up to n−1 controlled rotations per qubit, across n qubits: O(n²) gates total. Classically, evaluating the DFT at all N=2n frequencies directly from the sum definition costs O(N²) = O(4n) multiplications; even the classical FFT, which is itself a major algorithmic improvement, costs O(N log N) = O(n·2n). The QFT's O(n²) is exponentially cheaper than even the classical FFT.

QFT: O(n²) gates   |   classical FFT: O(n·2ⁿ) operationsA genuinely exponential advantage in gate/operation count for computing "the same transform" — with a crucial caveat coming next.
Common mistake — read this carefullyThe QFT's exponential gate-count advantage does not mean you can extract all N Fourier coefficients and read them out classically in O(n²) time. After running the QFT circuit, the result lives as amplitudes in a quantum superposition — invisible until measured, and a single measurement yields only one basis state, collapsing the rest. You cannot simply "read off" the full Fourier spectrum the way you would from a classical FFT's output array. The QFT is a tool for computations that only need to interrogate structure in the transformed state (like phase estimation, Week 7) — not a general-purpose way to compute and extract a classical Fourier transform faster than the classical FFT. This nuance is one of the most common points of confusion about the QFT, and it's worth internalizing now, before Week 7 puts it to direct use.
✦ Worked Example — QFT of |1⟩ for a single qubit (n=1, N=2)

Confirm the n=1 QFT circuit is literally just a Hadamard, by matching it against the formula.

  1. Formula: QFT|j⟩ = (1/√2) Σₖ e^(2πijk/2) |k⟩ = (1/√2)[ |0⟩ + e^(πij)|1⟩ ].
  2. Plug in j=1: QFT|1⟩ = (1/√2)[ |0⟩ + e^(πi)|1⟩ ] = (1/√2)[ |0⟩ − |1⟩ ] = |−⟩.
  3. Compare to a Hadamard: H|1⟩ = (1/√2)(|0⟩ − |1⟩) = |−⟩ — an exact match. The n=1 QFT is literally the Hadamard gate — the n-qubit QFT circuit of §6.2 is this single-qubit case, generalized with the controlled-rotation cascade added for the cross-qubit terms.
  4. Takeaway: every Hadamard you've used since Lecture 1 was already a (trivial, 1-qubit) quantum Fourier transform — the QFT circuit is what it takes to generalize that same idea to n qubits.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a 3-qubit QFT circuit and inspect the output

Qiskit ships a built-in QFT gate; build it from scratch once (matching the circuit diagram above) to see the controlled-rotation structure directly, then compare to the library version.

# qft_circuit.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from qiskit import QuantumCircuit
from qiskit.circuit.library import QFT
from qiskit.quantum_info import Statevector

def qft_by_hand(n):
    qc = QuantumCircuit(n)
    for i in range(n):
        qc.h(i)
        for j in range(i + 1, n):
            angle = 2 * np.pi / 2**(j - i + 1)
            qc.cp(angle, j, i)     # controlled phase rotation, halving each step away
    for i in range(n // 2):        # final swap to match standard qubit ordering
        qc.swap(i, n - i - 1)
    return qc

n = 3
qc_manual = QuantumCircuit(n)
qc_manual.x(0)   # prepare |001> BEFORE transforming, to make the output non-trivial
qc_manual.compose(qft_by_hand(n), inplace=True)   # then append the by-hand QFT on top

sv = Statevector.from_instruction(qc_manual)
print("QFT|001> amplitudes (by hand):", np.round(sv.data, 3))

qc_lib = QuantumCircuit(n)
qc_lib.x(0)
qc_lib.append(QFT(n), range(n))
sv_lib = Statevector.from_instruction(qc_lib)
print("QFT|001> amplitudes (library):   ", np.round(sv_lib.data, 3))

Expected output: both amplitude vectors match (up to Qiskit's little-endian qubit-ordering convention) — 8 complex amplitudes, each of magnitude 1/√8, with phases stepping through e^(2πik/8) for k=0..7, exactly the formula from §6.1 applied to j=1.

🔗 Where this is heading

Today's §6.3 warning — that the QFT doesn't hand you classical access to Fourier coefficients — sets up exactly the right question for next week: what can you usefully extract from a QFT-transformed state? Week 7's quantum phase estimation answers that: by combining the QFT with a controlled-unitary setup, you can extract a single real number (an eigenphase θ) with high precision from a quantum state, using the QFT's inverse as the final decoding step. That's the QFT finally earning its keep — not as a general transform you read out, but as the back-end of a measurement protocol.

A. a measurement operation
B. the classical DFT applied to the amplitudes of a quantum state
C. a classical algorithm with no quantum circuit equivalent

B. The QFT is exactly the DFT's linear transformation, applied to the amplitude vector of an n-qubit quantum state rather than to classical numbers in memory.

Q2. The QFT circuit is built from…
A. Toffoli gates only
B. measurement gates in a loop
C. Hadamards and controlled phase rotations, in a divide-and-conquer cascade
D. only CNOT gates
Show answer

C. Each qubit gets a Hadamard followed by controlled rotations from every later qubit, with rotation angles halving each step — directly echoing the classical FFT's recursive structure.

Q3. The QFT circuit's gate count scales as…
A. O(n²), versus O(n·2ⁿ) for the classical FFT
B. O(2ⁿ), same as the classical FFT
C. O(n), better than the classical FFT's O(n log n)
D. O(n!), worse than any classical approach
Show answer

A. The cascading controlled-rotation structure costs O(n²) gates — exponentially fewer than the O(n·2ⁿ) operations the classical FFT needs to evaluate all 2ⁿ frequencies.

Q4. Despite its O(n²)-gate efficiency, the QFT does NOT let you…
A. be implemented on a quantum circuit at all
B. act linearly on superpositions
C. directly read out all N classical Fourier coefficients from one run
D. be used as a subroutine in other algorithms
Show answer

C. The transformed coefficients exist only as amplitudes; a single measurement collapses to one basis state, so you cannot extract the full classical Fourier spectrum the way a classical FFT's output array lets you.

Q5. The n=1 (single-qubit) quantum Fourier transform is exactly…
A. the identity gate
B. the Hadamard gate
C. the Pauli-X gate
D. undefined for n=1
Show answer

B. Plugging n=1 into the QFT formula reproduces exactly H|0⟩ and H|1⟩ — every Hadamard used since Lecture 1 is a trivial, single-qubit instance of the QFT.
