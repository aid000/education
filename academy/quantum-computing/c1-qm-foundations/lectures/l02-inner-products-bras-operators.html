Inner Products, Bras & Operators

Every ket has a mirror-image bra. Every gate is a matrix. Two of those matrices — Hermitian and unitary — are the only ones physics ever lets you use.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Form the bra ⟨ψ| from a ket |ψ⟩ and compute inner products ⟨φ|ψ⟩.
  • Treat 2×2 matrices as operators that act on kets, and recognize a quantum gate as exactly that.
  • Define Hermitian operators (A = A†) and explain why every observable must be one.
  • Define unitary operators (U†U = I) and explain why every quantum gate must be one.
  • Find the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a 2×2 matrix by hand.
  • Build a Pauli matrix in QuTiP/NumPy and verify it numerically satisfies both properties.

2.1The Bra: a Ket's Mirror Image

Lecture 1 wrote a qubit as a column vector, the ket |ψ⟩ = [α, β]ᵀ. For every ket there is a partner row vector called the bra, written ⟨ψ|, formed by taking the conjugate transpose (flip rows/columns, then complex-conjugate every entry):

|ψ⟩ = [ α , β ]ᵀ    →    ⟨ψ| ≡ |ψ⟩† = [ α* , β* ]The dagger (†) means "conjugate transpose." Bras are row vectors of complex conjugates.

Putting a bra next to a ket — "bra-c-ket" — produces the inner product, a single complex number measuring how much two states overlap:

⟨φ|ψ⟩ = φ₁*ψ₁ + φ₂*ψ₂The inner product of ⟨φ| = [φ₁*, φ₂*] with |ψ⟩ = [ψ₁, ψ₂]ᵀ.

The basis states satisfy ⟨0|0⟩ = ⟨1|1⟩ = 1 and ⟨0|1⟩ = ⟨1|0⟩ = 0 — they're orthonormal. That is exactly why ⟨0|ψ⟩ = α and ⟨1|ψ⟩ = β: the inner product with a basis bra extracts that basis's amplitude. And ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = |α|² + |β|² = 1 is nothing new — it's Lecture 1's normalization condition, now written as an inner product of a state with itself.

2.2Operators: Matrices That Act on Kets

An operator is anything that takes one ket and returns another. For a single qubit, every operator we'll meet is a 2×2 matrix, and "applying the operator" is ordinary matrix-vector multiplication. This is the entire mathematical content of a quantum gate: a gate is a 2×2 matrix acting on a state vector.

X = [ 0 1 ; 1 0 ]      X|0⟩ = |1⟩      X|1⟩ = |0⟩The Pauli-X matrix, written row-by-row, flips the two basis states — the quantum NOT gate.

You'll meet its partners Y and Z throughout the course: Z = [[1,0],[0,−1]] leaves |0⟩ alone and flips the sign of |1⟩; Y mixes a flip with a phase. Sandwiching an operator between a bra and a ket, ⟨φ|A|ψ⟩, collapses the whole expression down to one complex number — first apply A to |ψ⟩, getting a new ket, then take the inner product with ⟨φ|. This "sandwich" is the single most common expression in quantum mechanics; you'll use it constantly starting next lecture.

2.3Hermitian Operators: the Observables

Not every matrix can represent something you measure. A physical observable — energy, spin along some axis, anything with a real-world reading — must be represented by a Hermitian operator, defined by:

A = A†     (equivalently: Ajk = Akj*)A matrix is Hermitian if it equals its own conjugate transpose.

The reason is almost forced on you: a measurement has to return a real number, and the average of many measurements, ⟨A⟩ = ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩, has to come out real for every possible state |ψ⟩ you might measure. It is a short exercise in linear algebra to show that the only matrices with that property are exactly the Hermitian ones. All three Pauli matrices X, Y, Z are Hermitian — which is exactly why they double as physical observables (spin components) and not just gates.

Common mistakeHermitian does not mean "all entries are real." A = [[0, −i],[i, 0]] (that's Y) has purely imaginary off-diagonal entries and is still Hermitian, because the mirror-image entries are complex conjugates of each other: A₁₂ = −i = (i)* = A₂₁*. What Hermitian does force is that the diagonal entries are real — Ajj = Ajj* only holds if Ajj is real.

2.4Unitary Operators: the Gates

Gates face a different constraint. Applying a gate must never destroy or create probability — if |ψ⟩ is normalized, U|ψ⟩ must be normalized too. Write |ψ'⟩ = U|ψ⟩ and demand ⟨ψ'|ψ'⟩ = 1 given ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ = 1:

⟨ψ'|ψ'⟩ = ⟨ψ|U†U|ψ⟩ = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩     →     U†U = IThis must hold for every |ψ⟩, which forces U†U = I — the definition of a unitary matrix.

A unitary operator is exactly one satisfying U†U = I (equivalently UU† = I). Geometrically, unitary operators rotate the state vector without stretching or shrinking it — they preserve length, hence preserve total probability. Every gate in every quantum circuit you will ever see, from a single-qubit flip to a thousand-qubit algorithm, is unitary for exactly this reason.

AnalogyThink of Hermitian and unitary as two different jobs, occasionally done by the same matrix. Hermitian operators are rulers — things you use to read a value off a state. Unitary operators are rotations — things you use to turn one state into another without changing its length. The Pauli matrices happen to be both at once (X†X = I and X† = X), which is a special feature of this 2-level case, not a general rule — most Hermitian matrices aren't unitary, and vice versa. In Week 4 "rotating without stretching" becomes literally spinning a point around the Bloch sphere.

2.5Eigenvalues & Eigenvectors

A ket |v⟩ is an eigenvector of operator A with eigenvalue λ if applying A simply rescales |v⟩ without rotating it:

A|v⟩ = λ|v⟩λ is in general a complex number; for Hermitian A it is always real (next lecture leans on this fact heavily).

For a 2×2 matrix, the eigenvalues solve the characteristic equation det(A − λI) = 0, a quadratic in λ with (for a 2-level system) exactly two roots. Each eigenvalue then has at least one eigenvector, found by solving (A − λI)|v⟩ = 0. These eigenvector/eigenvalue pairs are the backbone of next lecture's measurement theory — the eigenvalues turn out to be the only numbers a measurement can ever return.

✦ Worked Example — eigenvalues and eigenvectors of a Hermitian matrix

Find the eigenvalues and (normalized) eigenvectors of the Pauli-X matrix, A = [[0,1],[1,0]].

  1. Characteristic equation: det(A − λI) = det[[−λ, 1],[1, −λ]] = λ² − 1 = 0, so λ = +1 or λ = −1.
  2. Eigenvector for λ = +1: solve (A − I)|v⟩ = 0, i.e. [[−1, 1],[1, −1]][v₁, v₂]ᵀ = 0, which forces v₁ = v₂. Normalizing gives |v₊⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2.
  3. Eigenvector for λ = −1: solve (A + I)|v⟩ = 0, forcing v₁ = −v₂. Normalizing gives |v₋⟩ = (|0⟩ − |1⟩)/√2.
  4. Check: X|v₊⟩ should equal +|v₊⟩. X(|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 = (|1⟩+|0⟩)/√2 = |v₊⟩ ✓. Similarly X(|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 = (|1⟩−|0⟩)/√2 = −|v₋⟩... check sign: −(|0⟩−|1⟩)/√2 = (−|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 = (|1⟩−|0⟩)/√2 ✓.
  5. Note: both eigenvalues are real (+1, −1), as guaranteed for a Hermitian matrix, and the two eigenvectors are orthogonal (⟨v₊|v₋⟩ = 0) — also guaranteed. These two states will reappear in Week 4 as the east and west points on the Bloch sphere's equator.

🛠️ Hands-On: Hermiticity and unitarity, numerically

Build a Pauli matrix in QuTiP and check both properties directly instead of trusting algebra alone.

# operators.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
from qutip import sigmax, qeye

X = sigmax()                                  # QuTiP's built-in Pauli-X operator

# Hermiticity: does X equal its own conjugate transpose?
print("Hermitian?", X == X.dag())             # True

# Unitarity: does X-dagger times X equal the identity?
I2 = qeye(2)
print("Unitary?", (X.dag() * X) == I2)        # True

# Eigenvalues and eigenvectors, read straight off the operator
eigvals, eigvecs = X.eigenstates()
print("eigenvalues:", eigvals)                # [-1.  1.] -- matches the worked example
print("eigenvector for +1:", eigvecs[1])      # (|0> + |1>)/sqrt(2), up to a sign/phase convention

Repeat with sigmay() and sigmaz() — both come back Hermitian and unitary too, and their eigenvalues are always ±1. Then try a matrix that is Hermitian but not unitary, such as Qobj([[1,0],[0,0]]) (a projector) — confirm it fails the unitarity check, since a projector is exactly the kind of Hermitian operator that is not also a rotation.

🔗 Where this is heading

Hermitian and unitary are the two load-bearing walls of everything that follows. Next lecture turns Hermitian operators into full measurement theory: their eigenvalues become the only possible readings of an experiment, and their eigenvectors become the states you collapse to. Week 5 turns unitary operators into dynamics: the Schrödinger equation generates a continuous family of unitary operators U(t) that evolve a qubit smoothly in time. And in Week 6, once we have more than one qubit, the same two definitions — unchanged — are exactly what makes multi-qubit gates and multi-qubit observables well-defined.

Q1. If |ψ⟩ = [α, β]ᵀ, then the bra ⟨ψ| is…
A. [α*, β*], the conjugate transpose of |ψ⟩
B. [α, β], the same column vector written sideways
C. [β, α], the entries swapped
D. a 2×2 matrix built from α and β
Show answer

A. The bra is formed by transposing the ket into a row vector and complex-conjugating every entry — both steps are required, not just the transpose.

Q2. An operator A is called Hermitian when…
A. A is a rotation matrix
B. A equals its own conjugate transpose, A = A†
C. all of A's entries are real numbers
D. A has determinant 1
Show answer

B. A = A† is the definition. (Option C is the common mistake flagged in §2.3 — Hermitian matrices can have complex off-diagonal entries, as long as mirror-image entries are conjugates of each other.)

Q3. Why must every quantum gate U satisfy U†U = I?
A. So that U has only real eigenvalues
B. So that U can represent a measurement
C. So that applying U preserves normalization — total probability stays 1
D. It's a historical convention with no physical reason
Show answer

C. U†U = I is exactly the condition that keeps ⟨ψ'|ψ'⟩ = ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ after applying U — a gate that didn't satisfy this would turn a valid state into an invalid (non-normalized) one.

Q4. |v⟩ is an eigenvector of operator A with eigenvalue λ means…
A. A|v⟩ is orthogonal to |v⟩
B. |v⟩ is normalized to length λ
C. A|v⟩ = |v⟩ + λ, a vector plus a number
D. A|v⟩ = λ|v⟩ — applying A only rescales |v⟩, it doesn't rotate it
Show answer

D. That's the defining equation. Geometrically, an eigenvector is a direction that A leaves unrotated — it only stretches or shrinks (by factor λ), possibly flipping sign.

Q5. Which pair of matrices below are both Hermitian and unitary, for the reason given in §2.4?
A. The Pauli matrices X, Y, Z — because X† = X and X†X = I simultaneously hold for them
B. Any two matrices with determinant 1
C. Any Hermitian matrix is automatically unitary, and vice versa, in general
D. Only the identity matrix has this property
Show answer

A. The Pauli matrices are a special 2-level case where both properties hold at once. In general the two properties are independent — most Hermitian matrices are not unitary (e.g. a projector), and most unitary matrices are not Hermitian (e.g. a rotation by an arbitrary angle).
