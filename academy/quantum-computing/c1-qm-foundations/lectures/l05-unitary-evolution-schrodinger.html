Unitary Evolution & the Schrödinger Equation

Gates were "whatever unitary matrix you feel like applying" up to now. This lecture explains where unitary evolution actually comes from — and it's always the same equation.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why the time evolution of a closed quantum system must always be unitary.
  • Write the Schrödinger equation iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩ and identify the Hamiltonian's role.
  • Explain why H must be Hermitian, and define the time-evolution operator U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ).
  • Work a 2-level Hamiltonian example and show it rotates the Bloch vector (qubit precession).
  • Evolve a qubit state under a simple Hamiltonian in QuTiP with sesolve.

5.1Time Evolution Must Be Unitary

A qubit left alone — no measurement, no interaction with the outside world (a closed system) — doesn't sit still. Its state changes smoothly with time: |ψ(t)⟩. But Lecture 2's §2.4 argument applies at every instant: whatever maps |ψ(0)⟩ to |ψ(t)⟩ had better preserve normalization, or probabilities stop summing to 1. So time evolution of a closed system is always implemented by some unitary operator U(t):

|ψ(t)⟩ = U(t)|ψ(0)⟩      with   U(t)†U(t) = I   for every tThis is the same unitarity condition from Lecture 2, now required to hold continuously as t varies, not just for a single discrete gate.

Every quantum gate you've met so far (X, Z, the Pauli set) is really a snapshot: U(t) evaluated at one particular t, for one particular physical process. The question this lecture answers is: what actually generates this family of unitaries as t sweeps forward?

5.2The Schrödinger Equation

The answer is the single most famous equation in quantum mechanics — the Schrödinger equation:

iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩ℏ is the reduced Planck constant (a fixed number); H is the Hamiltonian operator.

The Hamiltonian H is the operator representing total energy, and it plays a double role: it's both an observable (Lecture 3 — measuring H returns one of its eigenvalues, an allowed energy level) and the generator of time evolution — the single piece of physics that decides how a given system's state changes moment to moment. Different physical setups (a qubit in a magnetic field, two coupled qubits, an atom) just mean different H; the equation itself never changes.

5.3The Time-Evolution Operator U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ)

For a Hamiltonian that doesn't itself change with time, the Schrödinger equation has a closed-form solution:

U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ)      |ψ(t)⟩ = exp(−iHt/ℏ)|ψ(0)⟩The matrix exponential of an operator is defined by the same power series as the ordinary exponential, applied to matrices.

This is where §5.1's unitarity requirement and §5.2's Hamiltonian meet: U(t) is automatically unitary, precisely because H is Hermitian. Writing U(t)† = exp(+iH†t/ℏ) and using H† = H gives U(t)†U(t) = exp(+iHt/ℏ)exp(−iHt/ℏ) = I. In other words, Hermitian generators always produce unitary evolution — this single fact is why the postulate "observables are Hermitian" (Lecture 2) and the postulate "evolution is unitary" (§5.1) are not two independent assumptions, but two faces of the same requirement on H.

AnalogyThink of H as a steering wheel fixed at one angle, and U(t) as "how far you've driven after turning the wheel and holding it for time t." A Hermitian H guarantees the car traces a circle (or a more general rotation) rather than drifting off and crashing (losing normalization). Different H's correspond to different fixed steering angles — different axes of rotation on the Bloch sphere.

5.4A 2-Level Example: Qubit Precession

Take the simplest nontrivial Hamiltonian for a qubit, proportional to Pauli-Z:

H = (ℏω/2) Zω is an angular frequency set by the physical system (e.g. an energy splitting between |0⟩ and |1⟩ divided by ℏ).

Because Z² = I, the exponential series for U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ) = exp(−i(ωt/2)Z) collapses to a clean closed form, derived in full in the worked example below. The result: U(t) leaves the Bloch sphere's polar angle θ completely fixed and rotates the azimuthal angle φ steadily, φ(t) = φ(0) + ωt — the state vector precesses around the z-axis at constant angular rate ω, like a spinning top. This is the single most common building block in all of quantum-computing hardware: a qubit sitting under a constant field just precesses, and applying a pulse for a controlled duration is exactly how single-qubit gates get implemented physically.

✦ Worked Example — U(t) for H = (ℏω/2)Z, and the Bloch-vector rotation it produces

Compute U(t) for H = (ℏω/2)Z, then show how it acts on a general Bloch-sphere state |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩.

  1. Expand the exponential. Since Z² = I, the matrix-exponential series splits into even and odd powers exactly like the scalar identity e−ia = cos(a) − i·sin(a): U(t) = exp(−i(ωt/2)Z) = cos(ωt/2)·I − i·sin(ωt/2)·Z.
  2. Write U(t) as a matrix. Since Z = diag(1, −1): U(t) = diag(cos(ωt/2) − i·sin(ωt/2), cos(ωt/2) + i·sin(ωt/2)) = diag(e−iωt/2, eiωt/2).
  3. Apply it to |ψ⟩: U(t)|ψ⟩ = e−iωt/2cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiωt/2eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩.
  4. Factor out the global phase e−iωt/2 (it's unobservable, per Lecture 1 §1.4 and Lecture 4 §4.2): U(t)|ψ⟩ ≡ cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + ei(φ+ωt)sin(θ/2)|1⟩ — the same θ, with φ replaced by φ(t) = φ + ωt.
  5. Interpretation: the Bloch point stays at the same latitude (θ fixed) and sweeps around the z-axis at constant rate ω — precession, exactly as claimed in §5.4.

🛠️ Hands-On: evolving a qubit with QuTiP's sesolve

sesolve ("Schrödinger equation solve") numerically integrates iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩ for you — no need to hand-derive U(t) for more complicated Hamiltonians.

# precession.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
from qutip import basis, sigmax, sigmay, sigmaz, sesolve

omega = 2 * np.pi                             # one full precession per unit of time
H = 0.5 * omega * sigmaz()                    # H = (hbar*omega/2) Z, with hbar set to 1

psi0 = (basis(2, 0) + basis(2, 1)).unit()     # start on the equator, theta = pi/2, phi = 0
times = np.linspace(0, 1, 200)

result = sesolve(H, psi0, times, e_ops=[sigmax(), sigmay(), sigmaz()])
# result.expect[0], [1], [2] trace <X>, <Y>, <Z> vs time --
# <Z> stays ~0 (theta fixed at the equator) while <X>, <Y> trace a circle: precession.

Feed result.expect into a 3D scatter or QuTiP's Bloch helper from Lecture 4 to literally watch the point spin around the z-axis. Try psi0 = basis(2, 0) (the north pole) instead — confirm <X>, <Y>, <Z> barely move, since a pole under Z-precession just spins in place.

🔗 Where this is heading

Every gate in a real quantum circuit is implemented exactly this way: engineers choose a Hamiltonian (a magnetic field, a microwave pulse, a laser) and let the system evolve under it for a controlled duration, producing a specific U(t). Once Week 6 introduces multiple qubits, the Hamiltonian can couple them together — and a coupling term in H is precisely what makes two separately-evolving qubits become entangled (Week 7), since it's no longer possible to factor U(t) into independent single-qubit rotations. Unitary evolution, run forward on an entangling Hamiltonian, is the single mechanism behind every two-qubit gate you'll meet later in this specialization.

Q1. Why must the time evolution of a closed quantum system be unitary?
A. Because Hamiltonians are always diagonal matrices
B. It's a notational convention with no physical necessity
C. To guarantee energy eigenvalues come out real
D. To preserve normalization — total measurement probability must always equal 1
Show answer

D. If U(t) weren't unitary, a perfectly valid starting state could evolve into one with total probability ≠ 1 — a physical impossibility.

Q2. In the Schrödinger equation iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩, the operator that generates time evolution is…
A. the Hamiltonian H
B. the time-evolution operator U(t)
C. the Pauli-Z operator specifically
D. the identity operator I
Show answer

A. H is the generator; U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ) is built from H by exponentiation, but H is the fundamental object the equation is written in terms of.

Q3. U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ) is guaranteed to be unitary because…
A. H is itself unitary
B. H is Hermitian, and exponentiating i times a Hermitian operator always yields a unitary operator
C. t is a real number
D. ℏ is a universal constant
Show answer

B. U(t)†U(t) = exp(+iH†t/ℏ)exp(−iHt/ℏ) = I exactly because H† = H — Hermiticity of the generator is what forces unitarity of the evolution.

Q4. Under H = (ℏω/2)Z, a qubit's Bloch vector…
A. jumps immediately to the opposite pole
B. changes its polar angle θ over time
C. precesses around the z-axis at constant rate ω, with θ fixed
D. shrinks toward the center of the sphere
Show answer

C. The worked example shows U(t) replaces φ with φ + ωt while leaving θ completely unchanged — pure azimuthal rotation, i.e. precession.

Q5. Which of the following is not generally preserved by unitary time evolution (and is, in fact, the whole point of applying a nontrivial gate)?
A. the normalization ⟨ψ|ψ⟩ of the state
B. total measurement probability
C. the Hermiticity of any fixed observable you later measure
D. the specific amplitudes α and β themselves
Show answer

D. Unitary evolution is designed to leave normalization and probability untouched, while actively changing the amplitudes α, β (equivalently, moving the Bloch point) — that's precisely what makes it useful as a gate.

  • The Schrödinger equation iℏ d|ψ⟩/dt = H|ψ⟩ with Hermitian Hamiltonian H generates that evolution.
  • The time-evolution operator U(t) = exp(−iHt/ℏ) is automatically unitary because H is Hermitian.
