Week 4 · Lecture 4

The Bloch Sphere

Every single-qubit pure state — infinitely many complex amplitude pairs — collapses onto exactly one point on a plain, ordinary sphere. This is the picture everything else in the course secretly points back to.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write a general single-qubit pure state as |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩.
  • Identify the polar angle θ and azimuthal angle φ and locate them on the sphere.
  • Explain why global phase leaves the Bloch point fixed while relative phase (φ) moves it.
  • Identify the poles (|0⟩, |1⟩) and the equator (equal-superposition states) on the sphere.
  • Convert given amplitudes (α, β) into Bloch coordinates (θ, φ) by hand.
  • Plot a qubit state on a Bloch sphere using QuTiP — the Week 4 project checkpoint.

4.1From Two Amplitudes to Two Angles

A general qubit state |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ has two complex amplitudes — four real numbers — constrained by one equation, normalization. Lecture 1's global-phase discussion removes one more real degree of freedom (an overall phase is never observable). What's left is exactly two real numbers, and it turns out any normalized qubit state can always be written as:

|ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩θ ∈ [0, π] is the polar angle; φ ∈ [0, 2π) is the azimuthal angle. This parametrization is automatically normalized for any θ, φ.

These are precisely the two angles that locate a point on the surface of an ordinary unit sphere — the Bloch sphere. θ measures how far "south" from the north pole you are; φ measures how far you've rotated around the vertical axis. Every single-qubit pure state is one point on this sphere, and every point on this sphere is some single-qubit pure state.

4.2Global Phase Doesn't Move the Point — Relative Phase Does

Lecture 1, §1.4 distinguished an unobservable global phase eiφ₀|ψ⟩ from a physically real relative phase between α and β. The Bloch sphere makes that distinction geometric: multiplying the whole state by eiφ₀ changes nothing in the parametrization above (cos(θ/2) and sin(θ/2) stay real and non-negative by convention, and φ is defined relative to the |0⟩ amplitude) — the point on the sphere doesn't move at all. But changing φ — the phase between the two amplitudes — spins the point around the vertical (z) axis. This is why φ, and only φ, survives as a meaningful coordinate: it's the relative phase, dressed up as an angle you can literally point to.

Common mistakeDon't say "the state has a phase of eiφ₀" as if that alone told you anything physical — a lone global phase is invisible on the Bloch sphere and invisible to every measurement. Only ask "what is the phase of β relative to α?" That's the φ that actually appears in the formula above and the only phase that ever moves the point.

4.3Reading the Sphere: Poles and Equator

Two landmarks orient everything else on the sphere:

θ = 0 : |ψ⟩ = |0⟩  (north pole)      θ = π : |ψ⟩ = |1⟩  (south pole)At the poles, sin(θ/2) or cos(θ/2) vanishes — φ becomes undefined/irrelevant, exactly as it should: the poles are single points, with no "around" left to rotate through.

The equator, θ = π/2, is the set of equal-superposition states cos(π/4)|0⟩ + eiφsin(π/4)|1⟩ = (|0⟩ + eiφ|1⟩)/√2 — every one of them gives 50/50 measurement probabilities in the Z basis, and they differ from each other only by relative phase φ. The two eigenvectors of X found in Lecture 2's worked example, (|0⟩±|1⟩)/√2, sit at φ = 0 and φ = π on this equator — directly across the sphere from one another, which is exactly why measuring Z on either gives 50/50 odds while measuring X on either gives a certain outcome.

4.4Converting Amplitudes to Bloch Coordinates

Given a normalized state α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, first use the global-phase freedom to make α real and non-negative (always possible — multiply the whole state by e−i·arg(α), which doesn't move the Bloch point). Then read off:

θ = 2·arccos(|α|)      φ = arg(β) − arg(α)Equivalently θ = 2·arcsin(|β|). φ is the phase of β relative to the (now real) α — exactly the relative phase from §4.2.
✦ Worked Example — amplitudes to Bloch coordinates

A qubit is in state |ψ⟩ = (√3/2)|0⟩ + (1/2)eiπ/3|1⟩. Find its Bloch coordinates (θ, φ).

  1. Check normalization: |√3/2|² + |1/2|² = 3/4 + 1/4 = 1 ✓.
  2. α is already real and non-negative (√3/2 > 0), so no global-phase adjustment is needed.
  3. Polar angle: θ = 2·arccos(|α|) = 2·arccos(√3/2) = 2·(π/6) = π/3 (60°).
  4. Azimuthal angle: α's phase is 0, β's phase is π/3, so φ = arg(β) − arg(α) = π/3 − 0 = π/3 (60°).
  5. Sanity check: cos(θ/2) = cos(π/6) = √3/2 ✓ and sin(θ/2) = sin(π/6) = 1/2 ✓ — both amplitude magnitudes match exactly.

🛠️ Hands-On: plotting a qubit on the Bloch sphere

QuTiP ships a Bloch helper that draws the sphere and plots a state's vector directly — this is also this week's project checkpoint: build a single-qubit state and render its Bloch plot for your report.

# bloch_plot.py — Lecture 4 / Project checkpoint
import numpy as np
from qutip import basis, Bloch

theta, phi = np.pi/3, np.pi/3                 # from the worked example above
psi = (np.cos(theta/2) * basis(2, 0)
       + np.exp(1j*phi) * np.sin(theta/2) * basis(2, 1))

b = Bloch()
b.add_states(psi)
b.show()                                      # opens an interactive 3D plot
# b.save('qubit_bloch.png')                  # or save a PNG for your project report

Plot all three Pauli eigenstates together — basis(2,0), basis(2,1), and (basis(2,0)+basis(2,1)).unit() — and confirm visually that the first two sit at the poles and the third sits on the equator, exactly as §4.3 described. For the project, plot two or three single-qubit states of your choosing and include the image in your report.

🔗 Where this is heading

The Bloch sphere isn't just a pretty picture — it's the geometric engine behind every single-qubit gate. Next lecture shows that unitary time evolution rotates the Bloch vector rigidly around some axis, at a rate set by the Hamiltonian — so "applying a gate" and "spinning a point on a sphere" become literally the same operation. Multi-qubit states (Week 6 onward) no longer fit on a single sphere — that visualization is special to one qubit — but every reduced single-qubit piece of a larger system still has its own Bloch point, and tracking how that point shrinks toward the sphere's center is exactly how Week 8's mixed states will be visualized.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Multiplying a qubit state by a global phase eiφ₀…
A. rotates the Bloch point around the z-axis
B. changes θ but not φ
C. has no effect on the Bloch-sphere point at all
D. moves the point to the opposite pole
Show answer

C. Global phase is unobservable; the Bloch point is built only from θ and the relative phase φ, neither of which a global phase touches.

Q2. In |ψ⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + eiφsin(θ/2)|1⟩, θ and φ are called the…
A. azimuthal angle and polar angle, respectively
B. radial distance and tangential angle, respectively
C. polar angle and radial distance, respectively
D. polar angle and azimuthal angle, respectively
Show answer

D. θ (measured down from the north pole) is the polar angle; φ (measured around the equator) is the azimuthal angle — standard spherical-coordinate naming.

Q3. States on the Bloch sphere's equator (θ = π/2)…
A. are equal superpositions of |0⟩ and |1⟩, differing from each other only by relative phase φ
B. are all identical to |0⟩
C. cannot be normalized
D. cannot be prepared in a lab
Show answer

A. Every equatorial state is (|0⟩ + eiφ|1⟩)/√2 for some φ — all give 50/50 odds measuring Z, but they are physically distinct states, distinguished only by their relative phase.

Q4. The north pole (θ = 0) of the Bloch sphere corresponds to…
A. an equal superposition state
B. the state |0⟩
C. the state |1⟩
D. an undefined state
Show answer

B. At θ = 0, cos(θ/2) = 1 and sin(θ/2) = 0, leaving |ψ⟩ = |0⟩ exactly — the north pole is |0⟩, the south pole (θ = π) is |1⟩.

Q5. A state has |α| = √3/2 (α real, positive). What is its polar angle θ?
A. 30°
B. 90°
C. 60°
D. 120°
Show answer

C. θ = 2·arccos(√3/2) = 2·30° = 60° — exactly the worked example above.
