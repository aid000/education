Multi-Qubit Systems & the Tensor Product

One qubit lives on a line of two amplitudes. Put several qubits together and the space of possibilities explodes — this is where quantum computers start to outgrow anything classical.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Combine two single-qubit states into one joint state using the tensor product ⊗.
  • Write the 4-dimensional two-qubit basis {|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩} and a general two-qubit superposition.
  • Explain why an n-qubit system needs 2ⁿ complex amplitudes, and why that is both a promise and a bottleneck.
  • Distinguish a product state (factorizable into two single-qubit states) from a general multi-qubit state.
  • Build a tensor-product state in QuTiP with tensor().

6.1Combining Two Qubits

Everything through Week 5 described one qubit at a time. Real quantum computers need many qubits working together, so we need a rule for combining their states into a single joint description. That rule is the tensor product, written ⊗. Given two single-qubit states |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩ and |φ⟩ = γ|0⟩ + δ|1⟩, the joint state is:

|ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ = αγ|00⟩ + αδ|01⟩ + βγ|10⟩ + βδ|11⟩Multiply every amplitude of |ψ⟩ by every amplitude of |φ⟩; the basis label just concatenates which qubit got which outcome.

Mechanically, you distribute just like multiplying two binomials — except the "variables" are kets. Each of the four resulting amplitudes is a product of one amplitude from each qubit, and the resulting basis ket records both qubits' labels in order: first qubit's digit, then second qubit's digit.

6.2The 4-Dimensional Two-Qubit Space

Two classical bits have four possible joint values: 00, 01, 10, 11. Two qubits live in the quantum superposition of all four at once — a 4-dimensional complex vector space with basis:

|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩Built by tensoring the single-qubit basis {|0⟩,|1⟩} with itself: |0⟩⊗|0⟩, |0⟩⊗|1⟩, |1⟩⊗|0⟩, |1⟩⊗|1⟩.

A fully general two-qubit state is a superposition of all four, with four independent complex amplitudes:

|Ψ⟩ = c₀₀|00⟩ + c₀₁|01⟩ + c₁₀|10⟩ + c₁₁|11⟩    |c₀₀|² + |c₀₁|² + |c₁₀|² + |c₁₁|² = 1Normalization generalizes exactly as before — the probabilities of all four outcomes must sum to 1.

Measuring both qubits returns one of the four two-bit outcomes, with probability given by the squared magnitude of the matching amplitude — the Born rule, unchanged, just with more basis states to apply it to.

AnalogyThink of one qubit's state space as a line of two "slots," and the tensor product as laying a second qubit's two slots out in every combination with the first — 2 × 2 = 4 slots total. Tensoring isn't addition; it's a combinatorial product, and that difference is exactly why the dimension explodes instead of just doubling.

6.3Exponential Scaling: 2ⁿ Amplitudes

Three qubits tensor together into an 8-dimensional space {|000⟩, …, |111⟩}. Four qubits give 16 dimensions. In general, n qubits need 2ⁿ complex amplitudes to fully describe a general state — exactly the point flagged in Lecture 1's extension box and this course's Week-6 discussion prompt. Ten qubits already need 1,024 amplitudes; fifty qubits need more numbers than there are atoms you could store them in classically.

n qubits  ⟹  dim(H) = 2ⁿEach additional qubit doubles the dimension of the joint Hilbert space — multiplicative, not additive, growth.

This cuts both ways. It is the reason a quantum computer can represent an astronomically large state with a small number of physical qubits — the whole promise of the field. It is also the reason you cannot simulate more than ~40-50 qubits on classical hardware: the amplitude vector itself becomes too large to store. Every lecture from here on is building toward algorithms (Course 3) that try to extract a useful answer from this huge space without ever writing out all 2ⁿ amplitudes explicitly.

6.4Product States vs. General States

Not every vector in the 4-dimensional space comes from tensoring two single-qubit states together. A product state (also called separable) is one that can be written as |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ for some single-qubit |ψ⟩ and |φ⟩. Every state built the way §6.1 describes is automatically a product state by construction.

But the general two-qubit state |Ψ⟩ = c₀₀|00⟩ + c₀₁|01⟩ + c₁₀|10⟩ + c₁₁|11⟩ has four independent amplitudes, while a product state's four amplitudes are built from only four underlying single-qubit numbers (α, β, γ, δ) that are not independent of each other — they must satisfy c₀₀c₁₁ = c₀₁c₁₀ (the cross-multiplication condition that comes straight out of §6.1's formula). Most points in the 4-dimensional space do not satisfy that condition.

Common mistakeDon't assume every valid, normalized two-qubit state can be "un-tensored" back into two separate qubit states. A state that fails the factoring condition above cannot be described as "qubit A is in this state and qubit B is in that state" — there is no such description for either qubit alone. That failure to factor is exactly the signature of entanglement, which Lecture 7 defines precisely.
✦ Worked Example — tensoring two qubits by hand

Tensor |ψ⟩ = (|0⟩ + |1⟩)/√2 with |φ⟩ = |1⟩ to get the joint two-qubit state.

  1. Write out the amplitudes: for |ψ⟩, α = β = 1/√2. For |φ⟩, γ = 0, δ = 1 (it's just |1⟩, no superposition).
  2. Apply the tensor-product rule: |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ = αγ|00⟩ + αδ|01⟩ + βγ|10⟩ + βδ|11⟩.
  3. Substitute: αγ = (1/√2)(0) = 0,  αδ = (1/√2)(1) = 1/√2,  βγ = (1/√2)(0) = 0,  βδ = (1/√2)(1) = 1/√2.
  4. Result: |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ = (|01⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 — only the two basis kets with the second qubit fixed at 1 survive, exactly as expected since |φ⟩ = |1⟩ has no superposition to spread.
  5. Check normalization: |1/√2|² + |1/√2|² = 1/2 + 1/2 = 1 ✓. And since it was built directly as |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩, it's a product state by construction — measuring the second qubit always returns 1, while the first qubit is still a 50/50 coin.

🛠️ Hands-On: tensor product in QuTiP

QuTiP's tensor() function builds the joint state directly from single-qubit kets — no manual index bookkeeping required.

# two_qubit_tensor.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from qutip import basis, tensor

ket0, ket1 = basis(2, 0), basis(2, 1)

psi = (ket0 + ket1).unit()                    # (|0> + |1>)/sqrt(2)
phi = ket1                                    # |1>, no superposition

joint = tensor(psi, phi)                       # |psi> (x) |phi>, a 4-dim ket
print(joint)                                   # amplitudes in |00>,|01>,|10>,|11> order
print("norm =", joint.norm())                  # 1.0

# verify it matches the hand-computed result (|01> + |11>)/sqrt(2)
expected = (tensor(ket0, ket1) + tensor(ket1, ket1)).unit()
print("matches hand calc:", (joint - expected).norm() < 1e-9)

Try tensor(basis(2,0), basis(2,0), basis(2,0)) for a three-qubit basis state, and check the dimension of the result with .shape — it should be (8, 1), matching 2³ = 8.

🔗 Where this is heading

You've now seen that the two-qubit space is 4-dimensional and that most states in it don't factor into two separate single-qubit descriptions. Lecture 7 makes that observation precise: a state is entangled exactly when it fails to factor, and the most extreme examples — the four Bell states — carry correlations between qubits that have no classical explanation at all. Lecture 8 then shows how to describe one qubit's own statistics when it's entangled with another, using the density-matrix language.

Q1. The two-qubit basis {|00⟩, |01⟩, |10⟩, |11⟩} is built by…
A. adding the two single-qubit bases together
B. tensoring the single-qubit basis {|0⟩,|1⟩} with itself
C. averaging the two qubits' amplitudes
D. picking only the states that are entangled
Show answer

B. ⊗ applied to {|0⟩,|1⟩} with itself gives all four combinations |0⟩⊗|0⟩, |0⟩⊗|1⟩, |1⟩⊗|0⟩, |1⟩⊗|1⟩ — relabeled |00⟩,|01⟩,|10⟩,|11⟩.

Q2. A general n-qubit state requires how many complex amplitudes to describe fully?
A. n
B. 2n
C. n²
D. 2ⁿ
Show answer

D. Each additional qubit doubles the dimension of the joint space — the growth is exponential, not linear or polynomial in n.

Q3. Given |ψ⟩ = |0⟩ and |φ⟩ = (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, the tensor product |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ equals…
A. (|00⟩ + |01⟩)/√2
B. (|00⟩ + |10⟩)/√2
C. |00⟩
D. (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2
Show answer

A. α=1,β=0 for |ψ⟩; γ=δ=1/√2 for |φ⟩. αγ|00⟩+αδ|01⟩+βγ|10⟩+βδ|11⟩ = (1/√2)|00⟩+(1/√2)|01⟩+0+0.

Q4. A two-qubit state is called a "product state" when…
A. both qubits are measured in the same basis
B. its four amplitudes are all equal
C. it can be written as |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ for some single-qubit |ψ⟩,|φ⟩
D. it has zero amplitude on |11⟩
Show answer

C. Separability/product-state status is exactly the ability to factor the joint state into independent single-qubit pieces — the defining property this lecture sets up for Lecture 7's entanglement test.

Q5. Why is the exponential growth of the state space both a "promise" and a "bottleneck" for quantum computing?
A. It makes qubits physically larger as n grows
B. It lets a quantum computer represent a huge amount of information with few qubits, but also makes classical simulation of large n intractable

B. The same 2ⁿ scaling that gives quantum computers their potential power is exactly what makes them hard to simulate classically beyond ~40-50 qubits — the promise and the bottleneck are two faces of the same fact.
