Week 8 · Lecture 8

Density Matrices & Mixed States

Ket notation can't describe a qubit that's entangled with something else, or one you're simply uncertain about. The density matrix can — and it resolves last lecture's "not pure" mystery.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write the density operator ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ| for a pure state and contrast it with a mixed-state ρ.
  • State and check the three defining properties of a valid density matrix: Hermitian, trace 1, positive semi-definite.
  • Compute expectation values as ⟨A⟩ = Tr(ρA).
  • Use the partial trace to find a subsystem's reduced density matrix from an entangled pair.
  • Show that the reduced state of one qubit from a Bell pair is the maximally mixed state I/2.

8.1From Kets to the Density Operator

Every pure state |ψ⟩ has an equivalent description as an operator rather than a vector — the density operator:

ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|The outer product of a ket with its own bra. For |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩+β|1⟩, ρ is the 2×2 matrix with entries ρ_jk = c_j c_k*.

For |ψ⟩ = α|0⟩ + β|1⟩, writing it out gives ρ = [[|α|², αβ*], [α*β, |β|²]]. The diagonal entries are exactly the Born-rule probabilities P(0)=|α|², P(1)=|β|²; the off-diagonal entries encode the superposition's relative phase (its "coherence") between |0⟩ and |1⟩. Nothing new is being claimed yet — ρ is just a repackaging of the same information already in |ψ⟩.

8.2Pure States vs. Mixed States

The density-matrix language lets us describe something kets alone cannot: classical ignorance. Suppose a qubit was prepared as |0⟩ with probability 1/2 and as |1⟩ with probability 1/2, by a process you didn't observe (e.g. a coin flip decided which). This is not the superposition (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 — it's a classical statistical mixture of two definite, known kets, and it's written:

ρ_mixed = ½|0⟩⟨0| + ½|1⟩⟨1| = [[½, 0], [0, ½]]A mixed state: a probability-weighted sum of pure-state density operators, with zero off-diagonal coherence.

Compare this to the pure superposition's density matrix [[½, ½], [½, ½]] from the Lecture 1 state (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 — same diagonal (50/50 probabilities either way) but very different off-diagonals. The off-diagonal terms are exactly what distinguishes "truly in superposition" from "classically uncertain about which definite state it's in."

AnalogyA pure superposition is like a spinning coin genuinely balanced on its edge — both outcomes exist at once, able to interfere. A mixed state is like a coin that has already landed under a cup you haven't lifted — it's definitely heads or definitely tails, you just don't know which. The math (off-diagonals present vs. absent) tells the two apart precisely.

8.3The Three Properties of a Valid Density Matrix

Any physically valid density matrix ρ — pure or mixed — must satisfy three properties:

1. Hermitian: ρ† = ρ    2. Trace one: Tr(ρ) = 1    3. Positive semi-definite: ⟨φ|ρ|φ⟩ ≥ 0 ∀|φ⟩Hermiticity makes diagonal entries real; trace-1 keeps total probability at 1; positivity keeps every probability non-negative.

Hermiticity (property 1) guarantees the diagonal entries — the probabilities — are real numbers, as they must be. Trace one (property 2) is just normalization: all outcome probabilities sum to 1, since the diagonal entries of ρ are exactly those probabilities. Positive semi-definiteness (property 3) guarantees you never predict a negative probability for any measurement outcome, no matter what basis you measure in. A matrix failing any of these three tests cannot represent a physical quantum state.

8.4Expectation Values as Tr(ρA)

For an observable represented by a Hermitian operator A, the expectation value (mean result of repeated measurement) in state ρ is given by a clean operator formula:

⟨A⟩ = Tr(ρA)Trace = sum of diagonal entries. This single formula works for both pure states (ρ=|ψ⟩⟨ψ|, recovering ⟨A⟩=⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩) and mixed states, where no single ket exists to write ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ with.

This is the density-matrix generalization of the Born rule: probabilities themselves are just a special case, Tr(ρ P), where P is the projector onto the outcome of interest. The power of Tr(ρA) is that it works identically whether ρ is pure or mixed — you never need to ask "is there even a ket for this system?" before computing a prediction.

8.5The Partial Trace: Finding a Subsystem's State

Lecture 7 ended with a QuTiP mystery: a pure, entangled two-qubit state whose single-qubit piece wasn't pure. The tool that resolves this is the partial trace — it takes a joint density matrix ρ_AB and produces the reduced density matrix of just one subsystem, "averaging away" everything about the other:

ρ_A = Tr_B(ρ_AB)Trace out ("sum over") qubit B's degrees of freedom, keeping only what's left to describe qubit A alone.

If the joint state is a product state |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩, the partial trace simply returns ρ_A = |ψ⟩⟨ψ| — qubit A's own pure state, exactly as expected, since nothing about B was ever tangled up with A. But if the joint state is entangled, tracing out B leaves qubit A in a mixed state — even though the full two-qubit state was perfectly pure. This is the precise resolution of Lecture 7's hands-on result, worked out in full below.

✦ Worked Example — the reduced state of a Bell pair is maximally mixed

Starting from |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2, compute the reduced density matrix of qubit A by tracing out qubit B.

  1. Full density matrix: ρ = |Φ⁺⟩⟨Φ⁺| = ½(|00⟩+|11⟩)(⟨00|+⟨11|) = ½(|00⟩⟨00| + |00⟩⟨11| + |11⟩⟨00| + |11⟩⟨11|).
  2. Partial trace over B: for each term |ab⟩⟨cd|, tracing out B keeps only terms where B's index matches (b=d), contributing |a⟩⟨c| weighted by that match.
  3. |00⟩⟨00| and |11⟩⟨11| survive (B-index matches: 0=0 and 1=1), contributing ½|0⟩⟨0| and ½|1⟩⟨1|.
  4. |00⟩⟨11| and |11⟩⟨00| vanish under the partial trace (B-index mismatches: 0≠1), since Tr_B of a |0⟩⟨1|-type term on B is ⟨1|0⟩=0.
  5. Result: ρ_A = ½|0⟩⟨0| + ½|1⟩⟨1| = [[½,0],[0,½]] = I/2 — the maximally mixed state. Despite the full two-qubit state being perfectly pure, qubit A alone looks like a fair coin with absolutely no coherence left — all of the "purity" is hiding in the correlation with qubit B, not in qubit A by itself.

🛠️ Hands-On: verify ρ_A = I/2 in QuTiP

This directly completes Lecture 7's hands-on, using the same ptrace() call now that you know what to expect.

# reduced_density_matrix.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np
from qutip import basis, tensor, qeye

ket0, ket1 = basis(2, 0), basis(2, 1)
phi_plus = (tensor(ket0, ket0) + tensor(ket1, ket1)).unit()

rho_full = phi_plus * phi_plus.dag()
rho_A = rho_full.ptrace(0)                     # trace out qubit B (index 1)

print(rho_A)                                   # should be [[0.5, 0], [0, 0.5]]
print("Tr(rho_A) =", rho_A.tr())               # 1.0 -- still a valid density matrix

I_over_2 = qeye(2) / 2
print("matches I/2:", (rho_A - I_over_2).norm() < 1e-9)   # True

# purity: Tr(rho^2) = 1 for a pure state, < 1 for a mixed one -- a single concrete "how mixed is this?" number
print("purity Tr(rho_A^2) =", (rho_A * rho_A).tr())        # 0.5 -- the minimum possible, confirming rho_A is maximally mixed

# expectation value via Tr(rho A), e.g. the Pauli-Z observable
from qutip import sigmaz
print("<Z> on qubit A:", (rho_A * sigmaz()).tr())          # 0.0 -- totally unbiased

The purity Tr(ρ²) is the single number to reach for when you want "how mixed is this state" as a scalar: it equals 1 only for a pure state and drops to a minimum of 1/2 for a maximally mixed qubit — equivalently, it's the squared Bloch-sphere radius r² from Lecture 4, so purity < 1 means the state has pulled inside the Bloch sphere rather than sitting on its surface.

The maximally mixed reduced state I/2 you just derived is the cleanest possible signature of entanglement: a subsystem of a pure entangled state carries zero information on its own, even though the whole system carries exactly one qubit's worth of "pure" information. Q1. The density operator for a pure state |ψ⟩ is defined as…
A. ⟨ψ|ψ⟩
B. |ψ⟩ + ⟨ψ|
C. the normalization constant of |ψ⟩
D. ρ = |ψ⟩⟨ψ|
Show answer

D. The density operator is the outer product of a ket with its own bra — a matrix, not a scalar or a vector.

Q2. What distinguishes a mixed state's density matrix from a pure superposition's, when both have the same diagonal (same outcome probabilities)?
A. Mixed states have a larger trace
B. Mixed states have zero (or reduced) off-diagonal coherence terms
C. Mixed states are not Hermitian
D. There is no mathematical difference
Show answer

B. Both can share the same diagonal (probabilities), but a mixed state lacks the coherent off-diagonal terms that a true superposition carries.

Q3. Which of these is NOT one of the three required properties of a valid density matrix?
A. Hermitian (ρ†=ρ)
B. Trace equal to 1
C. All off-diagonal entries equal to zero
D. Positive semi-definite
Show answer

C. Off-diagonals are allowed to be nonzero (they encode coherence/superposition) — only Hermiticity, trace 1, and positive semi-definiteness are required.

Q4. The expectation value of observable A in state ρ is computed as…
A. Tr(ρA)
B. ρ + A
C. det(ρA)
D. ρ⁻¹A
Show answer

A. ⟨A⟩ = Tr(ρA) is the general operator formula, reducing to ⟨ψ|A|ψ⟩ when ρ is pure, but also valid when no single ket exists.

Q5. Tracing out one qubit of the Bell state |Φ⁺⟩ leaves the other qubit in the state…
A. |0⟩, a pure state
B. (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2, a pure superposition
C. undefined, since partial trace doesn't apply to pure states
D. I/2, the maximally mixed state
Show answer

D. Despite the full two-qubit state being pure, the reduced single-qubit state after tracing out its entangled partner is I/2 — maximally mixed, with zero coherence left.
