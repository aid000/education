Entanglement & Bell States

Some two-qubit states refuse to be described as "qubit A does this, qubit B does that." That failure to split apart is entanglement — and it is physically real.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the definition of entanglement: a state is entangled iff it cannot be written as a tensor product of two single-qubit states.
  • Write down the four Bell states and recognize them as maximally entangled.
  • Explain the correlations a Bell state produces under measurement, and why they defy classical intuition.
  • Describe, at a sketch level, how Bell's inequality rules out "hidden variable" explanations for these correlations.
  • Build a Bell state in QuTiP and verify its reduced single-qubit state is not pure.

7.1Defining Entanglement

Lecture 6 showed that most two-qubit states cannot be factored back into |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩. That failure to factor is not a minor mathematical inconvenience — it is the definition of one of the most important phenomena in quantum mechanics:

|Ψ⟩ is entangled  ⟺  |Ψ⟩ ≠ |ψ⟩ ⊗ |φ⟩ for any single-qubit |ψ⟩, |φ⟩A state is entangled precisely when no "qubit A is in this state, qubit B is in that state" description exists.

This is a statement about the whole state, not about either qubit individually. When a two-qubit state is entangled, there is no well-defined pure state you can assign to qubit A alone or qubit B alone — the two qubits' fates are tied together in a way that only the joint description captures. Lecture 8 makes this precise with the density-matrix language; for now, focus on recognizing entanglement and its physical consequences.

7.2The Four Bell States

The most extreme, maximally entangled two-qubit states are the four Bell states (also called EPR pairs, after Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen):

|Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2    |Φ⁻⟩ = (|00⟩ − |11⟩)/√2
|Ψ⁺⟩ = (|01⟩ + |10⟩)/√2    |Ψ⁻⟩ = (|01⟩ − |10⟩)/√2All four are normalized, orthogonal to each other, and form a complete alternative basis for the two-qubit space.

Take |Φ⁺⟩ as the running example. It says the two qubits are simultaneously "both 0" and "both 1" — with zero amplitude on the mixed outcomes |01⟩ and |10⟩. No single-qubit state assigned to either qubit alone can reproduce that structure; the correlation lives only in the joint description.

7.3Correlated Measurement Outcomes

Suppose Alice holds qubit A and Bob holds qubit B of the state |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2, and they are light-years apart. Each of them individually measures 0 with probability 1/2 and 1 with probability 1/2 — exactly like a fair coin. But if Alice tells Bob her result, something classical coins can never do shows up: Bob's result is always identical to Alice's. If Alice got 0, Bob is guaranteed 0; if Alice got 1, Bob is guaranteed 1 — every single time, no matter the distance between them.

This is not a signal traveling from Alice to Bob — nothing is sent, and no information can be transmitted faster than light this way (Alice can't choose what Bob sees). It is a pre-existing correlation baked into the joint state before either measurement happens. Classically, you could try to explain this with a "hidden instruction" shared in advance — e.g. two coins pre-agreed to always land the same way. §7.4 explains why that classical explanation fails.

7.4Bell's Inequality: Ruling Out Hidden Variables

Could the correlations in §7.3 just be due to some shared classical "hidden variable" agreed upon when the pair was created — like two coins pre-set to match? John Bell showed this hypothesis makes a falsifiable prediction. If measurement outcomes come from any local hidden-variable theory, the correlations measured along three different directions must satisfy an inequality (Bell's inequality) relating them.

|P(a,b) − P(a,c)| ≤ 1 + P(b,c)P(x,y) is the measured correlation between detectors oriented along directions x and y. Any local hidden-variable theory must obey this bound. (Bell's original 1964 form of this inequality was derived for the singlet state |Ψ⁻⟩, not |Φ⁺⟩ — the exact numbers differ between Bell states, but every Bell state violates some such inequality.)

Quantum mechanics predicts specific correlation values for a Bell pair measured at particular angle combinations that violate this inequality — and when the experiment is actually run, the measured correlations match the quantum prediction, not the classical bound. This has been confirmed experimentally many times, for |Φ⁺⟩ and the other Bell states alike, each with its own violating angle combination. The conclusion: the correlations in Bell states are not explainable by any pre-agreed classical instruction set. The entanglement is physically real, not a stand-in for unmeasured classical information.

Common mistakeDon't picture entanglement as "the qubits secretly decided their outcomes in advance and we just didn't know yet" (a hidden-variable story). Bell's inequality violation rules that out directly — the correlation genuinely does not exist until measurement, yet is still perfectly matched between the two qubits.
✦ Worked Example — showing a Bell state can't be factored

Prove algebraically that |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2 cannot be written as |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ for any single-qubit states |ψ⟩ = a|0⟩+b|1⟩, |φ⟩ = c|0⟩+d|1⟩.

  1. Assume it factors: |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ = ac|00⟩ + ad|01⟩ + bc|10⟩ + bd|11⟩.
  2. Match coefficients to |Φ⁺⟩'s amplitudes: ac = 1/√2,  ad = 0,  bc = 0,  bd = 1/√2.
  3. From ad = 0: either a = 0 or d = 0. But ac = 1/√2 ≠ 0 forces a ≠ 0, so d = 0.
  4. Plug d = 0 into the fourth equation: bd = 1/√2 becomes b·0 = 1/√2, i.e. 0 = 1/√2 — a flat contradiction.
  5. Conclusion: no values of a,b,c,d can satisfy all four equations simultaneously — |Φ⁺⟩ cannot be factored into any |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩. It is entangled.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a Bell state and probe its subsystem

QuTiP can build a Bell state directly and compute the reduced state of just one qubit with ptrace() (partial trace — fully explained in Lecture 8).

# bell_state.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
from qutip import basis, tensor

ket0, ket1 = basis(2, 0), basis(2, 1)

phi_plus = (tensor(ket0, ket0) + tensor(ket1, ket1)).unit()   # |00> + |11>, normalized
print("Bell state norm:", phi_plus.norm())                    # 1.0

# full 2-qubit state is pure: its density matrix rho = |phi><phi| squares to itself
rho_full = phi_plus * phi_plus.dag()
print("Full state is pure:", (rho_full * rho_full - rho_full).norm() < 1e-9)   # True

# reduced state of qubit A alone, tracing out qubit B
rho_A = rho_full.ptrace(0)
print(rho_A)                                                   # NOT a pure-state density matrix!
print("Qubit A alone is pure:", (rho_A * rho_A - rho_A).norm() < 1e-9)   # False

The full two-qubit state is pure, but qubit A by itself is not — tracing out an entangled partner always leaves a mixed state behind. Lecture 8 explains exactly why, and what "mixed" means.

🔗 Where this is heading

The hands-on result above — a pure two-qubit state whose single-qubit piece is not pure — is not a bug. It's the mathematical fingerprint of entanglement, and Lecture 8 builds the exact tool (the density operator and the partial trace) needed to describe it properly. Entanglement is also the resource behind quantum teleportation, superdense coding, and quantum error correction — topics that return in later Academy courses once you have gates and circuits (Course 2) to actually create and manipulate these states on real hardware.

Q1. A two-qubit state is entangled exactly when…
A. both amplitudes on |01⟩ and |10⟩ are zero
B. it has exactly two nonzero amplitudes
C. it cannot be written as |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ for any single-qubit |ψ⟩, |φ⟩
D. the two qubits were created at the same time
Show answer

C. Entanglement is defined purely by the failure to factor into a tensor product of single-qubit states — nothing about timing or which amplitudes happen to be zero.

Q2. Which of these is one of the four Bell states?
A. (|00⟩ + |11⟩)/√2
B. |00⟩
C. (|0⟩+|1⟩)/√2 ⊗ |0⟩
D. |01⟩
Show answer

A. (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2 is |Φ⁺⟩, one of the four Bell states. The others (B, D) are unentangled basis states; C is manifestly a product state.

Q3. Two qubits share the state |Φ⁺⟩ = (|00⟩+|11⟩)/√2. Alice measures hers and gets 1. What does Bob get if he measures his?
A. 0, with certainty
B. 0 or 1, 50/50, independent of Alice's result
C. A value that depends on how far apart they are
D. 1, with certainty
Show answer

D. |Φ⁺⟩ only has amplitude on |00⟩ and |11⟩ — if Alice's outcome is 1, the state has collapsed to |11⟩, so Bob is guaranteed 1 too, regardless of distance.

Q4. Bell's inequality is significant because experiments show it is…
A. satisfied, confirming hidden-variable theories
B. violated, ruling out local hidden-variable explanations of entanglement
C. irrelevant to real quantum systems
D. only testable in principle, never in a real lab
Show answer

B. Measured correlations violate Bell's inequality and match the quantum prediction instead, ruling out any local hidden-variable ("secretly pre-agreed") explanation of the correlations.

Q5. In the Lecture 7 hands-on, after tracing out qubit B from the pure Bell state, qubit A's reduced state is…
A. still a pure state, identical to before
B. undefined — you cannot trace out a qubit
C. exactly |0⟩
D. not pure — it is a mixed state
Show answer

D. Even though the full two-qubit state is pure, tracing out an entangled partner leaves qubit A in a mixed state — the signature of entanglement that Lecture 8 explains in full.
