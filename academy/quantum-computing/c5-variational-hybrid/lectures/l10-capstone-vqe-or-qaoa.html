10.1Choose Your Path

Every lecture in this course has been building toward this moment: you now have everything you need to run a complete hybrid variational algorithm from scratch, start to finish, and you get to choose which flagship algorithm to build it with.

Track A — VQE: ground-state energy

Build on Lectures 3–4: construct a small Hamiltonian (the toy H = aZ, or a slightly richer multi-term toy like H = aZ₀ + bX₀X₁), attach a real ansatz (Lecture 2), and drive ⟨H⟩ down toward its true minimum with a classical optimizer (Lecture 8).

Track B — QAOA: Max-Cut

Build on Lectures 6–7: pick a small graph (a 4-node graph is a good step up from the triangle), build its cost and mixer Hamiltonians, assemble a p=1 or p=2 QAOA circuit, and optimize γ,β to find a good cut.

Both tracks are the exact same hybrid loop (Lecture 1) wearing a different ansatz and cost function — that's the whole point of this course's structure, and it's worth genuinely internalizing before you pick: the choice of track changes almost nothing about the shape of what you're about to build.

10.2Assembling the Full Pipeline

Whichever track you chose, your capstone needs to visibly combine every piece this course introduced, not just the two or three most central ones:

Lecture 1 (hybrid loop) + Lecture 2 (ansatz) + Lecture 3/4 or 6/7 (VQE or QAOA cost) + Lecture 8 (optimizer) + Lecture 5 (barren-plateau awareness) + C4 (noise awareness)A complete capstone touches every box in this chain — even the awareness-only ones (barren plateaus, noise) should show up as a line of discussion or a small code check, not just a mention.

Concretely: pick an ansatz small enough that Week 5's barren-plateau concern is a design note rather than an active crisis (2–4 qubits, 1–3 layers is plenty for a capstone-scale demonstration); pick an optimizer from Lecture 8 and justify the choice in one sentence (gradient descent/Adam if you trust the landscape, COBYLA/SPSA if you want gradient-free robustness); and run at least one comparison against a noise model (Lecture 9's style) to show you understand that your clean simulator result is not automatically what real hardware would give you.

Common mistakeTreating the capstone as "just run the Week 4 or Week 7 code again." The point of combining everything is the combination itself — a capstone that never mentions barren plateaus, never compares against any noise model, or never explains its optimizer choice is missing exactly the parts that distinguish a capstone from a lecture's hands-on exercise.
✦ Worked Example — sizing a capstone-scale QAOA graph

Suppose you choose Track B with a 4-node graph (a square: edges (0,1),(1,2),(2,3),(3,0)) at QAOA depth p=2. Before writing any code, work out what you're committing to.

  1. Qubits needed: one per vertex → 4 qubits.
  2. Parameters to optimize: 2p = 4 (γ₁,β₁,γ₂,β₂), per Lecture 7.
  3. Max possible cut: a 4-cycle (square) is bipartite — split it into alternating vertices {0,2} and {1,3} and every one of the 4 edges crosses groups, so the max cut is all 4 edges, unlike the triangle's unavoidable 2-of-3.
  4. Barren-plateau risk at this scale: 4 qubits, 2 layers is well within the "demonstration-scale, not yet barren-plateau-prone" range from Lecture 5 — a reasonable, honest choice for a capstone, worth stating explicitly in your write-up.

🛠️ Hands-On: the full capstone pipeline

Below is a complete, combined capstone script following Track B (QAOA on the 4-node square graph from the worked example), touching every piece named in §10.2. If you chose Track A (VQE), the same structure applies — swap the cost Hamiltonian and ansatz for Lecture 3/4's and keep the optimizer, barren-plateau note, and noise-comparison steps identical.

# capstone_qaoa.py — Lecture 10 (Track B: QAOA Max-Cut)
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# --- Lecture 6: cost Hamiltonian for the 4-node square graph ---
edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 0)]
coeffs, obs = [], []
for (i, j) in edges:
    coeffs += [0.5, -0.5]
    obs += [qml.Identity(i), qml.PauliZ(i) @ qml.PauliZ(j)]
# qml.ops.LinearCombination is current PennyLane's replacement for the deprecated qml.Hamiltonian
Hc = qml.ops.LinearCombination(coeffs, obs)
Hm = qml.ops.LinearCombination([1.0]*4, [qml.PauliX(w) for w in range(4)])

# --- Lecture 2/7: p=2 QAOA ansatz --- (4 qubits, 2 layers: Lecture 5-safe scale)
dev_clean = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=4)
dev_noisy = qml.device("default.mixed", wires=4)

def ansatz_clean(params):
    gammas, betas = params[:2], params[2:]
    for q in range(4):
        qml.Hadamard(wires=q)
    for p in range(2):
        qml.ApproxTimeEvolution(Hc, gammas[p], 1)
        qml.ApproxTimeEvolution(Hm, betas[p], 1)

def ansatz_noisy(params):
    gammas, betas = params[:2], params[2:]
    for q in range(4):
        qml.Hadamard(wires=q)
    for p in range(2):
        qml.ApproxTimeEvolution(Hc, gammas[p], 1)
        qml.ApproxTimeEvolution(Hm, betas[p], 1)
        if p == 1:                              # Lecture 9-style noise check on the final layer only
            for q in range(4):
                qml.DepolarizingChannel(0.02, wires=q)

clean_cost = qml.QNode(lambda p: (ansatz_clean(p), qml.expval(Hc))[-1], dev_clean)
noisy_cost = qml.QNode(lambda p: (ansatz_noisy(p), qml.expval(Hc))[-1], dev_noisy)

# --- Lecture 8: gradient-free COBYLA optimizer (robust choice for p=2) ---
def neg_cost(params):
    return -float(clean_cost(params))

result = minimize(neg_cost, x0=[0.3, 0.6, 0.5, 0.2], method="COBYLA",
                   options={"maxiter": 150})
best_params = result.x

print(f"Best params (gamma1,gamma2,beta1,beta2) = {np.round(best_params, 3)}")
print(f"Clean <Hc>  = {clean_cost(best_params):.3f}   (true max = 4.0)")
print(f"Noisy <Hc>  = {noisy_cost(best_params):.3f}   (Lecture 9-style noise comparison)")

The clean run should land close to the true maximum cut of 4.0 (the worked example's bipartite square); the noisy run should land measurably lower, exactly the kind of gap a real capstone write-up should name explicitly rather than hide. If you ran Track A instead, your write-up should show the equivalent gap between a clean and noisy ⟨H⟩ relative to the true ground energy.

  • Wk 2: Ansätze — hardware-efficient vs. problem-inspired, and the expressibility-vs-depth trade-off.

  • Wks 6–7: QAOA — Max-Cut, the cost/mixer Hamiltonian split, and circuit depth p.

🎓 The Full Quantum Computing Specialization — Five Courses, One Arc

  1. C1 — QM Foundations: state vectors, the Born rule, operators and measurement, the Bloch sphere, entanglement, density matrices, and no-cloning — the mathematical language every later course assumed fluently.
  2. C2: the gate-level toolkit — single- and multi-qubit gates, circuit construction, and the building blocks (including the RY, Hadamard, and CNOT gates this very course leaned on constantly).
  3. C3: fixed-circuit quantum algorithms run entirely on the quantum side — Deutsch–Jozsa, Grover, phase estimation — the "one long, fragile computation" model that C5 Lecture 1 explicitly contrasted itself against.
  4. C4 — Noise, Error Correction & NISQ: why real hardware can't yet run those long fixed circuits reliably — decoherence, T1/T2, and the NISQ-era constraints that motivated this entire course's hybrid-loop strategy from its first paragraph.
  5. C5 — Variational & Hybrid Algorithms (this course): the algorithmic answer to C4's constraints — shallow, parameterized circuits paired with a classical optimizer, culminating in VQE, QAOA, and the capstone you just built.

That's the whole arc: from "what is a qubit" (C1) to "here is a real, hardware-aware algorithm you built yourself, end to end" (C5's capstone, above). If you're heading toward an internship conversation at IBM, Google, or an academic quantum lab, this is genuinely the foundation those conversations assume — state vectors and gates as fluent vocabulary, fixed-circuit algorithms as context, NISQ constraints as the reason any of this course's material exists, and VQE/QAOA as the concrete, honestly-framed answer to "what can near-term quantum computers actually do."
