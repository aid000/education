Parameterized Quantum Circuits

Lecture 1 gave you one knob, θ, on one qubit. Every real variational algorithm needs dozens of knobs across many qubits — this week is about how you build that knob set, and what it costs you.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define an ansatz as a parameterized circuit template |ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|0⟩ built from rotation gates and entangling layers.
  • Contrast hardware-efficient ansätze with problem-inspired ansätze.
  • Define expressibility and explain the expressibility-vs-depth/parameter-count trade-off.
  • Work through a 2-qubit ansatz and write its output state as an explicit function of θ₁, θ₂.
  • Build that 2-qubit ansatz in PennyLane and inspect the states it reaches as θ₁, θ₂ vary.

2.1What an "Ansatz" Is

L1's toy circuit had exactly one tunable gate. A real variational circuit — the kind VQE and QAOA actually run — needs many: a whole template of gates whose angles are left as free parameters for the classical optimizer to fill in. That template is called an ansatz (German for "approach" or "starting point"), and it has the same general shape as L1's circuit, just with a vector of parameters instead of a single scalar:

|ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|0⟩    θ = (θ₁, θ₂, …, θₙ)U(θ) is built from single-qubit rotation gates RY(θᵢ)/RZ(θᵢ), one per parameter, interleaved with fixed entangling gates (usually CNOTs) that let qubits influence each other.

A typical ansatz is organized in layers: a layer of single-qubit rotations (one RY or RZ per qubit, each with its own θᵢ) followed by a layer of entangling CNOTs connecting neighboring qubits, repeated some number of times. The rotation layers are where the parameters live; the entangling layers are fixed and parameter-free, but without them the qubits would never interact and the ansatz could only ever produce unentangled product states.

2.2Hardware-Efficient vs. Problem-Inspired Ansätze

There are two broad philosophies for choosing U(θ), and you'll see both later in this course:

Hardware-efficient ansätze

Built entirely from whatever gates are cheap and native on the target device — single-qubit rotations and nearest-neighbor CNOTs, arranged in generic repeating layers with no reference to the specific problem being solved. They're flexible and easy to compile onto real hardware, but because they ignore the problem's structure, they can need many layers (and many parameters) to represent a good solution, and — as you'll see in Week 5 — can be notoriously hard to train.

Problem-inspired ansätze

Built to directly encode structure from the problem itself. QAOA's ansatz (Weeks 6–7) is the clearest example: its layers are literally built from the cost Hamiltonian of the optimization problem being solved, not from generic hardware-friendly gates. Chemistry has its own problem-inspired ansätze (e.g. "unitary coupled cluster," which this course won't derive) built from the physics of electron excitations. Problem-inspired ansätze often need far fewer parameters to reach a good answer, but only work for the class of problem they were designed around.

2.3Expressibility vs. Depth and Parameter Count

Expressibility describes how much of the full space of possible quantum states an ansatz can actually reach as θ ranges over all its values. A single RY(θ) on one qubit (L1) can only reach a one-dimensional arc of the Bloch sphere — very low expressibility. Add more qubits, more parameters, and more entangling layers, and the ansatz can reach a dramatically larger slice of the full state space.

More expressibility sounds strictly good — surely you want your ansatz able to reach the true answer. But it is not free. Every added parameter is one more dimension the classical optimizer (L1's loop) has to search, and every added layer is more gates for the real NISQ hardware (C4) to execute before decoherence catches up. Worse, Week 5 will show you something sharper still: ansätze that are too expressive can develop cost landscapes that are almost perfectly flat almost everywhere, making the gradient-based descent from L1 fail outright even though a good θ exists somewhere on the landscape.

AnalogyA hardware-efficient ansatz is like a generic set of woodworking chisels: with enough strokes, a skilled hand can carve almost any shape, but carving something specific — say, a particular statue — takes many careful passes and a lot of trial and error. A problem-inspired ansatz is like a custom mold shaped exactly like that statue: pour the material in, and you're done in one step — but that mold is useless for carving anything else. Expressibility is "how many different statues could this tool eventually produce," and it trades directly against how many strokes (parameters, layers) it takes to get there.
Common mistakeAssuming a more expressive ansatz is always the better choice. More parameters and more depth give the optimizer a bigger, harder-to-search landscape, and (Week 5) can make that landscape's gradients vanish almost everywhere. The right ansatz is usually the least expressive one that can still plausibly reach a good answer for your specific problem — not the most powerful one available.
✦ Worked Example — a 2-qubit ansatz, in closed form

Take the smallest ansatz that can produce entanglement: two qubits, starting at |00⟩, with independent rotations RY(θ₁) on qubit 0 and RY(θ₂) on qubit 1, followed by one CNOT with qubit 0 as control and qubit 1 as target. Find |ψ(θ₁,θ₂)⟩ explicitly.

  1. Apply the rotations (they act on separate qubits, so just tensor them): RY(θ₁)|0⟩⊗RY(θ₂)|0⟩ = [cos(θ₁/2)|0⟩+sin(θ₁/2)|1⟩] ⊗ [cos(θ₂/2)|0⟩+sin(θ₂/2)|1⟩].
  2. Expand the tensor product into the four computational-basis amplitudes: cos(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2)|00⟩ + cos(θ₁/2)sin(θ₂/2)|01⟩ + sin(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2)|10⟩ + sin(θ₁/2)sin(θ₂/2)|11⟩.
  3. Apply CNOT(0→1): it flips qubit 1's value whenever qubit 0 is |1⟩, so it swaps the roles of |10⟩ and |11⟩ while leaving |00⟩ and |01⟩ untouched.
  4. Read off the result by swapping the two |1_⟩ coefficients from step 2:
|ψ(θ₁,θ₂)⟩ = cos(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2)|00⟩ + cos(θ₁/2)sin(θ₂/2)|01⟩ + sin(θ₁/2)sin(θ₂/2)|10⟩ + sin(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2)|11⟩
  1. Check the entanglement claim: for most choices of θ₁,θ₂ this state cannot be factored back into |ψ⟩⊗|φ⟩ for any single-qubit |ψ⟩,|φ⟩ — the CNOT genuinely entangled the two rotation layers. (It's only unentangled for special cases, e.g. θ₁ = 0.) This is exactly what makes even this tiny two-parameter ansatz more expressible than two independent single-qubit ansätze with no entangling gate at all.

🛠️ Hands-On: build the 2-qubit ansatz in PennyLane

Build exactly the circuit from the worked example and print the full state vector for a few choices of (θ₁, θ₂), checking it against the closed form above.

# ansatz_2q.py — Lecture 2
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np

dev = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=2)

@qml.qnode(dev)
def ansatz_state(theta1, theta2):
    qml.RY(theta1, wires=0)
    qml.RY(theta2, wires=1)
    qml.CNOT(wires=[0, 1])
    return qml.state()                     # full 4-amplitude state vector

for t1, t2 in [(0.0, 0.0), (np.pi/2, 0.3), (1.1, 2.4)]:
    psi = ansatz_state(t1, t2)
    print(f"theta=({t1:.2f},{t2:.2f}) -> |00>,|01>,|10>,|11> amplitudes = {np.round(psi, 3)}")

Confirm the printed amplitudes match the closed-form expression term by term. Then try removing the qml.CNOT line entirely — the state becomes a simple, always-factorable tensor product, and you've just watched the entangling layer's contribution to expressibility disappear in real time.

Q1. What is an "ansatz" in the context of variational quantum algorithms?
A. A parameterized circuit template |ψ(θ)⟩ = U(θ)|0⟩ whose angles are tuned by a classical optimizer
B. A fixed, parameter-free circuit run exactly once
C. A classical neural network that replaces the quantum computer entirely
D. The final measured bitstring of a quantum algorithm
Show answer

A. An ansatz is the circuit template — rotation gates plus entangling layers — whose parameters the hybrid loop's classical optimizer searches over.

Q2. What distinguishes a problem-inspired ansatz from a hardware-efficient one?
A. Problem-inspired ansätze never use entangling gates
B. A problem-inspired ansatz's layers encode structure from the specific problem being solved, rather than just using generic hardware-native gates
C. Hardware-efficient ansätze can only run on error-corrected hardware
D. There is no real difference — the two terms describe the same thing
Show answer

B. Hardware-efficient ansätze use generic, device-friendly gates with no reference to the problem; problem-inspired ansätze (like QAOA's) are built directly from the problem's own structure.

Q3. "Expressibility" refers to…
A. How fast a circuit runs on real hardware
B. How many qubits a device physically has
C. How much of the full space of possible quantum states an ansatz can reach as its parameters vary
D. The number of classical bits needed to store a measurement result
Show answer

C. Expressibility measures the reach of an ansatz — how large a slice of the full state space U(θ)|0⟩ can cover as θ ranges over all its values.

Q4. In the worked example's ansatz, the coefficient of |11⟩ in the final state is…
A. cos(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2)
B. cos(θ₁/2)sin(θ₂/2)
C. sin(θ₁/2)sin(θ₂/2)
D. sin(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2)
Show answer

D. The CNOT swaps the pre-CNOT |10⟩ and |11⟩ coefficients; the pre-CNOT |10⟩ coefficient was sin(θ₁/2)cos(θ₂/2), which becomes the post-CNOT |11⟩ coefficient.

Q5. Why isn't "always use the most expressible ansatz available" good general advice?
A. More parameters and depth make the optimizer's search harder and the circuit more exposed to decoherence, and can even cause gradients to vanish almost everywhere (Week 5)
B. Expressible ansätze cannot be simulated classically at all
C. Expressibility is purely a theoretical concept with no practical consequence
D. Only problem-inspired ansätze are ever expressible
Show answer

A. Expressibility trades against trainability and against the shallow-circuit NISQ bargain from Lecture 1 — the common mistake this section warns against.
