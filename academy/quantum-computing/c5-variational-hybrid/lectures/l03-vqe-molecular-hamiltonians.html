VQE I: Molecular Hamiltonians

The first real hybrid algorithm. VQE takes Lecture 1's loop and Lecture 2's ansatz and points them at an actual question of chemistry: how low can a molecule's energy go?

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State and understand the variational principle: ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩ ≥ E₀ for any trial state, where E₀ is the true ground-state energy.
  • Describe, conceptually, how a molecular Hamiltonian gets mapped onto qubit operators.
  • Explain the VQE idea: use a classical optimizer to tune θ so ⟨H⟩ approaches E₀ from above.
  • Work a fully analytic toy VQE problem: H = aZ with an RY(θ) ansatz.
  • Discuss what VQE trades away in exchange for near-term feasibility (Discussion 1).

3.1The Variational Principle

Everything VQE does rests on one theorem from quantum mechanics, and it's worth stating carefully because the whole algorithm is really just this theorem run as a search. For a system with Hamiltonian H and true ground-state energy E₀ (its lowest possible eigenvalue), any normalized trial state |ψ⟩ — good, bad, or arbitrary — satisfies:

⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ ≥ E₀    for every normalized |ψ⟩The expectation value of H in any trial state is never below the true ground energy. Equality holds only when |ψ⟩ is exactly the ground state.

Why is this true? Expand |ψ⟩ in H's own eigenbasis, |ψ⟩ = Σᵢ cᵢ|Eᵢ⟩ with Σᵢ|cᵢ|²=1 and E₀ ≤ E₁ ≤ E₂ ≤ …. Then ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ = Σᵢ|cᵢ|²Eᵢ — a weighted average of eigenvalues, every one of which is at least E₀. A weighted average of numbers each ≥ E₀ is itself ≥ E₀, with equality exactly when all the weight sits on E₀. That's the entire proof, and it holds for any trial state — including a parameterized |ψ(θ)⟩ from an ansatz.

This single fact licenses the whole VQE strategy: you don't need to know anything about H's eigenstates in advance. Pick any trial state, compute ⟨H⟩, and you have a number you know sits at or above E₀. Searching over θ to push that number down is therefore always safe — you can never accidentally overshoot below the true answer.

3.2From Molecules to Qubits

The variational principle works for any Hamiltonian, but chemistry's Hamiltonians are written in the language of electrons and orbitals, not qubits. Conceptually, getting from a molecule like H₂ to something a qubit ansatz can measure takes two steps, neither of which this course derives in detail:

Second quantization

The molecule's electronic Hamiltonian — kinetic energy plus electron-nucleus and electron-electron interactions — gets rewritten in terms of "creation" and "annihilation" operators acting on electron orbitals, a standard reformulation from quantum chemistry.

Qubit mapping

Those orbital operators then get translated into Pauli operators (X, Y, Z, I) acting on qubits, via a qubit mapping (e.g. Jordan-Wigner). The end result, for any molecule, is a Hamiltonian written as a weighted sum of Pauli strings:

H = Σᵢ cᵢ Pᵢ    each Pᵢ a tensor product of Pauli operators (e.g. X₀Z₁, Y₀Y₁X₂, …)For H₂ in a minimal basis, this sum has just a handful of terms across 2–4 qubits — small enough to simulate on a laptop, which is exactly why H₂ is the standard "hello world" of VQE.

You will not derive this mapping in this course — treat it as a black box that hands you H in exactly this form. Lecture 4 picks up from here: once H is a sum of Pauli terms, there's a very concrete recipe for measuring ⟨H⟩ on a real circuit.

3.3The VQE Idea

Put 3.1 and 3.2 together and VQE's idea is almost a one-liner: build an ansatz |ψ(θ)⟩ (Lecture 2), measure ⟨ψ(θ)|H|ψ(θ)⟩ for the molecule's qubit Hamiltonian, and let a classical optimizer (Lecture 1's loop) search over θ to push that number as low as possible. Because of the variational principle, every value you see during the search is a valid upper bound on E₀ — the search is literally "approach the true ground energy from above, and stop when you can't push it down any further."

AnalogyThe variational principle is like knowing a hidden floor value in a building you can only ever stand above — every elevator you take lands you on some floor ≥ the true ground floor, never below it. VQE is the strategy of trying elevator after elevator (θ after θ), always landing legally at or above ground, and simply keeping whichever elevator got you lowest. You never risk landing in the basement by mistake; you just might not have found the best elevator yet.
✦ Worked Example — a fully analytic toy VQE

Take the simplest possible "molecular" Hamiltonian: one qubit, H = aZ with a > 0 (so the true ground energy is whichever Z-eigenvalue times a is smaller). Use the RY(θ) ansatz from Lecture 1 and find θ that minimizes ⟨H⟩, and confirm it recovers the exact ground energy.

  1. Trial state: |ψ(θ)⟩ = RY(θ)|0⟩ = cos(θ/2)|0⟩ + sin(θ/2)|1⟩ (Lecture 1's derivation).
  2. Expectation value: since H = aZ, ⟨H⟩ = a·⟨Z⟩ = a·cos(θ), reusing Lecture 1's result that ⟨Z⟩ = cos(θ) for this exact ansatz.
  3. Minimize over θ: with a > 0, cos(θ) is smallest (−1) at θ = π, so ⟨H⟩ is minimized at θ = π, giving ⟨H⟩ = a·(−1) = −a.
  4. Check against the true spectrum: Z's eigenvalues are +1 (eigenstate |0⟩) and −1 (eigenstate |1⟩), so H = aZ has eigenvalues +a and −a. With a > 0, the true ground energy is E₀ = −a — exactly what the variational search found.
min_θ ⟨H⟩ = −a = E₀    achieved at θ = π, where |ψ(π)⟩ = |1⟩ exactlyA toy case where the ansatz happens to be expressive enough to reach the true ground state exactly — not guaranteed in general, but a clean confirmation that the variational search works.

🛠️ Hands-On: scan ⟨H⟩ for the toy Hamiltonian in PennyLane

Build the H = aZ circuit, scan θ across a full range, and confirm numerically that the minimum sits at θ = π with value −a — the same result the worked example found by hand.

# toy_vqe.py — Lecture 3
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np

a = 0.7
dev = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=1)

@qml.qnode(dev)
def expH(theta):
    qml.RY(theta, wires=0)
    return qml.expval(a * qml.PauliZ(0))     # this is <psi(theta)|H|psi(theta)> for H = a*Z

thetas = np.linspace(0, 2 * np.pi, 9)
for t in thetas:
    print(f"theta={t:.2f}   <H>={expH(t):.4f}")

print(f"\nTrue ground energy E0 = -a = {-a:.4f}")

The scan should bottom out near θ = π at ⟨H⟩ ≈ −a. Lecture 4 replaces this manual scan with the real VQE loop — a classical optimizer driving θ there automatically, exactly as Lecture 1's loop drove θ toward π for the undressed ⟨Z⟩ cost.

💬 Week 3 DiscussionVQE is sold as a near-term, noise-tolerant alternative to full quantum phase estimation (the textbook algorithm for finding ground-state energies exactly). Given what you now know about the variational principle only guaranteeing an upper bound, and about ansätze (Lecture 2) only being able to reach some states exactly — what precisely is VQE trading away in exchange for that near-term tolerance? Is "an upper bound you can actually compute on today's hardware" always worth more than "an exact answer you can't"?

🔗 Where this is heading

You've now seen VQE's full logical skeleton, worked by hand on the simplest possible case. What's still missing is the practical machinery: real Hamiltonians are sums of several Pauli terms, not one, and a real quantum device doesn't hand you ⟨H⟩ directly — it hands you measurement outcomes you have to combine yourself. Lecture 4 fills in exactly that gap, and runs a genuine multi-term VQE optimization loop end to end.

Q1. The variational principle states that for any normalized trial state |ψ⟩ and Hamiltonian H with ground energy E₀…
A. ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ ≥ E₀, always
B. ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ = E₀, always
C. ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ ≤ E₀, always
D. there is no relationship between ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ and E₀
Show answer

A. Expanding |ψ⟩ in H's eigenbasis shows ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ is a weighted average of eigenvalues all ≥ E₀, so the average itself is always ≥ E₀.

Q2. After second quantization and a qubit mapping, a molecular Hamiltonian is expressed as…
A. A single classical number, the molecule's total energy
B. A weighted sum of Pauli-operator strings, H = Σᵢ cᵢPᵢ
C. A list of the molecule's atomic coordinates
D. A single Hadamard gate
Show answer

B. The qubit mapping rewrites the electronic Hamiltonian as a sum of Pauli strings with real coefficients — the form Lecture 4's measurement recipe is built around.

Q3. In the VQE loop, what role does the classical optimizer play?
A. It computes the exact molecular Hamiltonian from first principles
B. It performs the qubit mapping from orbitals to Pauli strings
C. It searches over the ansatz's θ to push the measured ⟨H⟩ as low as possible, exactly as in Lecture 1's loop
D. It measures the quantum circuit directly
Show answer

C. VQE is Lecture 1's hybrid loop with H set to a molecular Hamiltonian — the classical optimizer's job doesn't change.

Q4. For the toy Hamiltonian H = aZ (a > 0) with an RY(θ) ansatz, ⟨H⟩ = a·cos(θ) is minimized at…
A. θ = 0, giving ⟨H⟩ = a
B. θ = π/2, giving ⟨H⟩ = 0
C. θ = π, giving ⟨H⟩ = −a
D. There is no minimum
Show answer

C. cos(θ) reaches its minimum, −1, at θ = π, so a·cos(θ) reaches −a there — exactly H's true ground-state eigenvalue.

Q5. Why can a variational search never "overshoot" below the true ground energy E₀, no matter how bad the trial state is?
A. Because quantum computers cannot make computational errors
B. Because classical optimizers always converge to the global minimum
C. Because ansätze are always expressive enough to reach the exact ground state
D. Because ⟨ψ|H|ψ⟩ is a weighted average of H's eigenvalues, all of which are ≥ E₀ by definition
Show answer

D. The variational principle's proof is exactly this averaging argument — it holds for literally any normalized trial state, which is why VQE can safely minimize without ever dipping below E₀.
