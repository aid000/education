Week 9 · Lecture 9

Where NISQ-Era Variational Methods Stand Today

An honest wrap-up before the capstone. Variational algorithms are the most hyped near-term application of quantum computers — what does everything from Weeks 1–8 actually support claiming?

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Summarize what VQE/QAOA have actually demonstrated to date: small molecules, small Max-Cut instances, on real hardware.
  • Catalog the real obstacles: barren plateaus (Week 5), noise (C4), limited qubit counts/connectivity.
  • Describe the ongoing debate over when (or whether) "quantum advantage" will be demonstrated for practically useful variational problems.
  • Frame what a quantum-internship-track student should realistically be able to say in an interview.
  • Simulate a VQE circuit under a simple noise model and see how far it drifts from the noiseless result.

9.1What's Actually Been Demonstrated

Strip away the hype and the honest record, as of today, looks like this: VQE has computed ground-state energies for small molecules (H₂ and similar minimal-basis systems, exactly the toy-scale problems this course's worked examples have mirrored) on real superconducting and trapped-ion hardware, matching known exact answers to reasonable precision. QAOA has found good (though rarely provably optimal) cuts on small Max-Cut instances — tens of nodes, not thousands — on real devices as well.

These are genuine, real demonstrations — not simulations — and they're meaningful proof-of-concept results for the entire hybrid-loop paradigm this course has built from Lecture 1 onward. What they are not yet is a demonstration that either algorithm outperforms the best classical methods on a problem anyone outside a research paper actually needs solved. That gap is the honest center of this week's discussion.

9.2The Real Obstacles

Three obstacles, each one you've already met in this course, compound together on real hardware:

Barren plateaus (Week 5)

As ansätze scale toward the qubit counts needed for practically useful chemistry or optimization problems, gradient-based training can become unreliable — and the problem-inspired structure of QAOA (Weeks 6–7) only partially shields against it.

Noise (C4)

Every measurement in this course's hands-on examples ran on a noiseless simulator. Real NISQ devices add gate errors and decoherence on top of everything else — the entire premise of "shallow circuits trade depth for iterations" (Lecture 1) only buys you so much headroom before noise itself corrupts the cost estimate the optimizer is working from.

Limited qubit counts and connectivity

Real molecules of practical interest (beyond H₂-scale toys) and real optimization instances of practical interest (beyond small graphs) need more qubits, more connected, than most current devices offer — and adding qubits worsens both of the obstacles above simultaneously.

practical usefulness requires: more qubits + more connectivity + deeper circuits— and every one of those three directions makes barren plateaus and noise worse, not better. This tension, not any single obstacle alone, is the real story of where NISQ-era variational methods stand.

9.3The Quantum Advantage Debate, and Interview Framing

"Quantum advantage" for variational methods — a demonstration that VQE or QAOA solves a practically useful problem faster or better than the best available classical method — has not yet been achieved, and there's genuine, active debate among researchers about whether near-term (rather than fully error-corrected) hardware will ever get there for these specific algorithms, versus other quantum algorithm classes.

This course's stated goal has been quantum-internship-track readiness (IBM, Google, academic labs), and here is the realistic framing that goal actually calls for: you should be able to explain exactly what VQE and QAOA are, why they're built the way they are (the hybrid loop, Lecture 1; the ansatz trade-offs, Lecture 2), what's been genuinely demonstrated (§9.1), and why barren plateaus and noise (§9.2) are the two obstacles any serious conversation about scaling has to address head-on. An interviewer in this space is far more likely to respect "here's exactly what's proven and what isn't" than an overclaiming pitch — and far more likely to respect obvious ignorance of barren plateaus than a glossy answer that pretends they don't exist.

Common mistakeWalking into a quantum-computing interview and either (a) overclaiming — implying VQE/QAOA already beat classical methods on useful problems — or (b) underclaiming — dismissing variational methods as useless hype. Both responses signal you haven't actually engaged with where the field stands; the honest, defensible position is the nuanced one this lecture just walked through.
✦ Worked Example — a fair-claim checklist

A classmate claims: "VQE has already found the ground-state energy of large industrially relevant molecules faster than classical computers." Evaluate this claim against what §9.1–9.2 actually support.

  1. Check scale: §9.1 says real-hardware VQE demonstrations have been on small molecules (H₂-scale), not large industrially relevant ones — the claim overstates scale.
  2. Check speed/advantage: §9.3 says no demonstrated quantum advantage over classical methods exists yet for a practically useful variational problem — the claim overstates performance.
  3. What would be fair to say instead: "VQE has demonstrated correct ground-state energies for small molecules on real hardware, matching known classical answers — a genuine proof of the hybrid-loop concept, but not yet a demonstrated advantage over classical methods on a problem of practical industrial scale."
  4. Lesson: the corrected claim keeps everything that's actually true (real hardware, correct results, proof of concept) while dropping exactly the two overclaims (scale, advantage) that §9.1–9.3 don't support.

🛠️ Hands-On: how much does noise move a VQE result?

Rerun Lecture 3's toy VQE (H = aZ, RY(θ) ansatz) on a noiseless simulator and on a simple noisy one, and compare how far the measured ⟨H⟩ drifts from the exact −a — a small, concrete taste of the noise obstacle from §9.2 and C4.

# noisy_vqe_comparison.py — Lecture 9
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np

a = 0.7
theta = np.pi                                  # the exact minimizer from Lecture 3

dev_clean = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=1)
dev_noisy = qml.device("default.mixed", wires=1)   # supports noise channels

def circuit(theta):
    qml.RY(theta, wires=0)
    qml.DepolarizingChannel(0.05, wires=0)      # 5% depolarizing noise, applied only on the noisy device
    return qml.expval(a * qml.PauliZ(0))

clean_H = qml.QNode(lambda th: (qml.RY(th, wires=0), qml.expval(a * qml.PauliZ(0)))[-1], dev_clean)
noisy_H = qml.QNode(circuit, dev_noisy)

print(f"Exact ground energy E0 = {-a:.4f}")
print(f"Noiseless <H> at theta=pi: {clean_H(theta):.4f}")
print(f"Noisy (5% depolarizing) <H> at theta=pi: {noisy_H(theta):.4f}")

The noiseless run should land essentially exactly on −a; the noisy run will sit measurably closer to 0 — depolarizing noise pulls expectation values toward the maximally mixed state. Try raising the noise probability and watch the drift grow, a direct, hands-on echo of exactly the obstacle §9.2 and this course's parent, C4, spent ten weeks on.
