QAOA II: Mixer/Cost Layers & Circuit Depth p

Lecture 6 built half of QAOA's ansatz — the cost layer. This week adds the mixer layer, assembles the full p-layer circuit, and actually runs the optimization.

  • Explain why increasing p generally improves solution quality but costs circuit depth — tying back to Week 5's trainability concerns.
  • Build the p=1 QAOA circuit for Lecture 6's triangle graph in PennyLane.
  • Optimize γ, β and confirm the resulting bitstring distribution favors the correct Max-Cut.

7.1The Mixer Hamiltonian

The cost Hamiltonian Hc (Lecture 6) is diagonal in the computational basis — e^(−iγHc) only ever adds relative phases to computational basis states, it never moves probability between different bitstrings. Left alone, a circuit built only from Hc would never actually search anything; it would just sit wherever it started. QAOA needs a second ingredient that does move probability between bitstrings — that's the job of the mixer Hamiltonian:

Hm = Σᵢ Xᵢ    (sum of Pauli-X on every qubit)Each Xᵢ flips qubit i between |0⟩ and |1⟩. Exponentiating Hm (e^(−iβHm)) produces independent single-qubit rotations that drive transitions between different bitstrings — exactly the "explore neighboring assignments" step Hc alone can't provide.

Together, Hc and Hm play complementary roles: Hc "rewards" good bitstrings with a phase advantage (more cut edges → different accumulated phase), and Hm lets that phase information actually redistribute probability across different bitstrings, so that repeated measurement becomes more likely to land on a good one.

7.2The Full QAOA Circuit: p Layers, 2p Parameters

QAOA's full ansatz alternates the cost and mixer unitaries p times, starting from an easy-to-prepare uniform superposition (a layer of Hadamards on every qubit), with an independent pair of parameters (γₖ, βₖ) for each of the p layers:

|ψ(γ,β)⟩ = e^(−iβₚHm) e^(−iγₚHc) ⋯ e^(−iβ₁Hm) e^(−iγ₁Hc) · H⊗ⁿ|0⟩ⁿ2p total classical parameters (γ₁..γₚ, β₁..βₚ) — exactly the θ vector from Lecture 2's general ansatz framework, just organized into this specific alternating structure.

This is QAOA's complete circuit. The classical optimizer from Lecture 1 tunes all 2p parameters to maximize ⟨Hc⟩ (equivalently, minimize −⟨Hc⟩), exactly the same loop VQE ran in Lectures 3–4 — prepare, measure, update, repeat. The only thing that's changed is the ansatz's specific, problem-inspired shape.

7.3Does Increasing p Always Help?

In theory, yes — as p → ∞, QAOA's achievable solution quality provably approaches the true optimum (this course won't derive why). In practice, there's a real cost to increasing p that mirrors Lecture 2's expressibility-vs-depth trade-off directly: each additional layer adds more gates (more circuit depth, more exposure to NISQ-era decoherence from C4) and 2 more parameters for the classical optimizer to search over.

Worse, Week 5's barren-plateau concern doesn't disappear just because QAOA's ansatz is problem-inspired rather than generic — at large enough p and qubit count, QAOA's landscape can flatten out too, for essentially the same statistical reasons. "Increase p" is not a free lunch; it's a genuine trade between solution quality and trainability/hardware feasibility, decided case by case.

Common mistakeAssuming larger p is strictly better because it's "more quantum" or "more expressive." Exactly as with VQE's ansätze (Lecture 2, Lecture 5), more layers buys potential solution quality at the direct cost of circuit depth and optimizer trainability — the right p for a real device is a practical trade-off, not simply "as large as possible."
AnalogyThink of p as the number of rounds in a game of "telephone" where each round lets players (bitstrings) swap a little bit of standing based on how good they currently look (cost layer) and then shuffle positions a little (mixer layer). More rounds, in principle, converge toward a better final ranking — but each round also introduces more chances for the message to get garbled by noise, and more decisions for whoever is tuning each round's two knobs (γₖ, βₖ) to get right.
✦ Worked Example — counting parameters at p=1 vs p=3

For the triangle-graph QAOA circuit, how many classical parameters does the optimizer need to search at p=1 versus p=3, and how many cost/mixer unitary blocks does the circuit contain at each?

  1. At p=1: one cost layer (parameter γ₁) and one mixer layer (parameter β₁) → 2 total parameters, 2 unitary blocks.
  2. At p=3: three independent (γₖ,βₖ) pairs → 2×3 = 6 total parameters, 6 unitary blocks (3 cost + 3 mixer).
  3. Trade-off: p=3 searches a landscape three times larger in dimension and runs a circuit three times deeper than p=1 — generally capable of a better final cut, but Week 5's optimization and C4's decoherence concerns both scale up correspondingly.

🛠️ Hands-On: optimize a p=1 QAOA circuit for the triangle Max-Cut

Build the full p=1 QAOA circuit for Lecture 6's triangle graph, optimize γ and β to maximize the expected cut, then sample the final circuit and check that the good bitstrings (2-1 splits) dominate the output distribution.

# qaoa_triangle_p1.py — Lecture 7
import pennylane as qml
from pennylane import numpy as np

edges = [(0, 1), (1, 2), (0, 2)]
coeffs, obs = [], []
for (i, j) in edges:
    coeffs += [0.5, -0.5]
    obs += [qml.Identity(i), qml.PauliZ(i) @ qml.PauliZ(j)]
# qml.ops.LinearCombination is current PennyLane's replacement for the deprecated qml.Hamiltonian
Hc = qml.ops.LinearCombination(coeffs, obs)
Hm = qml.ops.LinearCombination([1.0]*3, [qml.PauliX(0), qml.PauliX(1), qml.PauliX(2)])

dev = qml.device("default.qubit", wires=3)

@qml.qnode(dev)
def qaoa_cost(params):
    gamma, beta = params[0], params[1]
    for q in range(3):
        qml.Hadamard(wires=q)                 # uniform superposition
    qml.ApproxTimeEvolution(Hc, gamma, 1)      # cost layer e^(-i*gamma*Hc)
    qml.ApproxTimeEvolution(Hm, beta, 1)       # mixer layer e^(-i*beta*Hm)
    return qml.expval(Hc)                      # maximize this -> minimize its negative

def neg_cost(params):
    return -qaoa_cost(params)

params = np.array([0.4, 0.8], requires_grad=True)
opt = qml.GradientDescentOptimizer(stepsize=0.2)
for step in range(15):
    params = opt.step(neg_cost, params)
    print(f"step {step+1}:  gamma,beta={np.round(params,3)}  <Hc>={qaoa_cost(params):.3f}")

⟨Hc⟩ should climb toward (though, at p=1, likely not perfectly reach) the true maximum of 2 found by hand in Lecture 6. Sampling this optimized circuit's final state would show the 2-1 split bitstrings coming up noticeably more often than the all-same bitstrings — QAOA genuinely learning to favor the good cuts.

Q1. What does the mixer Hamiltonian Hm = Σᵢ Xᵢ do that the cost Hamiltonian Hc cannot?
A. It drives transitions between different bitstrings, redistributing probability, since Hc alone is diagonal and only adds phases
B. It measures the final qubits
C. It encodes the graph's edges
D. It replaces the need for a classical optimizer
Show answer

A. Hc is diagonal in the computational basis and only adds relative phases; Hm's Pauli-X terms actually move probability between different bitstrings.

Q2. How many classical parameters does a p-layer QAOA circuit have?
A. p
B. 2p (γ₁..γₚ and β₁..βₚ)
C. p²
D. A fixed number, independent of p
Show answer

B. Each of the p layers contributes one independent γₖ (cost layer) and one independent βₖ (mixer layer), for 2p total.

Q3. Why doesn't "just increase p" straightforwardly make QAOA better on real NISQ hardware?
A. Increasing p never changes solution quality at all
B. Larger p is always strictly better with no downside
C. Larger p adds circuit depth (more decoherence exposure) and more parameters to optimize, and can also push the landscape toward a barren plateau
D. QAOA circuits are not allowed to have more than one layer
Show answer

C. More layers can improve achievable solution quality in principle, but at the real cost of deeper circuits and a harder-to-train, higher-dimensional landscape.

Q4. In the worked example, how many total classical parameters does a p=3 QAOA circuit need?
A. 3
B. 9
C. 6
D. 1
Show answer

C. 2 parameters per layer × 3 layers = 6 total parameters (γ₁,β₁,γ₂,β₂,γ₃,β₃).

Q5. In the PennyLane hands-on code, what is the classical optimizer actually maximizing (by minimizing its negative)?
A. The circuit depth
B. ⟨Hc⟩, the expected number of cut edges, by tuning γ and β
C. The number of qubits
D. The mixer Hamiltonian's eigenvalues directly
Show answer

B. qaoa_cost returns ⟨Hc⟩; the optimizer minimizes its negative, which is equivalent to maximizing the expected cut value.
