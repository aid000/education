Payoffs & No-Arbitrage · Weeks 1–2
1. A stock trades at S₀ = $50. You buy a European put with strike K = $55 for a $3 premium. At expiry ST = $42. Your profit is…
Answer: C. Payoff = max(55 − 42, 0) = $13; profit = payoff − premium = 13 − 3 = $10. (A) is the payoff before subtracting the premium; (B) and (D) misplace the premium entirely.
2. A put with strike K = $40 trades for $6 while the stock sits at $44. Its intrinsic value and time value are…
Answer: A. The put is out-of-the-money (44 > 40), so intrinsic = max(40 − 44, 0) = $0, and the entire $6 premium is time value. Intrinsic value can never be negative (D).
3. A non-dividend stock trades at $80. The continuously-compounded risk-free rate is 6%, and T = 2 years. The no-arbitrage forward price is…
Answer: D. F = S·erT = 80·e0.12 = 80·1.1275 ≈ $90.20. (A) ignores the rate entirely; (B) grows the spot with simple rather than compound interest; (C) has no basis in the formula.
4. S = $60, K = $60, r = 4% (continuous), T = 1 year. A dealer quotes C = $5.50 and P = $3.00. Is there an arbitrage, and which trade captures it?
Answer: B. K·e−rT = 60·e−0.04 ≈ 57.65, so S − K·e−rT ≈ 2.35, while C − P = 2.50. Since 2.50 > 2.35, the call+bond side is rich — sell it and buy the put+stock side (a conversion) to lock in the gap, risk-free. Volatility never enters parity, so D is wrong.
Binomial Pricing & Trees · Weeks 3–4
5. In a one-period binomial model, S = $50 moves to Su = $65 or Sd = $40. A call struck at K = $55 has hedge ratio Δ equal to…
Answer: A. Cu = max(65−55,0) = 10, Cd = max(40−55,0) = 0. Δ = (Cu−Cd)/(Su−Sd) = (10−0)/(65−40) = 10/25 = 0.4. Dividing by the wrong denominator or numerator gives the other choices.
6. With u = 1.15, d = 0.9, and a one-step bond growth erΔt = 1.04, the risk-neutral probability q is…
Answer: C. q = (erΔt − d)/(u − d) = (1.04 − 0.9)/(1.15 − 0.9) = 0.14/0.25 = 0.56. Since 0.9 < 1.04 < 1.15, the no-arbitrage sandwich holds and q is a valid probability.
7. Why does the real-world probability p of an up-move never appear in the one-period binomial option price?
Answer: D. The replicating portfolio matches the option's payoff in every state, so no-arbitrage pins the price to the cost of that portfolio — independent of p. q need not equal p (A), p is estimable but simply irrelevant here (B), and "risk-neutral investors" is not the mechanism — it's replication (C).
8. Why do we set d = 1/u when building a standard binomial tree?
Answer: B. With u·d = 1, up-then-down (or down-then-up) lands back on the original price, so paths merge and the node count grows linearly (n+1) rather than exponentially (2ⁿ). q is generally not 0.5 (A), and d = 1/u has nothing to do with arbitrage (C) or the real-world volatility estimate (D).
9. At an American-option node, the intrinsic value is $8.00 and the continuation value (discounted risk-neutral expectation of holding) is $6.50. The node's value is…
Answer: C. A rational American holder exercises whenever intrinsic value exceeds continuation value, so the node value is max(intrinsic, continuation) = max(8.00, 6.50) = $8.00. Taking only one term (A) ignores the early-exercise right; averaging (B) or summing (D) has no economic meaning.
Black-Scholes & the Continuous Limit · Weeks 5–6
10. As the number of CRR binomial steps n → ∞, the distribution of the terminal stock price ST converges to which shape, and why?
Answer: A. Each step adds ±σ√Δt to the log-price; the Central Limit Theorem makes that sum normal, so the price itself (its exponential) is lognormal. Prices can't be normal since they can't go negative (B); nodes aren't equally weighted (C); there is no rare-jump dynamic here (D).
11. The standard deviation of the log-return over a horizon T is σ√T. Doubling the horizon from T to 2T grows the total uncertainty by a factor of…
Answer: B. Uncertainty scales with √t, so σ√(2T) = √2·σ√T — a factor of about 1.41. Doubling (A) assumes linear-in-time growth; 4× (C) is the variance ratio, not the standard-deviation ratio; it certainly does change, ruling out D.
12. In C = S·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2), what does N(d2) represent?
Answer: D. N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability the terminal price exceeds the strike. The delta is N(d1), not N(d2) (A); the real expected return never enters the formula (B); the present value of the strike is K·e−rT, a separate factor (C).
13. By how much do d1 and d2 differ, by definition?
Answer: A. d2 = d1 − σ√T, the total volatility accumulated over the option's life. σ²T (B) is the total variance, not the standard deviation; rT (C) is the discount exponent; ½σ²T (D) is the half-variance drift inside d1's numerator, not the gap between the two.
14. Which of a stock's behaviors does not appear anywhere in the Black-Scholes formula?
Answer: C. Because the option is perfectly hedged, pricing happens under the risk-neutral measure where the drift is r, and the real-world μ drops out completely. σ (A), r (B), and T (D) are all genuine inputs.
The Greeks & Delta-Hedging · Weeks 7–8
15. A trader is short 20 call options, each with delta 0.35. To make the position delta-neutral they should…
Answer: B. Short 20 calls gives position delta −20×0.35 = −7. To offset it you need +7 of spot delta, i.e. buy 7 shares. Selling shares (A) doubles the negative exposure; 20 shares (C) ignores the per-option delta; short options carry plenty of directional exposure, so D is wrong.
16. Gamma is largest for a call that is…
Answer: D. Gamma peaks where delta changes fastest: at the money, sharpening as expiry nears. Deep ITM (A) and deep OTM (B) options are nearly linear in S, so gamma ≈ 0. Gamma is decidedly not constant (C) — that variation is the whole point.
17. For a long option position, the typical signs of gamma and theta are…
Answer: A. A long option has positive convexity (Γ > 0) but pays for it through time decay (Θ < 0) — the theta-gamma tradeoff. "Both positive" (C) and "both negative" (D) are wrong; (B) is the reversed pair that belongs to a short position.
18. In the replicating-portfolio view, the price of an option is best described as…
Answer: B. No-arbitrage pricing equates the option to the cheapest portfolio that replicates its payoff in every state. (A) is wrong because replication deliberately avoids forecasting; (C) confuses a single cash flow with the whole payoff; (D) is a market quote, not a fair value.
19. A book is delta-hedged (delta-neutral). Which statement is correct?
Answer: C. Delta-neutral kills only the linear (first-order) response; curvature (gamma) remains, so a large move still moves the P&L. (A) is the classic trap; (B) is wrong because delta drifts, forcing rebalancing; (D) is wrong — a delta-hedged book is direction-neutral, not bullish.
20. You currently rebalance a delta hedge daily. If you instead rebalance four times as often (same option, no transaction costs), the typical standard deviation of your replication error roughly…
Answer: D. Hedging error scales like 1/√N, so multiplying N by 4 divides the error by √4 = 2 — it halves. "Drops to one-quarter" (A) would require a 1/N law, which is too optimistic; (B) ignores the benefit of more frequent hedging; (C) has the direction backwards.
Monte-Carlo, Exotics & Capstone · Weeks 9–10
21. What justifies estimating a discounted expected payoff with a sample average of simulated paths?
Answer: B. The law of large numbers guarantees the sample mean of independent draws converges to the true expectation, which is what an option price is. The Itô correction (A) is the −½σ² term inside the GBM exponent, not a justification for averaging; parity (C) links calls and puts, not sampling; no-arbitrage (D) justifies the pricing formula itself, not the numerical step of averaging.
22. When simulating ST under the risk-neutral measure for Monte-Carlo pricing, which drift goes into the GBM exponent?
Answer: A. Under the risk-neutral measure every tradable asset drifts at the risk-free rate r. Using the real-world μ (B) is the classic biasing error; zero drift (C) is wrong unless r happens to be zero; the dividend yield alone (D) ignores r entirely.
23. A Monte-Carlo run with N = 40,000 paths gives a standard error of 0.12. Approximately how many paths are needed to cut the standard error to 0.03?
Answer: D. Error scales like 1/√N, so a 4× reduction in error (0.12 → 0.03) needs 4² = 16× the paths: 40,000 × 16 = 640,000. 160,000 (A) is only 4× the paths, giving ≈0.06; 320,000 (B) is 8× the paths, giving ≈0.042 — still short; 1,000,000 (C) overshoots what's needed.
24. A down-and-out barrier call's payoff is path-dependent because…
Answer: C. The knock-out depends on the minimum price over the whole life — a path feature — so it is path-dependent. It is not a function of ST alone (A), it certainly depends on the underlying (B), and being knocked out makes it worth strictly less than the vanilla (D).
25. Comparing convergence rates in the capstone, a binomial tree's pricing error shrinks roughly like 1/N in the number of steps, while Monte-Carlo's statistical error shrinks only like…
Answer: B. Monte-Carlo's standard error falls only like 1/√N in the number of paths — the same √n law from the Central Limit Theorem — which is why it needs far more effort than a tree for comparable accuracy. 1/N (A) describes the tree, not Monte-Carlo; 1/N² (C) is too optimistic; the error clearly does shrink, ruling out D.
