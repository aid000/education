Week 8 · Lecture 8

Delta Hedging & Replication

An option isn't priced by guessing where the stock goes — it's priced by building a stock-and-cash portfolio that tracks it move-for-move, then charging for the recipe.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why an option can be replicated by a self-financing portfolio of the underlying plus cash, and why that replication is the price.
  • Use delta as the hedge ratio and describe how a delta-hedged book must be rebalanced dynamically through time.
  • Quantify the hedging error introduced by rebalancing only at discrete moments rather than continuously.
  • Connect that error to gamma and to the gamma–theta trade-off, writing the discrete-hedging P&L of a single step.
  • Reason about how transaction costs penalize frequent rebalancing and force a real-world hedging compromise.

8.1Replication: Pricing by Building, Not Guessing

The central idea of derivatives pricing is almost subversive: to price an option you never need to know whether the stock will go up or down. Instead you ask a different question — what portfolio of things I already know how to price would move exactly like this option? If a mix of the underlying stock and a cash (money-market) position can be made to track the option payoff in every future state, then by no-arbitrage the option must cost exactly what that mix costs today. Pricing becomes a construction problem, not a forecasting problem.

That tracking portfolio is the replicating portfolio. At any instant it holds Δ shares of the stock and some amount B in cash. The amount of stock you hold, Δ, is the option's delta — the sensitivity of the option value to a small move in the stock (introduced last week as a Greek). Hold Δ shares and you've matched the option's first-order response to the stock; the leftover cash B makes the total value match the option value itself.

V(S, t) = Δ · S + B     with   Δ = ∂V/∂SThe option value equals a stock holding (Δ shares) plus a cash balance B. Δ is read straight off the option's price curve as its slope in S.

This is the engine behind the entire Black–Scholes framework. The model's price is not a forecast — it is the cost of the cheapest portfolio that reproduces the option's payoff. A trader who sells an option and runs this portfolio has, in principle, manufactured the option out of stock and cash and pocketed the spread, bearing no directional risk.

AnalogyReplication is like covering a song. You don't need the original master tape — if you can reproduce every note with your own instruments, the cover sounds identical to the audience. The replicating portfolio reproduces the option's payoff "note for note" using only stock and cash, so its cost is the option's fair price.

8.2Dynamic Hedging: The Portfolio Must Keep Moving

Here is the catch that makes this hard and interesting. Delta is not a constant — it changes as the stock moves and as time passes. A call that is far out-of-the-money has delta near 0 (the option barely reacts to the stock); deep in-the-money it has delta near 1 (it moves almost one-for-one with the stock). So the "right" number of shares to hold today is the wrong number tomorrow. The replicating portfolio is dynamic: you must continually buy and sell stock to keep Δ matched. This is delta hedging.

The rebalancing must be self-financing: every share you buy or sell is paid for out of (or deposited into) the cash account B, with no new money injected or withdrawn. All you do is rotate value between stock and cash. In the idealized continuous-time world you adjust Δ at every instant, the portfolio tracks the option perfectly, and its value rides the Black–Scholes price exactly to expiry.

dV = Δ dS + (1/2) Γ (dS)² + Θ dtAn infinitesimal change in the option value: the Δ term is killed by holding Δ shares; what's left is the Γ (curvature) and Θ (time-decay) terms — the heart of hedging error.

Holding Δ shares cancels the Δ dS term — that is the whole point of the hedge. But notice what survives: a term in gamma (Γ = ∂²V/∂S², the curvature of the value curve) multiplying the squared move, and a theta (Θ = ∂V/∂t) term for the passage of time. In continuous rebalancing these two exactly offset along the Black–Scholes equation. The moment you stop hedging continuously, they don't — and the gap becomes your hedging error.

Common mistakeBelieving a "delta-neutral" book is risk-free. Delta neutrality only cancels the first-order sensitivity to the stock. A delta-hedged position still has gamma exposure: a large or fast move changes delta before you can rebalance, and the book gains or loses on the curvature. Delta-neutral is not P&L-neutral.

8.3Discrete Rebalancing, Gamma, and the Hedging Error

No one trades continuously. You rebalance at discrete moments — daily, hourly, on a tick. Between rebalances delta drifts away from its correct value, and the leftover gamma and theta terms no longer cancel. The mismatch over one step is the discrete-hedging P&L. For a trader who has sold an option and is delta-hedging it, the P&L of the hedged book over a single step Δt during which the stock moves by ΔS is:

P&Lstep ≈ (1/2) Γ [ (ΔS)² − σ²S²Δt ]Selling the option ⇒ short gamma. You lose when the realized squared move (ΔS)² exceeds what was "paid for" via theta (σ²S²Δt), and gain when the market is quieter than implied.

Read this carefully, because it is the soul of options trading. The term σ²S²Δt is the move the option's theta (time decay) charged for — the volatility implied in the price you sold at. The term (ΔS)² is the move that actually happened — the realized volatility. A delta hedger is therefore not betting on direction at all; they are betting on realized volatility versus implied volatility. Sell options (short gamma) and you profit when markets are calmer than implied and bleed when they're wilder. Buy options (long gamma) and it's the reverse.

Now the error from hedging discretely. Each step contributes a random gamma term whose mean is roughly zero (you're as likely to over- as under-cover) but whose variance is non-zero. Summing over the life of the option, the standard deviation of the total hedging error scales like one over the square root of the number of rebalances:

std(hedging error) ∝ 1 / √NN = number of rebalances over the option's life. Hedge twice as often (N → 2N) and the typical replication error shrinks only by a factor of √2 ≈ 0.71 — the same √n law from Course 1.

This is a decisive practical fact: replication error never vanishes at finite N, and it dies slowly. The same √n that made Monte-Carlo converge frustratingly slowly (Course 1) now sets the floor on how perfectly you can manufacture an option. More gamma — a near-the-money option close to expiry — means a sharper value curve and a larger per-step error for the same rebalancing frequency.

AnalogyHedging a curved payoff with a straight line of shares is like driving around a circular track using only straight segments. With four straight segments you trace a crude square; with a hundred you hug the curve closely; with infinitely many you drive the perfect circle. Gamma is how sharp the curve is, and N is how many segments you allow yourself — but each extra segment (rebalance) costs gas (transaction costs).

8.4Transaction Costs: Why You Can't Just Hedge Faster

The 1/√N result would suggest hedging as often as physically possible. In a frictionless world you would. But every rebalance crosses a bid–ask spread and pays commissions, so each trade burns money. Hedging error and transaction cost pull in opposite directions in N, and that tension defines real-world hedging.

Suppose each round trip in the stock costs a fraction k of notional traded (half the bid–ask spread, roughly). Each rebalance trades an amount of stock proportional to how much delta moved, and the number of rebalances is N, so the total expected cost grows with the frequency even as the replication error falls:

total cost ≈ (replication error ∝ 1/√N) + (transaction cost ∝ k·√N)Two competing terms. Their sum has a minimum at an interior N* — hedge too rarely and tracking is poor; too often and fees eat you alive.

The two terms trade off, and there is an optimal rebalancing frequency N* (equivalently an optimal threshold: rebalance only when delta has drifted past some band). Higher transaction cost k pushes you to hedge less often and tolerate more replication error; higher gamma or higher volatility pushes you to hedge more often. Practitioners formalize this with "delta bands" — rebalance only when delta strays outside a no-trade zone — and the band width grows with costs. The clean Black–Scholes price is a frictionless ideal; the real cost of manufacturing an option is the model price plus an unavoidable hedging-cost premium.

Common mistakeAssuming transaction costs are a small footnote. For a frequently rebalanced book, cumulative hedging costs can rival or exceed the option's whole theoretical edge. This is precisely why a price that looks like free arbitrage on paper often isn't tradeable — the friction of running the hedge eats the profit.
✦ Worked Example — one-step P&L of a short, delta-hedged call

You sold one at-the-money call and delta-hedged it. At today's price the option has gamma Γ = 0.04 per $, the stock is S = $100, and the implied (priced-in) volatility is σ = 20% annual. Over one trading day (Δt = 1/252 year), what hedged P&L do you book if the stock (a) barely moves $0.30, or (b) jumps $2.50?

  1. What theta charged for (the "break-even" squared move): σ²S²Δt = (0.20)²·(100)²·(1/252) = 0.04·10000·0.003968 ≈ 1.587 dollars².
  2. Quiet day, ΔS = $0.30: (ΔS)² = 0.09. Step P&L ≈ ½·Γ·[(ΔS)² − σ²S²Δt] = ½·0.04·(0.09 − 1.587) = 0.02·(−1.497) ≈ +$0.030. Short gamma + calm market ⇒ you collect the time decay.
  3. Wild day, ΔS = $2.50: (ΔS)² = 6.25. Step P&L ≈ 0.02·(6.25 − 1.587) = 0.02·(4.663) ≈ −$0.093. The realized move dwarfed the implied move ⇒ short gamma bleeds.
  4. The break-even move: the day is a wash when (ΔS)² = σ²S²Δt, i.e. |ΔS| = √1.587 ≈ $1.26 — exactly one daily standard deviation (S·σ·√Δt = 100·0.20·√(1/252) ≈ 1.26). Move less than 1σ and you win; more and you lose.
  5. The lesson: the hedger never cared which direction the stock went — both ±$2.50 cost the same because P&L depends on (ΔS)². You are long/short volatility, paid via gamma, financed by theta.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate a discretely delta-hedged short call

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib — the install details block lives in the early lectures if you skipped it). We sell a call, delta-hedge it N times along a simulated stock path, and read off the leftover replication error — then watch that error shrink like 1/√N.

# delta_hedge.py — Lecture 8: discrete hedging error of a short call
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

S0, K, r, sigma, T = 100.0, 100.0, 0.0, 0.20, 1.0   # 1-yr ATM call, r=0 for clarity

def bs_call(S, K, r, sigma, tau):                    # Black-Scholes price + delta
    if tau <= 0: return max(S - K, 0.0), 1.0*(S > K)
    d1 = (np.log(S/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*tau) / (sigma*np.sqrt(tau))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(tau)
    price = S*norm.cdf(d1) - K*np.exp(-r*tau)*norm.cdf(d2)
    return price, norm.cdf(d1)                        # delta = N(d1)

def hedge_error(N, n_paths=20000, seed=0):
    rng = np.random.default_rng(seed)
    dt  = T / N
    S   = np.full(n_paths, S0)
    price0, delta = bs_call(S0, K, r, sigma, T)
    cash = price0 - delta*S                           # sold call, bought delta shares
    for i in range(1, N+1):
        z = rng.standard_normal(n_paths)              # GBM step (Week 7)
        S = S*np.exp((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*dt + sigma*np.sqrt(dt)*z)
        cash *= np.exp(r*dt)                          # cash earns r
        tau = T - i*dt
        _, new_delta = bs_call(S, K, r, sigma, tau)
        cash -= (new_delta - delta)*S                 # self-financing rebalance
        delta = new_delta
    payoff = np.maximum(S - K, 0.0)                   # pay the call buyer
    pnl = cash + delta*S - payoff                     # close book at expiry
    return pnl.std()

for N in [10, 50, 250, 1250]:
    print(f"N={N:5d}   hedging-error std = {hedge_error(N):.4f}")

The mean P&L stays near zero (the hedge is fair), but the std of the leftover error falls roughly by √5 each time N grows fivefold — the 1/√N law in action. Add a per-trade cost cost = k*np.abs(new_delta-delta)*S subtracted from cash and the picture flips: total cost now rises with N, and you can plot error-plus-cost to find the optimal N* from §8.4.

Q1. In the replicating-portfolio view, the price of an option is best described as…
A. the cost today of the self-financing stock-and-cash portfolio that reproduces its payoff
B. a forecast of the option's expected payoff at expiry
C. the strike price discounted to today
D. whatever the most recent trade printed
Show answer

A. No-arbitrage pricing equates the option to the cheapest portfolio that replicates its payoff in every state. B is wrong because replication deliberately avoids forecasting the underlying. C confuses a single cash flow with the whole payoff. D is a market quote, not a fair value.

Q2. A book is delta-hedged (delta-neutral). Which statement is correct?
A. It is completely risk-free until expiry
B. Its first-order sensitivity to the stock is cancelled, but it still has gamma exposure
C. It no longer needs rebalancing
D. It profits whenever the stock rises
Show answer

B. Delta-neutral kills only the linear (first-order) response; curvature (gamma) remains, so a large move still moves the P&L. A is the classic trap. C is wrong because delta drifts, forcing rebalancing. D is wrong — a delta-hedged book is direction-neutral, not bullish.

Q3. A trader who has sold options and delta-hedges them makes money when…
A. realized volatility exceeds the implied volatility they sold at
B. the stock trends strongly upward
C. interest rates fall
D. realized volatility is lower than the implied volatility they sold at
Show answer

D. A short-option (short-gamma) hedger's step P&L is ½Γ[σ²S²Δt − (ΔS)²]: they collect theta when the realized move is smaller than implied. A is exactly backwards (that's the long-gamma winner). B is irrelevant — P&L depends on (ΔS)², not direction. C is a secondary rates effect, not the gamma–theta bet.

Q4. You currently rebalance a delta hedge once a day. If you instead rebalance four times as often (same option, no transaction costs), the typical standard deviation of your replication error roughly…
A. drops to one-quarter
B. stays the same
C. halves
D. doubles
Show answer

C. Hedging error scales like 1/√N, so multiplying N by 4 divides the error by √4 = 2 — it halves. A would require a 1/N law (too optimistic). B ignores the benefit of more frequent hedging. D has the sign backwards.

Q5. Once realistic transaction costs are included, why is there an optimal rebalancing frequency rather than "hedge as often as possible"?
A. Because gamma becomes zero at high frequency
B. Because delta stops changing once you hedge fast enough
C. Because the exchange caps the number of trades per day
D. Because replication error falls with N but cumulative transaction cost rises with N, so their sum is minimized at an interior N*
Show answer

D. Error (∝1/√N) and cost (∝k√N) move oppositely in N, so the total has a minimum at a finite N* (or equivalently a no-trade delta band). A and B are false — gamma and delta don't vanish from hedging speed. C is not the economic reason; the trade-off is cost versus tracking.

✓ What you learned

  • Replication prices an option as the cost of a self-financing stock-and-cash portfolio that reproduces its payoff — pricing by building, not forecasting.
  • Delta (∂V/∂S) is the hedge ratio, and because delta drifts, the hedge must be rebalanced dynamically through time.
  • Discrete rebalancing leaves a gamma/theta remainder: the step P&L ≈ ½Γ[(ΔS)² − σ²S²Δt] is a bet on realized vs. implied volatility, not direction.
  • The replication error shrinks only like 1/√N in the number of rebalances — slow, and never zero at finite frequency.
  • Transaction costs (∝√N) oppose the error (∝1/√N), giving an optimal rebalancing frequency N* and real-world delta bands.
