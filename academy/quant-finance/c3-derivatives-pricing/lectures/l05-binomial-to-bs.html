Week 5 · Lecture 5

From Binomial to Black-Scholes

Slice the binomial tree into ever-finer steps and watch a jagged lattice melt into the smooth, lognormal world where the Black-Scholes price lives.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why sending the number of binomial steps n to infinity produces a sensible continuous-time price rather than nonsense.
  • Write down the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) parameters u, d, and p, and explain how each is calibrated to the time step Δt.
  • Show how the binomial distribution of the terminal price converges, via the Central Limit Theorem applied to log-returns, to a lognormal distribution.
  • Recognize the Black-Scholes call price emerging as the limit of the discounted binomial expectation under the risk-neutral measure.
  • Internalize why σ√t — volatility times the square root of time — is the single number that sets the scale of all uncertainty.

5.1Refining the Tree: the Continuous-Time Limit

In Lecture 4 you priced an option on a multi-period binomial tree: at each step the stock moves up by a factor u or down by a factor d, and you discounted the risk-neutral expected payoff back to today. That lattice is honest but coarse — a real stock does not jump once a month, it ticks thousands of times a day. The natural fix is to refine: chop the option's life T into n steps of length Δt = T/n, and let n grow without bound.

The danger in any limit is that the answer blows up or collapses to something trivial. It does neither here, and the reason is a careful piece of bookkeeping: as the steps get shorter, the up and down moves must get smaller too, scaled so that the total amount of randomness over the fixed horizon T stays constant. Get that scaling right and the price converges to a finite, smooth limit — the Black-Scholes price.

Δt = T / n,   n → ∞   ⟹    lattice price ⟶ Black-Scholes priceThe tree never disappears; it densifies into a continuum. The discrete model and the continuous model are the same model viewed at two resolutions.

This matters in practice because both pictures are useful. The continuous Black-Scholes formula is instant to evaluate and gives clean Greeks; the tree is what you actually reach for when an option can be exercised early (American style) or has path-dependent features the formula cannot handle. Knowing they agree in the limit is what lets a desk trust a fast tree as a stand-in for the "true" continuous price.

AnalogyThink of a photograph zoomed in until you see square pixels. The binomial tree is the pixel grid; Black-Scholes is the smooth image it represents. Add more, smaller pixels (steps) and the staircase edges vanish into a curve. You were always looking at the same picture — just at different resolutions.

5.2The Cox-Ross-Rubinstein Parameters

How do we shrink u and d "just right"? The standard recipe is the Cox-Ross-Rubinstein (CRR) calibration. Over one step of length Δt, a stock with annualized volatility σ should accumulate a random log-return whose standard deviation is σ√Δt — that is the √t scaling promised by the Central Limit Theorem in Course 1. CRR chooses the up- and down-factors so that exactly this much variability is injected per step:

u = eσ√Δt,   d = e−σ√Δt = 1/uAn up-move and a down-move are mirror images on the log scale, so the tree recombines: up-then-down lands you back where you started.

The probability of an up-move is not a real-world guess about whether the stock rises. It is the risk-neutral probability p from Lecture 3 — the unique value that makes the discounted stock a fair game, so that today's price equals the discounted expected future price at the risk-free rate r:

p = (erΔt − d) / (u − d)Choose p this way and the tree is arbitrage-free at every node — the engine behind all of derivatives pricing.

Three things are worth noting. First, u and d depend only on σ and Δt — not on the drift of the stock. Second, as Δt → 0 both u → 1 and d → 1, so each individual move becomes tiny. Third, p → 1/2 in the limit, but ever so slightly tilted by r; that tiny tilt is exactly what carries the risk-free growth into the final answer.

Common mistakeThe risk-neutral probability p is a pricing device, not a forecast. Students often plug in their own estimate of how likely the stock is to rise. Don't. The whole point of risk-neutral valuation is that the real-world probability cancels out of the hedged price — only σ, r, and Δt survive. Using a "true" up-probability reintroduces arbitrage and gives the wrong price.

5.3From Binomial to Lognormal

Now follow the terminal price. After n steps with j up-moves (and n − j down-moves), the stock lands at:

ST = S0 · uj · dn−j,   j ~ Binomial(n, p)

Take logarithms and the multiplicative structure becomes additive. Because d = 1/u, each step contributes ±σ√Δt to the log-price, and the total log-return is a sum of n independent ± moves:

ln(ST/S0) = (2j − n) · σ√Δt = sum of n independent ±σ√Δt steps

This is exactly the setting of the Central Limit Theorem. A sum of many independent, identically scaled increments converges to a normal distribution. So the log of the terminal price becomes normal — which means the terminal price itself is lognormal (its logarithm is normal). Working out the mean and variance of that limiting normal gives the celebrated result:

ln(ST/S0) ⟶ Normal( (r − ½σ²)T,  σ²T )Mean grows like T, variance grows like σ²T, so the standard deviation grows like σ√T. The −½σ² is the variance-drag correction that keeps the average price equal to S₀e^{rT}.

This is the headline of the whole lecture: the clumsy binomial counting of up-moves smooths into a clean lognormal law for the stock price. Lognormal is the right shape because prices can't go negative (a log is always defined) and because returns compound multiplicatively. Every continuous-time pricing model in this specialization rests on this one limiting distribution.

Common mistakeThe terminal price is lognormal, but the log-return is normal — don't swap them. And the drift of the log is (r − ½σ²)T, not rT. Forgetting the −½σ² "variance drag" makes the average return look too high and systematically misprices long-dated options.

5.4The Black-Scholes Price Emerges & the Role of σ√t

Pricing is now mechanical. The value of a European call is the discounted risk-neutral expectation of its payoff max(ST − K, 0). On the tree that is a finite sum over the n+1 terminal nodes weighted by binomial probabilities. Replace the binomial law with its lognormal limit and the sum becomes an integral — and that integral evaluates in closed form to the Black-Scholes formula:

C = S0 N(d1) − K e−rT N(d2)N is the standard-normal CDF. The discrete binomial sum literally converges to this expression term by term as n → ∞.
d1 = [ ln(S0/K) + (r + ½σ²)T ] / (σ√T),   d2 = d1 − σ√T

Look closely and you'll see the same character in every term: σ√T. It is the denominator of d1 and d2, it is the gap between them, and it is the standard deviation of the log-price from §5.3. This is the master scale of the whole theory.

Why σ√t is everything

Uncertainty does not grow with time t — it grows with √t. A position held four times as long is only twice as uncertain. Volatility σ sets the per-unit-time rate; multiply by √t and you get the total standard deviation of the log-return over the horizon. Traders quote a one-day move and scale it to a year by multiplying by √252. An option's entire value is a function of how its strike sits relative to this σ√T-wide cone of plausible prices.

total uncertainty over horizon t = σ · √tDouble the horizon and uncertainty grows by only √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2. This square-root-of-time law is why the binomial limit is stable.
AnalogyPicture a drop of ink in still water. After 4 seconds the blot is only twice as wide as after 1 second, not four times — diffusion spreads as √t. A stock price diffuses the same way: σ√t is the radius of the cloud of where it might be. Black-Scholes simply prices the option against the edge of that growing cloud.
✦ Worked Example — a 2-step CRR tree closing in on Black-Scholes

Price a 1-year European call. S₀ = 100, K = 100, r = 5% (continuous), σ = 20%. First build a 2-step CRR tree, then compare to the Black-Scholes limit.

  1. Step size: n = 2, so Δt = 0.5. Then σ√Δt = 0.20·√0.5 = 0.20·0.7071 = 0.1414.
  2. CRR factors: u = e0.1414 = 1.1519, d = 1/u = 0.8681.
  3. Risk-neutral p: erΔt = e0.025 = 1.02532. p = (1.02532 − 0.8681)/(1.1519 − 0.8681) = 0.15722/0.28384 = 0.5539.
  4. Terminal prices & payoffs: uu: 100·1.1519² = 132.69 → payoff 32.69. ud (=du, recombined): 100 → payoff 0. dd: 100·0.8681² = 75.36 → payoff 0.
  5. Node probabilities: P(uu) = p² = 0.3068; P(ud) = 2p(1−p) = 0.4942; P(dd) = (1−p)² = 0.1990. Only the uu node pays.
  6. Discount the expectation: C = e−rT · [0.3068·32.69] = e−0.05 · 10.029 = 0.9512 · 10.029 = $9.54.
  7. Compare to Black-Scholes: with d₁ = (ln1 + (0.05+0.02)·1)/(0.20) = 0.35, d₂ = 0.15, N(d₁)=0.6368, N(d₂)=0.5596: C = 100·0.6368 − 100·0.9512·0.5596 = 63.68 − 53.23 = $10.45.
  8. Read the gap: a 2-step tree gives $9.54 versus the exact $10.45 — already the right ballpark. Push n to 50 or 500 and the tree marches monotonically up toward $10.45, demonstrating the convergence of §5.1 in hard numbers.

🛠️ Hands-On: watch the tree converge to Black-Scholes

Reusing the Week-1 stack (NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib + Jupyter — the install block lives in Course 1, Lecture 1). We'll price the worked example with a CRR tree for growing n and plot the price closing in on the closed-form Black-Scholes value.

# crr_to_bs.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

S0, K, r, sigma, T = 100.0, 100.0, 0.05, 0.20, 1.0

def bs_call(S0, K, r, sigma, T):
    d1 = (np.log(S0/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(T)
    return S0*norm.cdf(d1) - K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2)

def crr_call(S0, K, r, sigma, T, n):
    dt = T / n
    u  = np.exp(sigma*np.sqrt(dt))          # CRR up-factor
    d  = 1.0 / u                            # recombining: d = 1/u
    p  = (np.exp(r*dt) - d) / (u - d)       # risk-neutral prob
    j  = np.arange(n + 1)                    # number of up-moves
    ST = S0 * u**j * d**(n - j)              # terminal prices
    payoff = np.maximum(ST - K, 0.0)
    # binomial weights C(n,j) p^j (1-p)^(n-j)
    from scipy.stats import binom
    w = binom.pmf(j, n, p)
    return np.exp(-r*T) * np.sum(w * payoff)

print("Black-Scholes price:", round(bs_call(S0, K, r, sigma, T), 4))
for n in [2, 10, 50, 200, 1000]:
    print(f"CRR n={n:>4}:", round(crr_call(S0, K, r, sigma, T, n), 4))

You'll see n=2 give ≈ 9.54 (matching the worked example) and the price oscillate-then-converge to the Black-Scholes ≈ 10.4506 as n climbs. The wobble is the well-known binomial "sawtooth": whether the strike lands on a node flips with n, which is why production code often averages consecutive n or uses smoothing. The takeaway is unmistakable — the discrete tree is Black-Scholes in disguise.

❓ Lecture 5 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the CRR calibration, as the number of steps n → ∞ (so Δt → 0), the up-factor u = eσ√Δt behaves how?
A. It grows without bound.
B. It shrinks toward 1, because the per-step move σ√Δt → 0.
C. It stays fixed regardless of Δt.
D. It goes to zero.
Show answer

B. As Δt → 0, σ√Δt → 0 and e0 = 1, so each move becomes vanishingly small — exactly what makes the lattice densify into a continuum. It does not blow up (A) or vanish (D), and it plainly depends on Δt, ruling out C.

Q2. The risk-neutral up-probability p = (erΔt − d)/(u − d) is chosen so that…
A. it matches the trader's forecast of the stock rising.
B. the variance of returns is maximized.
C. p always equals exactly 0.5.
D. the discounted stock price is a fair game (no arbitrage).
Show answer

D. p is the measure under which today's price equals the discounted expected future price at the risk-free rate, making the tree arbitrage-free. It is deliberately not a real-world forecast (A). It is not about maximizing variance (B), and while p → 1/2 in the limit it is slightly tilted by r, so it is not exactly 0.5 (C).

Q3. As n → ∞, the distribution of the terminal stock price ST converges to which shape, and why?
A. Normal, because prices add up.
B. Lognormal, because the log-return is a sum of many independent steps and the CLT makes that sum normal.
C. Uniform, because each node is equally likely.
D. Poisson, because moves are rare events.
Show answer

B. Each step adds ±σ√Δt to the log-price; the CLT makes that sum normal, so the price (its exponential) is lognormal. The price itself isn't normal — it can't go negative (A). Nodes aren't equally weighted (C), and there are no rare-jump dynamics here (D).

Q4. The standard deviation of the log-return over a horizon T is σ√T. If you double the horizon from T to 2T, the total uncertainty grows by a factor of…
A. 2
B. 4
C. √2 ≈ 1.41
D. it stays the same
Show answer

C. Uncertainty scales with √t, so σ√(2T) = √2 · σ√T — a factor of about 1.41. Doubling (A) would be the wrong linear-in-time assumption; 4× (B) is the variance ratio, not the standard-deviation ratio; and it certainly does change, ruling out D.

Q5. In the Black-Scholes limit, the mean of the log-return ln(ST/S₀) under the risk-neutral measure is…
A. (r − ½σ²)T, because of the variance-drag correction.
B. rT exactly, with no correction.
C. σ²T.
D. zero, since risk-neutral returns have no drift.
Show answer

A. The log-return drift is (r − ½σ²)T; the −½σ² term keeps E[ST] = S₀erT despite Jensen's gap between the mean of a log and the log of a mean. rT (B) ignores that drag, σ²T (C) is the variance not the mean, and the drift is not zero (D).

✓ What you learned

  • Refining the binomial tree (Δt = T/n, n → ∞) produces a finite, smooth continuous-time limit — the Black-Scholes price.
  • The CRR parameters u = eσ√Δt, d = 1/u, and risk-neutral p = (erΔt−d)/(u−d) inject exactly σ√Δt of randomness per step, independent of the real drift.
  • The terminal price is the product of independent moves, so its log is a CLT sum → the price converges to a lognormal with log-drift (r − ½σ²)T.
  • The discounted risk-neutral expectation on the tree converges term-by-term to the Black-Scholes formula C = S₀N(d₁) − Ke−rTN(d₂).
  • σ√t is the master scale: uncertainty grows with the square root of time, and it sits in d₁, d₂, and the whole pricing cone.
