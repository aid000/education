One-Period Binomial & Risk-Neutral Pricing

Squeeze the future into two outcomes and a startling truth falls out: an option's price has nothing to do with how likely the stock is to rise.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up the one-period binomial model with up/down factors u and d over a single time step Δt.
  • Build a replicating portfolio — Δ shares plus a bond — that exactly matches an option's two payoffs.
  • Solve for the hedge ratio Δ and price the option by no-arbitrage as the cost of that portfolio.
  • Derive the risk-neutral probability q = (erΔt − d)/(u − d) and the pricing formula C = e−rΔt[qCu + (1−q)Cd].
  • Explain the headline result: the real-world probability of an up-move never enters the price.

3.1The One-Period Binomial World

To price a derivative we need a model of how the underlying can move. The simplest honest model says: over one time step Δt the stock can do exactly two things — go up by a factor u or down by a factor d. Crude as it is, this two-outcome world contains the entire logic of modern option pricing in its purest form. Master it and the rest of the course is repetition at larger scale.

Let the stock start at price S. After one step it is either S·u (the up state) or S·d (the down state), with u > d. Alongside the stock there is a risk-free bank account paying continuously compounded rate r; one dollar grows to erΔt over the step. A European derivative pays Cu in the up state and Cd in the down state — for a call with strike K, Cu = max(Su − K, 0) and Cd = max(Sd − K, 0).

Up state: S → Su, derivative pays Cu
Down state: S → Sd, derivative pays Cd
Bond: 1 → erΔt in both statesTwo risky outcomes, one riskless growth factor. The question: what is the derivative worth today?

For the model to forbid free money, the bond's growth must sit strictly between the two stock outcomes: d < erΔt < u. If the stock beat the bond in both states you would borrow and buy it for a sure profit; if it lost in both you would short it. That sandwich condition is the no-arbitrage requirement from Week 2, and it is exactly what makes the pricing below work.

3.2The Replicating Portfolio

Here is the trick that unlocks everything. Instead of guessing the option's value, we build a copy of it from things whose prices we already know — the stock and the bond. If a portfolio pays exactly what the option pays in every future state, then by no-arbitrage it must cost exactly what the option costs today. Otherwise you could buy the cheaper one, sell the dearer, and pocket a riskless difference.

So consider a portfolio held today of Δ shares of the stock and B dollars in the bank account. After one step it is worth:

Up: Δ·Su + B·erΔt = Cu
Down: Δ·Sd + B·erΔt = CdTwo unknowns (Δ, B), two equations — one per future state. We demand the portfolio match the option in both.

This is just two linear equations. Subtract the second from the first: the bond term cancels (it grows the same in both states), leaving Δ·S(u − d) = Cu − Cd. Solve:

Δ = (Cu − Cd) / (Su − Sd)The hedge ratio: how many shares mimic the option's exposure. It is the "spread of payoffs" divided by the "spread of stock prices."

That Δ is the single most important number a derivatives desk computes. It is the option's sensitivity to the stock — the delta you will meet again as a calculus derivative in the Black–Scholes lecture — and it is literally how many shares a trader buys to hedge each option sold. Plug Δ back into either equation to get B, the borrowing or lending that completes the copy.

AnalogyReplication is mixing a paint color from primaries. You can't buy "option-green" directly, but you can blend a measured amount of stock-yellow with bond-blue until the result is indistinguishable from option-green in every light (every future state). Once the blend matches perfectly, its cost is the option's fair price — no opinion about the future required.
Common mistakeΔ is not a probability and is not bounded to [0, 1] in general (for a short or a put it is negative). It counts shares, set purely by the ratio of payoff-spread to price-spread. Confusing the hedge ratio with the up-probability is one of the most common beginner errors — they are different objects that happen to both appear in this model.

3.3Pricing & the Risk-Neutral Probability

The option's price today must equal the cost of the replicating portfolio: C = Δ·S + B. Substitute the solved Δ and B and grind through the algebra, and the messy expression collapses into something beautiful:

C = e−rΔt [ q·Cu + (1 − q)·Cd ],   where  q = (erΔt − d) / (u − d)Discount the average of the two payoffs — but weight them with q, not the true probability.

Read this slowly, because it is the central formula of the course. The price is a discounted expected payoff: take a weighted average of Cu and Cd, then pull it back to today with the discount factor e−rΔt. The weight q is called the risk-neutral probability (or pseudo-probability). Because d < erΔt < u under no-arbitrage, q lands strictly between 0 and 1 — it behaves like a probability, even though it is manufactured purely from u, d, r, and Δt.

Why "risk-neutral"? Notice what q does to the stock itself. Under q, the expected one-step stock price is q·Su + (1−q)·Sd = S·erΔt — the stock is expected to grow at exactly the risk-free rate, as if no one demanded extra return for bearing risk. In the real world investors do demand a premium, so the true up-probability is generally higher. q is the artificial measure under which every asset drifts at r, and under which discounted prices are "fair bets."

3.4The Punchline: Real Probabilities Vanish

Look hard at the pricing formula and notice what is missing: the real-world probability that the stock goes up — call it p — appears nowhere. Two traders who violently disagree about whether the stock will rise (one thinks p = 0.9, the other p = 0.1) must nonetheless quote the same option price, or one of them is offering free money to the other. This is the most counterintuitive idea in derivatives, and it is worth pausing on.

How can the price ignore the very thing everyone argues about? Because we are not forecasting the option — we are manufacturing it from the stock and the bond. The replicating portfolio reproduces the option's payoff in both states no matter how the dice fall, so its cost depends only on today's stock price and the geometry of the move (u, d, r), not on the odds. The real-world drift is already baked into the stock price you can see on the screen; pricing the option relative to that observable price needs no further opinion.

price depends on:  S, u, d, r, Δt, payoff
price does NOT depend on:  p (the real up-probability)Relative pricing: the derivative is valued against the traded underlying, not forecast from scratch.

This is why q is a pseudo-probability, not a belief. It is the unique weighting that makes the no-arbitrage math close. The deep theorem behind it — the First Fundamental Theorem of Asset Pricing — says "no arbitrage" is equivalent to "there exists a risk-neutral measure under which discounted prices are fair." Every pricing engine you build later, from binomial trees to Monte-Carlo to Black–Scholes, is computing an expectation under this measure, never under real-world probabilities.

Common mistakeDo not conclude "real probabilities are useless." They are essential for risk management — your expected profit, value-at-risk, and the actual chance the option finishes in the money all live under the real measure p. Risk-neutral pricing answers "what is the fair price and hedge?"; real-world probability answers "what will probably happen to my P&L?" Different questions, different measures — never mix them in one calculation.
✦ Worked Example — pricing a one-period call

A stock trades at S = $100. Over one step it either rises to $120 (u = 1.2) or falls to $90 (d = 0.9). The risk-free rate gives a step growth of erΔt = 1.05. Price a European call with strike K = $105 by (a) replication and (b) the risk-neutral formula, and confirm they agree.

  1. Payoffs: Up state Su = 120 ⟹ Cu = max(120 − 105, 0) = 15. Down state Sd = 90 ⟹ Cd = max(90 − 105, 0) = 0.
  2. Check no-arbitrage: d = 0.9 < 1.05 < 1.2 = u. ✓ The bond's growth is sandwiched, so a fair price exists.
  3. Hedge ratio: Δ = (Cu − Cd)/(Su − Sd) = (15 − 0)/(120 − 90) = 15/30 = 0.5 shares per call.
  4. Solve the bond from the down equation: Δ·Sd + B·1.05 = Cd ⟹ 0.5·90 + 1.05B = 0 ⟹ 45 + 1.05B = 0 ⟹ B = −45/1.05 ≈ −42.857 (borrow $42.86).
  5. Price by replication: C = Δ·S + B = 0.5·100 − 42.857 = 50 − 42.857 = $7.14.
  6. Risk-neutral probability: q = (erΔt − d)/(u − d) = (1.05 − 0.9)/(1.2 − 0.9) = 0.15/0.30 = 0.5.
  7. Price by formula: C = e−rΔt[q·Cu + (1−q)·Cd] = (1/1.05)[0.5·15 + 0.5·0] = 7.5/1.05 = $7.14. ✓ Identical.
  8. The punchline in action: nothing above used the real chance of an up-move. Even if up-moves are 80% likely, the fair price is still $7.14 — because the call is built from half a share minus a loan, not forecast.

🛠️ Hands-On: a one-period binomial pricer

Using the same free NumPy stack from Week 1's setup (the one-time install block lives there). We'll code the formula once and — crucially — prove the price ignores the real probability by re-pricing under several made-up values of p.

# binomial_one_period.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

S, K   = 100.0, 105.0       # spot and strike
u, d   = 1.2, 0.9           # up / down factors
growth = 1.05               # e^(r*dt) over one step

Cu = max(S*u - K, 0.0)      # 15.0
Cd = max(S*d - K, 0.0)      # 0.0

# --- replication ---
delta = (Cu - Cd) / (S*u - S*d)         # 0.5 shares
B     = (Cd - delta*S*d) / growth       # bond position, -42.857
price_replicate = delta*S + B

# --- risk-neutral formula ---
q = (growth - d) / (u - d)              # 0.5
price_rn = (q*Cu + (1 - q)*Cd) / growth

print(f"delta           = {delta:.4f}")
print(f"price (replicate)= {price_replicate:.4f}")
print(f"price (risk-neut)= {price_rn:.4f}")   # same number: 7.1429

# --- the punchline: real-world p does NOT change the price ---
for p in [0.1, 0.5, 0.8]:
    real_world_expected = (p*Cu + (1-p)*Cd) / growth   # the WRONG way
    print(f"p={p}:  real-world disc. E[payoff]={real_world_expected:.4f}"
          f"   fair price still={price_rn:.4f}")

Both methods print 7.1429 — replication and the risk-neutral formula are the same calculation wearing two hats. The loop drives the lesson home: the naïve "discount the real-world expected payoff" answer swings with p, but the arbitrage-free price never moves. Change u, d, or growth and watch q and the price respond; change p and watch the fair price sit perfectly still.

🔗 Where this is heading

This single fork is the seed of everything ahead. Next week we glue many one-period steps into a multi-period binomial tree, pricing American and path-dependent options by working backward node-by-node — each node is exactly the calculation you just learned. Let the steps shrink and grow numerous and the tree converges to Black–Scholes (later in C3): the hedge ratio Δ becomes the calculus derivative ∂C/∂S, and the discounted risk-neutral expectation becomes an integral against a lognormal density. The same risk-neutral measure powers the Monte-Carlo pricers of the simulation course — there you'll sample paths under q and average discounted payoffs, the continuous cousin of q·Cu + (1−q)·Cd.

Prompt: Two analysts look at the same stock. Ana is convinced it will rise (she'd put the real-world up-probability at 80%); Ben thinks it's a coin flip (50%). They are asked to quote the fair price of the same one-period European call. The risk-neutral formula says they must quote the identical price. Does this mean their opinions about the stock are worthless? Show a model response

Their opinions are not worthless — they're simply answering a different question than the pricing formula asks. Ana and Ben must quote the same fair price (about $7.14 in our example) because the call can be replicated: half a share funded partly by borrowing reproduces the call's payoff in both future states regardless of whether the stock actually rises. Since the copy costs the same to assemble for both analysts, no-arbitrage forces a single price. If Ana quoted higher because she's bullish, Ben could sell her the call, build the cheaper replicating portfolio, and lock in a riskless profit no matter what the stock does. The bullishness leaks out instantly.

So where do their views matter? In three places, all outside the pricing step: (1) Position-taking — Ana, believing p = 0.8, expects positive P&L from simply buying the fairly-priced call; her edge is in the trade, not the price. (2) Risk management — the true probability governs value-at-risk, expected shortfall, and the real chance of finishing in the money, all of which live under the real measure p, never under q. (3) Capital and sizing — how much to bet (think Kelly) depends on real odds. The clean way to explain it to a newcomer: pricing answers "what does it cost to manufacture this payoff?" — a question of carpentry, not prophecy — while forecasting answers "will I make money holding it?" The model isn't blind to opinion; it just refuses to let opinion contaminate a price that arbitrage has already pinned down.

Q1. In the one-period binomial model, the replicating portfolio is built from…
A. two different options on the same stock
B. Δ shares of the stock plus a position in the risk-free bond
C. the real-world probability p and the strike K
D. a forecast of next period's price
Show answer

B. The whole method copies the option from traded, priced instruments: Δ shares of stock and B dollars in the bond. (A) uses other derivatives we haven't priced; (C) and (D) bring in forecasts, but the entire point is that no forecast is needed.

Q2. A stock at S = 100 moves to Su = 120 or Sd = 80. A call pays Cu = 20 and Cd = 0. The hedge ratio Δ is…
A. 0.50
B. 0.25
C. 1.00
D. 0.20
Show answer

A. Δ = (Cu − Cd)/(Su − Sd) = (20 − 0)/(120 − 80) = 20/40 = 0.5. Answer D (0.20) wrongly divides by S instead of the price spread; B and C mis-handle the numerator or denominator.

Q3. With u = 1.25, d = 0.80, and a one-step growth erΔt = 1.04, the risk-neutral probability q is…
A. 0.80
B. 0.50
C. about 0.533
D. impossible — this set allows arbitrage
Show answer

C. q = (1.04 − 0.80)/(1.25 − 0.80) = 0.24/0.45 ≈ 0.533. Since 0.80 < 1.04 < 1.25, the no-arbitrage sandwich holds and q is a valid probability in (0,1), so D is wrong; A and B don't satisfy the formula.

Q4. Why does the real-world probability of an up-move not appear in the option's price?
A. Because up and down moves are always equally likely
B. Because the option is replicated from the stock and bond, so its price is fixed by no-arbitrage regardless of the odds
C. Because the real probability is too hard to estimate
D. Because options are always priced at zero in this model
Show answer

B. The replicating portfolio matches the payoff in every state, so arbitrage pins the price to the cost of that copy — independent of p. (A) is false (q need not equal p or 0.5); (C) is irrelevant — even with a perfect estimate p still wouldn't enter; (D) is nonsense.

Q5. Under the risk-neutral measure, the expected one-step return on the stock equals…
A. the up-factor u
B. whatever the real-world drift is
C. zero
D. the risk-free rate r
Show answer

D. By construction q makes q·Su + (1−q)·Sd = S·erΔt, i.e. the stock is expected to grow at the risk-free rate — the defining feature of the "risk-neutral" world. (B) is the real measure; (A) and (C) are not what the formula yields.
