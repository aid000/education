Week 7 · Lecture 7

The Greeks

An option's price is a surface; the Greeks are the slopes that tell a trader exactly which way — and how fast — that surface is about to move under their feet.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define each Greek as a partial derivative of the option price and explain why traders measure sensitivity rather than re-pricing.
  • State delta and read it as both a slope and the hedge ratio (the shares per option that flatten risk).
  • Explain gamma as the curvature of the price and the reason a delta hedge must be rebalanced.
  • Describe vega, theta, and rho, give their signs for a long option, and articulate the theta–gamma tradeoff.
  • Compute all five Greeks for a Black–Scholes call in SciPy and verify them against a finite-difference bump.

7.1Why Sensitivities, Not Just Prices

Last week the Black–Scholes formula gave us a single number: the fair value of an option for a given spot price S, strike K, volatility σ, time-to-expiry T, and rate r. But a trader who holds that option does not live in a frozen world. The spot moves every second, implied volatility drifts, the clock ticks, rates shift. The pressing question is not "what is the option worth now?" but "how will its value change when the world changes?"

That is a question about derivatives — in the calculus sense. The price V is a function V(S, σ, T, r), and each Greek is one of its partial derivatives: the first-order rate of change of value with respect to one input, holding the others fixed. Collectively they form a local linear-plus-quadratic map of the price surface, so a desk can re-state its risk in a handful of numbers instead of re-running a pricer for every conceivable scenario.

V = V(S, σ, T, r)The Greeks are the partial derivatives of this one function — each isolates the effect of moving a single input.

The five we study are named (mostly) after Greek letters: delta (∂V/∂S), gamma (∂²V/∂S²), vega (∂V/∂σ), theta (∂V/∂t), and rho (∂V/∂r). A first-order Taylor expansion of the price around today's market stitches them together:

ΔV ≈ Δ·ΔS + ½·Γ·(ΔS)² + 𝒱·Δσ + Θ·Δt + ρ·ΔrDelta and gamma are the first two terms in S; vega, theta, and rho cover volatility, time, and rates. Hedging means zeroing the terms you don't want exposure to.
AnalogyThink of the option value as the altitude of a hiker on a hillside. Delta is the steepness directly under your feet — step east and you know roughly how much you climb. Gamma is how quickly that steepness itself changes as you walk, so a flat-looking slope can suddenly turn into a cliff. The Greeks are the contour lines that let you navigate without re-surveying the whole mountain at every step.

7.2Delta — Slope and Hedge Ratio

Delta (Δ) is the first derivative of the option price with respect to the underlying spot: how many dollars the option gains when the stock rises by one dollar. For a Black–Scholes call and put it has a clean closed form:

Δcall = N(d1),   Δput = N(d1) − 1N is the standard-normal CDF; d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + ½σ²)T] / (σ√T) from Week 6.

Because N maps to (0, 1), a call's delta lives in [0, 1] and a put's in [−1, 0]. A deep in-the-money call moves almost dollar-for-dollar with the stock (Δ ≈ 1, it behaves like the share itself); a far out-of-the-money call barely reacts (Δ ≈ 0). Right at the money a call's delta sits near 0.5 — a coin-flip's worth of stock exposure.

The second reading of delta is the one that pays the rent: it is the hedge ratio. If you are short one call with delta 0.6, you can neutralize your spot exposure by buying 0.6 shares. Then a small move in S changes the option leg and the share leg by equal and opposite amounts, and your combined position is — to first order — immune to where the stock goes. This is the seed of delta hedging, the entire subject of next week.

Position delta = 0  ⟹  shares to hold = −(option delta) × (number of options)A delta-neutral book has no first-order exposure to the underlying's direction.
Common mistakeDelta is a local slope, valid only for a small move. People treat "Δ = 0.6" as if the option will always track 60% of the stock — but delta itself changes as S moves (that change is gamma, §7.3). A position that is delta-neutral right now drifts out of neutrality the instant the spot moves, which is precisely why hedges must be rebalanced.

7.3Gamma — Convexity and the Cost of Rebalancing

Gamma (Γ) is the second derivative of price with respect to spot — equivalently, the rate at which delta itself changes as the stock moves. It is the curvature of the option's value as a function of S. For both Black–Scholes calls and puts it is identical and always positive for a long option:

Γ = ∂Δ/∂S = ∂²V/∂S² = N′(d1) / (S·σ·√T)N′ is the standard-normal density. Gamma is largest at the money and near expiry, and decays for deep ITM/OTM options.

Positive gamma is the option buyer's friend. Because the value curve bends upward, a long option gains delta as the stock rises and loses delta as it falls — you are automatically longer when you're winning and shorter when you're losing. Delta-hedge such a position and you end up systematically "buying low and selling high" in your rebalancing trades, banking a small profit from every wiggle. That profit is the payoff for being long convexity.

Gamma is concentrated where the option is most uncertain about finishing in or out of the money: near the strike and close to expiry. A far-dated, deep-ITM option is nearly linear in S (gamma ≈ 0); an at-the-money option the afternoon before expiry has explosive gamma, its delta flipping between 0 and 1 on tiny moves.

AnalogyDelta is your car's speed; gamma is the accelerator. A high-gamma position is a twitchy sports car — touch the spot and your delta lurches, so you must steer (rebalance) constantly. A low-gamma position is a heavy truck that holds its line. Market-makers who are short gamma feel like they're driving that sports car downhill in the rain: every move forces them to trade in the wrong direction.

7.4Vega, Theta, Rho — and the Theta–Gamma Tradeoff

The remaining Greeks measure exposure to the other inputs. Vega (𝒱, not actually a Greek letter) is the sensitivity to volatility: how much value the option gains per one-point rise in σ. It is always positive for a long option — more volatility means a fatter chance of a big favorable move, and the option holder keeps only the upside.

𝒱 = ∂V/∂σ = S·N′(d1)·√T    (same for calls and puts)Vega peaks at the money and grows with time-to-expiry — long-dated options are the big volatility bets.

Theta (Θ) is the sensitivity to the passage of time: the value lost per day as expiry approaches, the famous "time decay." For a long option theta is typically negative — every day that passes with the stock going nowhere bleeds away the option's remaining time value. Rho (ρ) is the sensitivity to the interest rate; it is positive for calls, negative for puts, and is usually the smallest of the five for short-dated equity options.

Θ ≈ ∂V/∂t < 0 (long option),   ρcall = K·T·e−rT·N(d2) > 0Convention: theta is usually quoted per calendar day and as the decay (a negative number) for a long position.

The deepest relationship in this lecture is the theta–gamma tradeoff. They are two sides of one coin. Long gamma — the convexity that lets you scalp profits from every wiggle — must be paid for, and the rent is theta. Under Black–Scholes the connection is exact: for a delta-hedged book, the per-unit-time decay is balanced against the convexity gain at the realized variance:

Θ + ½·σ²·S²·Γ + r·S·Δ − r·V = 0The Black–Scholes PDE. For a delta-neutral position (Δ ≈ 0) it says Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ: positive gamma demands negative theta.

Read it plainly: if you are long gamma you are short theta, and you only come out ahead if the stock actually moves enough — if realized volatility beats the implied volatility you paid for. That single inequality is the engine of volatility trading.

Common mistakeDon't confuse vega with gamma — both rise with "more movement," but they answer different questions. Vega is exposure to a change in the implied volatility you're quoted (a re-pricing of the option), while gamma pays off from the realized volatility the stock actually delivers (a hedging P&L). You can be long one and short the other.

✦ Worked Example — Greeks of an at-the-money call

A 3-month call on a non-dividend stock: S = 100, K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 0.25. Find d1, d2, delta, gamma, and vega, then read off the hedge.

  1. Compute d1: d1 = [ln(100/100) + (0.05 + ½·0.20²)·0.25] / (0.20·√0.25) = [0 + (0.07)(0.25)] / (0.20·0.5) = 0.0175 / 0.10 = 0.175.
  2. And d2: d2 = d1 − σ√T = 0.175 − 0.10 = 0.075.
  3. Delta: Δ = N(0.175) ≈ 0.569. The call gains about 57¢ per $1 rise in the stock — slightly above 0.5 because the drift (r) tilts the odds toward finishing ITM.
  4. Gamma: N′(0.175) = (1/√2π)e−0.175²/2 ≈ 0.3925. Γ = 0.3925 / (100·0.20·0.5) = 0.3925 / 10 = 0.0393 per $1. So a $1 move changes delta by about 0.039.
  5. Vega: 𝒱 = S·N′(d1)·√T = 100·0.3925·0.5 = 19.6 per 1.00 (100-point) change in σ — i.e. about 0.196 per 1% (0.01) change in volatility.
  6. The hedge: short one call ⟹ buy 0.569 shares to be delta-neutral now. But with Γ = 0.039, a $5 rally pushes delta to ≈ 0.569 + 5·0.039 ≈ 0.76, so you'd need to buy ~0.19 more shares to stay neutral — gamma in action.

🛠️ Hands-On: Greeks in SciPy, checked by bumping

Reusing the Week-1/2 stack (NumPy + SciPy + Jupyter — the install block lives in C1 Lecture 1). We code the closed-form Greeks, then confirm delta and gamma with a finite-difference "bump" — the same sanity check a desk runs against its analytic library.

# greeks.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def d1_d2(S, K, r, sigma, T):
    d1 = (np.log(S/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
    return d1, d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(T)

def bs_call(S, K, r, sigma, T):
    d1, d2 = d1_d2(S, K, r, sigma, T)
    return S*norm.cdf(d1) - K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2)

def greeks_call(S, K, r, sigma, T):
    d1, d2 = d1_d2(S, K, r, sigma, T)
    delta = norm.cdf(d1)
    gamma = norm.pdf(d1) / (S*sigma*np.sqrt(T))
    vega  = S*norm.pdf(d1)*np.sqrt(T)                 # per 1.00 of sigma
    theta = (-S*norm.pdf(d1)*sigma/(2*np.sqrt(T))
             - r*K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2))          # per year
    rho   = K*T*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2)
    return delta, gamma, vega, theta, rho

S, K, r, sigma, T = 100, 100, 0.05, 0.20, 0.25
delta, gamma, vega, theta, rho = greeks_call(S, K, r, sigma, T)
print(f"delta {delta:.4f}  gamma {gamma:.4f}  vega {vega:.3f}")
print(f"theta/yr {theta:.3f}  (per day {theta/365:.4f})  rho {rho:.3f}")

# --- verify delta & gamma by bumping the spot ---
h = 1e-3
delta_fd = (bs_call(S+h,K,r,sigma,T) - bs_call(S-h,K,r,sigma,T)) / (2*h)
gamma_fd = (bs_call(S+h,K,r,sigma,T) - 2*bs_call(S,K,r,sigma,T)
            + bs_call(S-h,K,r,sigma,T)) / h**2
print(f"delta (bump) {delta_fd:.4f}   gamma (bump) {gamma_fd:.4f}")

You should see delta ≈ 0.5692, gamma ≈ 0.0393, vega ≈ 19.6, and the bumped values matching the closed form to several decimals. Sweep S from 80 to 120 and plot delta and gamma with Matplotlib to reproduce the S-curve and bell in Figure 1 — and watch gamma spike as you shrink T toward expiry.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A trader is short 10 call options, each with delta 0.40. To make the position delta-neutral they should…
A. sell 4 shares of the stock
B. buy 4 shares of the stock
C. buy 10 shares of the stock
D. do nothing — short calls are already neutral
Show answer

B. Short 10 calls gives position delta −10×0.40 = −4. To offset it you need +4 of spot delta, i.e. buy 4 shares. Selling shares (A) doubles the negative exposure; 10 shares (C) ignores the per-option delta; short options carry plenty of directional exposure, so D is wrong.

Q2. Gamma is largest for a call that is…
A. deep in the money with years to expiry
B. deep out of the money with years to expiry
C. at the money and close to expiry
D. the same everywhere — gamma is constant
Show answer

C. Gamma peaks where delta changes fastest: at the money, and it sharpens as expiry nears. Deep ITM (A) and deep OTM (B) options are nearly linear in S, so gamma ≈ 0. Gamma is decidedly not constant (D) — that variation is the whole point.

Q3. For a long option position, the typical signs of gamma and theta are…
A. gamma negative, theta positive
B. both positive
C. both negative
D. gamma positive, theta negative
Show answer

D. A long option has positive convexity (Γ > 0) but pays for it through time decay (Θ < 0) — the theta–gamma tradeoff. The signs are opposite, so "both positive" (B) and "both negative" (C) are wrong; a short option, not a long one, has the reversed pair in A.

Q4. Vega measures an option's sensitivity to…
A. a change in volatility, and is positive for a long option
B. a change in the underlying spot price
C. the passage of time
D. a change in the interest rate
Show answer

A. Vega = ∂V/∂σ, the value change per unit of volatility, and it is positive for long calls and puts. Sensitivity to spot is delta (B), to time is theta (C), and to rates is rho (D).

Q5. A desk is long gamma and has delta-hedged its book. Under the Black–Scholes relation Θ ≈ −½σ²S²Γ, the position makes money over the day only if…
A. the interest rate falls
B. the stock's realized move is large enough to outpace the theta decay
C. the stock stays perfectly still
D. implied volatility is exactly zero
Show answer

B. Long gamma pays off from realized movement; the gamma P&L must exceed the theta the desk bleeds — i.e. realized vol must beat the implied vol paid. A still stock (C) maximizes the loss to theta; rates (A) act through rho, not this tradeoff; zero implied vol (D) would mean a worthless option, not a profit.
