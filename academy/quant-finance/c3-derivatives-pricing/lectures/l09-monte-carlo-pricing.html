Week 9 · Lecture 9

Monte-Carlo Option Pricing

When the formula runs out, roll the dice: simulate thousands of futures, average what each one pays, and discount back to today's fair price.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain Monte-Carlo pricing as the law of large numbers applied to a discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure.
  • Simulate terminal stock prices from geometric Brownian motion in a single exact step, using the risk-free rate as the drift.
  • Average discounted payoffs to estimate an option price, and attach a standard error to that estimate.
  • Understand why Monte-Carlo error shrinks only like 1/√N, and what that costs in compute.
  • Price a path-dependent payoff that has no closed form, and cut the error with antithetic variates.

9.1An Option Price Is an Average of Futures

By Week 9 you have two ways to price a European option. The Black–Scholes formula (Week 6) gives an exact answer — but only for the simplest payoffs on the simplest dynamics. The moment a payoff depends on the path the price took, or on several assets at once, the integral stops having a tidy closed form. Monte-Carlo pricing is the escape hatch: instead of solving the integral with calculus, we estimate it by sampling.

The foundation is the risk-neutral pricing rule from Weeks 5–6. The fair price today of any European-style derivative paying H at expiry T is the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure Q:

V0 = e−rT · EQ[ H(ST) ]r is the continuously-compounded risk-free rate; the expectation is taken with the stock drifting at r, not its real-world return.

An expectation is just an average over all possible futures, weighted by probability. The law of large numbers (Course 1) says we can approximate any expectation by drawing many independent samples and taking their plain arithmetic mean. So the recipe writes itself: simulate N possible values of ST, compute the payoff in each future, average them, and discount. As N grows the average converges to the true expectation — and therefore to the true price.

V0 ≈ e−rT · (1/N) · Σi=1..N H(ST(i))Each ST(i) is one independently simulated terminal price; the sum runs over all N simulated futures.
AnalogyPricing by Monte-Carlo is like estimating the average rainfall of a city you can't visit. You can't measure every drop over every year — but you can simulate thousands of plausible years from the climate model, write down the rain in each, and average. The more simulated years you run, the steadier your estimate. The "climate model" here is the risk-neutral dynamics of the stock; each "year" is one random path to expiry.

9.2Simulating the Terminal Price

To draw samples of ST we need a model for how the price evolves. The standard choice — the same one underneath Black–Scholes — is geometric Brownian motion (GBM). Under the risk-neutral measure the drift is replaced by the risk-free rate r, and the solution of the GBM stochastic differential equation (Weeks 7–8) is an exact, closed expression for the price at any horizon:

ST = S0 · exp[ (r − ½σ²)·T + σ·√T · Z ],   Z ~ Normal(0, 1)S0 is today's price, σ the volatility, T the time to expiry in years. One standard-normal draw Z produces one terminal price.

This is the engine of the whole method, so it is worth reading slowly. The exponent is a normal random variable, which makes ST lognormal — prices stay positive, exactly as they must. The −½σ² term is the famous Itô correction: it is the gap between the drift of the log-price and the growth of the price itself. Drop it and your simulated stock drifts too fast, and every option you price comes out biased.

Because GBM has independent increments and we only care about the terminal value, we can jump straight from time 0 to time T in one exact step — no need to march through tiny intervals. That is what makes vanilla European Monte-Carlo so cheap: one normal draw per path. (Path-dependent payoffs in §9.4 will force us to take many small steps and trace the whole trajectory.)

Common mistakeDo not use the stock's real-world expected return μ as the drift. Pricing happens under the risk-neutral measure, where every tradable asset drifts at the risk-free rate r. Plugging in a juicy 10% expected return instead of, say, a 4% risk-free rate systematically overprices calls and underprices puts. The σ is real; the drift is not.

9.3Convergence: the Price of Randomness

Our estimate is a sample mean, so it is itself random — run the simulation twice with different seeds and you get two slightly different prices. How far off is it? The Central Limit Theorem (Course 1) tells us the estimate is approximately normal around the true price, with a spread set by the standard error:

standard error = s / √Ns is the sample standard deviation of the N discounted payoffs; the error of the price estimate shrinks like 1/√N.

That 1/√N is the defining feature — and frustration — of Monte-Carlo. To halve your error you need four times the simulations. To add one more decimal digit of accuracy (a 10× error reduction) you need one hundred times the paths. A 95% confidence interval is roughly the estimate ± 1.96 standard errors, and reporting it is not optional: a Monte-Carlo price without an error bar is a number with no claim to accuracy.

This slow convergence is the central trade-off against the lattice and PDE methods you will compare next week. Monte-Carlo barely cares how many dimensions the problem has — pricing a basket of 50 assets costs about the same per path as pricing one — which is why it dominates high-dimensional and path-dependent problems despite its lazy 1/√N crawl.

9.4Path-Dependence and Variance Reduction

Monte-Carlo earns its keep on payoffs Black–Scholes can't touch. An Asian option pays on the average price over the life of the contract; a barrier option knocks in or out if the price ever crosses a level; a lookback pays on the maximum reached. None depends only on ST — they depend on the entire path. To price them we cannot take a single jump to expiry; we must step through time, generating the price on a grid t₁, t₂, …, tm = T and recording whatever the payoff needs along the way:

St+Δt = St · exp[ (r − ½σ²)·Δt + σ·√Δt · Z ]The same GBM step, now applied repeatedly with a fresh independent Z each interval to build a full trajectory.

More steps and more paths both cost compute, and 1/√N is unforgiving — so practitioners lean on variance reduction: tricks that lower the standard deviation s of the payoffs without changing the answer, so the same N buys a tighter estimate. The simplest is antithetic variates.

Antithetic variates

The idea is to exploit symmetry. For every random draw Z you use to build a path, also build a mirror-image path from −Z. Because Z and −Z are equally likely under the standard normal, the mirrored path is a perfectly valid sample. You then average the two payoffs into one "antithetic pair" estimate. When a high payoff on the Z-path tends to come with a low payoff on the −Z-path, the two are negatively correlated, and averaging them cancels noise — shrinking the variance of each pair below that of two independent draws:

Ĥpair = ½ ( H(Z) + H(−Z) )If Cov(H(Z), H(−Z)) < 0, the variance of the paired estimate is lower than for two independent samples — free accuracy for the same number of draws.
Common mistakeAntithetic variates do not always help. The trick relies on a negative correlation between the payoff on Z and on −Z, which holds when the payoff is roughly monotone in Z (like a plain call). For a payoff that is symmetric in Z — for instance a straddle that pays on |ST − K| — the mirror move can land on the same payoff, the correlation turns positive, and antithetics can increase variance. Never assume; measure the standard error with and without.
✦ Worked Example — pricing a European call by hand-sized Monte-Carlo

Price a 1-year European call with S₀ = 100, K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%. We'll use a tiny N = 4 sample to show the mechanics by hand (real runs use millions), then compare to the exact Black–Scholes value.

  1. Set the per-path constants: drift term (r − ½σ²)·T = (0.05 − ½·0.04)·1 = 0.03; diffusion term σ·√T = 0.20·1 = 0.20. So ST = 100·exp(0.03 + 0.20·Z).
  2. Draw 4 standard normals (suppose Z = +1.2, −0.5, +0.3, −1.6) and map each to a terminal price: ST = 100·e0.03+0.20·1.2 ≈ 131.0; 100·e0.03−0.10 ≈ 93.2; 100·e0.03+0.06 ≈ 109.4; 100·e0.03−0.32 ≈ 74.8.
  3. Compute each call payoff H = max(ST − 100, 0): 31.0, 0, 9.4, 0.
  4. Average the payoffs: (31.0 + 0 + 9.4 + 0)/4 = 10.10.
  5. Discount to today: e−rT·10.10 = e−0.05·10.10 ≈ 0.9512·10.10 ≈ 9.61.
  6. Reality check: the exact Black–Scholes price here is ≈ 10.45. Our 4-path estimate (9.61) is in the right neighborhood but noisy — its standard error with only 4 draws is huge. Push N to 1,000,000 (next section) and the estimate locks onto 10.45 ± a couple of cents.

🛠️ Hands-On: a vanilla call and an antithetic upgrade

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + SciPy + Jupyter — the install block lives in Course 1, Lecture 1). We price the same call from the worked example, print a confidence interval, and show how antithetic variates shrink the error for free.

# mc_call.py — Lecture 9: Monte-Carlo European call
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

S0, K, r, sigma, T = 100.0, 100.0, 0.05, 0.20, 1.0
rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
N = 1_000_000

# --- plain Monte-Carlo: one exact GBM step to expiry ---
Z  = rng.standard_normal(N)
ST = S0 * np.exp((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*T + sigma*np.sqrt(T)*Z)
disc_payoff = np.exp(-r*T) * np.maximum(ST - K, 0.0)

price = disc_payoff.mean()
stderr = disc_payoff.std(ddof=1) / np.sqrt(N)
print(f"plain MC price : {price:.4f}  +/- {1.96*stderr:.4f} (95% CI)")

# --- antithetic variates: pair each Z with -Z ---
Zh   = rng.standard_normal(N // 2)
STp  = S0 * np.exp((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*T + sigma*np.sqrt(T)*Zh)
STm  = S0 * np.exp((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*T - sigma*np.sqrt(T)*Zh)
pair = 0.5*(np.maximum(STp-K,0) + np.maximum(STm-K,0)) * np.exp(-r*T)
print(f"antithetic     : {pair.mean():.4f}  +/- {1.96*pair.std(ddof=1)/np.sqrt(N//2):.4f}")

# --- exact Black-Scholes for comparison ---
d1 = (np.log(S0/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(T)
bs = S0*norm.cdf(d1) - K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2)
print(f"Black-Scholes  : {bs:.4f}")

All three numbers land near 10.45. Notice the antithetic CI is narrower than the plain one despite using the same total number of draws — that is variance reduction earning its keep. Replace the call payoff np.maximum(ST-K,0) with the same max(·,0) applied to a path average built from a stepped trajectory, and you have priced an Asian option no formula can give you.

🔗 Where this is heading

Monte-Carlo is your universal pricer, but it is not the only one. Next week you'll line it up against binomial lattices and finite-difference PDE solvers, and see exactly when each wins — Monte-Carlo for high-dimensional and path-dependent exotics, lattices and PDEs for low-dimensional American-style early exercise where MC struggles. The same simulation engine reappears across the specialization: in risk management it powers Value-at-Risk and stress scenarios by simulating whole portfolios, and in model calibration it prices the exotics whose market quotes you fit your volatility surface to. Master the discounted-average idea here and you carry it into every corner of quantitative finance.

Model response

Reach for the closed-form Black-Scholes formula whenever it applies: a European payoff on a single underlying following GBM. It's exact, instant, and carries no sampling noise — there's no reason to simulate anything when calculus already solved the integral. Reach for a binomial tree when the payoff needs early-exercise logic — an American option — because backward induction naturally compares "exercise now" against "hold" at every node, something a formula and a forward Monte-Carlo path can't express directly. The tree is an approximation that converges as steps grow, but it's cheap and low-dimensional problems handle it well. Reach for Monte-Carlo when the payoff is path-dependent (Asian, barrier, lookback) or depends on several underlyings at once — exactly the territory of §9.4 and the "Where this is heading" note above — because a lattice or PDE grid blows up combinatorially as dimensions or path-detail grow, while simulating one more path costs the same no matter how complicated the payoff's dependence on the trajectory is.

What Monte-Carlo buys that the other two can't: its cost barely scales with dimensionality (pricing a 50-asset basket costs about the same per path as pricing one asset, per §9.3), and it hands you an honest, quantified standard error s/√N on every estimate — neither Black-Scholes nor a tree tells you how uncertain its own number is. The price is the stubborn 1/√N convergence: quadrupling paths only halves the error, and plain Monte-Carlo is naturally a forward method, so it struggles with the backward-induction decision-making that early exercise needs (the subject of next week's method comparison). No single tool wins everywhere — the right choice depends on whether the payoff is path-independent-and-simple (closed form), early-exercisable (tree/PDE), or high-dimensional and path-dependent (Monte-Carlo).

Q1. In the Monte-Carlo pricing formula V₀ ≈ e−rT·(1/N)·Σ H(ST(i)), what justifies replacing the true expectation with a sample average?
A. The Itô correction
B. The law of large numbers
C. The put–call parity relation
D. The no-arbitrage assumption
Show answer

B. The law of large numbers guarantees the sample mean of independent draws converges to the expectation, which is what an option price is. The Itô correction (A) is the −½σ² term inside the GBM exponent, not a justification for averaging. Put–call parity (C) links call and put prices but says nothing about sampling. No-arbitrage (D) is what makes the discounted-expectation formula valid in the first place, but it does not justify the numerical step of replacing E with an average.

Q2. When simulating ST under the risk-neutral measure for pricing, which drift goes into the GBM exponent?
A. The stock's real-world expected return μ
B. Zero drift
C. The risk-free rate r
D. The dividend yield only
Show answer

C. Under the risk-neutral measure every tradable asset drifts at the risk-free rate r (adjusted to r − q if there are dividends). Using the real-world μ (A) is the classic error that biases prices. Zero drift (B) is wrong unless r happens to be zero. The dividend yield alone (D) ignores the risk-free rate entirely.

Q3. A Monte-Carlo run with N = 10,000 paths gives a standard error of 0.08. Approximately how many paths are needed to cut the standard error to 0.02?
A. 20,000
B. 40,000
C. 80,000
D. 160,000
Show answer

D. Error scales like 1/√N, so a 4× reduction in error (0.08 → 0.02) needs 4² = 16× the paths: 10,000 × 16 = 160,000. Doubling to 20,000 (A) only cuts error by √2. 40,000 (B) is 4× the paths, halving the error to 0.04. 80,000 (C) is 8× the paths, giving ≈0.028 — still short.

Q4. Why must a path-dependent payoff like an Asian (average-price) option be simulated step-by-step rather than with a single jump to expiry?
A. Because the payoff depends on the whole price trajectory, not just ST
B. Because GBM is only valid over small time steps
C. Because the risk-free rate changes every step
D. Because the single-step formula is only an approximation
Show answer

A. An Asian payoff uses the average price over the life of the option, so we need the price at many intermediate dates — the terminal value alone is not enough. GBM's single-step solution is exact, not an approximation (so B and D are false), and it holds for any horizon. The risk-free rate is taken as constant here, so C is also wrong.

Q5. Antithetic variates reduce Monte-Carlo variance most effectively when the payoffs from a draw Z and its mirror −Z are…
A. positively correlated
B. negatively correlated
C. statistically independent
D. always equal
Show answer

B. Averaging two negatively correlated payoffs cancels noise, pushing the paired variance below that of independent draws. Positive correlation (A) actually increases variance — the failure mode for symmetric payoffs like straddles. Independence (C) gives no benefit beyond ordinary extra samples. If the two payoffs were always equal (D), the mirror would add no new information at all.

✓ What you learned

  • An option price is a discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure — and an expectation is just an average, which the law of large numbers lets us estimate by sampling.
  • Terminal prices come from geometric Brownian motion, ST = S₀·exp[(r − ½σ²)T + σ√T·Z], drifting at the risk-free rate r, in a single exact step for vanilla options.
  • The estimate carries a standard error s/√N; the stubborn 1/√N convergence means 4× the paths to halve the error.
  • Path-dependent payoffs (Asian, barrier, lookback) require stepping through time to trace the whole trajectory.
  • Antithetic variates pair each Z with −Z to cut variance for free — when the payoffs are negatively correlated.
