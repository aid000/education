Exotic Options & Three Pricing Methods Compared

The capstone payoff: meet the path-dependent options that closed forms can't always reach, then choose the right tool — formula, tree, or simulation — to price and hedge them.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the three workhorse exotics — barrier, Asian, and digital options — and write down their payoffs.
  • Distinguish path-dependent from path-independent payoffs and explain why that distinction decides your pricing method.
  • Choose between a closed-form formula, a binomial tree, and Monte-Carlo on the axes of accuracy, speed, and flexibility.
  • Estimate a Greek (delta) under each method and understand why a digital payoff makes delta-hedging treacherous.
  • Frame and execute the capstone: price and delta-hedge one option three independent ways and reconcile the answers.

10.1Beyond the Vanilla: What Makes an Option "Exotic"

Through Week 9 every option we priced was vanilla: a European call or put whose payoff depends only on the terminal price ST. That single fact — payoff is a function of one number at one time — is exactly what let Black–Scholes (Week 6) collapse the whole problem into a tidy formula. An exotic option breaks that assumption. Its payoff may depend on the whole path the price took, or it may pay in a discontinuous, all-or-nothing way. Three exotics dominate real trading desks, and each illustrates a different way the vanilla recipe fails.

Barrier options

A barrier option behaves like a vanilla option but switches on or off if the underlying touches a level B during its life. A down-and-out call, for example, is an ordinary call that is knocked out (becomes worthless) the instant the price falls to B — even if it later recovers. Because the payoff cares whether the barrier was ever breached, it depends on the entire path, not just ST.

Down-and-out call payoff = max(ST − K, 0) · 𝟙{ mint≤T St > B }The indicator 𝟙{·} is 1 only if the price never touched the barrier. Knock-out options are cheaper than the vanilla — you give up the paths that breach B — which is why hedgers love them.

Asian options

An Asian (average-price) option replaces the terminal price with the average price over a set of monitoring dates. An Asian call pays on max(Ā − K, 0) where Ā is the average. Averaging smooths out a single closing-price spike, so Asians are the natural hedge for a firm exposed to an asset's average cost — a refiner buying crude all month, say.

Asian call payoff = max( (1/m)·Σi=1m Sti − K , 0 )The average Ā depends on m sampled prices along the path — again path-dependent.

Digital (binary) options

A digital (or binary) option pays a fixed amount Q if it finishes in the money and nothing otherwise — a bet on a threshold rather than on the size of the move. It is path-independent (only ST matters) but its payoff is a step function, which makes its hedging behavior wild near expiry.

Cash-or-nothing digital call payoff = Q · 𝟙{ ST > K }A clean knife-edge: full payout Q just above K, zero just below.
AnalogyThink of a vanilla call as a dimmer switch — payoff rises smoothly as the price climbs above K. A digital is a light switch: flat off, then suddenly fully on. A barrier is a switch wired to a trip-wire across the room — cross the wire once and the whole circuit dies, no matter what you do afterward. The shape of the switch is what dictates how hard the thing is to price and hedge.

10.2Path Dependence: The Property That Picks Your Tool

Every pricing method we've built reduces to the same risk-neutral statement: the price today is the discounted expected payoff under the risk-neutral measure (Week 5).

V0 = e−rT · 𝔼ℚ[ payoff ]r is the risk-free rate, T the maturity, ℚ the risk-neutral measure. Pricing is always "discount the expected payoff" — the methods differ only in how they compute that expectation.

The decisive question is what the payoff is a function of. If it depends only on ST — a single endpoint — the expectation is a one-dimensional integral, and we have a real shot at a closed-form answer (vanilla calls, digitals, and even geometric-average Asians fall here). If the payoff depends on the path — a minimum, a maximum, a running average sampled at many dates — the expectation lives in many dimensions and a clean formula usually does not exist.

This is why path dependence is the single most useful label to attach to an exotic. It tells you immediately whether a formula is even possible, and if not, whether a lattice (binomial tree) or Monte-Carlo simulation is the better fit. Barriers can be handled on a tree because the lattice records the price at every node. Arithmetic-average Asians and anything with several underlyings push you toward Monte-Carlo, which prices a path simply by walking it.

Common mistakeDo not assume "exotic" means "needs Monte-Carlo." Some exotics have exact formulas (a single-barrier option under Black–Scholes, a geometric-average Asian, every digital). Reaching for a slow simulation when a closed form exists wastes time and adds sampling error to an answer you could have gotten exactly. Always ask first: is a formula available?

10.3Three Methods on Three Axes: Accuracy, Speed, Flexibility

You now own three engines for the same expectation. The capstone asks you to run all three on one option, so it pays to know each one's character. Judge them on three axes: accuracy (how close to the true price for a given effort), speed (wall-clock cost), and flexibility (which payoffs it can handle at all).

MethodHow it computes 𝔼[payoff]Best atStruggles with
Closed form
(Black–Scholes & variants)		Evaluates an exact analytic integralInstant, exact, gives Greeks by differentiation — vanillas, digitals, single barriersOnly exists for special payoffs; no help for arithmetic Asians or multi-asset exotics
Binomial tree
(lattice)		Discretizes price into nodes, rolls payoff back step by stepAmerican/early-exercise & barriers; transparent; Greeks are cheap from the treeOne or two underlyings only; error decays like 1/N and oscillates; high-dimensional paths blow up
Monte-Carlo
(simulation)		Averages payoff over many simulated pathsMaximum flexibility — any path-dependent or multi-asset payoffSlow: error shrinks only like 1/√N; awkward for early exercise; Greeks need care

The accuracy story is the one to internalize. A binomial tree's pricing error falls roughly like 1/N in the number of steps, while Monte-Carlo's statistical error falls only like 1/√N in the number of paths — the same √n law from the Central Limit Theorem (Week 2). To cut Monte-Carlo error in half you need four times the paths. That is why simulation is the method of last resort on accuracy, but the method of first resort on flexibility: it is the only one that prices an arbitrary path-dependent payoff without new mathematics.

10.4The Capstone: Price AND Delta-Hedge One Option Three Ways

This week the capstone is due, and it is the payoff of the whole course. You take a single option and compute its price and its delta — the hedge ratio Δ = ∂V/∂S from Week 7 — by all three methods, then reconcile the numbers. Agreement across independent methods is the strongest evidence a price is right; disagreement is a bug hunt.

Delta is the bridge from pricing to hedging. If you sell an option for V and hold Δ units of the underlying, your combined position is (to first order) immune to small moves in S — the essence of the replicating portfolio Black–Scholes is built on. Each method delivers delta differently:

Δ = ∂V/∂S ≈ [ V(S + h) − V(S − h) ] / (2h)The central-difference "bump-and-revalue": reprice with the spot bumped up and down by a small h and divide. Works for any method; for Black–Scholes, Δ = N(d₁) is exact in closed form.

For closed forms, delta is an exact derivative (a vanilla call's is N(d₁)). On a binomial tree, delta falls out of the first step almost for free: Δ ≈ (Vup − Vdown)/(Sup − Sdown) — the very hedge ratio that built the tree in Weeks 3–4. In Monte-Carlo you typically bump-and-revalue, reusing the same random seed for the up and down runs so the difference reflects the bump, not the noise.

Common mistakeWhen you delta-hedge a digital near expiry, its delta spikes toward infinity right at the strike — the payoff is a vertical cliff, so an arbitrarily small move in S flips it from 0 to Q. Bump-and-revalue with too small an h then returns nonsense (it straddles the cliff). Desks manage this by hedging the digital with a tight call spread that smooths the cliff into a ramp. A naive constant-delta hedge of a digital is a classic way to lose money.
AnalogyPricing one option three ways is like surveying a mountain's height with a barometer, a theodolite, and GPS. Each instrument has its own error profile; when all three agree to within their tolerances you trust the number. When the barometer disagrees, you've learned something — maybe the weather changed, maybe you misread the dial. The reconciliation is the science.
✦ Worked Example — pricing a cash-or-nothing digital call three ways

Price a digital call that pays Q = $1 if ST > K at expiry. Take S₀ = 100, K = 100, r = 5%, σ = 20%, T = 1 year. We'll get the exact closed form, then check it with a tree and a simulation.

  1. Set up d₂ (closed form): for a cash-or-nothing call the price is e−rT·N(d₂), with d₂ = [ln(S₀/K) + (r − ½σ²)T] / (σ√T). Here ln(1) = 0, so the numerator is (0.05 − ½·0.04)·1 = 0.03, and d₂ = 0.03 / 0.20 = 0.15.
  2. Closed-form price: N(0.15) ≈ 0.5596, so V = e−0.05·0.5596 = 0.9512·0.5596 ≈ $0.5324. This is exact (up to the normal-CDF lookup).
  3. Binomial tree: build an N-step risk-neutral tree, set each terminal node's value to Q·𝟙{ST > K}, and discount back. With N = 500 steps the tree returns ≈ $0.532 — but watch it oscillate as N changes, because nodes straddle the knife-edge at K differently for odd vs. even N.
  4. Monte-Carlo: simulate ST = S₀·exp[(r − ½σ²)T + σ√T·Z] for Z ~ Normal(0,1), average Q·𝟙{ST > K}, and discount. With 1,000,000 paths you'll see ≈ $0.532 ± 0.0005 — the ±band is the 1/√N standard error.
  5. Reconcile: all three land near $0.532. The closed form is the gold standard; the tree confirms it but reveals oscillation at the cliff; Monte-Carlo confirms it but only to within its noise band. That triangulation — exact, fast-but-jittery, slow-but-flexible — is precisely what the capstone asks you to demonstrate.

🛠️ Hands-On: the digital call, three engines side by side

Reusing the Week-1/2 stack (NumPy + SciPy + Jupyter — the install block lives in C1 Lecture 1 if you skipped it). This is the skeleton of the capstone: one payoff, three methods, one reconciliation.

# digital_three_ways.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

S0, K, r, sigma, T, Q = 100.0, 100.0, 0.05, 0.20, 1.0, 1.0

# --- 1) CLOSED FORM: digital call = exp(-rT) * N(d2) ---
d2 = (np.log(S0/K) + (r - 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
cf = np.exp(-r*T) * norm.cdf(d2) * Q
print("closed form :", round(cf, 4))            # 0.5324

# --- 2) BINOMIAL TREE (Cox-Ross-Rubinstein) ---
def tree(N):
    dt = T/N;  u = np.exp(sigma*np.sqrt(dt));  d = 1/u
    p  = (np.exp(r*dt) - d) / (u - d)           # risk-neutral prob
    j  = np.arange(N+1)
    ST = S0 * u**j * d**(N-j)                    # terminal prices
    V  = np.where(ST > K, Q, 0.0)                 # digital payoff
    for _ in range(N):                           # roll back
        V = np.exp(-r*dt) * (p*V[1:] + (1-p)*V[:-1])
    return V[0]
print("binomial    :", round(tree(500), 4))     # ~0.532, oscillates in N

# --- 3) MONTE-CARLO ---
rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
n   = 1_000_000
Z   = rng.standard_normal(n)
ST  = S0 * np.exp((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*T + sigma*np.sqrt(T)*Z)
pay = np.where(ST > K, Q, 0.0)
mc  = np.exp(-r*T) * pay.mean()
se  = np.exp(-r*T) * pay.std() / np.sqrt(n)      # 1/sqrt(N) error
print("monte-carlo :", round(mc, 4), "+/-", round(se, 4))

Three numbers, one price. For the delta piece of the capstone, wrap each pricer in a function of S0 and bump-and-revalue: (price(S0+h) - price(S0-h)) / (2*h) with, say, h = 0.5 — and reuse the same rng seed in the two Monte-Carlo runs so the noise cancels. Try the digital's delta with a tiny h near the strike to see the cliff blow up, then widen h (a call-spread hedge) to tame it.

🔗 Where this is heading

You've now closed the loop from probability to a working pricing-and-hedging toolkit. From here the specialization turns the dials up: stochastic volatility models (where σ itself moves) and local-volatility calibration break the constant-σ assumption and lean heavily on Monte-Carlo and finite-difference PDE solvers — the continuous cousins of the tree you built. Variance-reduction tricks (antithetic variates, control variates) attack that stubborn 1/√N, and quasi-Monte-Carlo sequences push it toward 1/N. The portfolio & risk course reuses these engines to compute Value-at-Risk and to stress-test books of thousands of exotics at once. Every one of those advances is a refinement of the same idea you mastered today: pricing is a discounted expectation, and the art is computing it well.

Q1. A down-and-out call becomes worthless the moment the price touches a barrier B. Its payoff is therefore…
A. path-independent — only ST matters
B. path-dependent — it depends on whether B was ever touched
C. independent of the underlying entirely
D. identical to a vanilla call's payoff
Show answer

B. The knock-out depends on the minimum price over the whole life — a path feature — so it is path-dependent. It is not a function of ST alone (A), it certainly depends on the underlying (C), and being knocked out makes it worth strictly less than the vanilla (D).

Q2. Which option's payoff depends only on the terminal price ST and therefore is not path-dependent?
A. An arithmetic-average Asian call
B. An up-and-in barrier put
C. A cash-or-nothing digital call
D. A knock-out call
Show answer

C. A digital pays Q·𝟙{ST > K}, a function of the endpoint alone — path-independent. The Asian depends on the running average (A), and both barrier options depend on whether a level was touched along the path (B, D).

Q3. Monte-Carlo pricing error shrinks like 1/√N in the number of paths. To cut the error in half you must…
A. use four times as many paths
B. double the number of paths
C. halve the number of paths
D. nothing — error does not depend on N
Show answer

A. Because error ∝ 1/√N, halving it requires √N to double, i.e. N to quadruple. Doubling N (B) only cuts error by a factor 1/√2 ≈ 0.71; halving N (C) makes it worse; and error clearly depends on N (D).

Q4. You need to price an arithmetic-average Asian option with daily monitoring and no known closed form. The most appropriate primary tool is…
A. the Black–Scholes vanilla formula, unchanged
B. the put–call parity relation
C. ignoring the averaging and using ST
D. Monte-Carlo simulation of the price path
Show answer

D. A heavily path-dependent payoff with no analytic form is the canonical Monte-Carlo job — just simulate the path and average. The vanilla formula ignores the averaging (A, C), and parity (B) relates calls and puts, not averages.

Q5. Why is delta-hedging a digital option dangerous near expiry and at the strike?
A. Because its delta vanishes to zero everywhere
B. Because digitals cannot be priced at all
C. Because the step-shaped payoff makes delta spike toward infinity at the strike
D. Because the risk-free rate becomes negative
Show answer

C. The all-or-nothing payoff is a vertical cliff at K; near expiry a tiny move in S flips it from 0 to Q, so Δ = ∂V/∂S blows up. Delta does not vanish (A), digitals are perfectly priceable (B), and the rate has nothing to do with it (D). Desks smooth the cliff with a tight call spread.
