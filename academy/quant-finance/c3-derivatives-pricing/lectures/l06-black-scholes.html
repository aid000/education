The Black-Scholes Formula

One closed-form equation turns a stock price, a strike, a clock, and a single volatility number into the fair value of an option — and tells you exactly how to hedge it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the Black-Scholes formula for a European call and put, and identify each of its five inputs.
  • Compute d1 and d2 and explain why they differ by exactly σ√T.
  • List the model's assumptions — geometric Brownian motion, constant volatility, no arbitrage, continuous frictionless hedging — and know which ones markets break.
  • Interpret N(d1) as the option's delta and N(d2) as the risk-neutral probability of finishing in the money.
  • Read the Black-Scholes PDE as a hedging identity, and predict how the price moves when each input changes.

6.1From a Binomial Tree to a Single Formula

Last week you priced an option by building a binomial tree: at each tiny step the stock could tick up or down, and you replicated the option with a self-financing portfolio of stock and cash. Push the number of steps to infinity while shrinking each step toward zero, and the jagged tree becomes a smooth random process. The option's value collapses onto one closed-form expression — the Black-Scholes formula. No tree to roll back, no loop to run: plug in five numbers and read off the price.

The genius of the construction is the same one you met in the tree. Because you can continuously hedge the option with the underlying stock, the option becomes risk-free over each instant. A risk-free position can only earn the risk-free rate — otherwise there is free money. That single no-arbitrage demand is enough to pin down a unique fair value, with no reference to anyone's appetite for risk or their forecast of where the stock is heading.

AnalogyThink of the binomial tree as a staircase and Black-Scholes as the ramp it approximates. With two steps the staircase is crude; with a thousand it hugs the ramp so closely you might as well walk the ramp directly. Black-Scholes is that ramp — the continuous-time limit of the discrete hedging argument you already trust.

6.2The Formula, and What d1 / d2 Are

For a European call on a non-dividend-paying stock — exercisable only at expiry — the price C depends on five inputs: the spot price S, the strike K, the time to expiry T (in years), the continuously-compounded risk-free rate r, and the volatility σ. The formula is:

C = S·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2)N(·) is the standard-normal cumulative distribution function — the area under the bell curve up to its argument.

The two arguments d1 and d2 bundle all five inputs into pure, unit-free numbers:

d1 = [ ln(S/K) + (r + ½σ²)T ] / (σ√T)    d2 = d1 − σ√T

Read d1 piece by piece. The term ln(S/K) measures how far the stock already sits above (or below) the strike, on a log scale — its moneyness. The (r + ½σ²)T term drifts that moneyness forward to expiry under the risk-neutral measure. Dividing by σ√T — the total volatility accumulated over the option's life — converts everything into standard deviations. So d2, in plain English, is "how many standard deviations the (risk-neutral) terminal price sits above the strike." That is why N(d2) is a probability.

The put price follows from put-call parity (Week 4), C − P = S − K·e−rT, or directly by flipping the signs and the N(·) arguments:

P = K·e−rT·N(−d2) − S·N(−d1)
Common mistakeThe gap between d1 and d2 is exactly σ√T, never σ²T or σT. A frequent slip is to write d2 = d1 − σ²T or to use T in days instead of years. Keep T in years and remember the half-variance ½σ² lives only in d1's numerator — subtracting σ√T removes it cleanly to give d2's (r − ½σ²)T drift.

6.3The Assumptions Behind the Magic

Black-Scholes is exact only in an idealized world. Four assumptions do the heavy lifting, and knowing where they crack is what separates a quant from a calculator:

AssumptionWhat it meansHow markets break it
Geometric Brownian motionThe stock follows dS = μS dt + σS dW; log-returns are normal with no jumps.Real prices gap on news — fat tails and jumps the model can't see.
Constant volatility σA single σ governs every strike and maturity.Implied vol smiles and skews across strikes (Week 8).
No arbitrageNo risk-free profit exists; the hedge must earn rate r.Holds well — the most robust assumption.
Continuous, frictionless hedgingYou rebalance the hedge instantly with no transaction costs.Trading is discrete and costly; perfect replication is impossible.

Notice that the stock's real-world expected return μ never appears in the formula. This is the deepest idea in the subject: because the option is perfectly hedged, your view on where the stock is going washes out entirely. Pricing happens under the risk-neutral measure, where every asset is assumed to drift at the risk-free rate r. The drift you care about for trading is irrelevant for valuation.

Common mistakePlugging your bullish price target for the stock into Black-Scholes does nothing — there is no slot for it. Beginners hunt for where to enter their forecast of μ. There isn't one. The only forward-looking input is σ, your forecast of how much the stock moves, not which way.

6.4The PDE and the Sensitivity of Each Input

Where does the formula come from? Build a portfolio that is long one option and short Δ = N(d1) shares of stock. Over an instant the random dW terms cancel, leaving a riskless position that, by no-arbitrage, must grow at rate r. Writing that out with Itô's lemma gives the Black-Scholes partial differential equation that every European derivative on this stock must satisfy:

∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S² + rS·∂V/∂S − rV = 0V(S,t) is the option value. The call formula of §6.2 is the solution with payoff max(S−K, 0) at t = T.

You don't need to solve a PDE by hand — the formula already did. But reading it tells you the hedging story: the ½σ²S² term (the option's convexity, or gamma) is paid for by the time-decay term ∂V/∂t (theta). An option holder profits from movement and pays for the passage of time; the PDE says those two exactly offset along a risk-free hedge. Those sensitivities — the Greeks — are next week's entire topic.

For now, build intuition for what each input does to the call price:

Input rises →Call priceWhy
Spot SupThe right to buy is worth more when the asset is worth more (delta > 0).
Strike KdownA higher purchase price you've locked in is less valuable.
Volatility σupMore dispersion fattens the upside while the downside stays capped at zero (vega > 0).
Time Tup*More time means more chance to move into the money (for a vanilla call).
Rate rupDiscounting the strike harder lowers its present cost (rho > 0 for calls).

The most important of these for a trader is σ. Spot, strike, time, and rate are all observable; volatility is a forecast. Two desks looking at the same option will quote different prices precisely because they disagree about σ — which is why options markets are, in the end, markets in volatility.

✦ Worked Example — pricing a six-month at-the-money call

Price a European call with spot S = 100, strike K = 100, time T = 0.5 years, rate r = 4% (0.04), and volatility σ = 20% (0.20).

  1. Compute σ√T: σ√T = 0.20·√0.5 = 0.20·0.7071 = 0.1414.
  2. Numerator of d1: ln(S/K) = ln(1) = 0; (r + ½σ²)T = (0.04 + 0.5·0.04)·0.5 = (0.04 + 0.02)·0.5 = 0.06·0.5 = 0.03.
  3. d1 and d2: d1 = 0.03 / 0.1414 = 0.2122; d2 = 0.2122 − 0.1414 = 0.0708.
  4. Normal CDFs: N(0.2122) ≈ 0.5840; N(0.0708) ≈ 0.5282.
  5. Discount the strike: K·e−rT = 100·e−0.02 = 100·0.9802 = 98.02.
  6. Assemble the price: C = 100·0.5840 − 98.02·0.5282 = 58.40 − 51.78 = 6.62.
  7. Sanity check: the call costs about $6.62 per share, roughly 6.6% of spot — sensible for a six-month at-the-money option at 20% vol. N(d1) ≈ 0.58 is the delta: hedge by shorting ~0.58 shares per call.

🛠️ Hands-On: Black-Scholes in eight lines

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + SciPy + Matplotlib + Jupyter — the full install block lives in Course 1, Lecture 1). SciPy's norm.cdf is the N(·) you need. Let's price the worked example and then plot how the call's value bends with spot.

# black_scholes.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def bs_call(S, K, T, r, sigma):
    d1 = (np.log(S / K) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    return S * norm.cdf(d1) - K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(d2)

def bs_put(S, K, T, r, sigma):
    d1 = (np.log(S / K) + (r + 0.5 * sigma**2) * T) / (sigma * np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma * np.sqrt(T)
    return K * np.exp(-r * T) * norm.cdf(-d2) - S * norm.cdf(-d1)

S, K, T, r, sigma = 100, 100, 0.5, 0.04, 0.20
print("call :", round(bs_call(S, K, T, r, sigma), 4))   # 6.6178
print("put  :", round(bs_put (S, K, T, r, sigma), 4))   # 4.6378

# put-call parity check: C - P should equal S - K*exp(-rT)
lhs = bs_call(S, K, T, r, sigma) - bs_put(S, K, T, r, sigma)
print("parity:", round(lhs, 4), "vs", round(S - K*np.exp(-r*T), 4))

The call prints ≈ 6.62 — matching the worked example to the penny — and the parity line confirms C − P = S − Ke−rT exactly. Now sweep S from 60 to 140 and plt.plot the call value against the kinked payoff max(S−K, 0): the smooth Black-Scholes curve sits above the payoff everywhere, and the gap (the time value) is fattest right at the money. That curvature is gamma — the star of Week 7.

🔗 Where this is heading

You now have a price; next you need its sensitivities. Week 7 (The Greeks) reads delta = N(d1), gamma, theta, vega, and rho straight off this formula and turns them into a live hedging desk. Week 8 confronts the constant-σ lie head-on: by inverting Black-Scholes you back out the implied volatility the market is quoting, and discover it isn't flat at all — it smiles. Later courses replace geometric Brownian motion with stochastic-volatility and jump models to price the fat tails this lecture quietly assumed away. Black-Scholes is not the last word; it is the coordinate system every richer model is described in.

💬 Discussion

The Black-Scholes formula has no input for the stock's expected return μ, and it assumes a single constant volatility that real markets demonstrably do not honor. Yet trading desks worldwide still quote, hedge, and risk-manage options through it every day. Why does a model built on assumptions everyone knows are false remain the industry's common language? In your answer, take a position: is Black-Scholes best understood as a pricing model, or as a translation device between prices and a single intuitive number?

Show a model response

Black-Scholes survives precisely because the desk does not treat it as a literal forecast. Its real job is to be a translation device: a one-to-one map between an option's dollar price and a single, intuitive number — implied volatility. Because μ cancels out of the hedging argument, two traders who disagree wildly about where the stock is going can still agree on the formula's structure and bargain in the one dimension that matters, σ. The formula compresses five inputs into one quotable figure, and that shared coordinate makes a deep, liquid market possible.

The "false" assumptions are handled not by abandoning the model but by letting the one free input absorb their failure. Constant volatility is wrong, so the market quotes a different implied vol at each strike and maturity — the smile — bending Black-Scholes around reality instead of replacing it. The formula becomes the lens through which the smile is even visible. In that light it is far more a translation layer than a belief about the future: a calculator everyone agrees to disagree inside of.

❓ Lecture 6 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In the Black-Scholes call formula C = S·N(d1) − K·e−rT·N(d2), what does N(d2) represent?
A. The option's delta — the number of shares to hold in the hedge
B. The risk-neutral probability that the call finishes in the money
C. The expected return of the underlying stock
D. The present value of the strike price
Show answer

B. N(d2) is the risk-neutral probability the terminal price exceeds the strike. The delta is N(d1), not N(d2), so A is wrong. The stock's real expected return (C) never enters the formula at all. The present value of the strike is K·e−rT (D), a separate factor multiplying N(d2).

Q2. By how much do d1 and d2 differ?
A. σ²T
B. rT
C. σ√T
D. ½σ²T
Show answer

C. By definition d2 = d1 − σ√T, the total volatility accumulated over the life of the option. σ²T (A) is the total variance, not the standard deviation; rT (B) is the discount exponent; ½σ²T (D) is the half-variance drift term, which lives inside d1's numerator but is not the gap between the two.

Q3. A trader raises her volatility forecast σ while leaving S, K, T, and r unchanged. What happens to the Black-Scholes call price?
A. It rises, because greater dispersion increases upside while downside stays capped at zero
B. It falls, because higher volatility means more risk
C. It is unchanged, because σ cancels out like μ does
D. It rises only if the option is already in the money
Show answer

A. Vega is positive for vanilla options: more volatility fattens the upside while the loss is floored at the premium, so the call is worth more. B confuses risk with value for the buyer. σ emphatically does not cancel — it is the one forward-looking input that survives (C is false). The effect holds regardless of moneyness, so D is wrong.

Q4. Which input to the stock's behavior does not appear anywhere in the Black-Scholes formula?
A. The volatility σ
B. The risk-free rate r
C. The time to expiry T
D. The stock's real-world expected return μ
Show answer

D. Because the option is perfectly hedged, pricing happens under the risk-neutral measure where the drift is r, and the real-world μ drops out completely. σ (A), r (B), and T (C) are all genuine inputs — three of the five the formula requires.

Q5. The Black-Scholes PDE is ∂V/∂t + ½σ²S²·∂²V/∂S² + rS·∂V/∂S − rV = 0. What financial idea forces this equation to hold?
A. That the stock's expected return must equal the market's risk premium
B. That a continuously delta-hedged option portfolio is riskless and must therefore earn the risk-free rate
C. That option prices follow a random walk with constant drift
D. That the strike price equals the forward price at expiry
Show answer

B. Holding the option and shorting N(d1) shares cancels the random dW term; the remaining riskless portfolio must grow at r or arbitrage exists — and that condition is the PDE. A misstates the risk-neutral argument; option values are deterministic functions of S and t, not a free random walk (C); and D describes a special at-the-money-forward case, not the PDE's origin.
