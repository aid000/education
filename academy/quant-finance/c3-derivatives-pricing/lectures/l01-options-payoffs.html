Week 1 · Lecture 1

Options, Forwards & Payoff Diagrams

A derivative is a bet whose value is borrowed from something else — and the shape of that bet, drawn at expiry, tells you everything about the risk you just bought.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a derivative and explain why its value depends on an underlying asset.
  • Contrast a forward/futures (an obligation) with an option (a right), and write the payoff of each at expiry.
  • Distinguish calls from puts and long from short positions, and draw the four basic option payoff diagrams.
  • Separate an option's price into intrinsic value and time value, and classify moneyness as ITM / ATM / OTM.
  • Explain the three economic reasons options exist — leverage, hedging, and insurance — and plot a payoff in Matplotlib.

1.1What Is a Derivative?

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from the price of something else — the underlying. The underlying can be a stock, an index, a barrel of oil, a bushel of wheat, an interest rate, or even another derivative. You never own the underlying directly; you own a claim whose payoff is a function of where the underlying ends up.

That single idea — "value borrowed from another asset" — is the whole of Course 3. Everything we build, from a simple forward to the Black–Scholes model, is just a different recipe for turning the uncertain future price of an underlying into a payoff today. The two great families are forwards/futures (a binding obligation to trade) and options (a right, but not an obligation, to trade).

AnalogyA derivative is like a weather bet on a farmer's harvest. You don't plant or harvest anything — you hold a slip of paper whose payout rises and falls with the rain. The harvest is the underlying; your slip is the derivative. Its value lives or dies on something you never physically touch.

1.2Forwards: an Obligation to Trade

A forward contract locks in today a price K (the delivery or strike price) at which two parties must trade the underlying at a future date T. Nothing changes hands now; at expiry the long side must buy at K and the short side must sell at K, whatever the market price ST turns out to be. A futures contract is the same idea, standardized and traded on an exchange with daily cash settlement.

The payoff of a long forward at expiry is simply the gap between the market price and the locked-in price:

Long forward payoff = ST − KYou pay K, receive something worth ST. The short side gets the mirror image, K − ST.

Notice the payoff is a straight line through the strike — it slopes up at 45° and can be deeply negative. A forward is symmetric: equal upside and downside. That symmetry is the key difference from options, which slice the line in half and keep only the favorable side.

Common mistakeA forward is not free insurance — it is a two-sided obligation. If you are long a forward and the price collapses, you are still forced to buy at the high K and absorb the full loss ST − K. Unlike an option, you cannot simply walk away.

1.3Options: Calls, Puts, Long & Short

An option gives its holder the right, but not the obligation, to trade the underlying at strike K on (or before) expiry T. Because a right is only ever exercised when it helps you, the payoff is kinked: it bends at the strike and never goes below zero for the holder. There are two flavors:

  • A call is the right to buy at K. You exercise when ST > K (the asset is cheap relative to the market). Its holder profits when prices rise.
  • A put is the right to sell at K. You exercise when ST < K (you can dump the asset above market). Its holder profits when prices fall.

The buyer (the long) pays a premium upfront for this right. The seller (the short, or "writer") collects the premium and takes on the obligation to deliver if the long exercises. The two payoffs at expiry, before premium, are:

Long call = max(ST − K, 0)    Long put = max(K − ST, 0)The "max(…, 0)" is the right to walk away: you never exercise into a loss.

The short positions are the exact negatives — short call = −max(ST − K, 0) and short put = −max(K − ST, 0). A long's gain is the short's loss, dollar for dollar; options are a zero-sum transfer of risk. These four building blocks — long call, long put, short call, short put — combine into every options strategy in existence.

1.4Payoff vs Profit, Moneyness & Time Value

The diagrams above show payoff — the value at expiry. To get profit, slide the whole curve down by the premium you paid (for a long) or up by the premium you collected (for a short). A long call only turns a real profit once ST clears K plus the premium; that point is the breakeven.

Long-call profit = max(ST − K, 0) − premium    Breakeven: ST = K + premium

An option's premium splits cleanly into two pieces. Intrinsic value is what the option would pay if expiry were right now — max(S − K, 0) for a call. Time value is everything left over, the extra you pay for the chance the price moves further your way before expiry:

Premium = Intrinsic value + Time valueTime value is always ≥ 0 and decays to zero at expiry — "theta," the bleed you'll meet in Week 6.

Moneyness classifies where the spot sits relative to the strike. For a call:

MoneynessCall conditionPut conditionIntrinsic value
In-the-money (ITM)S > KS < Kpositive
At-the-money (ATM)S ≈ KS ≈ K≈ 0
Out-of-the-money (OTM)S < KS > K0 (all time value)

An OTM option has zero intrinsic value — every cent of its premium is time value, a pure bet that the price will travel into the money before the clock runs out.

Why options exist: leverage, hedging, insurance

Options are not just abstractions; markets created them to do three concrete jobs. Leverage: a cheap OTM call controls a large notional for a small premium, so a modest price move can multiply your stake many times (and can also vaporize it). Hedging: a producer who will sell oil next quarter buys puts to lock in a price floor, neutralizing downside while keeping upside. Insurance: a portfolio holder buys puts as a crash policy — the premium is the deductible, the payoff covers the loss. Same instrument, three motives.

✦ Worked Example — a long call, end to end

A stock trades at S0 = $100. You buy one 3-month call with strike K = $105 for a premium of $3 (one contract is on one share here, for clarity). Work out the moneyness, intrinsic/time value, breakeven, and the profit if the stock finishes at $112 or at $98.

  1. Moneyness now: spot $100 is below strike $105, so the call is out-of-the-money.
  2. Intrinsic & time value today: intrinsic = max(100 − 105, 0) = $0; so all $3 of premium is time value — a pure bet on the stock climbing past $105.
  3. Breakeven: you need the stock above K plus premium = 105 + 3 = $108 at expiry to come out ahead.
  4. If ST = $112: payoff = max(112 − 105, 0) = $7; profit = 7 − 3 = +$4. The option is now ITM and you more than doubled the premium.
  5. If ST = $98: payoff = max(98 − 105, 0) = $0; profit = 0 − 3 = −$3. You walk away — the loss is capped at the premium no matter how far the stock falls.
  6. Leverage check: the stock rose 12% (100→112) but your $3 stake returned 133% — and a 2% drop would also have cost you the full premium. That asymmetry, paid for upfront, is the essence of an option.

🛠️ Hands-On: plot the four payoffs

Python + NumPy + Matplotlib is the free stack for this course. Week 1 setup: install it once (below), then reuse it every lecture. We'll build payoff arrays with NumPy and draw them with Matplotlib — the exact diagrams from §1.3.

# payoffs.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

K       = 105.0          # strike
premium = 3.0            # cost of the long call / put
S = np.linspace(80, 130, 400)   # range of terminal prices S_T

long_call  = np.maximum(S - K, 0)          # right to buy at K
long_put   = np.maximum(K - S, 0)          # right to sell at K
short_call = -long_call                     # writer's mirror image
short_put  = -long_put

call_profit = long_call - premium           # slide down by premium
breakeven   = K + premium                   # 108.0

print("breakeven        :", breakeven)
print("profit at S=112  :", np.maximum(112 - K, 0) - premium)   # +4.0
print("profit at S=98   :", np.maximum(98  - K, 0) - premium)   # -3.0

for y, lbl in [(long_call,"long call"), (long_put,"long put"),
               (short_call,"short call"), (short_put,"short put")]:
    plt.plot(S, y, label=lbl)
plt.axhline(0, color="grey", lw=.8); plt.axvline(K, ls="--", color="grey", lw=.8)
plt.xlabel("terminal price $S_T$"); plt.ylabel("payoff"); plt.legend(); plt.show()

The four lines reproduce the figure in §1.3: long call and long put bend upward away from zero, the shorts are their negatives. Change K and premium and re-plot call_profit — watch the breakeven slide right as the premium grows. Building strategies later is just adding these arrays together.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The single feature that most fundamentally separates an option from a forward is that the option…
A. always settles in cash rather than physical delivery
B. gives a right rather than imposing an obligation on its holder
C. can only be traded on an exchange
D. has no underlying asset
Show answer

B. An option's holder may walk away; a forward binds both parties to trade. (A) settlement style varies for both and isn't the defining trait; (C) options trade both on exchanges and over-the-counter; (D) every derivative has an underlying.

Q2. A long call has strike K = $50 and the stock finishes at ST = $44. The payoff at expiry (ignoring premium) is…
A. −$6
B. $6
C. $0
D. $44
Show answer

C. A call pays max(ST − K, 0) = max(44 − 50, 0) = max(−6, 0) = 0. You simply don't exercise. (A) confuses payoff with the negative intrinsic of exercising into a loss — which you never do; (B) is the put's payoff here; (D) is the spot, not the payoff.

Q3. A call has strike $100, the stock trades at $93, and the call's premium is $2. Its intrinsic and time value are…
A. intrinsic $7, time value −$5
B. intrinsic $2, time value $0
C. intrinsic −$7, time value $9
D. intrinsic $0, time value $2
Show answer

D. The call is out-of-the-money (93 < 100), so intrinsic = max(93 − 100, 0) = $0, and the entire $2 premium is time value. Intrinsic value can never be negative (A, C); (B) wrongly treats the whole premium as intrinsic.

Q4. A farmer who will sell a crop in three months buys put options on that crop. The economic role of these options is best described as…
A. hedging / insurance against a price fall, with upside preserved
B. maximizing leverage on a rising price
C. committing to sell at a fixed price no matter what
D. eliminating the need to ever deliver the crop
Show answer

A. Long puts set a price floor while leaving the farmer free to sell higher if prices rise — classic hedging/insurance. (B) describes a speculative long call, not a producer's hedge; (C) describes a forward, which removes the upside; (D) puts don't change the physical sale, only its price protection.

Q5. You write (sell) a call for a $4 premium, strike $60. At expiry the stock is at $75. Your profit on the short call is…
A. +$15
B. −$11
C. +$4
D. −$15
Show answer

B. The short call pays −max(75 − 60, 0) = −$15 at expiry; add the $4 premium collected: −15 + 4 = −$11. (A) ignores your obligation and the premium; (C) is the best case only when the call expires worthless (ST ≤ 60); (D) forgets the premium you kept.

✓ What you learned

  • A derivative takes its value from an underlying; the two families are forwards/futures (obligations) and options (rights).
  • A long forward pays the symmetric line ST − K, while options keep only the favorable side: call = max(ST − K, 0), put = max(K − ST, 0).
  • The four basic positions — long/short call and put — are mirror images, and combine into every options strategy.
  • Profit = payoff − premium; premium splits into intrinsic + time value, and moneyness (ITM/ATM/OTM) says where spot sits versus strike.
  • Options exist for leverage, hedging, and insurance — and you plotted all four payoffs in NumPy + Matplotlib.
