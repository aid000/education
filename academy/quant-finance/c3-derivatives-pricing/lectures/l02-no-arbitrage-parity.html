No-Arbitrage & Put-Call Parity

Before any model, one ironclad rule prices derivatives: two portfolios with identical future payoffs must cost the same today — or someone is handing out free money.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the law of one price and the no-arbitrage principle, and explain why they hold in liquid markets without any probability model.
  • Build a replicating portfolio and use it to derive the fair forward price F = S·erT.
  • Derive and apply put-call parity: C − P = S − K·e−rT.
  • State the model-free no-arbitrage bounds that pin every European option between known limits.
  • Construct an explicit arbitrage trade — a conversion or reversal — when parity is violated.

2.1The Law of One Price & No-Arbitrage

Lecture 1 gave us payoff diagrams — the value of a call, a put, or a forward at expiry, as a function of the underlying price. The natural next question is: what should these contracts cost today, before we know how the underlying moves? Remarkably, much of the answer requires no forecast, no distribution, no model of the future at all. It follows from a single principle.

An arbitrage is a trade that costs nothing (or less than nothing) to enter and can never lose money, yet has a strictly positive chance of a payoff — a guaranteed "free lunch." The no-arbitrage principle says such opportunities cannot persist: the instant one appears, traders pile in, and their buying and selling pushes prices until the gap closes. We therefore price every derivative as if no arbitrage exists, because in a liquid market it essentially doesn't.

A direct corollary is the law of one price: if two portfolios deliver exactly the same cash flows in every future state of the world, they must trade at the same price now. If they didn't, you would buy the cheap one, sell the dear one, pocket the difference, and walk away with zero future risk — an arbitrage.

If payoffA(ω) = payoffB(ω) for every future state ω,  then  PriceA = PriceB today.This is the engine behind every pricing result in this course. Notice there is no probability anywhere — it holds whatever the odds.
AnalogyThink of two vending machines side by side. Both dispense the identical sealed bottle of water, guaranteed. If one charges \$1 and the other \$3, every shopper buys from the cheap machine and nobody touches the expensive one — so the \$3 price cannot survive. A derivative is just a "machine" that dispenses a state-dependent payoff; if you can build the same payoff more cheaply from other instruments, the expensive version must fall into line.

2.2Replicating Portfolios & the Forward Price

The law of one price only bites if we can actually build a matching payoff. A replicating portfolio is a basket of simpler instruments — typically the underlying asset plus borrowing or lending cash — whose future value copies the contract we want to price. Once we have a replica, the contract's fair price is just the replica's cost. This "build it from parts" move is the single most important technique in derivatives.

The cleanest example is a forward contract: an agreement struck today to buy one unit of a non-dividend-paying asset at a fixed delivery price F at time T, with no money changing hands now. What F makes this contract fair (worth zero today)?

Replicate the forward's payoff with a self-financing trade you can do right now. Today, borrow S dollars at the continuously compounded risk-free rate r and use it to buy one share at its spot price S. You have spent nothing of your own — the loan funded the purchase. At time T you hold the share and owe the loan back with interest, S·erT.

Borrow S, buy the asset  ⟶  at time T: own 1 share, owe S·erT cashThis "cash-and-carry" package has the identical payoff to being long a forward struck at F = S·erT: in both cases you end up owning the share having paid that amount.

Delivering into a forward with price F leaves you owning the share having paid F. The carry package leaves you owning the share having paid S·erT. Same asset, same final state — by the law of one price the forward must be costless only when its delivery price equals the carry cost:

F = S · erTThe fair forward price is the spot grown at the risk-free rate. Higher rates or longer maturities push F above spot; this gap is the cost of carry.

The logic is purely about not leaving free money on the table. If a dealer quoted a forward above S·erT, you would sell the rich forward and run the carry package to deliver into it, banking the difference risk-free. If quoted below, you reverse every leg. Either way the quote is forced back to S·erT.

Common mistakeThe forward price is not the market's forecast of the future spot. F = S·erT contains no view on whether the asset will rise or fall — it is a pure cost-of-carry relationship enforced by arbitrage. Two traders who violently disagree about tomorrow's price still agree on today's fair forward.

2.3Put-Call Parity

Now apply replication to options. Consider two portfolios over a European call and put on the same asset, same strike K, same expiry T. Let C and P be the call and put prices today, S the spot.

Portfolio A: own one call and lend K·e−rT in cash (which grows to exactly K at T). Portfolio B: own one put and one share of the underlying. Look at what each is worth at expiry, in the two possible regions of the final price ST:

At expiry TIf ST > KIf ST ≤ K
A: call + cash K(ST − K) + K = ST0 + K = K
B: put + share0 + ST = ST(K − ST) + ST = K

In every future state the two portfolios deliver the same value — max(ST, K). By the law of one price they must cost the same today. Equating the costs gives the celebrated put-call parity for a non-dividend-paying asset:

C + K·e−rT = P + S  ⟺   C − P = S − K·e−rTIt ties together four instruments — call, put, stock, bond — with no assumption about volatility, drift, or the price distribution. It is exact, not approximate.

Parity is enormously useful in practice. Quote a call and parity instantly hands you the matching put (and vice versa), so you never need to model both. It also reveals a hidden bond: rearranged, a call minus a put is a synthetic forward on the stock. Desks use parity as a sanity check on their quotes and as a recipe for manufacturing whichever leg a client wants out of the legs they already hold.

2.4No-Arbitrage Bounds & Building the Arbitrage

Even before any model gives an exact price, no-arbitrage alone boxes in every option. For a European call on a non-dividend-paying stock, the price can never be negative (you can throw the option away), can never exceed the stock itself (the call delivers at most the stock, and only after paying K), and can never sit below its discounted intrinsic value (else the synthetic-forward leg of parity would be free money):

max(S − K·e−rT, 0) ≤ C ≤ S     and     max(K·e−rT − S, 0) ≤ P ≤ K·e−rTThese hold with no distributional assumption. Any quote outside the box is itself an arbitrage.

The deepest payoff of this lecture is operational: when parity breaks, you can write down the exact trade that harvests the mispricing. Suppose the market shows the left side of parity rich — that is, the call-plus-bond package is dearer than the put-plus-share package:

C + K·e−rT > P + SThe synthetic security on the left is overpriced relative to the one on the right.

Sell the expensive side, buy the cheap side. This trade is called a conversion: write the call, borrow K·e−rT (sell the bond), buy the put, and buy the share. The opening cash flow is C + K·e−rT − P − S > 0 — a strictly positive amount in your pocket today. At expiry, as the parity table showed, the long put-plus-share exactly funds whatever the short call demands, and the loan is repaid from the K you set aside. Net future cash flow: zero in every state. Money today, no risk later — a textbook arbitrage. (If the inequality flips, you run the mirror trade, the reversal.)

Common mistakeDon't forget the time value of money when checking parity. Comparing C − P directly against S − K (using the raw strike instead of the discounted strike K·e−rT) will make almost every quote look mispriced. The discounting is doing essential work — it is the bond leg of the replica.
✦ Worked Example — spotting and trading a parity violation

A non-dividend stock trades at S = \$100. A 1-year European call and put, both struck at K = \$100, are quoted at C = \$10.00 and P = \$4.00. The risk-free rate is r = 5% (continuous). Is parity satisfied? If not, build the arbitrage.

  1. Discount the strike: K·e−rT = 100·e−0.05·1 = 100·0.95123 = \$95.123.
  2. Check parity, C − P vs S − K·e−rT: left side = 10.00 − 4.00 = 6.00; right side = 100 − 95.123 = 4.877. They disagree by \$1.123, so parity is violated.
  3. Identify the rich side: C − P (6.00) is too high relative to S − K·e−rT (4.877). Equivalently C + K·e−rT = 105.123 > P + S = 104.00 — the call-plus-bond package is the expensive one.
  4. Run the conversion: write the call (+\$10.00), buy the put (−\$4.00), buy the share (−\$100), and borrow 95.123 (+\$95.123). Net cash in today = 10 − 4 − 100 + 95.123 = +\$1.123.
  5. Confirm no future risk: at T you owe the loan 95.123·e0.05 = \$100 = K. If ST > 100 the short call delivers your share for K = 100, paying the loan exactly; the put expires worthless. If ST ≤ 100 the put lets you sell the share for K = 100, again paying the loan; the call expires worthless. Either way you end flat — having kept the \$1.123 risk-free.

🛠️ Hands-On: a put-call parity arbitrage checker

This is an early week, so set up the course Python stack once with the install block below. We'll write a tiny function that takes live-style quotes, tests parity, and — when it breaks — prints the exact conversion or reversal to run, then verifies the payoff is zero in both expiry regions.

⬇ One-time setup — the C3 Python stack (all free)

Create an isolated environment and install the free scientific stack used throughout this course:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Launch a notebook with jupyter notebook, or just run the script with python parity_check.py.

# parity_check.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

def check_parity(C, P, S, K, r, T, tol=1e-6):
    rhs   = S - K * np.exp(-r * T)        # S − K e^(−rT)
    lhs   = C - P                          # C − P
    gap   = lhs - rhs
    print(f"C - P            = {lhs:8.4f}")
    print(f"S - K*e^(-rT)    = {rhs:8.4f}")
    print(f"parity gap       = {gap:8.4f}")
    if abs(gap) < tol:
        print("=> parity holds, no arbitrage."); return
    side = "conversion (sell call+bond, buy put+share)" if gap > 0 \
           else "reversal (buy call+bond, sell put+share)"
    print(f"=> VIOLATION: run a {side}; lock-in = {abs(gap):.4f} per unit")

    # verify the conversion nets zero in BOTH expiry regions
    for ST in (K * 0.8, K * 1.2):          # below and above the strike
        call = max(ST - K, 0); put = max(K - ST, 0)
        # conversion: short call, long put, long share, short bond (owe K)
        payoff = -call + put + ST - K
        print(f"   S_T={ST:6.1f}  expiry payoff = {payoff:+.4f}")

check_parity(C=10.0, P=4.0, S=100.0, K=100.0, r=0.05, T=1.0)

The parity gap prints +1.1228, the script flags a conversion, and the expiry payoff is +0.0000 for both ST = 80 and ST = 120 — confirming the lock-in is risk-free. Feed it your own quotes; if the gap ever drifts past transaction costs, you've found a (fleeting) free lunch.

🔗 Where this is heading

No-arbitrage is the load-bearing wall of the whole specialization. Next week we let the stock move in a one-period binomial tree and replicate an option with a delta of shares plus a bond — the same "build it from parts" move, now state by state — which leads to risk-neutral pricing. Push the tree to a continuous limit and you reach Black–Scholes (Week 5), whose formula automatically satisfies the parity you derived here by hand. The conversion/reversal logic also underlies real desk activity: market-makers quote calls and puts off a single volatility surface precisely because parity chains them together, and any drift is arbitraged away in seconds.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The fair forward price of a non-dividend-paying asset, F = S·erT, is best described as…
A. the market's forecast of the spot price at time T
B. the spot price grown at the risk-free rate — a pure cost-of-carry relationship
C. always equal to the spot price S
D. the expected payoff discounted at the asset's growth rate
Show answer

B. F = S·erT comes from the cash-and-carry replication and contains no forecast (A is the classic misconception). It equals S only when rT = 0 (C is false in general). It is not a discounted expected payoff — no probabilities enter at all (D is wrong).

Q2. Put-call parity for a non-dividend stock states that C − P equals…
A. S − K·e−rT
B. S − K
C. K·e−rT − S
D. S + K·e−rT
Show answer

A. Parity is C − P = S − K·e−rT. Choice B forgets to discount the strike (the most common error). C has the sign of the whole expression flipped. D adds rather than subtracts the bond, which doesn't replicate anything.

Q3. Why must two portfolios with identical payoffs in every future state have the same price today?
A. Because their expected returns must match under the real-world probabilities
B. Because otherwise you could buy the cheaper and sell the dearer for a risk-free profit — an arbitrage
C. Because regulators require derivative prices to be consistent
D. Because volatility is the same for both
Show answer

B. This is the law of one price, enforced by no-arbitrage: any price gap between identical-payoff portfolios is itself a free lunch that traders instantly close. It needs no probabilities (A), no regulator (C), and says nothing about volatility (D).

Q4. You observe S = \$50, K = \$50, r = 4%, T = 1 yr, C = \$6, P = \$4. Is there an arbitrage, and on which side?
A. No arbitrage; parity holds exactly
B. Yes — C − P is too low; buy the call-plus-bond side (a reversal)
C. Cannot tell without knowing the volatility
D. Yes — C − P is too high; sell the call-plus-bond side (a conversion)
Show answer

D. C − P = 2.00; S − K·e−rT = 50 − 50·e−0.04 = 50 − 48.04 = 1.96. Since 2.00 > 1.96, the call-plus-bond side is rich, so you sell it (write call, sell bond) and buy the put-plus-share side — a conversion. B has the direction backwards; A is false because the sides differ; volatility is irrelevant to parity, so C is wrong.

Q5. Which of these is a valid model-free no-arbitrage bound on a European call (non-dividend stock)?
A. C can exceed the stock price S whenever volatility is high
B. C must equal its intrinsic value S − K at all times
C. C ≥ max(S − K·e−rT, 0) and C ≤ S, with no distributional assumption
D. C can be negative if the option is far out of the money
Show answer

C. A call is bounded below by its discounted intrinsic value (and 0) and above by the stock itself — both follow from no-arbitrage alone. The call can never exceed S (A is false) nor go negative (D is false, you can always discard it). It generally trades above intrinsic value because of time value, so B is wrong.
