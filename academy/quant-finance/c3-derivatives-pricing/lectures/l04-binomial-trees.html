Week 4 · Lecture 4

Multi-Period Binomial Trees

Glue many one-step bets end to end and a single fork blossoms into a lattice that prices almost any option — by walking the future backward into today's fair value.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Extend the one-period binomial model to a recombining multi-period tree of stock prices.
  • Price a European option by backward induction — discount the risk-neutral expectation node by node from expiry to today.
  • Distinguish European from American options and value early exercise by comparing continuation value against intrinsic value at every node.
  • Calibrate the up/down factors to volatility with u = eσ√Δt, d = 1/u, and recompute the risk-neutral probability per step.
  • Explain why a finer tree (more steps) converges to a single price — the bridge to Black–Scholes next week.

4.1From One Fork to a Lattice

Last week (Lecture 3) the world had exactly two outcomes: over one period the stock moved up to S·u or down to S·d, and we priced a derivative by finding the risk-neutral probability q that makes the discounted expected stock price equal today's price. One period is a toy. Real options live for months and the underlying takes many small steps along the way. The fix is mechanical: chain the one-period model to itself.

Split the option's life T into N equal steps of length Δt = T/N. At each step the price either multiplies by u or by d. After two steps the possible prices are S·u², S·u·d, and S·d². The crucial modeling choice is to set d = 1/u, so an up-move followed by a down-move (u·d = 1) lands you back exactly where you started. The tree recombines: an "up-then-down" path and a "down-then-up" path meet at the same node.

After n steps, j ups:  Sn,j = S0 · uj · d(n−j),   j = 0, 1, …, nA recombining tree has only n+1 nodes at step n — not 2n. That linear growth is what makes the method fast enough to use in practice.

This is the whole reason the binomial method is workable. A non-recombining tree of 50 steps would have 250 ≈ 1015 terminal nodes; a recombining one has just 51. The lattice is the data structure; pricing is what we do on it.

AnalogyThink of Pascal's triangle tilted on its side. Each node feeds two children, but children are shared between parents, so the rows widen by exactly one node at a time. The number of distinct paths that reach a given terminal node is the binomial coefficient C(n, j) sitting in that same triangle — which is no coincidence, since the count of up-moves is Binomial(n, q) under the risk-neutral measure.

4.2Backward Induction: Pricing on the Tree

We know the option's payoff at expiry exactly — for a call it is max(S − K, 0) at every terminal node. The problem is collapsing those known final values into a single number today. The answer is the same one-step argument from Lecture 3, applied repeatedly, right to left.

At any node, the option value one step in the future is one of two numbers: Vup if the stock rises, Vdown if it falls. The no-arbitrage value here and now is the risk-neutral expectation of those two, discounted back one step:

V = e−rΔt [ q·Vup + (1−q)·Vdown ]
q = ( erΔt − d ) / ( u − d )q is the per-step risk-neutral probability of an up-move; it depends only on r, u, d and Δt — never on the real-world odds or the stock's drift.

The recipe, then, is:

(1) Build the price lattice forward to expiry. (2) Write the payoff at each of the n+1 terminal nodes. (3) Sweep backward one column at a time, replacing each node with the discounted risk-neutral average of its two children. (4) The lone value left at the root is today's fair price.

This is dynamic programming: the value at a node depends only on the two nodes immediately to its right, so a single backward pass solves the whole problem in O(N²) work. Notice that the only probability that ever appears is q — the real probability p of an up-move never enters the price at all, exactly as risk-neutral pricing demands.

4.3European vs American: Pricing Early Exercise

A European option can be exercised only at expiry; an American option can be exercised at any time up to expiry. The binomial tree handles this distinction with one tiny change to the backward sweep — which is precisely why the lattice method earns its keep, because the Black–Scholes formula you will meet next week has no closed form for most American options.

For a European option, each node holds only the continuation value — the discounted risk-neutral average of its children. For an American option, at every node you also have the right to stop and collect the intrinsic value (max(S − K, 0) for a call, max(K − S, 0) for a put) right now. A rational holder takes whichever is larger:

Vnode = max( intrinsic value ,  e−rΔt[ q·Vup + (1−q)·Vdown ] )European: keep only the right-hand term. American: take the max of "exercise now" versus "hold."

Because the American holder has strictly more rights, an American option is never worth less than its European twin. A classic result: it is never optimal to exercise an American call on a non-dividend-paying stock early, so that call equals its European value. American puts, by contrast, can be worth more — deep in the money, grabbing the cash K now and earning interest beats waiting. The tree finds the optimal exercise boundary automatically, node by node.

Common mistakeDo not compare the intrinsic value against the terminal payoff — compare it against the continuation value at that same node, i.e. the discounted expected value of holding one more step. And recompute the option's future values with the American rule too: early-exercise decisions deeper in the tree change the continuation values that feed earlier nodes.

4.4Calibrating u, d to Volatility & Convergence

So far u and d were arbitrary. To make the tree describe a real asset, we tie them to its volatility σ. The standard Cox–Ross–Rubinstein (CRR) calibration matches the variance of the tree's log-return over one step to σ²Δt:

u = eσ√Δt,   d = e−σ√Δt = 1/u,   q = ( erΔt − d ) / ( u − d )Bigger σ ⇒ u and d spread farther apart, widening the cone of future prices. As Δt → 0 the up/down jumps shrink like √Δt — the same square-root-of-time scaling from Course 1.

Notice the √Δt: the step size shrinks like the square root of the time step, not linearly. That is the fingerprint of Brownian motion, and it is the reason the binomial tree converges to a continuous diffusion. With this calibration, q is automatically between 0 and 1 (a valid probability) as long as Δt is small enough that d < erΔt < u — which the no-arbitrage condition guarantees.

Now refine the tree: double N, then double it again. Each price estimate jitters but homes in on a single limit. As N → ∞, the binomial price of a European option converges to the Black–Scholes price — the CLT from Course 1 doing its work, since the sum of many small log-returns becomes normal. The convergence is oscillatory (odd vs even N straddle the limit) but the trend is unmistakable.

AnalogyRefining the tree is like increasing the frame rate of a film of the stock price. Two steps is a flip-book — jerky and crude. Five hundred steps is smooth motion: individual jumps become invisible and the path looks like the continuous wandering of Brownian motion. Black–Scholes is the "infinite frame rate" limit.
✦ Worked Example — a 2-step European call, calibrated to volatility

Price a 2-step European call. Spot S₀ = 100, strike K = 100, time to expiry T = 1 year (so Δt = 0.5), volatility σ = 20%, continuously-compounded rate r = 5%.

  1. Calibrate the factors: u = e0.20·√0.5 = e0.1414 = 1.1519; d = 1/u = 0.8681.
  2. Risk-neutral probability: erΔt = e0.025 = 1.02532. q = (1.02532 − 0.8681)/(1.1519 − 0.8681) = 0.15722/0.28386 = 0.5539.
  3. Build the price tree (forward): top S₀u² = 100·1.3269 = 132.69; middle S₀ud = 100.00; bottom S₀d² = 100·0.7536 = 75.36.
  4. Terminal payoffs (call, K = 100): top max(132.69−100,0) = 32.69; middle max(100−100,0) = 0; bottom 0.
  5. Step back to t = Δt: upper node Vu = e−0.025[0.5539·32.69 + 0.4461·0] = 0.97531·18.107 = 17.66. Lower node Vd = e−0.025[0.5539·0 + 0.4461·0] = 0.
  6. Step back to today: V₀ = e−0.025[0.5539·17.66 + 0.4461·0] = 0.97531·9.782 = $9.54.
  7. Sanity check: the exact Black–Scholes price for these inputs is ≈ $10.45. A 2-step tree is coarse, so $9.54 is in the right neighborhood but not yet converged — push N to a few hundred (see the code) and it climbs toward $10.45.

🛠️ Hands-On: a vectorized binomial pricer that converges

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + SciPy + Jupyter — the install block lives in C1 Lecture 1). Below is a CRR pricer that handles both European and American options and prints the convergence to Black–Scholes as N grows.

# binomial_pricer.py — C3 Lecture 4
import numpy as np
from scipy.stats import norm

def binomial_price(S0, K, T, r, sigma, N, call=True, american=False):
    dt = T / N
    u  = np.exp(sigma * np.sqrt(dt))
    d  = 1.0 / u
    q  = (np.exp(r * dt) - d) / (u - d)          # risk-neutral prob
    disc = np.exp(-r * dt)

    j = np.arange(N + 1)                          # number of up-moves
    ST = S0 * u**j * d**(N - j)                   # terminal prices
    V  = np.maximum(ST - K, 0) if call else np.maximum(K - ST, 0)

    for n in range(N - 1, -1, -1):                # backward induction
        V = disc * (q * V[1:n+2] + (1 - q) * V[0:n+1])
        if american:
            S = S0 * u**np.arange(n+1) * d**(np.arange(n+1)[::-1])
            intrinsic = np.maximum(S - K, 0) if call else np.maximum(K - S, 0)
            V = np.maximum(V, intrinsic)          # exercise vs hold
    return V[0]

def bs_call(S0, K, T, r, sigma):
    d1 = (np.log(S0/K) + (r + 0.5*sigma**2)*T) / (sigma*np.sqrt(T))
    d2 = d1 - sigma*np.sqrt(T)
    return S0*norm.cdf(d1) - K*np.exp(-r*T)*norm.cdf(d2)

args = dict(S0=100, K=100, T=1.0, r=0.05, sigma=0.20)
print("Black-Scholes call :", round(bs_call(**args), 4))
for N in (2, 10, 50, 200, 1000):
    print(f"binomial N={N:>4} :", round(binomial_price(**args, N=N), 4))

# American put is worth MORE than the European put:
print("Euro put  :", round(binomial_price(**args, N=500, call=False, american=False), 4))
print("Amer put  :", round(binomial_price(**args, N=500, call=False, american=True), 4))

The call row at N = 2 prints ≈ 9.54 (matching the worked example), then climbs and settles near the Black–Scholes 10.45 by N = 1000. The American put prints strictly above the European put — your code has just discovered the early-exercise premium without you telling it when to exercise.

🔗 Where this is heading

The convergence you just watched is the whole plot of Week 5: shrink Δt to zero and the recombining tree melts into Brownian motion, and the backward-induction formula becomes the celebrated Black–Scholes PDE and closed-form price. Q1. Why do we set d = 1/u in a standard binomial tree?
A. So the tree recombines — up-then-down returns to the start, giving n+1 nodes at step n instead of 2n
B. To force the risk-neutral probability q to equal 0.5
C. Because the real-world up probability must equal the down probability
D. To make the option always finish in the money
Show answer

A. With u·d = 1, an up move followed by a down move (or vice-versa) lands on the original price, so paths merge and the node count grows linearly, not exponentially. q is generally not 0.5 (B) — it depends on r, u, d. The real-world probability is irrelevant to pricing (C), and d = 1/u says nothing about moneyness (D).

Q2. Backward induction prices an option by…
A. averaging all terminal payoffs with the real-world probabilities, then discounting once
B. starting from known terminal payoffs and folding each node into the discounted risk-neutral average of its two children
C. simulating thousands of random price paths forward and averaging
D. solving a system of linear equations across all nodes simultaneously
Show answer

B. Backward induction sweeps right-to-left, replacing each node with e−rΔt[qVup+(1−q)Vdown]. It uses the risk-neutral q, not the real probability (A). Forward simulation is Monte Carlo (C); the tree is solved by a single recursive sweep, not a linear system (D).

Q3. At an American-option node, the value is…
A. always the intrinsic value, max(S−K,0)
B. always the continuation value, e−rΔt[qVup+(1−q)Vdown]
C. the maximum of the intrinsic value and the continuation value
D. the average of the intrinsic value and the continuation value
Show answer

C. A rational holder takes whichever is larger: exercise now (intrinsic) or hold (continuation). Choosing only one (A, B) ignores the holder's right to optimize; averaging them (D) has no economic meaning. The European value is just the special case that drops the intrinsic branch.

Q4. Under the CRR calibration with σ = 20% and Δt = 0.25, the up factor u equals…
A. e0.20·0.25 = 1.0513
B. e0.20·√0.25 = 1.1052
C. 1 + 0.20·0.25 = 1.05
D. e0.20/0.25 = 2.2255
Show answer

B. The CRR rule is u = eσ√Δt. With √0.25 = 0.5, u = e0.20·0.5 = e0.10 = 1.1052. Option A uses Δt instead of √Δt; C is a linear approximation that ignores the exponential; D divides by Δt, which is dimensionally wrong.

Q5. As the number of steps N grows large, the binomial price of a European call…
A. diverges to infinity because there are more nodes to sum
B. stops changing exactly after N = 2 steps
C. drifts steadily upward without ever settling
D. oscillates with shrinking amplitude and converges to the Black–Scholes price
Show answer

D. Odd and even N straddle the limit, so the estimate oscillates, but the amplitude shrinks and the series converges to Black–Scholes (the CLT at work). It does not diverge (A), a 2-step tree is far from converged (B), and the motion is convergent, not a steady one-way drift (C).
