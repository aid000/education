Pipeline, Strategies & Pairs Trading · Weeks 1–4
1. Which of these is the correct order of the trading pipeline?
Answer: C. Data feeds the signal, sizing turns the view into a position, execution applies real-world frictions, then P&L is tallied and finally evaluated. The other orderings all place a stage before something it depends on.
2. What most clearly distinguishes a strategy from a raw signal?
Answer: A. The strategy is the full machine that converts a view into a sized, costed, realized P&L stream. Machine learning is not required (B); a strategy is decidedly not guaranteed profitable (C); and neither signals nor strategies are bound to be discrete or continuous (D).
3. The core empirical claim of a momentum strategy is that…
Answer: D. Momentum bets on continuation: the sign of the recent move predicts the sign of the next move. (A) is mean-reversion/valuation, the opposite idea; (B) would make momentum impossible by construction; (C) confuses risk with direction.
4. A strategy ranks 500 stocks by trailing 12-month return, buys the top 50, and short-sells the bottom 50. This is…
Answer: B. Ranking assets against each other and going long the winners / short the losers is cross-sectional momentum. Time-series momentum (A) judges each asset against its own zero return, not against peers; (C) is an extreme-price rule; (D) is the opposite bet.
5. A price has a 20-day rolling mean of 50 and a rolling standard deviation of 4. It currently trades at 42. Its z-score is…
Answer: D. z = (P − μ)/σ = (42 − 50)/4 = −8/4 = −2.0, two standard deviations cheap. +2.0 (A) flips the sign; −8.0 (C) forgets to divide by σ; −0.5 (B) inverts the ratio.
6. Why is cointegration a stronger screening criterion for a pairs trade than correlation alone?
Answer: C. Cointegration is the structural claim that a linear combination of two non-stationary prices is itself stationary — a bounded, reverting spread. Correlation only describes co-movement and can be spuriously high between independent random walks. (A) and (D) are simply false; (B) confuses cointegration with a directional bet.
Backtester & Performance Metrics · Weeks 5–6
7. In a vectorized backtester, the single line that prevents look-ahead bias is…
Answer: A. Lagging the signal one bar means the position you hold during a bar was decided before that bar's return is known. (B) compounds into equity; (C) just computes asset returns; (D) builds the raw signal — none of these address timing.
8. The per-bar strategy return is computed as…
Answer: D. Your P&L over a bar is the position you already held times what the asset did during that bar. (C) uses the same-bar position, which is exactly the look-ahead bug; (A) drops the position entirely; (B) describes the equity curve, not a single-bar return.
9. Which is the strongest reason to choose an event-driven backtester design over a vectorized one?
Answer: B. Walking bar by bar lets the strategy keep state, so stops and account-aware sizing are natural, and the same class can later trade live. (A) and (C) are advantages of the vectorized approach, which is the fast one; (D) is nonsense — every backtest needs data.
10. A strategy turns $100,000 into $121,000 over exactly 2 years. Its annualized return is closest to…
Answer: C. Total return is 21%, so Rann = (1.21)1/2 − 1 = 1.1 − 1 = 10% exactly. Dividing 21% by 2 gives 10.5% (D) — that ignores compounding, the classic error. 21% (A) is the total, not annualized; 42% (B) doubles it the wrong way.
11. The key difference between the Sortino ratio and the Sharpe ratio is that the Sortino…
Answer: A. Sortino replaces total volatility with downside deviation, so big upside moves no longer count as "risk." Return over max drawdown (B) is the Calmar ratio. Both Sharpe and Sortino subtract the risk-free rate (C is false), and frequency (D) is irrelevant to the definition.
12. An equity curve peaks at $150,000, falls to $96,000, then recovers to a new high. The maximum drawdown over this episode is…
Answer: D. Drawdown is measured from the prior high-water mark: 96,000 / 150,000 − 1 = 0.64 − 1 = −36%. Dividing by the trough instead of the peak (B) and other mis-arithmetic (A, C) are the common mistakes here.
Transaction Costs · Week 7
13. Which trade cost grows fastest as you increase the size of a single order relative to the market's liquidity?
Answer: B. Market impact is the cost that punishes size — walking up the order book makes each extra share fill worse, rising roughly with √(Q/V). Commissions (A) and the spread (C) are largely fixed per trade regardless of size; the risk-free rate (D) is not a trading cost at all.
14. Inside a backtest, the standard per-period cost charged when the portfolio weight moves from wt−1 to wt is…
Answer: D. You pay for the amount you actually trade, which is the absolute change in position, |wt − wt−1|, times the cost rate. Charging on the level wt (A) or the sum (C) misstates turnover; (B) is dimensionally meaningless.
15. A strategy earns a gross annual return of 12%, charges an all-in cost of c = 0.0010 per unit of turnover, and trades with annual turnover T = 100. Its approximate net return is…
Answer: C. Cost drag = c × T = 0.0010 × 100 = 0.10 = 10%, so net return ≈ 12% − 10% = 2%. (A) ignores costs entirely; (B) is the drag itself, not the net return; (D) would need a higher cost rate or more turnover.
Overfitting & Walk-Forward Validation · Weeks 8–9
16. You test 80 independent, genuinely worthless strategies and keep any that pass at the 5% significance level. About how many do you expect to pass by chance alone?
Answer: A. The expected count of false winners is N·α = 80 × 0.05 = 4. 40 (B) would be N·0.5; the count is an expectation, not a fixed value like exactly 1 (C); and (D) is wrong because the 5% threshold lets worthless rules pass by luck.
17. A strategy posts a Sharpe of 2.3 in-sample but 0.2 out-of-sample. The most likely diagnosis is…
Answer: B. A large positive in-sample-minus-out-of-sample gap is the signature of overfitting. A one-sided bull market (A) would not specifically crush only the out-of-sample Sharpe; a formula error (C) would not produce a clean strong-then-weak split; ignored costs (D) would lower both windows similarly, not just one.
18. Using a Bonferroni correction, if you searched 40 strategy variants and want an overall false-positive rate of 5%, each variant should be tested at roughly…
Answer: C. Bonferroni uses α/N = 0.05/40 = 0.00125. Keeping 0.05 (A) ignores the search entirely; 0.25 (B) and 0.50 (D) loosen rather than tighten the bar, which is backwards.
19. A parameter-sensitivity plot shows performance that stays high across a wide band of lookback values rather than spiking at one. This is evidence the strategy is…
Answer: D. A broad plateau means performance does not depend on hitting one magic value — the hallmark of robustness. A lonely spike would suggest overfitting (A), but a plateau is the opposite; no backtest guarantees live profit (B); and a flat, wide response is a virtue, not a sign of too few parameters (C).
20. Why do simpler strategies with fewer free parameters tend to generalize better than heavily parameterized ones?
Answer: B. Data per parameter shrinks as parameters are added, so each one is pinned down by less information and mostly fits noise. In-sample performance is actually monotone in complexity (A is backwards — more knobs can only improve the fit to the past); (C) and (D) are simply false.
21. Why does ordinary shuffled k-fold cross-validation leak information on a price time series?
Answer: C. Shuffling destroys time order, so a training fold can contain timestamps later than test timestamps — pure look-ahead bias. (D) is about fold count, not the leak itself; (B) is the opposite of what shuffling does; (A) is an unrelated implementation detail.
22. Your label is the forward 10-day return. What does purging remove, and what does embargoing remove?
Answer: D. Purging drops training rows whose label window reaches into the test period (closing the backward leak); embargoing deletes a short buffer right after the test block so autocorrelation can't leak forward into later training rows. (A), (B), and (C) all describe deletions unrelated to label overlap or forward bleed.
Portfolio & Capstone · Week 10
23. Nine strategies are mutually uncorrelated, each with the same volatility and a Sharpe of 0.4, held in equal weight. The portfolio Sharpe is approximately…
Answer: A. For N uncorrelated, equal-vol, equal-weight strategies, Sharpe scales as √N times the single Sharpe: √9 × 0.4 = 3 × 0.4 = 1.2. (B) ignores the diversification benefit entirely; (C) uses N instead of √N; (D) divides instead of multiplying.
24. Under inverse-volatility weighting across a set of strategies, which one receives the largest capital allocation?
Answer: A. Weight is proportional to 1/σ, so the calmest strategy gets the most dollars, equalizing each strategy's risk contribution. (C) is backwards; (B) confuses risk weighting with return chasing; (D) describes equal-dollar weighting, the very thing risk budgeting replaces.
25. Which equity curve is the only legitimate one to put in a capstone performance report?
Answer: B. Only the out-of-sample, net-of-cost curve reflects what you could actually have traded. (A) is inflated by overfitting and ignored costs; (C) is cherry-picking (selection bias); (D) fits and tests on the same data, the cardinal sin walk-forward exists to prevent.
