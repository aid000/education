Momentum & Trend-Following

The oldest, simplest, and most stubbornly profitable idea in trading: what has been going up tends to keep going up — until, suddenly and violently, it doesn't.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define momentum and trend-following from first principles, and distinguish them from prediction of price level.
  • Contrast time-series momentum (an asset versus its own past) with cross-sectional momentum (an asset versus its peers).
  • Construct the two canonical entry rules — the moving-average crossover and the channel breakout — and write each as a signal.
  • Explain the leading behavioral and risk-based theories for why momentum persists, and where it fails.
  • Build a vectorized momentum signal in pandas and reason about how to choose the lookback window.

2.1What Momentum Actually Claims

In Week 1 you built a trading pipeline: data in, a signal in the middle, orders out. A strategy is just a rule for turning the signal into a position. Momentum is the simplest non-trivial rule there is, and it makes one bold empirical claim: the recent past direction of a price contains information about its near-future direction. If a market has trended up over the last several months, the momentum bet is that it keeps trending up a little longer.

Crucially, momentum does not try to forecast the level of a price ("oil belongs at $80"). That is the job of valuation and mean-reversion (next week). Momentum forecasts the sign of the next move from the sign of the recent move. It is a bet on continuation, not on a fair value.

Formally, define the trailing return over a lookback window of L periods. If it is positive, go long; if negative, go short (or flat). Using log-returns r so they add cleanly over time:

momt(L) = ln(Pt / Pt−L) = Σi=t−L+1t ri
positiont = sign(momt(L))The signal is the trailing L-period return; the position is its sign. Everything else in momentum is a refinement of this one line.

Trend-following is the practitioner's name for the same idea, usually applied across many markets (equities, bonds, currencies, commodities) with explicit risk controls. "Momentum" tends to be the academic term for the measured premium; "trend-following" is the live strategy that harvests it. We will use them almost interchangeably and flag the difference where it matters.

AnalogyMomentum is the flywheel of markets. A heavy spinning wheel resists changes to its motion: push it and it keeps turning long after your hand leaves. Crowds of investors behave the same way — flows, mandates, and slow-diffusing news keep a move going past the point where the original reason has been priced in. The momentum trader rides the wheel; the danger is being on it when it abruptly jams.

2.2Time-Series vs Cross-Sectional Momentum

There are two distinct ways to ask "is this trending," and confusing them is the most common beginner error in this space.

Time-series momentum (absolute)

Time-series momentum compares an asset only to its own past. Was this asset's trailing return positive? Then own it. Each market is judged on an absolute basis, in isolation. A portfolio can end up long everything (in a broad rally) or short everything (in a broad sell-off). This is the engine of classic trend-following across futures.

wt(i) = sign( rt−L:t(i) )     (each asset i judged against zero)

Cross-sectional momentum (relative)

Cross-sectional momentum compares assets to each other. Rank a universe by trailing return, go long the top winners and short the bottom losers. The bet is relative — winners keep beating losers — and the book is roughly market-neutral by construction, since you are long and short at the same time.

rank assets by rt−L:t → long top decile, short bottom decileCross-sectional momentum is a relative bet and is naturally dollar-balanced; time-series momentum is an absolute bet whose net exposure floats.

The two often disagree. In a market where everything falls but tech falls least, cross-sectional momentum is long tech (it is the relative winner) while time-series momentum is short tech (its absolute return is negative). Neither is "right" — they answer different questions and carry different risks.

Common mistakeDo not assume cross-sectional momentum protects you in a crash. Because it is long the recent winners, it can be heavily exposed to a sudden reversal where yesterday's losers violently rebound — the notorious "momentum crash." Market-neutral does not mean risk-free; it means a different risk.

2.3Two Canonical Signals: Crossover & Breakout

The raw trailing-return rule of §2.1 is noisy. Two classic constructions smooth it into tradeable entry/exit signals.

Moving-average crossover

A simple moving average (SMA) of window n is the mean of the last n closes — a low-pass filter that strips out day-to-day noise and leaves the slow drift. The crossover rule uses two of them, a fast (short window, responsive) and a slow (long window, steady):

SMAn(t) = (1/n) Σi=0n−1 Pt−i
long when SMAfast > SMAslow,   flat/short when SMAfast < SMAslowThe fast average sits above the slow one precisely when the recent trend is up — a smoothed version of "the trailing return is positive."

The fast line crossing above the slow line ("golden cross") is a long entry; crossing below ("death cross") exits or flips short. A popular pair is the 50-day and 200-day average. Note that a crossover is mathematically equivalent to a sign rule on a weighted trailing return — it is momentum in disguise, with the windows controlling how much smoothing you buy at the cost of lag.

Channel breakout

A breakout rule ignores averages and watches extremes. Go long when the price closes above its highest high of the last N periods (a new N-day high); exit or reverse when it closes below the lowest low. The logic: a fresh extreme says the prior range has been decisively broken, and the move that broke it tends to continue.

long when Pt > max(Pt−N … Pt−1)    exit when Pt < min(Pt−N … Pt−1)

Crossovers tend to fire smoothly and lag turns; breakouts fire sharply and can whipsaw in range-bound markets but catch trends early. Both are continuation bets — just different ways of declaring "the trend is now real."

2.4Why Does Momentum Persist? And the Lookback Window

If buying winners simply worked, an efficient market should arbitrage the edge away. Yet time-series and cross-sectional momentum have been documented across centuries, continents, and asset classes. Two families of explanation compete — and they are not mutually exclusive.

Behavioral explanations

Under-reaction: news diffuses slowly. Investors anchor to old beliefs and update too gradually, so prices drift toward fair value over weeks and months instead of jumping there — leaving a trail of continuation to ride. Herding and over-reaction: later, momentum traders and trend-chasing flows pile in, pushing the price past fair value and setting up the eventual reversal. The drift is the edge; the overshoot is the risk.

Risk-based explanations

Perhaps momentum is not a free lunch but compensation for a risk nobody wants: the strategy delivers steady gains punctuated by rare, brutal crashes (it is short a "crash-tail"). In this view the premium is the rent you earn for holding a position that occasionally detonates — much like selling insurance. Both stories predict the same headline edge but very different tail behavior.

AnalogyChoosing the lookback window is tuning a radio dial. Too short (a few days) and you pick up mostly static — noise the strategy mistakes for trend, generating churn and transaction costs. Too long (multiple years) and you are so smoothed that you only notice a trend after it is over. The classic equity momentum signal tunes to a 3-to-12-month band, which empirically carries the clearest trend signal.

Choosing the lookback window L

The window L is the single most important knob. Short L makes the signal responsive but noisy and trade-heavy; long L makes it stable but slow to enter and exit. A well-known wrinkle: in equity cross-sectional momentum the most recent month is usually skipped (rank on months t−12 to t−2, not t−12 to t) because the latest month tends to mean-revert, contaminating the trend signal.

Common mistakeDo not pick L by trying dozens of values on one price history and keeping the best Sharpe — that is overfitting, and the "optimal" window will not survive out-of-sample. Choose L from economic reasoning (the horizon over which the under-reaction story plausibly operates) and confirm robustness across nearby windows and across markets, not a single lucky peak.
✦ Worked Example — a 12-month time-series momentum signal

An index has these month-end prices. Using a 12-month lookback (skipping the most recent month), decide the position to hold next month and size it by inverse volatility.

  1. Prices: 12 months ago Pt−12 = 100; last month Pt−1 = 118; today Pt = 115.
  2. Signal return (skip the last month): use months t−12 to t−1, so r = ln(Pt−1/Pt−12) = ln(118/100) = ln(1.18) ≈ +0.1655 — a positive trailing return.
  3. Direction: sign(+0.1655) = +1 → the time-series momentum position is long, even though the most recent month (118 → 115) ticked down. Skipping that month is exactly why the short-term wobble does not flip the trade.
  4. Volatility sizing: suppose realized annualized volatility is σ = 16% and our risk target is 10%. The position scalar is target/σ = 0.10 / 0.16 ≈ 0.625 — hold 0.625 units of notional so the trade contributes the intended risk.
  5. Resulting position: +0.625 (long, scaled down because this market is more volatile than our target). A calmer market with σ = 8% would get scalar 0.10/0.08 = 1.25 — a larger long for the same signal.
  6. Read-through: the sign came from momentum; the size came from risk. Real trend-followers always split the decision this way — direction from the signal, magnitude from volatility.

🛠️ Hands-On: build a momentum signal in pandas

We compute both a trailing-return momentum signal and a 50/200 SMA crossover on a price series. The key discipline: shift the signal by one bar so today's position is decided from yesterday's data — never peek at the close you are about to trade on.

# momentum_signal.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

# daily closes indexed by date (load your own CSV in practice)
px = pd.read_csv("prices.csv", index_col="date", parse_dates=True)["close"]

# --- 1) time-series momentum: sign of trailing L-day log return ---
L = 126                                  # ~6 trading months
logret   = np.log(px).diff()
mom      = np.log(px).diff(L)            # trailing L-day log return
ts_signal = np.sign(mom)                 # +1 long / -1 short / 0 flat

# --- 2) moving-average crossover signal ---
fast = px.rolling(50).mean()
slow = px.rolling(200).mean()
ma_signal = np.where(fast > slow, 1.0, -1.0)
ma_signal = pd.Series(ma_signal, index=px.index)

# --- 3) AVOID LOOKAHEAD: trade tomorrow on today's signal ---
position    = ts_signal.shift(1)         # shift by one bar!
strat_ret   = position * logret          # strategy daily log return

print("days long :", int((position == 1).sum()))
print("days short:", int((position == -1).sum()))
print("ann. mean :", strat_ret.mean() * 252)
print("ann. vol  :", strat_ret.std()  * np.sqrt(252))

Change L to 21, 63, 126, 252 (1, 3, 6, 12 months) and watch the trade count and volatility shift — short windows churn, long windows lag. The .shift(1) is not optional: drop it and your backtest will look spectacular and be a fiction, because it trades on information it would not have had in time.

Q1. The core empirical claim of a momentum strategy is that…
A. a price always returns to its long-run fair value
B. the recent direction of a price has information about its near-future direction
C. prices follow a perfect random walk with no exploitable structure
D. volatility predicts the level of returns
Show answer

B. Momentum bets on continuation: the sign of the recent move predicts the sign of the next. (A) is mean-reversion/valuation, the opposite idea; (C) would make momentum impossible; (D) confuses risk with direction.

Q2. A strategy ranks 500 stocks by trailing 12-month return, buys the top 50 and short-sells the bottom 50. This is…
A. cross-sectional momentum
B. time-series momentum
C. a channel breakout
D. a mean-reversion strategy
Show answer

A. Ranking assets against each other and going long winners / short losers is cross-sectional momentum. Time-series momentum (B) judges each asset against its own zero return, not against peers; (C) is an extreme-price rule; (D) is the opposite bet.

Q3. In a moving-average crossover, a long signal is generated when…
A. the slow average crosses above the fast average
B. price touches the lower band of its range
C. the fast average crosses above the slow average
D. both averages are flat
Show answer

C. The fast (short-window) average rising above the slow (long-window) one — the "golden cross" — signals an upturn and goes long. (A) reverses fast and slow and would be an exit/short; (B) is a mean-reversion idea; (D) gives no trend signal at all.

Q4. Why is the most recent month often skipped when forming an equity cross-sectional momentum signal?
A. Because exchanges do not report the latest month's data in time
B. Because skipping it doubles the Sharpe ratio guaranteed
C. Because momentum only works on monthly data
D. Because the most recent month tends to mean-revert and contaminates the trend signal
Show answer

D. The latest month shows short-term reversal, so including it weakens the longer-horizon trend signal — hence ranking on t−12 to t−2. (A) is false; (B) is an unfounded guarantee; (C) is false — momentum works at many frequencies.

Q5. You test 40 different lookback windows on one price series and keep the one with the highest backtest Sharpe. The main danger is that…
A. the chosen window is overfit and unlikely to perform out-of-sample
B. a longer window always beats a shorter one
C. momentum has no behavioral explanation
D. volatility sizing becomes impossible
Show answer

A. Cherry-picking the best of many windows on one history fits noise, not signal — it rarely survives out-of-sample. (B) is false (there is no universally best length); (C) and (D) are unrelated to the search procedure.
