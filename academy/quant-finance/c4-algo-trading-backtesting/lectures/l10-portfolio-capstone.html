Portfolio of Strategies & Capstone Backtest

One good strategy is fragile; a basket of uncorrelated ones is a business — this is the week you assemble them into a single capstone backtester and ship the report.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why combining several imperfectly correlated strategies raises the portfolio Sharpe ratio even when no single strategy improves.
  • Compute the volatility and Sharpe of a blended return stream from the covariance of its components.
  • Allocate capital across strategies by risk budgeting — equal-risk and inverse-volatility weighting — rather than naive equal dollars.
  • Assemble a full capstone backtester: signal layer, walk-forward validation, realistic costs, and a one-page performance report.
  • Run a multi-strategy walk-forward backtest in Python with vectorbt and reconcile the blended equity curve against its parts.

10.1Why Hold a Portfolio of Strategies?

Over the last nine weeks you built individual strategies — a momentum rule (Week 2), a pairs trade (Week 4), and others — each with its own edge and its own bad stretches. The hard truth is that any single strategy is fragile: it has regimes where it loses, drawdowns that test your nerve, and a real chance the edge quietly decays. Running one strategy is a bet; running several uncorrelated ones is a business.

The reason is the same diversification math behind a stock portfolio (Markowitz, Course 2), but applied to return streams instead of assets. If two strategies each earn the same expected return but stumble at different times, blending them keeps the average return while smoothing the path. A smoother path means a higher Sharpe ratio, smaller drawdowns, and — crucially — a strategy you can actually stick with through a rough quarter.

Treat each strategy as if it were a synthetic asset whose "price" is its equity curve. The daily P&L stream of strategy i has a mean return μi, a volatility σi, and a correlation ρij with every other strategy. Everything about the combined book follows from those numbers.

AnalogyThink of strategies as a relay team, not a single sprinter. A lone sprinter who pulls a hamstring loses the whole race. A relay of four runners — each strong on a different leg, each tiring at different moments — finishes reliably even when one has an off day. You don't need every runner to be world-class; you need them to fail at different times.

10.2Diversification Across Uncorrelated Signals

Put equal capital in two strategies with the same volatility σ and correlation ρ. The blended daily return is rp = ½r1 + ½r2, and its variance follows directly from the rules of variance you met in Course 1:

σp2 = ¼σ2 + ¼σ2 + 2·½·½·ρσ2 = ½σ2(1 + ρ)Two equal-weight, equal-vol strategies. The cross term carries the correlation — it is the only place ρ enters.

Read the extremes. If the strategies are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1), σp2 = σ2 — no benefit at all; you just own the same thing twice. If they are uncorrelated (ρ = 0), σp = σ/√2 ≈ 0.707σ — you cut risk by ~30% for free. If they are negatively correlated (ρ < 0), the risk falls even further. Expected return is unchanged at the average, so a lower σ at the same μ means a strictly higher Sharpe.

Generalize to N strategies that are each volatility σ, mutually uncorrelated, equally weighted. The portfolio volatility collapses as the now-familiar 1/√N:

σp = σ / √N  ⟹  Sharpep = √N · SharpesingleUncorrelated, equal-vol, equal-weight. Adding a 4th independent strategy doubles the Sharpe of a single one.

This is the single most valuable equation in the course: uncorrelated edges add Sharpe like the square root of their count. Four genuinely independent strategies, each with a mediocre Sharpe of 0.5, combine into a book with Sharpe 1.0. The catch lives entirely in the word uncorrelated — and in practice correlations are never zero and never stable.

Common mistakeDo not confuse number of strategies with diversification. Ten momentum strategies on ten tickers are nearly the same trade — their correlations cluster near 1, so √N buys you almost nothing. What diversifies is distinct return drivers (a trend-follower, a mean-reverter, a carry trade), not more copies of one idea. Always measure the correlation matrix; never assume it.

10.3Capital Allocation & Risk Budgeting

Once you have several strategies, how much capital should each get? The naive answer — equal dollars — is usually wrong, because a volatile strategy and a calm one with the same dollar allocation contribute wildly unequal risk. A book that is "1/3 in each" can in fact be 80% driven by its single jumpiest strategy.

The fix is to budget risk, not dollars. The simplest practical rule is inverse-volatility weighting: give each strategy a weight proportional to 1/σi, so the calm strategies get more capital and the wild ones get less, equalizing their dollar-risk contributions:

wi = (1/σi) / Σj (1/σj)Inverse-vol weighting. When all correlations are equal, this is also the equal-risk-contribution (risk-parity) solution.

A strategy's risk contribution is the share of total portfolio variance it is responsible for, accounting for its correlations with the others. Formally, the contribution of strategy i is wi times the partial derivative of portfolio volatility with respect to wi:

RCi = wi · (Σw)i / σp,   Σi RCi = σpΣ is the covariance matrix of strategy returns; (Σw)i is row i of Σw. Risk contributions sum to total portfolio vol.

An equal-risk-contribution (risk-parity) book chooses weights so every RCi is identical — no single strategy dominates the risk. Beyond that you can apply risk limits: cap any one strategy at, say, 40% of total risk, scale total exposure so the book targets a fixed annualized volatility (e.g. 10%), and de-lever when realized vol spikes. Kelly sizing (Week 8) sets the aggregate leverage; risk budgeting sets the split beneath it.

AnalogyRisk budgeting is a household budget for danger, not for dollars. You wouldn't give your reckless teenager and your careful retiree the same credit limit just because they're both family members. You hand out spending power in proportion to how much trouble each can cause — that's exactly what dividing by σi does.

10.4The Capstone: A Full Backtester

The capstone — due this week — is the payoff of the whole course: a single, honest backtester that turns raw price data into a defensible performance report. It strings together every technique you've learned into four layers, each guarding against a way backtests lie.

1. Signal layer

Each strategy maps price history to a target position (long, short, flat, or a sized weight). Signals must use only information available at the time — no peeking at tomorrow's close. This is where look-ahead bias creeps in; lag every feature by one bar.

2. Walk-forward validation

From Week 9: never fit and test on the same data. Tune parameters on an in-sample window, then trade them untouched on the next out-of-sample window, rolling forward through history. The concatenated out-of-sample equity curve is the only curve you are allowed to report.

3. Realistic costs

Every trade pays the spread, commission, and slippage. A strategy that looks brilliant gross can be a loser net. Model costs as a charge per unit of turnover (Week 5):

net return ≈ gross return − turnover × (½ spread + commission + slippage)Turnover = fraction of the book traded. High-frequency, high-turnover signals are punished hardest by costs.

4. Performance report

Reduce the out-of-sample, net-of-cost equity curve to numbers a reader can judge in thirty seconds: annualized return and volatility, Sharpe and Sortino, max drawdown and its recovery time, hit rate, turnover, and the per-strategy correlation matrix that justifies the blend.

Common mistakeReporting the in-sample, gross Sharpe. It is almost always inflated by overfitting and by ignored costs, sometimes turning a true Sharpe of 0.3 into a fantasy 2.0. The grade — and reality — only rewards the out-of-sample, net-of-cost numbers. If you remember one rule from this course: the equity curve you trade is the one built on data the strategy never saw.
✦ Worked Example — blending three strategies by risk budget

You have three out-of-sample strategy streams with annualized stats: a trend-follower (μ=8%, σ=16%), a mean-reverter (μ=6%, σ=8%), and a carry trade (μ=5%, σ=10%). Pairwise correlations are all ρ=0.1. Risk-free rate is 0. Build an inverse-volatility book and find its Sharpe.

  1. Single-strategy Sharpes: 8/16 = 0.50, 6/8 = 0.75, 5/10 = 0.50. Decent, none spectacular.
  2. Inverse-vol weights: 1/σ = 1/16, 1/8, 1/10 = 0.0625, 0.1250, 0.1000; sum = 0.2875. Weights w = (0.217, 0.435, 0.348). The calm mean-reverter rightly gets the most capital.
  3. Portfolio return: μp = 0.217·8 + 0.435·6 + 0.348·5 = 1.74 + 2.61 + 1.74 = 6.09%.
  4. Portfolio variance: Σ wi2σi2 = 0.217²·16² + 0.435²·8² + 0.348²·10² = 12.05 + 12.11 + 12.11 = 36.3 (note the equal risk contributions — that's the point). Cross terms: 2·ρ·Σi<j wiwjσiσj = 2·0.1·(0.217·0.435·16·8 + 0.217·0.348·16·10 + 0.435·0.348·8·10) = 2·0.1·(12.08 + 12.08 + 12.11) = 7.26. Total = 43.6.
  5. Portfolio vol and Sharpe: σp = √43.6 ≈ 6.6%. Sharpe = 6.09 / 6.6 ≈ 0.92 — higher than the best single strategy (0.75) and far above the average. Low correlation plus risk budgeting bought roughly a 30% Sharpe lift for free.

🛠️ Hands-On: a multi-strategy walk-forward backtest

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, vectorbt — the install details lives in Lecture 1). We build two simple strategy signals, blend them by inverse volatility, deduct costs on turnover, and report the out-of-sample Sharpe.

# capstone_portfolio.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, vectorbt as vbt

# 1. DATA — daily closes for one liquid instrument (offline CSV or vbt fetch)
price = pd.read_csv("prices.csv", index_col=0, parse_dates=True)["close"]
ret   = price.pct_change().fillna(0.0)

# 2. SIGNAL LAYER — two distinct return drivers, both lagged 1 bar (no look-ahead)
fast, slow = price.rolling(20).mean(), price.rolling(100).mean()
mom   = np.sign(fast - slow).shift(1).fillna(0.0)          # trend-follower: +1/-1
z     = (price - price.rolling(20).mean()) / price.rolling(20).std()
rev   = (-np.sign(z)).shift(1).fillna(0.0)                 # mean-reverter: fade extremes

# 3. RISK BUDGET — inverse-vol weights from a trailing window (recomputed daily)
pnl   = pd.DataFrame({"mom": mom * ret, "rev": rev * ret})
vol   = pnl.rolling(60).std().shift(1)                     # trailing vol, lagged
w     = (1.0 / vol).div((1.0 / vol).sum(axis=1), axis=0).fillna(0.5)
gross = (w * pnl).sum(axis=1)                              # blended gross return

# 4. COSTS — charge per unit of turnover (change in net position)
pos   = (w["mom"] * mom + w["rev"] * rev)
cost  = pos.diff().abs().fillna(0.0) * 0.0005              # 5 bps round-turn proxy
net   = gross - cost

# 5. WALK-FORWARD-STYLE REPORT — evaluate only the out-of-sample tail
oos   = net.iloc[252:]                                     # skip the first year (warm-up)
sharpe = oos.mean() / oos.std() * np.sqrt(252)
equity = (1 + oos).cumprod()
dd     = (equity / equity.cummax() - 1).min()
print(f"OOS net Sharpe : {sharpe:5.2f}")
print(f"max drawdown   : {dd:6.1%}")
print(f"corr(mom,rev)  : {pnl['mom'].corr(pnl['rev']):5.2f}")  # justify the blend

The blended net Sharpe should land above either single signal's iff the printed correlation is low. Swap in your own strategies, lengthen the warm-up, and replace the crude 5 bps cost with the spread+slippage model from Week 5 — that scaffold is the capstone. For production-grade walk-forward splits, vectorbt's Splitter automates the rolling in-sample / out-of-sample windows.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Four strategies are mutually uncorrelated, each with volatility σ and Sharpe 0.5, held in equal weight. What is the portfolio Sharpe?
A. 0.5 (diversification can't raise Sharpe)
B. 1.0
C. 2.0
D. 0.25
Show answer

B. For N uncorrelated, equal-vol, equal-weight strategies, Sharpe scales as √N times the single Sharpe: √4 · 0.5 = 2 · 0.5 = 1.0. A (no lift) ignores the diversification benefit; C uses √N=4 instead of 2; D divides instead of multiplying.

Q2. Two equal-weight strategies each have volatility σ. If their correlation is ρ = 1, the blended volatility is…
A. σ/√2
B. 0
C. σ
D. 2σ
Show answer

C. σp² = ½σ²(1+ρ) = ½σ²(2) = σ², so σp = σ. Perfectly correlated strategies give no diversification — you own the same thing twice. A is the ρ=0 case; B would need ρ=−1; D wrongly adds volatilities.

Q3. Under inverse-volatility weighting, which strategy receives the largest capital allocation?
A. The one with the lowest volatility
B. The one with the highest past return
C. The one with the highest volatility
D. They all get equal capital by definition
Show answer

A. Weight is proportional to 1/σ, so the lowest-volatility (calmest) strategy gets the most dollars — equalizing each strategy's risk contribution. C is backwards; B confuses risk weighting with return chasing; D describes equal-dollar weighting, the very thing risk budgeting replaces.

Q4. Which equity curve is the only legitimate one to put in your capstone performance report?
A. The in-sample, gross-of-cost curve
B. The best single backtest run out of 50 tried
C. The full-sample curve with the optimal parameters
D. The out-of-sample, net-of-cost walk-forward curve
Show answer

D. Only the out-of-sample, net-of-cost curve reflects what you could actually have traded. A is inflated by overfitting and ignored costs; B is cherry-picking (selection bias); C fits and tests on the same data, the cardinal sin walk-forward exists to prevent.

Q5. You add a fifth strategy to a four-strategy book, but it is 0.95-correlated with an existing one. The expected effect on portfolio Sharpe is…
A. A large increase, because more strategies always help
B. The Sharpe must fall to zero
C. Little to none, because it adds almost no independent return driver
D. It guarantees the Sharpe doubles
Show answer

C. The √N benefit requires uncorrelated streams. A 0.95-correlated strategy is nearly a duplicate, so it contributes negligible diversification — just more of the same trade. A and D wrongly assume count alone drives Sharpe; B is false, since a near-duplicate doesn't destroy the existing edge.
