Week 1 · Lecture 1

From Signals to Strategies: The Trading Pipeline

A noticed pattern is just a hunch; a strategy is the whole machine that turns that hunch into a sized position, a filled order, and a measured profit or loss.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Map the end-to-end quant pipeline: data → signal → position sizing → execution → P&L → evaluation.
  • Distinguish a raw signal from a tradable strategy, and explain what each stage adds.
  • Define alpha and risk, and compute the P&L of long and short positions.
  • Contrast vectorized and event-driven backtesting and know when each is appropriate.
  • Run a minimal vectorized backtest in pandas/NumPy that turns a signal into a P&L curve.

1.1The Pipeline: Data to Evaluation

Algorithmic trading sounds glamorous, but underneath it is a plain assembly line. A number comes in, gets transformed at each station, and a profit-or-loss number comes out the other end. Everything in this course is one of those stations or the glue between them. The line has six stages:

StageWhat it doesOutput
1. DataClean, aligned prices/volumes/fundamentals indexed by timea tidy time series
2. SignalA rule or model that reads the data and emits a view (buy / sell / how strong)a number per asset per time
3. Position sizingTurns the view into how much to hold, given risk limits and capitala target weight or share count
4. ExecutionSends orders and accounts for slippage, fees, and fill delayactual holdings & trade costs
5. P&LMultiplies positions by realized returns, subtracts costsa daily/period return stream
6. EvaluationSummarizes that return stream into risk-adjusted metricsSharpe, drawdown, hit rate…

The crucial mental shift for a beginner is that profit lives at the end of the line, not the start. A clever signal that is sized wrong, executed expensively, or evaluated naïvely can lose money. Most of the work — and most of the ways to lose money — happen after the idea.

AnalogyThink of a kitchen. The signal is the recipe idea ("seafood pasta sounds good tonight"). But you still need ingredients (data), a sense of how much to cook for the number of guests (sizing), a stove and the time to actually cook (execution), the finished plates (P&L), and finally diners rating the meal (evaluation). A brilliant recipe served raw, cold, or in the wrong portion still earns a bad review.

1.2Signal vs. Strategy

Beginners conflate the two constantly, so pin down the difference now. A signal is a single number that expresses a view at a point in time — for example, "the 10-day average price is above the 50-day average, so trend is up." On its own it is just an opinion; it specifies neither how much to trade nor what it costs nor whether it actually made money.

A strategy is the full mapping from signals all the way to a realized return stream. Formally, if st is the signal and rt+1 is next period's asset return, the strategy first converts the signal into a position (a weight wt of capital) and then earns:

strategy return:  Rt+1 = wt · rt+1 − coststThe position wt is decided using only information available at time t — never with tomorrow's return.

That last clause is the soul of backtesting. The signal is computed from the past, the position is set, and only then does the future return arrive. Mixing those time stamps up — peeking at rt+1 when forming wt — is the single most common way a backtest lies to you.

Common mistakeTreating "the signal is positive" as if it were "the strategy is profitable." A signal can be statistically real yet unprofitable once you add transaction costs, sizing, and realistic execution. Always carry the idea all the way to the P&L line before you celebrate.

1.3Alpha vs. Risk, Long vs. Short

Two forces oppose each other at every stage. Alpha is the return your skill adds beyond simply holding the market — the edge in the signal. Risk is the variability you must endure to harvest it. A strategy is worth running only when its alpha is large relative to its risk, which is why we evaluate with risk-adjusted ratios rather than raw return.

The cleanest summary is the Sharpe ratio: average excess return per unit of volatility. Higher means more reward for each unit of stomach-churning.

Sharpe = (E[R] − rf) / σRR is the strategy return, rf the risk-free rate, σR its volatility. Annualize a daily Sharpe by multiplying by √252.

To express a view you take a position. Going long (positive weight, w > 0) profits when the price rises; going short (negative weight, w < 0) profits when the price falls — you borrow the asset, sell it, and buy it back cheaper. Both directions earn the same formula, because the sign of the weight does the work:

P&L = w · (Pexit − Pentry) / Pentry = w · rLong w = +1 wins if r > 0; short w = −1 wins if r < 0. One formula, both directions.

The ability to go short is what lets a quant profit in falling markets and build market-neutral books (longs and shorts that cancel out broad-market exposure, isolating the alpha). It also doubles the ways to lose: a short position's loss is unbounded, since a price can rise without limit.

Common mistakeJudging a strategy by total return alone. A book that returned 40% with terrifying 30% swings is usually worse than one that returned 15% with 8% swings — the second has the higher Sharpe and is far easier to hold (and to leverage). Always divide return by the risk taken to earn it.

1.4Vectorized vs. Event-Driven Backtesting

A backtest replays history through your strategy to estimate how it would have performed. There are two architectures, and choosing the wrong one wastes weeks.

A vectorized backtest treats the whole price history as arrays and computes positions and returns in a few bulk operations — multiply the position column by the next-period return column, subtract costs, and sum. It is blazingly fast and perfect for research: you can sweep thousands of parameter combinations in seconds. The price you pay is realism — it assumes you can hold an exact target weight every period and glosses over order-by-order mechanics.

An event-driven backtest steps through time one event at a time (a new bar, a new tick, a fill), updating a simulated portfolio as a live system would. It is slower but faithfully models partial fills, queue position, cash constraints, and path-dependent logic. It is what you graduate to before deploying — and it shares code with the live trading engine, so the same logic that you tested is what runs.

vectorized:  R = (w · rshift).sum()    |    event-driven:  for each bar: update(portfolio)Same strategy, two engines: one math expression over arrays vs. an explicit loop over time.

The professional rhythm is to prototype vectorized, validate event-driven. You explore ideas cheaply in arrays, then confirm the survivors in a realistic simulator before risking capital. We will use both in this course; this week and next stay vectorized so you can iterate fast.

AnalogyVectorized backtesting is a wind tunnel: it scores a thousand wing shapes quickly under idealized airflow. Event-driven backtesting is a test flight: slower, one aircraft at a time, but it reveals the gusts, the bumpy runway, and the fuel you actually burn. You sketch in the tunnel and you certify on the runway.
✦ Worked Example — a three-day signal through the whole pipeline

A signal says: go long (w = +1) when today's price is above yesterday's, else go short (w = −1). Prices on days 0–3 are $100, $102, $101, $104. Costs are 0.1% of notional each time the position flips. Walk the full pipeline and find the strategy return.

  1. Daily asset returns (data → returns): day1 r = 102/100 − 1 = +2.0%; day2 r = 101/102 − 1 = −0.98%; day3 r = 104/101 − 1 = +2.97%.
  2. Signal each day (set using only the past): after day1's up-move set w = +1 for day2; after day2's down-move set w = −1 for day3.
  3. P&L day2: w = +1 against r = −0.98% ⟹ −0.98% (we were long into a down day — a loss).
  4. P&L day3: w = −1 against r = +2.97% ⟹ −2.97% (we were short into an up day — another loss; this trend rule got whipsawed).
  5. Execution costs: the position flipped from +1 to −1 between day2 and day3 — one flip ⟹ 0.1% cost. Day2 had no prior position change charged here, so total cost ≈ 0.1%.
  6. Total strategy return: −0.98% − 2.97% − 0.10% ≈ −4.05%. The raw signal "felt" reasonable, yet sized and costed across the path it lost — exactly why we never stop at the signal. (A momentum rule that whipsaws in choppy data is the motivation for Week 2.)

🛠️ Hands-On: your first vectorized backtest

This is an early week, so set up the course stack once below, then reuse it all course. We'll build a minimal vectorized backtest: synthesize a price series, form a simple signal, shift it to avoid look-ahead, and turn it into a P&L curve — the entire pipeline in a dozen lines.

⬇ Install the C4 stack (one-time, Week 1 setup)

Get Python 3 from python.org or your package manager. Then create an isolated environment and install the free backtesting stack:

python3 -m venv quant-env
# activate it:
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux
quant-env\Scripts\activate         # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install pandas numpy matplotlib statsmodels vectorbt backtrader

Verify: run python -c "import pandas, numpy, vectorbt; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set. Launch notebooks with jupyter lab if you also installed it. (vectorbt is our fast vectorized engine; backtrader is the event-driven engine for later weeks; statsmodels for stats.)

# pipeline_demo.py — C4 Lecture 1: the whole pipeline, vectorized
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# 1. DATA: a synthetic daily price series (random walk with slight drift)
n = 500
rets  = rng.normal(0.0003, 0.01, n)          # daily asset returns
price = pd.Series(100 * np.exp(np.cumsum(rets)))

# 2. SIGNAL: trend = +1 if price > its 20-day average, else -1
sma    = price.rolling(20).mean()
signal = np.sign(price - sma)                # view in {-1, 0, +1}

# 3. POSITION: SHIFT by 1 day so we trade on yesterday's signal (no look-ahead!)
position = signal.shift(1).fillna(0)

# 4/5. EXECUTION + P&L: cost = 0.05% each time the position changes
asset_ret = price.pct_change().fillna(0)
cost      = 0.0005 * position.diff().abs().fillna(0)
strat_ret = position * asset_ret - cost      # R_t = w_t * r_t - costs

# 6. EVALUATION: equity curve + annualized Sharpe
equity = (1 + strat_ret).cumprod()
sharpe = np.sqrt(252) * strat_ret.mean() / strat_ret.std()
print(f"final equity : {equity.iloc[-1]:.3f}")
print(f"ann. Sharpe  : {sharpe:.2f}")

Notice the .shift(1) — that one line is the firewall against look-ahead bias. Try deleting it and watch the Sharpe leap to an impossible value: you'd be trading on information you couldn't have had. Change the seed or the 20-day window and the result shifts, which is your first lesson in how fragile a single backtest can be.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Which of these is the correct order of the trading pipeline?
A. signal → data → execution → sizing → evaluation → P&L
B. data → signal → position sizing → execution → P&L → evaluation
C. data → execution → signal → P&L → sizing → evaluation
D. signal → sizing → data → execution → evaluation → P&L
Show answer

B. Data feeds the signal, sizing turns the view into a position, execution applies frictions, then P&L is tallied and finally evaluated. The others scramble the stages — e.g. you cannot execute (C) or size (D) before you have a signal, and the signal cannot precede the data it reads (A).

Q2. What most clearly distinguishes a strategy from a raw signal?
A. A strategy uses machine learning; a signal does not
B. A strategy is always profitable; a signal may not be
C. A strategy maps the signal through sizing, execution, and costs to a realized return; a signal is only a view
D. A signal is continuous while a strategy must be discrete
Show answer

C. The strategy is the full machine that converts the view into an actual sized, costed P&L stream. ML is not required (A); a strategy is decidedly not guaranteed profitable (B); and both signals and strategies can be continuous or discrete (D).

Q3. You hold a short position of weight w = −1 and the asset returns +3% over the period (ignore costs). Your position P&L is…
A. −3%
B. +3%
C. 0%
D. +1.5%
Show answer

A. P&L = w · r = (−1)(+3%) = −3%. A short loses when the price rises. +3% (B) would be a long; the sign of the weight flips the outcome, so it is not zero (C), and there is no halving (D).

Q4. Which statement best describes when to prefer a vectorized backtest over an event-driven one?
A. When you need to model partial fills and queue position precisely
B. When you want to sweep many parameter combinations quickly during research
C. When you are about to deploy and want maximum realism
D. When your strategy depends on tick-by-tick order mechanics
Show answer

B. Vectorized backtests are fast and ideal for cheaply exploring many parameters in research. Partial fills (A), pre-deployment realism (C), and tick-level mechanics (D) are exactly the things the event-driven engine handles and the vectorized one glosses over.

Q5. In the hands-on code, why is signal.shift(1) used before computing returns?
A. To make the code run faster
B. To convert daily returns into annual returns
C. To smooth the signal with a moving average
D. To trade on the prior period's signal so you never use information from the period you are trading
Show answer

D. Shifting the signal forward one period enforces that the position for period t was decided using only data through t−1, preventing look-ahead bias. It does not affect speed (A), annualize anything (B), or smooth the series (C) — that would be a rolling mean.
