Building a Backtester

Before you ever risk a dollar, you replay history bar by bar — and a single misplaced row can turn a losing strategy into a fantasy of riches.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Trace the backtest pipeline: turn a signal into a position, a position into strategy returns, and returns into an equity curve.
  • Build a fully vectorized backtester in pandas using shift, pct_change, and cumprod.
  • Identify and eliminate look-ahead bias — and explain why you must lag the signal by one bar.
  • Contrast vectorized and event-driven backtester designs and know when each is the right tool.
  • Run the same strategy through a free backtesting library and compare it to your hand-rolled version.

5.1The Backtest Pipeline: Signal → Position → Returns → Equity

A backtest answers one question: "If I had run this strategy on past data, what would have happened to my money?" It is the laboratory of quantitative trading. You cannot afford to discover that a rule loses money by trading it live — so you replay history first. The entire machinery, no matter how fancy the library, is just a four-stage pipeline.

Stage one is the signal: a number your strategy computes from the data available at each bar. A moving-average crossover, for example, emits +1 when the fast average is above the slow one and −1 when it is below. Stage two converts the signal into a position — how much of the asset you actually hold (long, short, or flat). For a simple all-in strategy the position is the signal; for a sized strategy it might be scaled by volatility or by a Kelly fraction.

Stage three turns positions into strategy returns. The asset earns a simple return rt from bar t−1 to bar t. If you held a position of size wt−1 entering that bar, your return for the bar is just the position times the asset's return:

rstratt = wt−1 · rtYour P&L over a bar = the position you were already holding when the bar began, times what the asset did during that bar.

The subscript on w is the whole ballgame: the position must be the one you established before the bar's return is known. We will hammer this point in §5.2. Stage four compounds those per-bar returns into an equity curve — the running value of one dollar invested at the start:

ET = E0 · ∏t=1T (1 + rstratt)Wealth multiplies, it does not add — a +50% bar followed by a −50% bar leaves you at 0.75, not 1.0.
AnalogyA backtester is a flight simulator for a trading strategy. It can't promise the real sky will behave the same way, but it lets you crash a hundred times for free and learn which maneuvers are suicidal before you ever leave the runway. The signal is the pilot's decision, the position is the control surface, the per-bar return is the gust of wind, and the equity curve is the altimeter.

5.2The Cardinal Sin: Look-Ahead Bias

Every backtest that has ever lied to its author did so for the same reason: it used information that would not have been available at the moment of the trade. This is look-ahead bias, and it is the single most expensive mistake in the field. The classic form is subtle. You compute a signal from a bar's closing price, then apply that signal to the same bar's return. But you only learn the close at the end of the bar — by then the return has already happened. You are trading on a number you could not have known in time.

The fix is mechanical and absolute: shift the signal forward by one bar before multiplying it by returns. The position you hold during bar t is the signal computed from data through bar t−1:

wt = signalt−1  ⟹  rstratt = signalt−1 · rtIn pandas: position = signal.shift(1). The single most important line in any vectorized backtest.

Why exactly one bar? Because a signal computed at the close of bar t−1 can, at the earliest, be acted on at the open of bar t — and the simplest honest assumption is that you enter at the close of t−1 and earn the t return. Shifting by one bar encodes the unbreakable arrow of time: decide first, then find out what happens. Skip the shift and your backtester quietly buys every winner the instant before it rises. The resulting equity curve looks glorious and is pure fiction.

Common mistakeForgetting .shift(1) is the look-ahead bug, but over-shifting is a quieter cousin. shift(2) introduces a one-bar delay that understates a real edge, and shifting returns instead of the signal scrambles the alignment entirely. The rule: lag the signal/position by exactly one bar; never touch the return series. And watch for indicators that secretly peek — a centered moving average or a fillna(method='bfill') both leak the future.

5.3Vectorized vs. Event-Driven Designs

There are two philosophies for building a backtester, and a working quant should understand both.

Vectorized

A vectorized backtester treats the whole price history as columns in a table and operates on entire arrays at once — no explicit loop over time. The whole pipeline of §5.1 becomes four lines of pandas. Because NumPy and pandas push the arithmetic down into compiled C, a vectorized backtest over a decade of daily bars runs in milliseconds. This makes it ideal for research: sweeping hundreds of parameter combinations, prototyping an idea, sanity-checking a signal.

The cost of that speed is realism. Vectorized code naturally assumes you can transact at the exact bar price with infinite liquidity, and it gets awkward the moment your decision today depends on your account state today — a stop-loss that depends on the current open P&L, position sizing that depends on remaining cash, or order fills that depend on intraday highs and lows. You can bolt these on, but each one fights the array-at-once model.

Event-driven

An event-driven backtester instead walks through time one bar (or one tick) at a time, feeding each new market event to a strategy object that maintains state, emits orders, and receives fills from a simulated broker. It is slower — a real Python loop — but it mirrors the architecture of a live trading system almost exactly. The same strategy class you backtested can, with a different data feed, trade for real. That structural fidelity is why event-driven engines dominate production and why most serious libraries are built that way.

PropertyVectorizedEvent-driven
Speedvery fast (array ops in C)slower (Python loop over bars)
Code shapea few pandas/NumPy linesstrategy + broker + event loop
Path-dependent logicawkward (stops, sizing on state)natural — state lives in the loop
Look-ahead riskhigh if you forget to shiftlow — you only see the current bar
Best forresearch, parameter sweepsrealistic sims, path to live trading

The healthy workflow uses both: prototype fast with a vectorized backtest, then re-validate any survivor in an event-driven engine before trusting it. An idea that only looks good in the vectorized version often dies the moment realistic frictions and path-dependence enter.

5.4Standing on Shoulders: Using a Backtesting Library

You should hand-roll a vectorized backtest at least once — it is the only way to truly internalize the shift and the cumprod. But for anything you intend to trust, reach for a battle-tested library. Two free, pip-installable choices anchor the Python ecosystem:

vectorbt is a vectorized engine on steroids: it keeps the millisecond speed but adds correct handling of fees, slippage, position sizing, and a rich set of performance statistics — so you get research-grade throughput without re-deriving the arithmetic each time. backtrader is the classic event-driven framework: you write a Strategy class with a next() method called once per bar, and it handles the broker, orders, commissions, and the bar-by-bar bookkeeping that prevents look-ahead by construction.

The value of a library is not that it is faster to write — for a one-liner crossover it may be slower. The value is that the library's authors have already fought the bugs you would otherwise discover with real money: off-by-one alignment, fee accounting, partial fills, corporate-action adjustments. When your hand-rolled curve and the library's curve agree, you have real confidence; when they disagree, you have found a bug — almost always in your own version.

AnalogyHand-rolling the backtest once is like learning to do long division by hand before you trust a calculator. You must understand the algorithm to recognize when the calculator's answer is nonsense — but once you do, you reach for the calculator every time that matters.
✦ Worked Example — a 5-bar crossover, by hand, with and without the shift

An asset's closes over five days are 100, 102, 101, 104, 103. A signal says "be long (+1) on a day whose close is above the previous close, else flat (0)." Compute the honest strategy return — then show what look-ahead bias fakes.

  1. Daily asset returns rt: from 100→102 = +2.0%; 102→101 = −0.98%; 101→104 = +2.97%; 104→103 = −0.96%. (Day 1 has no prior close, so no return.)
  2. Raw signal (close > prior close): day2 up ⟹ 1; day3 down ⟹ 0; day4 up ⟹ 1; day5 down ⟹ 0.
  3. Honest position = signal.shift(1): the day-2 signal is acted on starting day 3. So positions held entering days 3,4,5 are 1, 0, 1. (Days 1–2 are flat — no signal yet.)
  4. Honest strategy returns wt−1·rt: day3: 1·(−0.98%) = −0.98%; day4: 0·(+2.97%) = 0%; day5: 1·(−0.96%) = −0.96%.
  5. Honest equity: (1−0.0098)(1+0)(1−0.0096) ≈ 0.9806 — a −1.9% loss. This naive momentum rule simply bought right before the two down days.
  6. Look-ahead (the lie): skip the shift and apply each day's signal to that same day's return: day2: 1·(+2.0%); day3: 0; day4: 1·(+2.97%); day5: 0 ⟹ (1.02)(1.0297) ≈ 1.050, a fake +5.0% gain. The unshifted backtester "knew" the up-days in advance — a 7-point swing of pure fiction.

🛠️ Hands-On: a vectorized backtester in four lines

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + NumPy + Matplotlib — the one-time install lives in the early lectures). This lecture adds one new optional tool, vectorbt, so we include a small install note for just it. We'll backtest a moving-average crossover and compare the honest curve to the look-ahead lie.

⬇ New this week: install vectorbt (optional)

With your quant-env active (built in Week 1), add the one new package:

pip install vectorbt        # vectorized backtesting + stats
# backtrader is the event-driven alternative:  pip install backtrader

pandas, numpy and matplotlib are already in quant-env; the core demo below needs only those.

# backtester.py — Lecture 5  (core demo needs only pandas + numpy)
import numpy as np, pandas as pd

# --- toy price series: a gentle uptrend plus noise ---
rng   = np.random.default_rng(5)
ret   = rng.normal(0.0004, 0.01, 750)          # daily log-ish returns
price = pd.Series(100 * np.exp(np.cumsum(ret)))

# 1) SIGNAL: long when fast MA > slow MA, else flat
fast, slow = price.rolling(20).mean(), price.rolling(50).mean()
signal = (fast > slow).astype(float)           # 1.0 or 0.0

# 2) POSITION: shift by ONE bar — the look-ahead guard
position = signal.shift(1).fillna(0.0)

# 3) RETURNS: position you held times the asset's bar return
asset_ret = price.pct_change().fillna(0.0)
strat_ret = position * asset_ret

# 4) EQUITY: compound the per-bar returns
equity = (1 + strat_ret).cumprod()

print("final equity (honest)    :", round(equity.iloc[-1], 4))

# the LIE: forget the shift -> uses same-bar signal (look-ahead)
cheat = ((signal * asset_ret) + 1).cumprod()
print("final equity (look-ahead):", round(cheat.iloc[-1], 4))

The honest equity reflects a realistically modest edge; the cheat curve is inflated because it trades on signals it could not have known in time. Swap in vectorbt (pf = vbt.Portfolio.from_signals(price, entries, exits, fees=0.001)) and you get the same honest curve plus fees, slippage, and a full statistics table — a preview of Week 6.

❓ Lecture 5 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a vectorized backtester, the single line that prevents look-ahead bias is…
A. returns = price.pct_change()
B. position = signal.shift(1)
C. equity = (1 + strat_ret).cumprod()
D. signal = (fast > slow).astype(float)
Show answer

B. Lagging the signal one bar means the position you hold during a bar was decided before that bar's return is known. (A) just computes asset returns; (C) compounds into equity; (D) builds the signal — none of these address timing.

Q2. The per-bar strategy return is computed as…
A. the asset return alone, ignoring the position
B. the position at bar t times the asset return at bar t
C. the position you held entering the bar (wt−1) times the asset return rt
D. the cumulative product of all past asset returns
Show answer

C. Your P&L over a bar is the position you already held times what the asset did. (B) uses the same-bar position, which is the look-ahead bug; (A) drops the position entirely; (D) is the equity curve, not a single-bar return.

Q3. Which is the strongest reason to choose an event-driven design over a vectorized one?
A. It runs faster on a decade of daily bars
B. It can sweep thousands of parameter combinations in seconds
C. It avoids ever needing price data
D. It handles path-dependent logic (stops, state-based sizing) naturally and mirrors a live system
Show answer

D. Walking bar by bar lets the strategy keep state, so stops and account-aware sizing are natural, and the same class can later trade live. (A) and (B) are advantages of the vectorized approach, which is the fast one; (C) is nonsense — every backtest needs data.

Q4. An equity curve is built from per-bar strategy returns by…
A. taking the cumulative product of (1 + rstrat)
B. summing the returns and adding 1
C. averaging the returns
D. taking the maximum return seen so far
Show answer

A. Wealth compounds multiplicatively, so equity = ∏(1 + r). Summing (B) ignores compounding; averaging (C) gives a typical bar, not a running balance; the running max (D) is a building block for drawdown, not equity itself.

Q5. A backtest of a momentum rule shows a suspiciously smooth, steeply rising equity curve far above the asset itself. The most likely culprit is…
A. the signal was lagged by too many bars
B. look-ahead bias — the signal was applied to the same bar's return without a shift
C. the cumprod was replaced with a cumsum
D. transaction costs were modeled too aggressively
Show answer

B. An implausibly perfect curve is the signature of trading on information not yet available. Over-lagging (A) would understate the edge; a cumsum (C) changes the curve's scale, not its plausibility; and aggressive costs (D) would make returns worse, not better.
