Mean-Reversion Strategies

When a price stretches too far from its own average, mean-reversion bets that the rubber band snaps back — and tells you exactly when it won't.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the core idea of mean reversion: that a price tends to be pulled back toward a moving average or fair value.
  • Build a z-score signal that measures how stretched a price is, in units of its own volatility.
  • Use Bollinger Bands and the RSI as practical mean-reversion entry and exit tools.
  • Estimate the half-life of mean reversion and use it to size your lookback and holding period.
  • Recognize the regime where mean reversion fails — strong trends — and see why it is the natural complement to momentum.

3.1The Rubber-Band Idea

Last week's momentum strategy bet that a move continues: what went up keeps going up. Mean reversion makes the opposite wager. It assumes a price has a "fair" level — often proxied by a moving average — and that whenever the price wanders far from that level, an invisible elastic force pulls it back. Buy when the price is unusually low relative to its average, sell when it is unusually high, and collect the snap-back.

Why would prices behave this way? Real economic forces create it. A liquidity shock — a big seller dumping shares — pushes a price below fair value; bargain hunters step in and lift it back. Two assets tied by the same fundamentals (a stock and its sector, two refiners, gold and a gold miner) drift apart temporarily and then re-converge. Over short horizons, overreaction to news is routinely corrected. The art is telling a temporary dislocation apart from a permanent repricing.

The first job is to make "far from the mean" precise. We need a yardstick, and the natural one is the price's own variability: a $2 move means very different things for a calm bond ETF and a jumpy small-cap. That yardstick is the standard deviation, and it leads directly to the z-score.

AnalogyThink of the price as a dog on a retractable leash held by a walker strolling at a steady pace (the moving average). The dog darts ahead and lags behind, but the leash keeps yanking it back toward the walker. Mean reversion trades the leash. The danger — covered in §3.4 — is the day the walker breaks into a sprint (a trend): then the dog out front isn't overextended, it's simply keeping up.

3.2The Z-Score Signal

Pick a lookback window of N bars. Compute the rolling mean μt and rolling standard deviation σt of the price (or of a spread) over that window. The z-score measures how many standard deviations today's price sits above or below its recent average:

zt = (Pt − μt) / σtμt and σt are the rolling mean and standard deviation over the last N bars. z is unitless — it works the same for a $5 stock and a $5000 index.

The z-score turns every asset onto the same scale. A reading of z = +2 means the price is two standard deviations rich; z = −2 means it is two standard deviations cheap. A mean-reversion rule is then disarmingly simple: go long when z drops below −k, go short (or exit a long) when z rises above +k, and flatten the position as z returns toward 0. A common starting choice is k = 2 to enter and 0 to exit.

Notice the symmetry with momentum. Momentum buys high z (strength) expecting more strength; mean reversion buys low z (weakness) expecting recovery. Same signal, opposite sign — which is exactly why the two styles hedge each other, a theme we return to in §3.4.

Common mistakeDon't compute μt and σt using the full dataset including future bars — that is look-ahead bias and it makes any backtest look brilliant. The rolling statistics at time t must use only data up to and including t. In pandas, .rolling(N).mean() is safe; .mean() over the whole series is not.

3.3Bollinger Bands, RSI & the Half-Life

The z-score has two famous packaged forms that you'll meet on every charting platform.

Bollinger Bands

Bollinger Bands are literally the z-score drawn as price levels. Around an N-period moving average you plot an upper and lower band at ±k standard deviations:

Upper = μt + k·σt,   Lower = μt − k·σtClassic settings are N = 20 bars and k = 2. "Price touches the lower band" is identical to "z ≤ −2".

A price touching the lower band is a candidate buy; touching the upper band a candidate sell. The bands also breathe: they widen when volatility rises and narrow when it falls, so the same z-threshold automatically demands a bigger dollar move to trigger in turbulent markets.

RSI — the Relative Strength Index

The RSI is a bounded momentum oscillator used as a mean-reversion gauge. It compares the size of recent up-moves to recent down-moves over (classically) 14 bars and squashes the result onto a 0–100 scale:

RSI = 100 − 100 / (1 + RS),   RS = (avg gain) / (avg loss)RSI below 30 is conventionally "oversold" (a buy candidate); above 70 is "overbought" (a sell candidate).

RSI and the z-score usually agree — a deeply negative z and an RSI under 30 are two readings of the same oversold condition — but RSI is bounded, so it never blows up to absurd values the way a raw z-score can during a volatility collapse.

Half-life: how fast does it snap back?

Knowing a price is stretched is useless if you don't know how long to wait for the snap-back. Model the spread xt as an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck / AR(1) process pulled toward its mean at speed θ. Regress the daily change on the prior level:

Δxt = −θ · (xt−1 − μ) + εt,   half-life = ln(2) / θθ > 0 means reversion. The half-life is the expected number of bars to close half the gap to the mean. A negative slope coefficient (−θ) is the regression output you read off.

The half-life is the single most useful number in the strategy. It tells you how to set the lookback (a window a few half-lives long), how long to hold (exit near one half-life if the gap hasn't closed), and whether the trade is even worth it: a half-life of 3 days is tradeable; a half-life of 200 days ties up capital forever for a tiny edge.

Common mistakeAn "oversold" reading is not a buy signal on its own. A stock falling on genuine bad news can stay oversold for weeks while it keeps dropping — RSI pinned near 10 the whole way down. Always confirm that the series actually mean-reverts (a positive θ, a short half-life) before trusting any z-score or RSI extreme.

3.4When Mean Reversion Fails: Trends & the Momentum Pair

Every mean-reversion strategy has the same fatal weakness: a strong trend. In a trend the price keeps making new extremes, so the rolling mean is dragged along behind it and the z-score keeps signalling "oversold" (in a downtrend) or "overbought" (in an uptrend) again and again. Each signal is a loser. The strategy quietly "catches a falling knife" — buying every dip in a market that is repricing permanently lower.

This is the mirror image of momentum's weakness. Momentum bleeds money in choppy, range-bound markets where every breakout reverses; mean reversion bleeds money in strong directional markets where every dip extends. Their payoff profiles are roughly opposite:

Market regimeMean reversionMomentum / trend-following
Range-bound / choppyWins — prices oscillate around a stable meanLoses — false breakouts whipsaw the position
Strong trendLoses — keeps fading a move that continuesWins — rides the move

Because their winning regimes are complementary, a portfolio that runs both styles is far smoother than either alone — when one is bleeding, the other is usually earning. This is a real, practical form of diversification: not across assets, but across strategy logic. A common defense for a stand-alone mean-reversion book is a trend filter: only take reversion trades when a long-horizon moving average is flat, and stand aside when it is steeply sloped.

AnalogyMean reversion and momentum are like a convertible top and an umbrella. One is useless exactly when the other is essential. Carry both and you're comfortable in any weather; carry only one and you're betting the climate never changes — which markets guarantee it will.
✦ Worked Example — z-score entry and half-life

An ETF trades at P = 98.40. Its 20-day rolling mean is μ = 101.00 with a rolling standard deviation σ = 1.30. A regression of the daily change on the prior gap gives a slope of −0.15 (so θ = 0.15). Should we enter, and how long might we hold?

  1. Compute the z-score: z = (P − μ)/σ = (98.40 − 101.00)/1.30 = −2.60/1.30 = −2.0. The price is exactly two standard deviations cheap.
  2. Apply the entry rule (k = 2): z ≤ −2 triggers a long. We buy at 98.40, with a profit target back at the mean μ = 101.00 (z = 0).
  3. Size the target: the expected snap-back is μ − P = 101.00 − 98.40 = $2.60 per share, roughly +2.6% — the reward if reversion plays out.
  4. Estimate the half-life: half-life = ln(2)/θ = 0.693/0.15 ≈ 4.6 days. We expect about half the gap (≈ $1.30) to close in ~5 days, so a holding horizon of one to two half-lives (5–10 days) is sensible.
  5. Risk check: set a stop if z keeps falling past, say, −3.5 (price ≈ 96.45) — that would signal a regime change (a trend, not a dislocation), exactly the §3.4 failure mode. The edge is real only while the series keeps reverting.

🛠️ Hands-On: a Bollinger / z-score mean-reversion backtest

Reusing the course stack from Weeks 1–2 (pandas, NumPy, Matplotlib, plus statsmodels for the half-life regression — the full install details block lives in Lecture 1). We compute a rolling z-score, generate long/flat/short signals, estimate the half-life, and vectorise the equity curve.

⬇ Need statsmodels? (one extra package on top of Week-1 setup)

If you built quant-env in Week 1, just add the one library used for the half-life regression:

source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install statsmodels
# mean_reversion.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm

# price: a pandas Series of daily closes (from your Week-1 data loader)
N = 20                                  # lookback window (bars)
mu  = price.rolling(N).mean()           # rolling mean  (no look-ahead)
sig = price.rolling(N).std()
z   = (price - mu) / sig                # the z-score signal

# position: +1 long when very cheap, -1 short when very rich, hold until z crosses 0
raw = pd.Series(0, index=price.index)
raw[z < -2.0] =  1
raw[z >  2.0] = -1
pos = raw.replace(0, np.nan).ffill().fillna(0)   # stay in trade until next signal
pos = pos.shift(1)                       # TRADE ON NEXT BAR — avoids look-ahead

ret    = price.pct_change()
strat  = pos * ret                       # strategy daily return
equity = (1 + strat).cumprod()

# --- half-life of mean reversion via an AR(1) regression ---
spread = (price - mu).dropna()
lag    = spread.shift(1).dropna()
delta  = (spread - spread.shift(1)).dropna()
lag, delta = lag.align(delta, join="inner")
beta   = sm.OLS(delta, sm.add_constant(lag)).fit().params[1]   # = -theta
half_life = -np.log(2) / beta            # bars to close half the gap

print("half-life (bars):", round(half_life, 1))
print("total return    :", round(equity.iloc[-1] - 1, 4))

Two habits to internalize: the .rolling(N) statistics use only past data, and pos.shift(1) makes you trade on the bar after the signal — together they kill the most common backtest lie, look-ahead bias. Try N = 10, 20, 50 and watch how the half-life suggests which lookback fits the data. In the coming weeks you'll wrap this exact logic into vectorbt/backtrader and add the transaction costs that decide whether the edge survives.

💬 Discussion

A teammate shows you a mean-reversion backtest on a single stock with a glittering Sharpe ratio: buy whenever the 20-day z-score drops below −2, exit at z = 0. They want to deploy it live tomorrow with real capital. Before anyone clicks "go," what is the single most important question you would ask, and what one diagnostic would you run to answer it? Think about which regime the backtest period happened to live in, and how you'd know whether the edge is real or an artifact of a lucky range-bound stretch.

Show a model response

The most important question is: does this series actually mean-revert, and over what horizon? A beautiful backtest can come entirely from the stock having traded in a range during the test window — survivorship of a regime, not a genuine edge. The one diagnostic I would run is the half-life regression (the AR(1) fit, half-life = ln 2 / θ). If θ is reliably positive with a short half-life (say a few days to a couple of weeks), the reversion is structural and the chosen 20-day lookback is roughly compatible with it. If θ is near zero, negative, or the half-life is hundreds of bars, the "edge" is almost certainly an artifact and will reverse into a trend disaster the moment the stock starts repricing. I would also split the sample (in-sample vs. out-of-sample, and across a known trending period) and check whether the Sharpe survives outside the lucky range — and confirm the backtest used shift(1) with rolling stats so it isn't quietly peeking at the future.

✓ Self-check rubric — a strong answer should: (1) identify regime dependence as the core risk, not just "overfitting" in the abstract; (2) name a concrete reversion test (half-life / θ, or an ADF/stationarity test); (3) propose out-of-sample or trending-period validation; (4) flag look-ahead bias (rolling stats + shifting the signal) as a separate must-check.

Q1. A price has a 20-day rolling mean of 50 and a rolling standard deviation of 4. It currently trades at 42. Its z-score is…
A. −2.0
B. −8.0
C. +2.0
D. −0.5
Show answer

A. z = (P − μ)/σ = (42 − 50)/4 = −8/4 = −2.0 — two standard deviations cheap, a classic long signal. −8 (B) forgets to divide by σ; +2 (C) flips the sign; −0.5 (D) inverts the ratio.

Q2. Bollinger Bands are best described as…
A. a fixed dollar offset above and below the closing price
B. a moving average plus and minus k rolling standard deviations
C. the highest and lowest prices of the last N bars
D. a 0–100 bounded oscillator of average gains over losses
Show answer

B. Bollinger Bands are μt ± k·σt — literally the z-score drawn as price levels, so they widen and narrow with volatility. A fixed offset (A) ignores volatility; the high/low channel (C) is a Donchian channel; the bounded gain/loss oscillator (D) describes the RSI.

Q3. A mean-reverting spread has a mean-reversion speed θ = 0.10 per day. Its half-life is approximately…
A. 10 days
B. 0.07 days
C. 6.9 days
D. 14.4 days
Show answer

C. half-life = ln(2)/θ = 0.693/0.10 ≈ 6.9 days. 10 (A) is 1/θ, the full time-constant, not the half-life; 0.07 (B) multiplies instead of divides; 14.4 (D) is 1/(θ·ln2)-style mis-arithmetic.

Q4. In which market regime is a pure mean-reversion strategy most likely to lose money repeatedly?
A. A quiet, range-bound market oscillating around a stable level
B. A market with normally distributed daily returns
C. A market with zero volatility and no signals
D. A strong, persistent trend that keeps making new extremes
Show answer

D. In a strong trend the rolling mean lags behind, so the z-score keeps reading "oversold/overbought" and every reversion trade fades a move that continues. Range-bound markets (A) are where reversion wins; normality (B) is irrelevant to the regime; zero volatility (C) means no signals and no trades at all.

Q5. Why are mean reversion and momentum often run together in one portfolio?
A. They use identical signals, so combining them doubles the position size
B. Their winning regimes are complementary, so one tends to earn while the other struggles
C. Momentum eliminates the need to estimate any volatility
D. Mean reversion guarantees a profit whenever momentum loses
Show answer

B. Momentum wins in trends and loses in chop; mean reversion does the opposite — so blending them smooths the combined equity curve (diversification across strategy logic). They use the same signal with opposite sign, not identical positions (A); volatility still matters to both (C); and no strategy guarantees a profit (D).
