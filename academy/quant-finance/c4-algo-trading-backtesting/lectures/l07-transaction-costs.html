Week 7 · Lecture 7

Transaction Costs, Slippage & Market Impact

Every trade pays a toll at the gate — and the strategies that look best on paper are usually the ones that hand the most money back to the market.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Break a trade's total cost into its parts: commissions, the bid–ask spread, slippage, and market impact.
  • Explain why frictions scale with how much and how often you trade, not just whether your signal is right.
  • Model transaction costs inside a backtest as a per-trade haircut tied to turnover and order size.
  • Show quantitatively how high turnover combined with costs silently turns a profitable gross strategy into a losing net one.
  • Distinguish gross from net Sharpe and use the net figure as the only number that matters.

7.1Why Frictions Decide Everything

In Lectures 5 and 6 you built a backtester and measured a strategy's return and Sharpe ratio. Those numbers were gross — they assumed you could buy and sell at the printed price, instantly, for free. Real markets charge you for the privilege of trading, and that charge is not a rounding error. A signal that earns 8 basis points per trade is worthless if each round trip costs you 10 basis points to execute.

The brutal arithmetic is this: a trading edge is earned once per signal, but costs are paid every single time you trade. Double your trading frequency and you double your costs while your per-trade edge stays the same. This is why the most common way a backtest lies is not a bug in the signal — it is the quiet omission of frictions. We split those frictions into four buckets.

The four costs of a trade

CostWhat it isRoughly scales with
Commission / feesbroker, exchange, and regulatory charges per order or per sharenumber of trades, shares
Bid–ask spreadyou buy at the higher ask, sell at the lower bid — the gap is a cost you pay just to crossnumber of trades
Slippagethe price moved between deciding and filling; you got a worse price than you modelledvolatility, latency, order size
Market impactyour own order pushes the price against you, especially for large sizeorder size relative to liquidity

The first two are nearly fixed and predictable; the last two grow with how aggressively and how large you trade. Together they form what practitioners call transaction costs or, more honestly, implementation shortfall — the gap between the paper price and the price you actually achieved.

AnalogyThink of a toll bridge. The spread and commission are the fixed toll — you pay it every crossing, no matter how few cars you bring. Market impact is the traffic jam you cause by sending a thousand trucks across at once: the more you push through, the slower and more expensive every truck becomes. A great destination on the far side is no good if the toll plus the jam eats your whole trip.

7.2The Bid–Ask Spread and Slippage

At any instant a market shows two prices: the best bid (the highest price a buyer will pay) and the best ask or offer (the lowest price a seller will accept). A market order to buy lifts the ask; a market order to sell hits the bid. The mid-price sits halfway between, and it is the price your backtest probably used. The instant you cross the spread you are already down half the spread relative to mid.

spread = ask − bid     mid = (ask + bid) / 2     half-spread cost ≈ spread / 2A round trip (buy then later sell) crosses the spread twice, so it costs roughly one full spread per round trip if you trade at the touch.

Slippage is everything that makes your realized price worse than the price you assumed when the signal fired. Even with no impact, the market moves in the milliseconds between your decision and your fill; in fast or volatile conditions that drift is real money. A clean and common way to express the combined spread-plus-slippage cost is as a fraction of the traded notional, quoted in basis points (1 bp = 0.01% = 0.0001):

cost per trade = c × |notional traded|     (c in fractional terms, e.g. 5 bp ⇒ c = 0.0005)For a liquid large-cap, c might be a few bp; for a thin small-cap, tens of bp. The model is crude but captures the dominant effect.
Common mistakeBacktesting on the mid-price and assuming you trade there is one of the most seductive bugs in all of quant finance. You can never buy at mid with a market order — you buy at the ask. Always fill buys at the ask (or mid + half-spread) and sells at the bid (or mid − half-spread), or your equity curve is fiction.

7.3Market Impact and Modelling Costs in a Backtest

The first three costs are largely independent of your size. Market impact is not. When you try to buy more than the resting liquidity at the best offer, you walk up the order book — each successive share fills at a worse price, and you also signal your intent, drawing the price away from you. Impact is the cost that punishes size, and it is why a strategy that works with $1 million can be impossible to run with $1 billion.

A widely used empirical model says impact grows with the square root of the fraction of daily volume you consume — small orders are cheap, but cost rises faster than linearly as you demand a bigger slice of the market:

impact (bp) ≈ k × σ × √( Q / V )Q = your order size, V = average daily volume, σ = volatility, k a constant. Doubling Q raises impact by ~√2, not 2 — but it never gets cheaper per share.

To put all of this into a backtest you do not need exchange-level detail. The standard approach is to charge a cost every time the position changes. If wt is the portfolio weight (position) at time t, then the amount you traded is |wt − wt−1|, and the cost subtracted from that period's return is:

net returnt = gross returnt − c × |wt − wt−1|c bundles spread, commission, and slippage into one per-unit-turnover rate. The sum of |wt − wt−1| over a year is the strategy's annual turnover.

This single line is the heart of cost-aware backtesting. It says: your net edge is your gross edge minus your cost rate times how much you churn. A strategy is viable only if its gross per-unit-turnover return exceeds c.

AnalogyMarket impact is like buying every apple at the farmers' market. The first crate is at the listed price; but once vendors see you cleaning out the stalls, they raise prices on the remaining crates. Buy a little and you barely move the market; try to buy the whole town's supply at once and you pay dearly for the last crates — and tip everyone off to what you are doing.

7.4Turnover, the Silent Killer, and Net Sharpe

Now combine the per-trade cost with frequency. Define annual turnover T as the total fraction of the portfolio bought and sold over a year (T = 1 means you replace the whole portfolio once; T = 50 means you churn it fifty times). If each unit of turnover costs c, then the annual drag on return is simply c × T. A high Sharpe means little if the strategy trades so often that c × T devours the gross return.

net return ≈ gross return − c × T     net Sharpe ≈ (gross return − c × T) / σnetCosts lower the numerator (mean return) and barely touch the denominator (volatility), so net Sharpe falls faster than you would guess.

This is why turnover is the silent killer. A fast strategy can show a glorious gross Sharpe of 2.5 and a net Sharpe near zero — all the edge is paid to the market in tolls. The fix is not always to trade less; sometimes it is to trade smarter (cheaper venues, patient limit orders, smaller slices). But the diagnostic discipline is fixed: only the net Sharpe counts. Gross Sharpe is a vanity metric.

Common mistakeComparing two strategies on gross Sharpe alone. A slow strategy with gross Sharpe 1.0 and turnover 4 can easily beat a fast one with gross Sharpe 2.0 and turnover 200 once costs are applied. Always rank candidates on net Sharpe at a realistic cost rate — and stress-test by doubling c to see how fragile the edge is.
✦ Worked Example — how turnover eats a strategy alive

A strategy earns a gross annual return of 12% with annualized volatility of 10% (gross Sharpe = 1.2, assuming ~0 risk-free). You estimate the all-in cost per unit of turnover at c = 8 bp = 0.0008. Evaluate it at two trading frequencies.

  1. Slow version, turnover T = 5: cost drag = c × T = 0.0008 × 5 = 0.004 = 0.4% per year.
  2. Net return (slow): 12% − 0.4% = 11.6%. Net Sharpe ≈ 11.6% / 10% = 1.16 — essentially unscathed.
  3. Fast version, turnover T = 150: same signal, but rebalanced far more often. Cost drag = 0.0008 × 150 = 0.12 = 12% per year.
  4. Net return (fast): 12% − 12% = 0%. Net Sharpe ≈ 0 / 10% = 0.0 — the entire edge is gone, paid to the market in tolls.
  5. The lesson: identical gross edge, identical gross Sharpe of 1.2. Yet at high turnover the net Sharpe collapses from 1.16 to 0. Now double the cost estimate to 16 bp and the fast version goes negative (−12% net). The signal was never the problem — the trading frequency was.

🛠️ Hands-On: charge costs and compare net Sharpe

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + NumPy — the full install details lives in Lecture 1). Below we take a daily strategy expressed as position weights, compute turnover, subtract a per-turnover cost, and compare gross vs net Sharpe — the exact discipline from §7.4.

# costs_demo.py — Lecture 7: gross vs net Sharpe
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
T_days = 1260                              # ~5 trading years

# daily asset returns and a noisy daily signal in [-1, 1]
asset_ret = rng.normal(0.0004, 0.01, T_days)        # ~10% vol/yr, slight drift
signal    = pd.Series(rng.uniform(-1, 1, T_days)).rolling(5).mean().fillna(0)

weight    = signal.clip(-1, 1)             # today's target position
strat_ret = weight.shift(1).fillna(0).values * asset_ret   # trade on yesterday's signal

# turnover = how much the position changed each day
turnover  = weight.diff().abs().fillna(weight.abs()).values
c = 0.0008                                 # 8 bp all-in cost per unit turnover
net_ret   = strat_ret - c * turnover

def sharpe(r):                             # annualized, rf ~ 0
    return np.mean(r) / np.std(r) * np.sqrt(252)

print("annual turnover   :", turnover.sum() / 5)       # units of portfolio/yr
print("gross Sharpe      :", round(sharpe(strat_ret), 3))
print("net Sharpe        :", round(sharpe(net_ret), 3))
print("cost drag (ann %) :", round(c * turnover.sum() / 5 * 100, 2))

Because the signal is pure noise, the gross Sharpe hovers near zero — but the point is structural: the turnover array drives the cost, and net Sharpe is always below gross by an amount set by c and how much you churn. Swap in a real signal, then sweep c = 0.0002, 0.0008, 0.0016 and watch the net curve sink. In vectorbt or backtrader the same idea is a fees= / slippage= argument — but knowing what it does to net Sharpe is the skill.

🔗 Where this is heading

Costs are the reason a backtest can lie even when the code is perfect — which makes them the natural lead-in to Week 8: Overfitting & Multiple Testing, where we attack the other great source of false confidence. The square-root impact model and turnover budgeting reappear in execution algorithms (slicing a large order over time to minimize impact) and in portfolio construction, where the Markowitz optimizer of Course 2 gets a transaction-cost penalty bolted on so it stops churning. Net-of-cost thinking is also what separates a paper edge from a live one when you connect a strategy to a broker in the final capstone.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Which of the four trade costs grows fastest as you increase the size of a single order relative to the market's liquidity?
A. Fixed commission
B. The bid–ask spread
C. Market impact
D. The risk-free rate
Show answer

C. Market impact is the cost that punishes size — walking up the order book makes each extra share fill worse, rising roughly with √(Q/V). Commissions (A) and the spread (B) are largely fixed per trade regardless of size, and the risk-free rate (D) is not a trading cost at all.

Q2. Your backtest fills every buy and sell at the mid-price. Why is its equity curve likely too optimistic?
A. Because you actually buy at the ask and sell at the bid, paying the spread you never charged
B. Because the mid-price is always stale by one day
C. Because mid-prices are higher than both bid and ask
D. Because commissions are negative for retail traders
Show answer

A. A market order buys at the ask and sells at the bid, so trading "at mid" silently skips the half-spread on each side. (B) confuses spread with lag; (C) is false — mid sits between bid and ask; (D) is nonsense.

Q3. A strategy has gross annual return 12%, volatility 10%, an all-in cost of c = 0.0008 per unit turnover, and annual turnover T = 150. What is its approximate net return?
A. 11.6%
B. 0%
C. 12%
D. −12%
Show answer

B. Cost drag = c × T = 0.0008 × 150 = 0.12 = 12%, so net = 12% − 12% = 0%. (A) is the low-turnover (T = 5) case; (C) ignores costs entirely; (D) would require c ≈ 16 bp or twice the turnover.

Q4. Inside a backtest, what is the standard per-period cost charged when the portfolio weight moves from wt−1 to wt?
A. c × wt
B. c × (wt + wt−1)
C. c divided by the Sharpe ratio
D. c × |wt − wt−1|
Show answer

D. You pay for the amount you trade, which is the absolute change in position, |wt − wt−1|, times the cost rate. Charging on the level wt (A) or the sum (B) misstates turnover; (C) is meaningless dimensionally.

Q5. Two strategies: Slow (gross Sharpe 1.0, turnover 4) and Fast (gross Sharpe 2.0, turnover 200). At a realistic cost rate, which comparison is the right one to make?
A. Pick Fast — a higher gross Sharpe always wins
B. Compare net Sharpe after applying c × turnover; Slow may well win
C. Pick whichever has lower volatility, ignoring costs
D. They are identical because gross return is all that matters
Show answer

B. Costs scale with turnover, so the Fast strategy's huge churn can erase its gross edge, letting the low-turnover Slow strategy win on net Sharpe — the only figure that counts. (A) and (D) ignore costs; (C) ignores the return numerator entirely.

  • Split a trade's cost into commission, bid–ask spread, slippage, and market impact, and saw which scale with frequency vs size.
  • Why costs are paid on every trade while edge is earned once — so frequency, not just signal quality, decides survival.
  • Charged costs in a backtest as c × |wt − wt−1|, with market impact following a √(Q/V) law.
  • Watched turnover act as the silent killer: drag = c × T, collapsing a gross Sharpe of 1.2 to a net 0.
  • Made net Sharpe — not gross — the only metric for ranking and stress-testing live-viable strategies.
