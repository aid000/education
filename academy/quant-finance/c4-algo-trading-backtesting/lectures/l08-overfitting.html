Week 8 · Lecture 8

Overfitting & the Multiple-Testing Trap

Torture the data long enough and it will confess to anything — this week is about telling a real edge apart from a backtest that simply memorized the past.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a backtest fit on historical data systematically overstates future performance (overfitting and data snooping).
  • Quantify how testing many strategy variants manufactures false positives, and compute the expected number of "lucky" winners.
  • Describe the deflated Sharpe ratio and use it to discount a Sharpe earned after many trials.
  • Run parameter-sensitivity checks and split data into in-sample and out-of-sample windows to detect curve-fitting.
  • Argue, from a degrees-of-freedom standpoint, why simpler strategies generalize better than over-parameterized ones.

8.1The Central Danger: Overfitting & Data Snooping

A backtest is a model fit to one realized history of prices. Overfitting happens when that model captures the noise in that particular history rather than any repeatable structure. The equity curve looks magnificent in-sample because the rules were — knowingly or not — sculpted to flatter exactly the data you measured them on. When the same rules meet fresh data, the noise it memorized is gone, replaced by new noise, and the edge evaporates.

Data snooping (also called data dredging) is the process that produces overfitting: you reuse the same dataset to both generate and evaluate hypotheses. Every time you tweak an entry threshold, add a filter, or re-pick a lookback window after peeking at the result, you are spending statistical credibility you can never get back. The data has not changed; only your knowledge of its quirks has grown.

Markets make this uniquely dangerous because the signal-to-noise ratio is tiny. A genuine daily edge might shift expected return by a few basis points, while daily volatility is hundreds of basis points. With so much noise drowning so little signal, a flexible search will almost always find a pattern — and almost always the wrong one.

AnalogyOverfitting is like a student who memorizes last year's exam paper instead of learning the subject. They ace a re-print of that exact paper (the in-sample backtest) and fail the moment the questions change (live trading). The teacher's defense — a brand-new paper they have never seen — is exactly the out-of-sample test we build in §8.3.

8.2The Multiple-Testing Trap

Here is the statistical engine behind snooping. Suppose you adopt the rule "trade it if the backtest is significant at the 5% level." Test one truly worthless strategy and you have a 5% chance of a false positive — bad luck, but manageable. Now test N independent worthless strategies. The probability that at least one looks significant is no longer 5%:

P(at least one false winner) = 1 − (1 − α)Nα is the per-test false-positive rate (e.g. 0.05); N is the number of independent strategies tried.

With α = 0.05, just N = 14 trials push that probability past 50%, and at N = 100 it is over 99%. The expected count of false winners is even simpler — it is just the per-test rate times the number of tests:

E[false winners] = N · αTry 1,000 random rules at the 5% level and you should expect about 50 that look "significant" — purely by chance.

The trap is that the search is usually invisible. Optimizing a moving-average crossover over 50 fast-period and 50 slow-period combinations is 2,500 silent tests. The single best cell of that grid is selected because it had the most favorable noise, so its in-sample Sharpe is biased upward — the more you search, the larger the inflation.

The fix from classical statistics is to make the bar harder as N grows. The Bonferroni correction tests each strategy at α/N instead of α, holding the overall false-positive rate near α:

αadjusted = α / NSearched 100 variants and want 5% overall? Demand p < 0.0005 from any single one before believing it.
Common mistakeReporting only the best backtest you found and quoting its raw p-value or Sharpe as if it were the only test you ran. The relevant number of trials N includes every variant you silently discarded — every parameter you nudged and every idea you abandoned because it "didn't work." Honest accounting of N is the single hardest discipline in backtesting.

8.3Defenses: In-Sample / Out-of-Sample & the Deflated Sharpe

The first and cheapest defense is to hold out data. Split your history into an in-sample (IS) window where you are free to develop and optimize, and an out-of-sample (OOS) window that you do not look at until the strategy is frozen. The OOS window is fresh noise the strategy never fit, so its performance is an honest preview of live trading. A strategy that shines in-sample and collapses out-of-sample was curve-fit.

degradation = SharpeIS − SharpeOOSLarge positive degradation is the fingerprint of overfitting. A robust strategy keeps most of its IS Sharpe out-of-sample.

The second defense corrects the Sharpe ratio itself for the number of trials. A Sharpe of 2.0 found after one honest test is impressive; the same 2.0 plucked as the best of 1,000 grid cells is nearly worthless. The deflated Sharpe ratio idea asks: given that I ran N trials, what Sharpe would I expect to see from pure luck, and does my best beat it? The expected maximum Sharpe of N independent strategies with zero true edge grows with N:

E[max Sharpe] ≈ σSR · √(2 ln N)σSR is the standard error of a single backtest's Sharpe estimate. This is the "luck hurdle" your best strategy must clear.

The deflated Sharpe ratio takes your observed Sharpe, subtracts this luck hurdle, and re-expresses the remainder as a probability that the true Sharpe is positive. If your best strategy's Sharpe does not comfortably exceed the √(2 ln N) hurdle, you have found nothing but the right tail of noise.

Parameter-sensitivity checks

A third, very practical defense is to inspect the neighborhood of your chosen parameters. A genuine effect is smooth: a 20-day lookback should perform much like a 18- or 22-day lookback. If performance spikes at exactly one parameter value and craters on either side, that spike is almost certainly noise the optimizer latched onto. Plot the performance surface across parameters and look for a broad plateau, never a lonely needle.

AnalogyA robust parameter region is a wide mesa you can stand on safely even if you misjudge your footing by a step. An overfit optimum is a tightrope: the single value that worked, with a fatal drop on each side. In live trading the market will always nudge you a step off your exact backtested value — you want the mesa.

8.4Why Simpler Strategies Generalize Better

Every adjustable knob in a strategy — a threshold, a filter, a lookback, a weight — is a degree of freedom the optimizer can use to bend the rules around the in-sample noise. The more knobs, the more perfectly the backtest can be carved to fit the past, and the less of that fit survives into the future. This is the bias-variance trade-off seen through a trading lens.

A useful mental model is that your finite price history contains only so much genuine, repeatable information. Spend it across a few parameters and each is estimated on plenty of data. Spread it across dozens and each parameter is pinned down by almost nothing, so it mostly fits noise:

data per parameter ≈ (number of observations) / (number of free parameters)Hold observations fixed and add parameters: the information backing each one shrinks, and overfitting grows.

This is why a two-parameter trend rule with a clear economic rationale often beats a fifteen-parameter machine that scored higher in-sample. Simplicity is not aesthetic preference — it is a direct reduction in the number of ways noise can masquerade as signal. Prefer strategies whose logic you could defend to a skeptic before seeing the backtest, and resist adding a parameter unless it earns its keep out-of-sample.

Common mistakeTreating a higher in-sample Sharpe as proof a more complex strategy is better. In-sample performance is monotone in complexity — adding parameters can only improve the fit to the past. The real question is OOS performance, where added complexity usually hurts. Never rank strategies by their in-sample numbers.
✦ Worked Example — discounting a Sharpe found by grid search

You optimize a moving-average crossover over a 30 × 30 grid of fast/slow periods (N = 900 combinations) on 5 years of daily data. The best cell reports a Sharpe of 1.9. Is it real?

  1. Count the true number of trials: the grid is N = 900 silent tests, not 1. You only reported the maximum.
  2. Standard error of one Sharpe estimate: for T years of data, σSR ≈ √(1/T). With T = 5 years, σSR ≈ √(1/5) ≈ 0.447.
  3. Luck hurdle (expected best of N): E[max Sharpe] ≈ σSR·√(2 ln N) = 0.447 · √(2 · ln 900) = 0.447 · √(2 · 6.80) = 0.447 · √13.6 ≈ 0.447 · 3.69 ≈ 1.65.
  4. Compare: a portfolio of 900 worthless rules would be expected to throw up a best Sharpe around 1.65 by luck alone. Your winner scored 1.9 — only modestly above the hurdle.
  5. Deflate: the "excess" over luck is just 1.9 − 1.65 ≈ 0.25 of a Sharpe, well within the noise band of σSR ≈ 0.45. The deflated Sharpe is near zero: this result is not convincing.
  6. Action: demand an out-of-sample confirmation, check the parameter neighborhood for a plateau, and reduce the grid. Had the best Sharpe been, say, 3.5, it would have cleared the 1.65 hurdle by several standard errors and merited a closer look.

🛠️ Hands-On: manufacture false positives, then split the data

Reusing the Week-1/2 stack (NumPy + pandas + Matplotlib — the install block lives in the early lectures). First we simulate the multiple-testing trap on pure noise, then we contrast in-sample vs out-of-sample on a single strategy.

# overfit_demo.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
T   = 1260                      # ~5 years of daily returns
N   = 900                       # number of "strategies" we try

# Each strategy is pure noise: ZERO true edge by construction.
rets    = rng.normal(0.0, 0.01, size=(N, T))         # N x T daily returns
sharpes = rets.mean(axis=1) / rets.std(axis=1) * np.sqrt(252)

print("strategies tested      :", N)
print("best in-sample Sharpe  :", round(sharpes.max(), 2))   # looks great...
print("...but TRUE edge is     : 0.0  (all noise)")

# luck hurdle: expected max Sharpe of N zero-edge trials
sigma_SR = np.sqrt(252 / T)                          # SE of one annualized Sharpe
hurdle   = sigma_SR * np.sqrt(2 * np.log(N))
print("luck hurdle  E[max SR] :", round(hurdle, 2))  # best SR is near this, as expected

# --- in-sample vs out-of-sample on the single 'best' strategy ---
best = sharpes.argmax()
r    = pd.Series(rets[best])
half = T // 2
sr   = lambda x: x.mean() / x.std() * np.sqrt(252)
print("chosen strategy  IS Sharpe :", round(sr(r[:half]), 2))   # high (we selected on it)
print("chosen strategy OOS Sharpe :", round(sr(r[half:]), 2))   # collapses ~ 0

The best of 900 noise strategies posts a Sharpe near the √(2 ln N) hurdle — proof the number came from searching, not skill. Splitting that winner into halves drives the point home: the in-sample half looks good because we picked it, while the out-of-sample half reverts to ~0. Swap the synthetic returns for a real moving-average grid in vectorbt and you will see the same degradation on live price data.

🔗 Where this is heading

A single IS/OOS split wastes data and can itself be gamed if you peek too often. Week 9 (Walk-Forward Validation) generalizes the holdout into a rolling sequence of re-optimize-then-test windows, simulating how a strategy would actually be re-tuned through time. Beyond this course, the multiple-testing lens powers the false-discovery-rate controls used across modern factor research, and the deflated Sharpe idea reappears whenever you compare many models in Course 5's machine-learning module. Capstone checkpoint: by the end of this week your project should report its headline strategy twice — once in-sample and once on a never-touched out-of-sample window — alongside an honest count of how many variants you tried. That single discipline separates a research result from a press release.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. You test 60 independent, genuinely worthless strategies and keep any that pass at the 5% significance level. About how many do you expect to pass by chance?
A. 0 — worthless strategies never pass
B. about 3
C. about 30
D. exactly 1
Show answer

B. The expected count of false winners is N·α = 60 · 0.05 = 3. (A) is wrong because the 5% threshold lets worthless rules pass by luck; 30 (C) would be N·0.5; the count is an expectation, not a fixed 1 (D).

Q2. A strategy posts a Sharpe of 2.4 in-sample but 0.1 out-of-sample. The most likely diagnosis is…
A. transaction costs were ignored
B. the out-of-sample period was a bull market
C. overfitting — the rules fit in-sample noise that did not recur
D. the Sharpe ratio formula was applied incorrectly
Show answer

C. Large positive IS-minus-OOS degradation is the signature of overfitting. Costs (A) would lower both windows similarly; a bull market (B) would not specifically crush only the OOS Sharpe; a formula error (D) would not produce a clean strong-then-weak split.

Q3. Using a Bonferroni correction, if you searched 50 strategy variants and want an overall false-positive rate of 5%, each variant should be tested at roughly…
A. p < 0.05
B. p < 0.25
C. p < 0.50
D. p < 0.001
Show answer

D. Bonferroni uses α/N = 0.05/50 = 0.001. Keeping 0.05 (A) ignores the search entirely; 0.25 (B) and 0.50 (C) loosen rather than tighten the bar, which is backwards.

Q4. Why does the deflated Sharpe ratio shrink as the number of trials N grows, all else equal?
A. Because the luck hurdle E[max Sharpe] ≈ σ·√(2 ln N) rises with N, so more of the observed Sharpe is attributable to luck
B. Because more trials reduce the standard error of each estimate
C. Because the true Sharpe falls when you test more strategies
D. Because Sharpe ratios are always negative after correction
Show answer

A. More trials raise the expected best-of-N Sharpe from luck, so the same observed Sharpe clears a higher bar and deflates. (B) is false — testing more strategies does not sharpen any single estimate; the true Sharpe is a property of the strategy, not of how many you tried (C); and deflated Sharpes are not uniformly negative (D).

Q5. A parameter-sensitivity plot shows performance that is high across a wide band of lookback values rather than spiking at one. This is evidence that the strategy is…
A. overfit and should be discarded
B. more robust, because small parameter errors barely change the result
C. guaranteed to be profitable live
D. using too few parameters to be useful
Show answer

B. A broad plateau means performance does not depend on hitting one magic value, the hallmark of robustness. A lonely spike would suggest overfitting (A), but a plateau is the opposite. No backtest guarantees live profit (C), and a flat, wide response is a virtue, not a sign of too few parameters (D).

✓ What you learned

  • Defined overfitting and data snooping: fitting and re-fitting one history captures noise, not repeatable edge, and inflates the backtest.
  • Quantified the multiple-testing trap: P(false winner) = 1 − (1−α)N and E[false winners] = N·α, with the Bonferroni α/N fix.
  • Used the deflated Sharpe idea — comparing your best Sharpe to the luck hurdle σ·√(2 ln N) — to discount results found by search.
  • Applied in-sample vs out-of-sample splits and parameter-sensitivity plateaus to detect curve-fitting before risking capital.
  • Argued from degrees of freedom that simpler strategies generalize better: fewer knobs mean less room for noise to pose as signal.
