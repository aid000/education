Pairs Trading & Cointegration

Two stocks that wander apart but always drift back let you bet on the gap closing — a market-neutral trade built on a statistical leash called cointegration.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the core idea of pairs trading — going long one asset and short another so the position profits when their relationship reverts, not when the market rises or falls.
  • Recall what cointegration means and why it is stronger than mere correlation for building a stationary spread.
  • Construct the spread between two cointegrated assets and estimate the hedge ratio by ordinary least-squares regression.
  • Turn the spread into a z-score and design concrete entry, exit, and stop rules around it.
  • Identify the dominant risk — the cointegration relationship breaking down — and how to detect and defend against it.

4.1What Pairs Trading Actually Bets On

Last week's mean-reversion strategies bet that a single series — a price, a return, an oscillator — would snap back to its own average. Pairs trading applies the same instinct to the difference between two related assets. Pick two securities driven by the same economic forces — two refiners, two payment networks, a stock and its sector ETF — and their prices should march together. When one temporarily races ahead of the other, you short the rich one and buy the cheap one, then wait for the gap to close.

The beauty is that the trade is roughly market-neutral. You are long one asset and short another of similar size, so if the whole market jumps or crashes, the two legs largely cancel. What remains is a bet purely on the relative mispricing — the spread — closing. That is a fundamentally different, and often less crowded, source of return than simply being long the market.

The catch is that "they move together" is a vibe, not a strategy. We need a precise, testable statement that the gap between them is bounded and keeps returning to a stable level. That statement is cointegration.

AnalogyThink of two dogs being walked on a single leash by the same owner. Each dog wanders freely — neither path is predictable on its own (each is a random walk). But the leash means the distance between them can never grow without bound; stretch it and it snaps back. Pairs trading bets on the leash: when the dogs drift far apart, you wager they will close the gap.

4.2Cointegration, Recapped

In the time-series course you met stationarity: a series whose mean and variance don't drift over time. A raw stock price is not stationary — it is a random walk that can wander anywhere. Differencing it (taking returns) makes it stationary; we call such a price series integrated of order one, written I(1).

Here is the subtle part. Two series can each individually be I(1) random walks, yet a particular linear combination of them can be stationary — I(0). When that happens, the two series are cointegrated. The combination that is stationary is exactly the spread we want to trade.

If PA, PB are each I(1) but  St = PA,t − β·PB,t is I(0),
then A and B are cointegrated with hedge ratio β.Each price wanders forever; the combination St does not — it has a stable mean and bounded swings.

Why insist on cointegration rather than just correlation? Correlation measures whether two series move together day to day. Two independent random walks that happen to trend the same way can show high correlation for a long stretch — pure coincidence that evaporates without warning. Correlation says nothing about whether the gap stays bounded. Cointegration is the stronger, structural claim: there is a genuine long-run equilibrium tying the two together, and deviations from it are temporary.

The practical test (Engle–Granger): regress one price on the other, take the residuals, and run a stationarity test (the augmented Dickey–Fuller test) on those residuals. If the test rejects the "random walk" null, the residual — the spread — is stationary and the pair is cointegrated.

Common mistakeScreening pairs by correlation alone is the classic rookie error. High correlation with no cointegration gives you a spread that looks tame in-sample but has no force pulling it back — it can drift to infinity and bankrupt the trade. Always test for cointegration on the levels, not just correlation on the returns.

4.3Building the Spread & Estimating the Hedge Ratio

To form the spread we need the number β, the hedge ratio: how many units (or dollars) of asset B to short for every unit of asset A you hold, so that the combined position has no net directional drift. We estimate it by ordinary least-squares regression of one price on the other:

PA,t = α + β·PB,t + εt  ⟹   St = PA,t − β·PB,t = α + εtβ is the OLS slope; the spread St is just the regression residual (plus the constant α). The intercept α sets the spread's mean.

The slope β answers: "when B moves one dollar, how much does A move on average?" Shorting β units of B against one unit of A neutralizes that co-movement, leaving only the stationary residual. If A and B are genuinely cointegrated, that residual εt is mean-reverting — exactly the tradeable signal.

Once you have the spread series, summarize it by its own historical mean μ and standard deviation σ. These let you measure, at any moment, how far the spread has stretched from equilibrium in units of its own typical wobble — the z-score:

zt = (St − μ) / σzt = 0 means the spread is at its average; zt = +2 means it is 2 standard deviations rich (A too expensive vs B).
Common mistakeComputing μ and σ over your whole dataset, including the future, is look-ahead bias. In a live or backtested system you must compute the z-score from a rolling or expanding window that only uses data available up to time t. A spread that looks beautifully reverting with hindsight statistics can be untradeable in real time.

4.4Z-Score Trading Rules & the Breakdown Risk

With a z-score in hand, the strategy becomes a clean set of thresholds. A common rule set:

Signalz-score conditionAction
Entry (short spread)zt ≥ +2Short A, buy β·B — bet the rich gap narrows
Entry (long spread)zt ≤ −2Buy A, short β·B — bet the cheap gap widens back
Exit|zt| ≤ 0.5 (or z crosses 0)Close both legs — spread is back near equilibrium
Stop|zt| ≥ 3.5Cut the loss — the relationship may be broken

The logic mirrors §4.3: a large positive z means A is expensive relative to B, so you short the spread; a large negative z means A is cheap, so you buy it. You harvest the move back toward z = 0. Entry at ±2 and exit near 0 means each round trip earns roughly the swing from the band to the mean, scaled by your position size.

Now the central danger. Everything above assumes the spread keeps reverting. The dominant risk in pairs trading is cointegration breakdown: the structural link that held the pair together dissolves. A merger, a regulatory change, a product failure, a credit downgrade — any of these can make A and B permanently re-rate relative to each other. The spread then doesn't revert; it walks off to a new level and never comes back.

A widening spread is ambiguous:
temporary dislocation (add / hold)  vs.  structural break (cut)The same z = +3 could be the best entry of the year — or the moment the leash snapped. The stop exists precisely because you cannot tell in real time.

This is why a hard stop on z (e.g. |z| ≥ 3.5) and a time stop (exit if the trade hasn't reverted within N days) are not optional decorations — they are the strategy's survival mechanism. Pairs trading without stops is the textbook "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller": many small wins punctuated by the occasional ruinous loss when a pair de-couples for good. You should also periodically re-test cointegration and re-estimate β on a rolling basis, retiring any pair whose ADF test stops rejecting.

AnalogyThe breakdown risk is the leash breaking, not just stretching. As long as the leash holds, every stretch is an opportunity. But you can't see the frayed strand until it snaps — so you back away (stop out) whenever the dogs run unusually far, because that is exactly when a snapped leash and a mere long stretch look identical.
✦ Worked Example — sizing and a round-trip on a cointegrated pair

A regression of A on B over the past year gives slope β = 0.80 and intercept α = $5.00. The residual spread St = PA − 0.80·PB has mean μ = $5.00 and standard deviation σ = $1.20. Today PA = $52.00 and PB = $55.00.

  1. Compute today's spread: S = 52.00 − 0.80·55.00 = 52.00 − 44.00 = $8.00.
  2. Z-score it: z = (S − μ)/σ = (8.00 − 5.00)/1.20 = 3.00/1.20 = +2.5. The spread is 2.5σ rich, comfortably past the +2 entry threshold.
  3. Direction: z is large and positive ⟹ A is expensive relative to B ⟹ short the spread: short 1 unit of A, buy β = 0.80 units of B per unit of A.
  4. Dollar-neutral check: short $52.00 of A vs. long 0.80·$55.00 = $44.00 of B. Roughly balanced; the residual $8 directional tilt is the deliberate bet on the spread, not on the market.
  5. Exit: two weeks later the gap has closed and S = $5.30, so z = (5.30 − 5.00)/1.20 = +0.25 — below the 0.5 exit band. Close both legs. The spread fell from $8.00 to $5.30, a $2.70 per-unit gain on the short-spread position (you sold the spread at 8.00, bought it back at 5.30).
  6. Risk note: had the spread instead climbed to S = $9.20, z = (9.20−5.00)/1.20 = 3.5 — the hard stop fires and you exit for a loss, on the suspicion the cointegration may have broken.

🛠️ Hands-On: test cointegration and z-score a spread

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + NumPy + Matplotlib — the full install details lives in the early lectures). This lecture adds statsmodels for the OLS hedge ratio and the cointegration test, so a one-line install note is below.

⬇ New tool this week: statsmodels

Activate your existing quant-env, then add statsmodels (it pulls in the Engle–Granger coint test and OLS):

pip install statsmodels
# pairs_demo.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import coint

# --- load two price series (replace with your own CSV / data feed) ---
# df has a DatetimeIndex and columns "A" and "B" (adjusted close prices)
df = pd.read_csv("pair_prices.csv", index_col=0, parse_dates=True).dropna()
A, B = df["A"], df["B"]

# --- 1. cointegration test (Engle-Granger) ---
score, pvalue, _ = coint(A, B)
print(f"coint p-value: {pvalue:.4f}")          # p < 0.05 -> cointegrated

# --- 2. hedge ratio beta via OLS: A = alpha + beta*B ---
X = sm.add_constant(B)                          # adds the intercept column
beta = sm.OLS(A, X).fit().params["B"]
spread = A - beta * B

# --- 3. z-score on a ROLLING window (no look-ahead) ---
win  = 30
mu   = spread.rolling(win).mean()
sd   = spread.rolling(win).std()
z    = (spread - mu) / sd

# --- 4. signal: short spread at z>=+2, long at z<=-2, flat near 0 ---
pos = pd.Series(0, index=z.index)
pos[z >=  2.0] = -1                              # short the spread
pos[z <= -2.0] =  1                              # long the spread
pos = pos.where((z.abs() > 0.5)).ffill().fillna(0)   # hold until exit band

# spread P&L of holding the position from t-1 to t
pnl = pos.shift(1) * spread.diff()
print("beta:", round(beta, 3), " total spread P&L:", round(pnl.sum(), 2))

Note the rolling mu/sd: the z-score at time t uses only the prior 30 observations, so the backtest is honest. In Week 5 you will feed exactly this signal into a proper backtester that adds transaction costs, borrow fees on the short leg, and the hard/time stops from §4.4.

Q1. Why is cointegration a better screening criterion for a pairs trade than correlation alone?
A. Correlation is impossible to compute for stock prices
B. Cointegration guarantees both stocks will always rise
C. Cointegration implies a stationary, mean-reverting spread, whereas high correlation can occur between two unrelated random walks whose gap drifts without bound
D. Correlation only works for bonds, not stocks
Show answer

C. Cointegration is the structural claim that a linear combination of the (non-stationary) prices is stationary — a bounded, reverting spread. Correlation only describes co-movement and can be spuriously high for independent random walks (A and D are simply false; B confuses cointegration with a directional bet — the spread reverts, the prices need not rise).

Q2. Two price series PA and PB are each individually I(1) random walks. They are cointegrated when…
A. some linear combination S = PA − βPB is stationary, I(0)
B. their daily returns have correlation above 0.9
C. both prices are trending upward over the sample
D. their variances are exactly equal
Show answer

A. That is the definition: individually non-stationary series whose linear combination is stationary. High return correlation (B) is neither necessary nor sufficient; a shared uptrend (C) is often the source of spurious correlation, not cointegration; equal variances (D) are unrelated to the concept.

Q3. You regress PA on PB and get slope β = 1.5. To form a market-neutral spread you should…
A. ignore β and trade one share of each
B. hold 1 unit of A against 1.5 units of B (S = PA − 1.5·PB)
C. hold 1.5 units of A against 1 unit of B
D. short both A and B equally
Show answer

B. The hedge ratio β is the OLS slope: for every unit of A, take β = 1.5 units of B on the opposite side, so the spread S = PA − 1.5·PB is the stationary residual. Trading one share each (A) leaves a net directional exposure; (C) inverts the ratio; (D) shorting both is not a spread trade at all.

Q4. The spread's z-score reaches +2.4. Under the standard rule set, you should…
A. buy the spread (long A, short B), betting it widens further
B. do nothing until z exceeds +5
C. close any existing position immediately
D. short the spread (short A, buy βB), betting the rich gap reverts toward the mean
Show answer

D. A high positive z means A is expensive relative to B, so you short the spread and profit as it falls back toward zero. Buying it (A) bets the wrong way; +2.4 already clears the +2 entry, so waiting for +5 (B) misses the trade; closing (C) is the exit rule (|z| near 0), not the entry.

Q5. What is the single biggest risk that justifies a hard stop in a pairs trade?
A. The cointegration relationship breaks down structurally, so the spread walks off and never reverts
B. Both stocks pay a dividend on the same day
C. The market index rises sharply
D. The two stocks become more correlated
Show answer

A. A merger, downgrade, or regime change can dissolve the equilibrium that tied the pair together; the spread then trends away indefinitely, turning a "reverting" trade into an unbounded loss — exactly what the stop guards against. A broad market move (C) is hedged away by the market-neutral construction; greater correlation (D) is benign or helpful; synchronized dividends (B) are a minor accounting detail.

✓ What you learned

  • Pairs trading goes long one asset and short a related one, profiting from the relative mispricing closing — a market-neutral bet on the spread, not on market direction.
  • Cointegration means two I(1) prices have a stationary linear combination; it is the structural, testable claim that mere correlation cannot make.
  • The hedge ratio β is the OLS slope of one price on the other, and the spread is the regression residual S = PA − βPB.
  • The z-score (S − μ)/σ on a rolling window drives concrete entry (±2), exit (near 0), and stop (±3.5) rules — avoiding look-ahead bias.
  • The dominant risk is cointegration breakdown; hard stops, time stops, and rolling re-tests of the ADF/coint statistic are the survival mechanism.
