Walk-Forward & Out-of-Sample Validation

A backtest only earns your trust if it was tested on data it never saw being built — this week we engineer that firewall between fitting and judging.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a single in-sample backtest overstates performance, and what out-of-sample data is for.
  • Run a walk-forward analysis with rolling (or anchored) train/test windows that respect the arrow of time.
  • Articulate precisely why ordinary k-fold cross-validation leaks information in a time series.
  • Describe purging and embargoing as the fixes that close the leak around label horizons.
  • Build a leakage-free validation routine in Python that fits on the past and scores only on the strict future.

9.1Why One Backtest Lies

In Week 5 you built a backtester; in Week 8 you saw how easily it overfits when you tune parameters against the very data you measure on. A single backtest run over your whole history answers the wrong question. It tells you "what set of rules would have worked best on this exact past" — and the answer to that is always flattering, because you handpicked the rules to fit the bumps that already happened. That is in-sample performance, and it is the résumé a strategy writes about itself.

What you actually trade on is the future — data the strategy has never touched. So the only honest test is to fit the strategy on one slice of history and then judge it on a different, later slice it was never allowed to see. That later slice is the out-of-sample (OOS) data. The gap between in-sample and out-of-sample performance is the single most informative number in all of backtesting: it estimates how much of your beautiful equity curve was skill and how much was hindsight.

Optimism gap = Sharpein-sample − Sharpeout-of-sampleA large positive gap is the fingerprint of overfitting. Honest strategies degrade a little out of sample; overfit ones collapse.

The discipline here is simple to state and hard to honor: the test set must stay sealed until every modeling decision is final. If you peek — adjusting a stop-loss after seeing the OOS curve dip — the test data quietly becomes training data, and your "out-of-sample" estimate is a fiction.

AnalogyThink of a closed-book exam. Studying is fitting; the exam is the test set. If a student sees the exam questions while studying, a perfect score proves nothing about what they know — only that they memorized that paper. A backtest that has touched its own test data is exactly that student. Walk-forward analysis is the proctor who keeps the exam locked in a drawer until study time is over.

9.2Walk-Forward Analysis

A single train/test split wastes data: you fit on the first 70% and test on the last 30%, but then your verdict rests on just one market regime. Walk-forward analysis generalizes the idea. Slide a train window forward through time; after each train window, test on the block of data that immediately follows it; then advance and repeat. You stitch the test blocks together into one continuous out-of-sample track record.

Two flavors differ in how the training window grows:

SchemeTrain windowBest for
Rolling (sliding)fixed length; oldest data drops off as the window advancesadapting to regime change; emphasizes recent behavior
Anchored (expanding)always starts at t = 0 and grows; nothing is discardedstable processes where more history strictly helps

Crucially, every test block comes strictly after the data used to fit it. The model is always predicting forward, never backward. If you have N folds, you get N out-of-sample scores; their average and — just as important — their dispersion tell you whether the edge is robust across time or just lucky in one window.

OOS Sharpe estimate = mean( Sharpefold 1, …, Sharpefold N )Report the spread too. A high mean with huge fold-to-fold variance is a warning, not a green light.

9.3Why k-Fold Cross-Validation Leaks

In ordinary machine learning the gold standard is k-fold cross-validation: shuffle the rows, split into k folds, train on k−1 and test on the one held out, rotate, average. It works beautifully when rows are exchangeable — independent draws with no order. Financial time series are the opposite: rows are ordered, autocorrelated, and labels often span time. Two distinct leaks follow.

Leak 1 — training on the future

If you shuffle and split by row, a fold used for training will contain timestamps that come after some of your test timestamps. The model effectively learns from tomorrow to predict today. Out of sample you will never have tomorrow's data, so the cross-validated score is wildly optimistic — pure look-ahead bias.

Leak 2 — overlapping labels at the boundary

Most trading labels look forward: "the return over the next 5 days," or a triple-barrier outcome that resolves whenever a stop or target is hit. A label stamped at the last day of the training block depends on prices that fall inside the test block. So even with a clean time-ordered split, the rows straddling the train/test boundary share information. The test set is contaminated by features and labels that overlap its training neighbors.

leakage window ≈ label horizon h  ⟹  rows within h of the split are entangledIf your label looks h bars ahead, the last h training rows and first h test rows are not independent.
Common mistakeCalling sklearn.model_selection.KFold (which shuffles by default) or train_test_split(shuffle=True) on price data. It silently mixes future into past and reports a Sharpe that evaporates live. For time series you must split by time — use TimeSeriesSplit at minimum, and add purging/embargo when labels overlap.

9.4Purging & Embargoing

Time-ordered splits fix Leak 1. To close Leak 2 we surgically remove the entangled rows near the boundary. Two complementary operations do this.

Purging

Purge any training observation whose label window overlaps the test set. If a training row's outcome is determined by prices that fall inside the test period, drop that training row. Purging removes the contamination flowing backward from the test set into training.

Embargoing

Autocorrelation and microstructure can let information bleed forward too: prices just after the test block still echo it. An embargo deletes a small buffer of training rows immediately following the test block — typically a fraction (say 1%) of the sample, or a few label horizons. The combination is often called purged, embargoed cross-validation.

embargo length ≈ max(label horizon, autocorr decay)   (e.g. 1% of T)Purge across the test boundary; embargo a buffer after it. Both shrink the usable data — and that honest shrinkage is the point.
AnalogyPurging and embargoing are quarantine. The test block is a patient with a contagious sample; you isolate the rows that shook hands with it just before (purge) and just after (embargo) so the "healthy" training set stays uninfected. You lose a few data points to the cordon — a cheap price for an uncontaminated verdict.

✦ Worked Example — sizing a purge and reading the optimism gap

You have 5 years of daily bars (≈ 1260 trading days). Your label is the forward 10-day return, and you run a 4-fold time-ordered walk-forward with a 1% embargo. Set up the cordon and interpret the result.

  1. Label horizon: h = 10 days. Any training row within 10 days before a test block has a label that reaches into that block — those rows get purged.
  2. Embargo size: 1% of T = 0.01 × 1260 ≈ 13 days. After each test block, delete the next ~13 training days so post-test autocorrelation can't leak back.
  3. Data lost per boundary: ≈ 10 purged + 13 embargoed = 23 days sacrificed at each train/test seam — about 1.8% of the sample. Honest, and cheap.
  4. Fold scores: suppose the four out-of-sample folds give Sharpe = 1.4, 0.3, 0.9, 0.5 → mean 0.78, with wide dispersion (one fold near zero).
  5. Read the gap: the in-sample fit boasted Sharpe 2.1, so the optimism gap is 2.1 − 0.78 ≈ 1.3 — most of the in-sample glory was hindsight. The honest expectation going live is ~0.78, and the fold spread warns it could be far lower in a bad regime.

🛠️ Hands-On: a leakage-free walk-forward loop

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + NumPy + scikit-learn + Matplotlib — the install block lives in Lecture 1). We use scikit-learn's TimeSeriesSplit for the time-ordered folds, then add a manual purge + embargo so labels can't leak across the seam.

# walk_forward.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np, pandas as pd
from sklearn.model_selection import TimeSeriesSplit

# --- toy daily data: features X and a FORWARD-looking label y ---
T, H = 1260, 10                          # 5y of bars; label = next-10-day return
px   = pd.Series(100 * np.exp(np.cumsum(
        np.random.default_rng(0).normal(0, .01, T))))
ret  = px.pct_change().fillna(0.0)
y    = px.shift(-H) / px - 1.0           # forward 10-day return (look-ahead BY DESIGN)
X    = pd.DataFrame({"mom": ret.rolling(20).mean(),
                     "vol": ret.rolling(20).std()}).fillna(0.0)

def purge_embargo(train_idx, test_idx, horizon=H, embargo_pct=0.01):
    # drop train rows whose label window overlaps the test block...
    t0, t1 = test_idx.min(), test_idx.max()
    emb    = int(len(X) * embargo_pct)
    keep   = [i for i in train_idx
              if (i + horizon) < t0          # purge: label ends before test starts
              or i > t1 + emb]               # embargo: buffer after test block
    return np.array(keep)

tscv, scores = TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=4), []
for tr, te in tscv.split(X):
    tr = purge_embargo(tr, te)
    # fit ONLY on purged train, score ONLY on the strictly-future test block
    mu  = X.iloc[tr].mean(); sd = X.iloc[tr].std()
    sig = ((X.iloc[te] - mu) / sd)["mom"]    # standardize w/ TRAIN stats only
    pnl = np.sign(sig) * y.iloc[te]          # trade on the signal, realize OOS pnl
    scores.append(np.sqrt(252) * pnl.mean() / (pnl.std() + 1e-9))

print("per-fold OOS Sharpe :", np.round(scores, 2))
print("mean / std          :", round(np.mean(scores), 2), round(np.std(scores), 2))

Note two leakage guards: the standardization statistics (mu, sd) are computed on the training rows only — never on the full series — and purge_embargo deletes the entangled rows around each seam. Swap in vectorbt or backtrader for the inner trade simulation; the walk-forward scaffold around it is unchanged. Report the mean and the spread of the per-fold Sharpe, not a single headline number.

💬 Discussion

You backtest a strategy on 2015–2024 and it shows a Sharpe of 1.6. A teammate proposes: "Let's run the walk-forward, and if any fold looks weak, we'll re-tune the parameters on the full history until all folds look good, then ship it." What is wrong with this plan, and how would you redesign the validation so the final number is trustworthy? Consider where the test data leaks, what role a final untouched holdout plays, and how you'd report uncertainty rather than a single Sharpe.

Show a model response

The plan re-introduces exactly the leak walk-forward is meant to prevent. Re-tuning parameters until "all folds look good" means the out-of-sample folds are now steering the model choice — they have become a second training set. After enough re-tuning, the walk-forward score is in-sample again, and the 1.6 Sharpe is hindsight wearing a disguise. This is a form of multiple testing (Week 8): try enough parameter sets and one will fit the folds by chance.

A trustworthy redesign: (1) Carve off a final holdout — say the last 1–2 years — and lock it in a drawer, untouched. (2) Do all tuning inside a purged, embargoed walk-forward on the earlier data only, fixing the parameter selection rule (not the parameters) before looking at folds. (3) Run the frozen strategy once on the holdout; that single number is your honest OOS estimate. (4) Report the distribution of per-fold Sharpes — mean and spread — and, if you tried many configurations, deflate the Sharpe for the number of trials. A wide fold spread or a holdout that collapses is a reject signal, not an invitation to tune again.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (a) identifies the re-tuning loop as turning OOS data into training data / multiple testing; (b) proposes a sealed final holdout used exactly once; (c) keeps purge + embargo in the walk-forward; and (d) reports uncertainty (fold dispersion or deflated Sharpe) instead of a lone headline figure.

Q1. The "optimism gap" of a backtest is best described as…
A. the difference between in-sample and out-of-sample performance
B. the strategy's maximum drawdown
C. the transaction cost per trade
D. the gap between the bid and the ask
Show answer

A. The optimism gap is Sharpe(in-sample) − Sharpe(out-of-sample); a large positive gap signals overfitting. B (drawdown), C (costs), and D (bid-ask spread) are real quantities but unrelated to how in-sample flatters a backtest.

Q2. Why does shuffled k-fold cross-validation leak information on a price time series?
A. It uses too few folds to be statistically valid
B. It can place future-dated rows in the training fold, letting the model learn from data that comes after the test rows
C. It always overweights the most recent data
D. It cannot handle missing values
Show answer

B. Shuffling destroys time order, so a training fold can contain timestamps later than test timestamps — pure look-ahead bias. A is about fold count (not the leak), C is the opposite of what shuffling does, and D is an unrelated implementation detail.

Q3. In a rolling walk-forward, what must be true of every test block relative to its training data?
A. The test block must be drawn randomly from all of history
B. The test block must come strictly after the data used to train the model for that fold
C. The test block must be larger than the training window
D. The test block must overlap the training window for continuity
Show answer

B. Walk-forward always predicts forward: each test block sits strictly after its training window. A reintroduces shuffling/look-ahead; C is false (test blocks are usually smaller); D is exactly the overlap that purging exists to eliminate.

Q4. Your label is the forward 10-day return. Which rows does purging remove?
A. All rows in the test block
B. A random 10% of the training data
C. Training rows whose 10-day label window overlaps the test block
D. The most volatile rows in each fold
Show answer

C. Purging drops training rows whose forward-looking label reaches into the test period, removing the backward contamination. A would delete the test set itself; B and D describe arbitrary deletions unrelated to label overlap.

Q5. An embargo in purged cross-validation does which of the following?
A. Halts trading whenever volatility spikes
B. Caps the position size during the test fold
C. Replaces the test set with synthetic data
D. Deletes a small buffer of training rows immediately after the test block to stop forward information bleed
Show answer

D. The embargo removes a short buffer of training rows just after the test block, so autocorrelation can't leak the test period into later training data. A and B are risk-management actions, not validation steps; C describes data synthesis, which embargoing never does.

✓ What you learned

  • A single in-sample backtest flatters itself; only a sealed out-of-sample test estimates live performance, and the optimism gap measures the overfitting.
  • Walk-forward analysis slides train/test windows forward in time — rolling or anchored — yielding many OOS folds whose mean and spread both matter.
  • Ordinary k-fold CV leaks two ways on time series: shuffling trains on the future, and forward-looking labels entangle rows across the train/test boundary.
  • Purging drops training rows whose labels overlap the test block; embargoing deletes a buffer just after it — together closing the boundary leak.
  • Built a leakage-free routine: time-ordered folds, train-only standardization, and a final sealed holdout used exactly once.
