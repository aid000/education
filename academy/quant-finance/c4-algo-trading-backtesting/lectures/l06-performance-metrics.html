Week 6 · Lecture 6

Performance Metrics

A backtest hands you a single equity curve and a thousand ways to fool yourself — these few numbers are how you find out whether the strategy is actually good.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Convert a backtest's equity curve into total and annualized return, and explain why compounding and annualizing matter.
  • Measure risk as the annualized volatility of returns, and combine return and risk into the Sharpe ratio.
  • Refine risk-adjustment with the Sortino ratio (downside-only) and the Calmar ratio (return over max drawdown).
  • Compute maximum drawdown, win rate, and turnover, and know what each does and does not tell you.
  • Read an equity curve critically — spotting the smooth-looking curves that hide ruinous risk.

6.1From Equity Curve to Return

In Week 5 you built a backtester that produces one core artifact: the equity curve — the value of your account over time, day by day. Every metric in this lecture is just a different lens on that curve. The first question is the obvious one: how much did the money grow?

If the account starts at value V0 and ends at VT, the total return is simply the ratio minus one. But total return alone is meaningless until you know over what period. A 50% gain over six months is spectacular; the same 50% over twelve years is mediocre. To compare strategies fairly we put everything on a per-year footing — we annualize.

Rtotal = VT / V0 − 1     Rann = (VT / V0)(1/y) − 1y = number of years. Annualizing uses a geometric root, not division, because returns compound.

The geometric root is the crucial subtlety. Money that grows compounds on itself, so you cannot simply divide total return by the number of years. If you double your money over 4 years, your annualized return is 21/4 − 1 ≈ 18.9%, not 100%/4 = 25%. The same logic applies when annualizing from daily data: a strategy with daily simple returns is compounded over the ~252 trading days in a year.

Rann = (1 + r̄daily)252 − 1   or, on log returns:    Rann,log = 252 · r̄logLog returns add cleanly, which is why most metric code works in log space then exponentiates once at the end.
AnalogyTotal return is the final score; annualized return is the average speed. Two cars can both travel 300 miles, but one did it in 3 hours and the other in 10. Annualizing converts every trip to miles-per-hour so you can line them up side by side — exactly what you need when comparing a 6-month crypto bot against a 5-year equity strategy.

6.2Volatility and the Sharpe Ratio

Return is only half the story. A strategy that earns 20% a year by lurching wildly between +40% and −30% is very different from one that earns 20% in a near-straight line — yet both report the same return. The missing dimension is risk, and the workhorse measure of risk is volatility: the standard deviation of the strategy's returns.

Like return, volatility must be annualized so strategies are comparable. Because the variance of a sum of (roughly) independent daily returns adds, the standard deviation scales with the square root of time — the same √n you met in Course 1's Central Limit Theorem. With ~252 trading days in a year:

σann = σdaily · √252The "square-root-of-time" rule. A daily volatility of 1% becomes ~15.9% annualized.

Now combine the two. The Sharpe ratio asks: how much excess return do you earn per unit of volatility? Subtract the risk-free rate rf (what you'd earn with no risk at all), then divide by volatility. It is the single most cited number in all of quant finance.

Sharpe = (Rann − rf) / σannEquivalently, from periodic returns: Sharpe = √252 · mean(r − rf,daily) / std(r).

As a rough field guide: a Sharpe below 1 is unimpressive, around 1 is respectable for a long-only strategy, 2 is very good, and anything claiming 4+ on a backtest should make you deeply suspicious of a bug or lookahead leak. The Sharpe is what lets you rank a high-return-high-risk strategy against a steady-but-modest one on a level playing field.

Common mistakeDon't forget the √252 when annualizing the Sharpe from daily data, and don't double-annualize. A frequent bug is annualizing the mean return by ×252 but the volatility by ×√252 inconsistently, or applying √252 twice. Sharpe is a ratio, so the time-scaling is exactly √252 overall — annualize numerator and denominator coherently.

6.3Sortino, Drawdown, and Calmar

The Sharpe ratio has a famous flaw: it treats upside volatility as a bad thing. A strategy that occasionally spikes +20% in a single day is penalized for that spike exactly as if it had dropped −20%. But no investor complains about big gains. The Sortino ratio fixes this by dividing only by downside deviation — the standard deviation computed using only the returns that fell below a target (usually zero or rf).

Sortino = (Rann − rf) / σdownside    σdownside = √(252 · mean( min(r − target, 0)² ))Same idea as Sharpe, but only losses count as "risk." Sortino ≥ Sharpe for any strategy with positive-skew returns.

The other risk an investor truly feels is not day-to-day wiggle but the peak-to-trough collapse — how far the equity curve fell from its highest point before recovering. This is the maximum drawdown (MDD), and it is what actually triggers margin calls, redemptions, and panic. Computing it is mechanical: track the running maximum of the curve, then the worst percentage gap below it.

Drawdown(t) = Vt / maxs≤t Vs − 1    MDD = mint Drawdown(t)MDD is negative (e.g. −35%): the deepest the account ever sat below its prior high-water mark.

The Calmar ratio then pairs annualized return against the pain of that worst drop — return per unit of "how bad did it get." It speaks directly to the gut-check question every investor asks: can I stomach the worst stretch this strategy will likely put me through?

Calmar = Rann / |MDD|A Calmar of 0.5 means your yearly return is half of your worst historical drawdown.

6.4Win Rate, Turnover, and Reading the Curve Honestly

Two more numbers describe how a strategy makes its money. The win rate is the fraction of trades that were profitable. It is intuitive but treacherous: a strategy can win 90% of the time and still lose money if its rare losses are huge, or win only 35% of the time and be wildly profitable if its winners dwarf its losers. Win rate must always be read together with the average win/loss size (the "payoff ratio").

Win rate = (# profitable trades) / (total trades)Meaningless alone. Expectancy = WinRate · AvgWin − LossRate · AvgLoss is what determines profitability.

Turnover measures how much trading a strategy does — roughly, the total value traded relative to the portfolio size over a period. It matters because every trade pays the spread, commissions, and slippage (the entire subject of Week 7). A strategy with a beautiful gross Sharpe and turnover of 5,000% a year can be a guaranteed money-loser once costs bite. High turnover is a red flag that your backtest's profits may be an illusion that evaporates in live trading.

Finally, the most important skill: reading an equity curve honestly. A pretty, smooth, upward curve is seductive — and a smooth backtest curve is more often a symptom of a bug than a sign of genius.

What a suspiciously good curve is hiding

Warning signLikely causeMetric that exposes it
Implausibly smooth, Sharpe > 4Lookahead bias — using data not yet available at decision timeOut-of-sample Sharpe collapses
Steady up then one cliffHidden tail risk / selling volatilityMax drawdown, Sortino
Gorgeous gross, dies netExcessive tradingTurnover, net Sharpe
Great backtest, weak recent yearsOverfitting to old regimesRolling/out-of-sample returns
Common mistakeJudging a strategy by its final equity or its win rate alone. A curve that ends high can still be uninvestable if it endured a 60% drawdown along the way (no real investor would have stayed in), and a high win rate can mask catastrophic tail losses. Always look at return and drawdown and Sharpe together — never one number in isolation.
AnalogyA great equity curve is like a mountaineer's summit photo. The photo (final equity) looks triumphant, but it tells you nothing about the avalanche they barely survived (max drawdown), how often they slipped (volatility), or whether they got lucky on a route they could never repeat (overfitting). The metrics are the full expedition log — that is what you actually evaluate.
✦ Worked Example — scoring a 2-year backtest

A backtest runs over exactly 2 years (504 trading days). The account grows from $100,000 to $144,000. The daily returns have a standard deviation of 0.9%. The worst peak-to-trough fall was 18%. The risk-free rate is 2%. Score it.

  1. Total return: Rtotal = 144,000 / 100,000 − 1 = 44%.
  2. Annualized return: over y = 2 years, Rann = (1.44)1/2 − 1 = 1.2 − 1 = 20%. (Note: not 44%/2 = 22% — compounding makes the per-year figure lower.)
  3. Annualized volatility: σann = 0.9% · √252 ≈ 0.9% · 15.87 ≈ 14.3%.
  4. Sharpe ratio: (20% − 2%) / 14.3% = 18 / 14.3 ≈ 1.26 — respectable, the kind of number a real long-only strategy might honestly show.
  5. Calmar ratio: Rann / |MDD| = 20% / 18% ≈ 1.11 — the yearly return slightly exceeds the worst drawdown, a tolerable risk profile.
  6. Verdict: a Sharpe near 1.3 and a Calmar above 1, with an 18% max drawdown, describe a plausible, investable strategy — not a too-good-to-be-true curve. Now check turnover and re-run net of costs before believing it.

🛠️ Hands-On: a one-screen metrics report

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + NumPy + Matplotlib — the install block lives in the early lectures). Below is a self-contained function that turns any daily-return series into the full metric panel. Feed it the returns out of the backtester you built in Week 5.

# metrics.py — Lecture 6: turn a daily return series into a scorecard
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd

DAYS = 252

def performance_report(returns: pd.Series, rf_annual: float = 0.02) -> dict:
    r        = returns.dropna()
    rf_daily = rf_annual / DAYS

    # --- returns ---
    equity    = (1 + r).cumprod()              # equity curve, starting at 1.0
    total_ret = equity.iloc[-1] - 1
    years     = len(r) / DAYS
    ann_ret   = equity.iloc[-1] ** (1 / years) - 1

    # --- risk ---
    ann_vol   = r.std() * np.sqrt(DAYS)
    sharpe    = (r.mean() - rf_daily) / r.std() * np.sqrt(DAYS)

    downside  = r[r < 0]                        # target = 0
    dd_dev    = np.sqrt((np.minimum(r, 0) ** 2).mean()) * np.sqrt(DAYS)
    sortino   = (ann_ret - rf_annual) / dd_dev

    # --- drawdown ---
    running_max = equity.cummax()
    drawdown    = equity / running_max - 1
    max_dd      = drawdown.min()               # negative number
    calmar      = ann_ret / abs(max_dd)

    # --- trade stats (returns > 0 used as a proxy for winning days) ---
    win_rate  = (r > 0).mean()

    return {
        "total_return": round(total_ret, 4),
        "annual_return": round(ann_ret, 4),
        "annual_vol":   round(ann_vol, 4),
        "sharpe":       round(sharpe, 2),
        "sortino":      round(sortino, 2),
        "max_drawdown": round(max_dd, 4),
        "calmar":       round(calmar, 2),
        "win_rate":     round(win_rate, 3),
    }

if __name__ == "__main__":
    rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
    # simulate ~2 years of daily returns: small positive drift, 0.9% daily vol
    sim = pd.Series(rng.normal(0.0008, 0.009, 504))
    for k, v in performance_report(sim).items():
        print(f"{k:>14}: {v}")

Two notes that prevent the classic bugs: (1) sortino divides by downside deviation, so it is always ≥ the Sharpe here; (2) win rate on daily returns is a proxy — for true per-trade win rate you'd group by trade, which the Week-5 backtester's trade log gives you directly. Libraries like vectorbt expose all of these as portfolio.stats(), but writing them once yourself is how you learn to distrust a pretty number.

💬 Discussion

Two candidate strategies cross your desk. Strategy A: annualized return 12%, Sharpe 1.8, max drawdown 9%, win rate 41%, turnover 200%/yr. Strategy B: annualized return 28%, Sharpe 1.1, max drawdown 41%, win rate 78%, turnover 4,000%/yr. You can only fund one, with real client money. Which do you choose, and which single metric would most change your mind if you could learn one more thing about each? Defend your reasoning using the metrics from this lecture.

Show a model response

I'd fund Strategy A. Although B's headline 28% return is tempting and its 78% win rate looks reassuring, the metrics tell a darker story. B's Sharpe of 1.1 versus A's 1.8 means A earns far more return per unit of risk — B's high return is being bought with a lot of volatility. B's 41% max drawdown is the dealbreaker: real clients redeem during a drawdown that deep, so I'd likely never get to realize B's long-run return. A's 9% drawdown is something an investor can actually sit through.

B's 78% win rate is a classic trap — a high win rate paired with a 41% drawdown screams "many small wins, rare catastrophic losses" (likely selling volatility). And B's 4,000% turnover is alarming: after Week 7's transaction costs, much of that 28% gross could vanish, while A's modest 200% turnover will survive costs far better. A's lower 41% win rate is fine because its drawdown shows the losers are well-controlled.

The single metric that would most change my mind: for A, I'd want its out-of-sample Sharpe — a 1.8 that holds up on unseen data is excellent; one that collapses signals overfitting. For B, I'd want its net (post-cost) Sharpe given that turnover — if costs barely dent it, B becomes far more interesting.

✓ Self-check rubric. A strong answer: (1) prioritizes risk-adjusted return (Sharpe/Sortino) over raw return; (2) flags the 41% drawdown as a redemption/behavioral risk, not just a number; (3) reads the high win rate together with drawdown as a tail-risk red flag rather than a positive; (4) connects high turnover to next week's transaction costs; and (5) names a forward-looking metric (out-of-sample or net-of-cost performance) rather than trusting the backtest as-is.

Q1. A strategy turns $100,000 into $121,000 over exactly 2 years. Its annualized return is closest to…
A. 21%
B. 10%
C. 10.5%
D. 42%
Show answer

B. Total return is 21%, so Rann = (1.21)1/2 − 1 = 1.1 − 1 = 10% exactly. Dividing 21% by 2 gives 10.5% (C) — that ignores compounding and is the classic error. 21% (A) is the total, not annualized; 42% (D) doubles it the wrong way.

Q2. A strategy's daily returns have a standard deviation of 1%. Its annualized volatility (252 trading days) is closest to…
A. 252%
B. 1%
C. 12%
D. 15.9%
Show answer

D. Volatility scales with the square root of time: 1% · √252 ≈ 1% · 15.87 ≈ 15.9%. Multiplying by 252 (A) is the wrong scaling — that's how you'd annualize variance, not standard deviation. B forgets to annualize; C uses √144 by mistake.

Q3. The key difference between the Sortino ratio and the Sharpe ratio is that the Sortino…
A. uses return over maximum drawdown instead of volatility
B. penalizes only downside (below-target) deviation, not upside volatility
C. ignores the risk-free rate entirely
D. can only be computed on monthly data
Show answer

B. Sortino replaces total volatility with downside deviation, so big upside moves no longer count as "risk." Return over max drawdown (A) is the Calmar ratio. Both Sharpe and Sortino subtract the risk-free rate (C is false), and frequency (D) is irrelevant to the definition.

Q4. An equity curve peaks at $150,000, falls to $96,000, then recovers to a new high. The maximum drawdown over this episode is…
A. −36%
B. −56%
C. −54%
D. −24%
Show answer

A. Drawdown is measured from the prior high-water mark: 96,000 / 150,000 − 1 = 0.64 − 1 = −36%. Measuring the recovery from the trough up (D, +24% to get back partway) or dividing by the trough instead of the peak (C) are the common mistakes. −56% (B) is just wrong arithmetic.

Q5. A backtest shows a remarkably smooth equity curve with a Sharpe ratio of 5.2 and turnover of 6,000% per year. The most prudent first reaction is to…
A. deploy it immediately at maximum size — high Sharpe means low risk
B. conclude the win rate must be 100%
C. suspect lookahead bias and check the net-of-cost, out-of-sample performance
D. ignore the turnover since the Sharpe is so high
Show answer

C. A Sharpe above ~4 on a backtest is a red flag for lookahead bias or a bug, and 6,000% turnover means costs (Week 7) could erase the edge — so you verify out-of-sample and net of costs before trusting it. Deploying blindly (A) and ignoring turnover (D) invert the right caution; Sharpe says nothing about win rate (B).
