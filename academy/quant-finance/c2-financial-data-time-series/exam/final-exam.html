Returns, Data Handling & Stylized Facts · Weeks 1–3
1. A stock's price moves from $100 to $103. The simple (net) return R and the log-return r are related by…
Answer: C. The log-return is r = ln(1+R). The Taylor expansion ln(1+R) ≈ R − R²/2 + … shows r ≈ R for small moves but r is always a touch below R for a positive R.
2. Over two trading days, an asset has log-returns r₁ and r₂. The two-day log-return r(0→2) equals…
Answer: A. Time-additivity: ln(P₂/P₀) = ln(P₁/P₀) + ln(P₂/P₁) = r₁ + r₂. Simple (gross) returns multiply instead; logs turn that product into a sum.
3. A stock does a 2-for-1 split. Computing daily returns from the raw (unadjusted) Close instead of the Adjusted Close will show…
Answer: D. A 2-for-1 split halves the quoted price while doubling share count — wealth is unchanged, but the raw close shows a mechanical ~−50% drop. The Adjusted Close removes this artifact.
4. When resampling a daily price series to month-end values in pandas, the correct aggregation for the price column is…
Answer: B. A month-end price series takes the period's final close. Summing prices, counting days, or differencing are each the wrong operation for a price level (volume, by contrast, is the case where you'd sum).
5. Daily equity index returns typically show positive excess kurtosis. Compared to a normal with the same variance, this means the return distribution has…
Answer: D. Leptokurtic ("fat-tailed") returns have a taller, narrower peak around zero and heavier tails — mass is pulled out of the normal's "shoulders" into both the center and the extremes.
6. For liquid equity returns, which best describes the autocorrelation pattern across lags?
Answer: C. This is the central puzzle: direction is unpredictable (near-zero ACF of returns) while magnitude is highly predictable (strong ACF of |returns|/R²) — the signature of volatility clustering.
Autocorrelation & Stationarity · Week 4
7. The autocorrelation function ρ(k) of a time series measures…
Answer: A. Autocorrelation is the correlation of a series with a lag-k copy of itself. Ordinary correlation between two different variables, a single-point variance, and a normality test are all different concepts.
8. A series' ACF decays gradually across many lags, while its PACF shows one large spike at lag 1 and then drops inside the noise band. This fingerprint best matches…
Answer: B. "PACF cuts off, ACF tails off" is the AR(p) fingerprint (here p=1) — the lag-2+ ACF correlation is inherited through lag 1, not direct. MA(1) would show the reverse pattern; white noise would show no spikes anywhere.
9. The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test's null hypothesis is that the series…
Answer: B. ADF's null is "has a unit root." A large p-value only fails to reject non-stationarity; a small p-value (say <0.05) is needed to reject it and call the series stationary.
10. A stock price series has a unit root (it wanders with no fixed level). The standard fix before time-series modeling is to…
Answer: D. Differencing removes a unit root; one difference of a log price is exactly the log-return, which is much closer to stationary and safe to model.
Smoothing, AR/MA & ARIMA Models · Weeks 5–7
11. Lengthening a simple moving average's window from 20 days to 100 days will…
Answer: A. Longer windows average away more noise (smoother) but center further into the past (more lag) — the smoothing-lag tradeoff; you cannot get both smoother and faster at once.
12. For an AR(1) model Xt = c + φXt−1 + εt to be stationary, φ must satisfy…
Answer: D. |φ| < 1 ensures shocks decay rather than compounding; φ = 1 is a random walk (non-stationary) and φ > 1 explodes. φ = 0 is stationary too but is just the special case of white noise, not the general condition.
13. The key structural difference between an AR(p) and an MA(q) process is that…
Answer: C. AR regresses on its own lagged values with infinitely-decaying memory; MA is a weighted sum of recent white-noise shocks with memory of exactly q lags. B reverses which family has finite memory.
14. A sample ACF cuts off sharply after lag 2 (nothing significant beyond), while the PACF decays gradually across many lags. This combination points to fitting…
Answer: C. "ACF cuts off, PACF tails off" is the MA(q) fingerprint (mnemonic: the PACF counts AR terms, the ACF counts MA terms). An AR(2) would show the reverse pattern.
15. In the ARIMA(p, d, q) notation, the parameter d represents…
Answer: A. d is the integration/differencing order — the "I" in ARIMA. The AR lags are p and the MA error terms are q; the forecast horizon is a separate choice entirely.
16. For a random-walk (ARIMA(0,1,0)) price forecast, the width of the 95% confidence band at horizon h grows approximately…
Answer: D. Forecast variance accumulates with each future shock; for a random walk the h-step standard error grows like σ√h, so the band fans out but more slowly than linearly.
17. A well-tuned ARIMA fit to daily returns barely beats the naive "tomorrow = today" forecast out-of-sample, yet the same toolkit forecasts squared returns well. The right interpretation is…
Answer: C. Efficient markets erase predictable structure in return levels, but volatility clusters — giving squared returns real, exploitable persistence. There is no bug, and the volatility signal is not luck.
GARCH Volatility Modeling · Week 8
18. A GARCH(1,1) model is fit with α = 0.08 and β = 0.90. The persistence α+β = 0.98 being close to 1 means…
Answer: A. The persistence α+β sets the decay rate of a volatility shock toward the long-run level σ̄² = ω/(1−α−β); since 0.98 < 1 the model is stationary and that long-run variance exists — it just takes a long time to get there.
19. A GARCH model's conditional variance forecast tells you…
Answer: B. GARCH models the variance of the shock, not its sign. It never claims to forecast direction or price level; the mean return is handled by a separate (usually constant) mean equation.
20. Compared with ARCH(q), the key advantage of GARCH(1,1) is that…
Answer: D. The βσ²t−1 feedback term recursively packs the entire past into one number, letting GARCH(1,1) match slow decay with three parameters where ARCH would need many lags. α+β > 1 would make variance explode — the opposite of desirable.
21. In σt² = ω + αεt−1² + βσt−1², the term "conditional" in "conditional heteroskedasticity" means…
Answer: B. "Heteroskedasticity" means non-constant variance; "conditional" means given the past information set. σt² is a new number every day, built from yesterday's shock and variance — the opposite of a fixed constant.
Correlation, Cointegration & Capstone Signals · Weeks 9–10
22. The key difference between correlation and cointegration is that…
Answer: C. Correlation is a returns co-movement statistic; cointegration says a specific linear combination of the price levels is stationary. A has the two backwards, and one can hold without the other.
23. In the Engle–Granger procedure, after regressing price Y on price X you run an ADF test on the residual. A small ADF p-value means…
Answer: D. The ADF null is "has a unit root." A small p-value rejects it, meaning the residual (the spread) is stationary — the signature of cointegration. β is whatever OLS estimates, rarely exactly 1.
24. Two statistically independent random-walk price series will, over many samples…
Answer: A. Two unrelated random walks routinely produce a large, deceptively significant spurious correlation (and regression t-stat) over any given sample — exactly why you must test for a stationary spread, not trust correlation.
25. Strong negative lag-1 autocorrelation observed in high-frequency transaction prices (but not in mid-prices) is best explained by…
Answer: B. Bid–ask bounce makes consecutive trade prices ricochet between bid and ask, manufacturing spurious negative autocorrelation with zero real price movement behind it. It is not tradable — capturing it means paying the very spread that creates it — and it is a real microstructure effect, not bad data.
