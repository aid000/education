Week 7 · Lecture 7

ARIMA & Forecasting

Differencing turns a wandering price into a stationary series an ARMA model can chew on — but the same exercise reveals the brutal truth that the next return is almost pure noise.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the ARIMA(p, d, q) model and explain the role of the integration order d as repeated differencing.
  • Decide when a series needs differencing (prices) and when it does not (returns), and recognize over-differencing.
  • Produce multi-step forecasts and understand why they decay toward the mean as the horizon grows.
  • Distinguish in-sample fit from out-of-sample accuracy, and build forecast confidence intervals that widen with horizon.
  • Confront the hard truth: returns are nearly unforecastable, while volatility and volume carry real persistence you can model.

7.1From ARMA to ARIMA: the Missing "I"

Last week's ARMA model came with a fine-print requirement: the series must be stationary — its mean, variance, and autocorrelation cannot drift over time. Returns mostly satisfy this. But the raw object traders actually stare at, the price, does not. A price wanders; it has no fixed level to revert to. Feed a trending price straight into ARMA and the estimates are meaningless.

The fix is the I in ARIMA — "Integrated." Before modeling, we difference the series: replace each value by its change from the previous step. One difference of a price is a return (in level terms). The differencing order d counts how many times we do this.

∇yt = yt − yt−1
ARIMA(p, d, q): difference d times, then fit ARMA(p, q) to the resultp = autoregressive lags, d = differencing order, q = moving-average lags. ARIMA(p, 0, q) is just ARMA(p, q); ARIMA(0, 1, 0) is a pure random walk.

So the recipe is mechanical: difference the series d times until it looks stationary, fit an ARMA(p, q) to that stationary remainder, then integrate back up (cumulatively sum the forecasts) to express predictions in the original units. The word "integrated" is just the inverse of "differenced": summing undoes differencing the way integration undoes a derivative.

AnalogyThink of a price as the position of a car and the return as its velocity. Position rambles all over the map (non-stationary), but velocity fluctuates around a steady cruising speed (stationary). Differencing position once gives you velocity — and velocity is the quantity worth modeling. ARIMA simply automates "work in changes, then add them back up."

7.2Choosing d: Just Enough Differencing

The integration order d is almost never larger than 1 for financial prices, and 0 for returns. The decision rule is: difference until the series is stationary, and not one step more.

How to tell you have differenced enough

Recall the tools from Week 4. After differencing, run the ADF test (Augmented Dickey–Fuller) — a small p-value rejects the "unit root" and declares stationarity. The autocorrelation function (ACF) of a stationary series decays quickly to zero; the ACF of a non-stationary one stays stubbornly high across many lags.

SeriesTypical dWhy
Stock / index price1One difference gives returns, which are stationary
Log price1One difference gives log-returns (the cleanest case)
Returns directly0Already stationary — no differencing needed
Some yield / spread levels0 or 1Mean-reverting series may already be stationary
Common mistakeOver-differencing. If you difference an already-stationary series, you inject a spurious negative autocorrelation at lag 1 and inflate the variance — the model gets worse, not better. A tell-tale sign is a sharp ACF spike near −0.5 at lag 1 after differencing. Rule of thumb: stop at the smallest d that passes the stationarity test. For most equity work, that is d = 1 on prices, d = 0 on returns.

7.3Producing Forecasts & Their Confidence Bands

Once an ARIMA model is fitted, forecasting is iterative. To predict one step ahead, plug the most recent observations and residuals into the fitted equation. To go further, feed each forecast back in as if it were observed, and keep stepping. This is the recursive forecast.

A crucial property emerges: as the horizon h grows, an AR forecast decays toward the unconditional mean. Intuition: an AR(1) with coefficient φ predicts ŷt+h = μ + φh(yt − μ), and since |φ| < 1 for a stationary series, φh → 0. The further out you look, the more the model shrugs and says "probably the long-run average."

AR(1) forecast:  ŷt+h = μ + φh(yt − μ)For a differenced price (d = 1), this is a forecast of the return; you then cumulatively sum to get the price forecast.

Confidence intervals that widen with horizon

A point forecast alone is a half-truth. The honest output is an interval. Because each future step compounds fresh shocks, forecast variance accumulates and the band fans out. For a random walk (the d = 1, no-ARMA case), the h-step forecast standard error grows like σ√h — the same square-root-of-time scaling from Course 1.

95% band ≈ ŷt+h ± 1.96 · σ · √h  (random-walk case)The interval widens with √h: doubling the horizon widens the band by √2, not 2× — but it never stops growing.
Common mistakeReading only the central forecast line and ignoring the band. A flat point forecast at the last price is not a confident prediction — for a random walk it simply means "your best guess is no change," surrounded by an ever-widening cone of uncertainty. The band, not the line, is the risk-relevant output.

7.4In-Sample vs Out-of-Sample, and the Hard Truth

It is trivially easy to fit a model that hugs the data you trained it on. Adding lags always improves the in-sample fit — the model is just memorizing. The only test that matters for trading is out-of-sample: fit on the past, predict the future you withheld, and measure the error there.

The right way to evaluate

Split the series chronologically (never shuffle time series!): a training window and a later test window. Better still, use a walk-forward scheme — refit as time advances, always predicting the next unseen point. Score with out-of-sample RMSE or MAE, and compare against the dumbest possible benchmark.

RMSE = √( (1/m) Σ (yt − ŷt)² )  over the m test pointsThe benchmark to beat for a price is the naive forecast: ŷt+1 = yt (tomorrow ≈ today). Beating it on returns is the real bar.

The hard truth about returns

Here is the result that humbles every newcomer: for liquid assets, returns are very close to unforecastable. A well-tuned ARIMA on daily returns rarely beats the naive "no change" forecast out-of-sample — which is exactly what efficient-markets reasoning predicts. If reliable return autocorrelation existed, it would be arbitraged away.

But "returns" is not the whole story. Other series derived from the same data are persistent and forecastable:

SeriesForecastable?Why
Daily returnsBarelyNear-zero autocorrelation; efficient markets erase signals
Squared returns / volatilityYesStrong, slow-decaying autocorrelation — volatility clusters (Week 8)
Trading volumeYesHighly persistent, with intraday and day-of-week seasonality
Bid–ask spread / liquidityOftenMean-reverting and regime-driven; useful for execution

So the lesson is not "forecasting is useless" — it is "forecast the right thing." The level of returns resists you; their magnitude (risk) does not. That single insight is the doorway to next week's volatility models.

AnalogyPredicting tomorrow's return is like predicting the next coin flip — the direction is essentially fair. But predicting tomorrow's volatility is like predicting the weather: a stormy day is usually followed by another stormy day. You cannot say which way the market moves, but you can often say how violently it will move.
✦ Worked Example — ARIMA(0,1,0) forecast & band on a price

A stock closes today at $100. Its daily log-returns have mean ≈ 0 and daily standard deviation σ = 1.5% (0.015). You model the log price as ARIMA(0,1,0) — a pure random walk with no drift. Forecast the price 10 trading days out with a 95% band.

  1. Identify the model: ARIMA(0,1,0) on log price means the differenced series (log-returns) is pure white noise. No AR or MA terms, so the best forecast of every future return is 0.
  2. Point forecast: with zero expected return, the 10-day-ahead point forecast of the log price is unchanged. In price terms, ŷt+10 ≈ $100. (The honest central guess for a random walk is "no change.")
  3. Forecast standard error: variance accumulates linearly, so the 10-day log-return SE is σ·√h = 0.015·√10 = 0.015·3.162 ≈ 0.0474 (about 4.74%).
  4. 95% band (log scale): ±1.96 · 0.0474 ≈ ±0.0930, i.e. roughly ±9.3% in log terms.
  5. Translate to price: upper ≈ 100·e0.0930 ≈ $109.7; lower ≈ 100·e−0.0930 ≈ $91.1. So your honest 10-day call is "about $100, but plausibly anywhere from ~$91 to ~$110." The band, widening as √h, is the entire value of the forecast.

🛠️ Hands-On: fit ARIMA & back-test out-of-sample

Reusing the Week-1/2 stack (pandas + yfinance + statsmodels + NumPy + Matplotlib — the install block lives in Lecture 2). We pull a price series, fit an ARIMA, forecast with a confidence band, and — most importantly — check whether it beats the naive forecast out-of-sample.

# arima_forecast.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, yfinance as yf
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

# 1) pull a daily price series
px = yf.download("SPY", start="2018-01-01", end="2023-01-01")["Close"].dropna()
logpx = np.log(px)

# 2) chronological train / test split (NEVER shuffle time)
split = int(len(logpx) * 0.8)
train, test = logpx.iloc[:split], logpx.iloc[split:]

# 3) fit ARIMA(p=1, d=1, q=1) on the LOG PRICE (d=1 -> models log-returns)
model = ARIMA(train, order=(1, 1, 1)).fit()
print(model.summary().tables[1])           # AR/MA coefficients

# 4) forecast with a 95% confidence band
fc = model.get_forecast(steps=len(test))
mean_fc = fc.predicted_mean
ci = fc.conf_int(alpha=0.05)               # lower / upper columns

# 5) the honest test: beat the NAIVE forecast (tomorrow = today)?
naive = train.iloc[-1]                      # flat at last train value
rmse_arima = np.sqrt(np.mean((test.values - mean_fc.values) ** 2))
rmse_naive = np.sqrt(np.mean((test.values - naive) ** 2))
print(f"out-of-sample RMSE  ARIMA: {rmse_arima:.4f}")
print(f"out-of-sample RMSE  naive: {rmse_naive:.4f}")
print("ARIMA beats naive?  ", rmse_arima < rmse_naive)

Run it and notice: on raw price the long-horizon forecast flattens out and the band fans wide, while ARIMA only barely edges (or loses to) the naive benchmark — the empirical face of "returns are nearly unforecastable." Now swap the target for logpx.diff().pow(2) (squared returns, a volatility proxy) and refit: the autocorrelation is real, the model has something to grip, and out-of-sample skill appears. That contrast is the lecture.

❓ Lecture 7 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In ARIMA(p, d, q), what does the parameter d represent?
A. The number of autoregressive lag terms
B. The number of times the series is differenced to reach stationarity
C. The number of moving-average error terms
D. The forecast horizon in days
Show answer

B. d is the integration/differencing order. The AR lags are p (A) and the MA error terms are q (C); the horizon is a forecasting choice, not a model parameter (D).

Q2. You take a series that is already stationary and difference it one extra time. The most likely consequence is…
A. The model fits better because more differencing is always safer
B. Nothing changes; differencing a stationary series is harmless
C. The series becomes a perfect random walk
D. Over-differencing: a spurious negative lag-1 autocorrelation and inflated variance
Show answer

D. Over-differencing injects an artificial negative autocorrelation (near −0.5 at lag 1) and raises the variance, making the model worse. So more is not safer (A) and it is not harmless (B); differencing does not create a random walk — that is the thing you difference away (C).

Q3. For a stationary AR(1) with |φ| < 1, what happens to the point forecast as the horizon h grows large?
A. It diverges to infinity
B. It oscillates with growing amplitude
C. It decays toward the unconditional mean
D. It stays pinned exactly at the last observed value forever
Show answer

C. Since ŷt+h = μ + φh(yt − μ) and |φ| < 1, the term φh → 0, so the forecast converges to the mean μ. It cannot diverge (A) for a stationary process; it decays rather than oscillating wildly (B); only a random walk (φ = 1) stays at the last value (D).

Q4. Why does the forecast confidence band widen as the horizon increases?
A. Because forecast variance accumulates with each future shock — for a random walk it grows like σ√h
B. Because the model's coefficients change every day
C. Because the point forecast always trends upward
D. Because RMSE is computed in-sample
Show answer

A. Each step into the future adds an unobserved shock, so the cumulative forecast variance grows (σ√h for a random walk), fanning the band out. Coefficients are fixed once fitted (B); the point forecast need not trend (C); RMSE evaluation is unrelated to why the band widens (D).

Q5. An ARIMA model on daily returns barely matches the naive "tomorrow = today" forecast out-of-sample, yet the same toolkit forecasts squared returns well. The best interpretation is…
A. The ARIMA code has a bug, since it should always beat naive
B. Return levels are nearly unforecastable, but volatility (magnitude) is persistent and forecastable
C. Squared returns are random and the result is luck
D. Out-of-sample testing is the wrong way to evaluate forecasts
Show answer

B. Efficient markets erase autocorrelation in return levels, so beating naive is hard — but volatility clusters, giving squared returns strong, exploitable persistence. There is no bug (A); the volatility signal is real, not luck (C); out-of-sample testing is precisely the correct, honest method (D).

✓ What you learned

  • ARIMA(p, d, q) = difference d times, fit ARMA(p, q), then integrate back; d = 1 for prices, d = 0 for returns.
  • Choose the smallest d that passes a stationarity test (ADF / fast-decaying ACF) — and beware over-differencing.
  • Forecasts are recursive and AR forecasts decay to the mean; the honest output is a cone whose band widens like σ√h.
  • Judge models by out-of-sample RMSE in a chronological / walk-forward split, against the naive benchmark — never by in-sample fit.
  • The hard truth: return levels are nearly unforecastable, while volatility and volume are persistent and worth modeling.
