Week 6 · Lecture 6

AR, MA & ARMA Models

Two opposite instincts — "today rhymes with yesterday" and "today still feels last week's shock" — combine into the workhorse model of every trading desk's forecast.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Build the autoregressive model AR(p) from first principles as "regress the series on its own recent past."
  • Build the moving-average model MA(q) as a weighted memory of recent random shocks, and contrast it with AR.
  • Combine the two into ARMA(p, q) and state the stationarity and invertibility conditions each part must satisfy.
  • Fit an ARMA model in statsmodels and read off coefficients, standard errors, and information criteria.
  • Use the ACF and PACF "fingerprints" to choose the orders p and q before you ever fit a model.

6.1The Autoregressive Model: Today Echoes Yesterday

Week 5 smoothed a series with moving averages — a purely descriptive tool. This week we build generative models: small equations that could plausibly have produced the data, and that we can therefore turn around to forecast it. The first instinct is the most natural one in markets: what happened recently tends to persist. A volatility spike doesn't vanish overnight; an order-flow imbalance lingers. The autoregressive model encodes exactly that — it regresses the series on its own lagged values.

An AR(1) — order one, one lag — says today's value is a fraction φ of yesterday's, plus a fresh random shock:

AR(1):  Xt = c + φ·Xt−1 + εtεt is white noise (mean 0, variance σ², uncorrelated). φ is the persistence; c sets the long-run level.

The coefficient φ is the entire story. If φ is close to 1, shocks decay slowly and the series wanders far and persistently — strong momentum. If φ is near 0, the series snaps back to its mean almost immediately — strong mean reversion, the foundation of pairs trading and statistical-arbitrage spreads. A negative φ makes the series zig-zag, over-correcting each period. Generalizing to p lags gives AR(p):

AR(p):  Xt = c + φ1Xt−1 + φ2Xt−2 + … + φpXt−p + εt

For an AR(1) the unconditional mean is c/(1−φ) and the autocorrelation at lag k is simply φk — a clean geometric decay. That decaying tail is the AR's signature, and we will exploit it in §6.4.

AnalogyAn AR process is a swinging pendulum with a memory of its own position. Push it once (a shock) and it doesn't stop where you left it — it keeps drifting back toward rest at a rate set by φ. A high-friction pendulum (φ near 0) settles instantly; a near-frictionless one (φ near 1) coasts for ages. The pendulum never remembers the push itself — only where it currently sits.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the AR model with the moving-average smoother of Week 5. Despite the shared word, a "moving average" filter and the "moving-average (MA) model" of §6.2 are unrelated objects — one averages past data to denoise, the other weights past shocks to generate. Quants keep them straight by always saying "MA model" vs. "rolling mean."

6.2The Moving-Average Model: Today Carries Last Week's Surprise

The second instinct is different. Instead of remembering past values, what if the series remembers past shocks? An earnings surprise or a macro headline is a one-off jolt, but its effect bleeds into the next few observations before fully fading. The moving-average model, MA(q), is a weighted sum of the current and last q random innovations:

MA(1):  Xt = μ + εt + θ·εt−1μ is the mean; θ controls how much of yesterday's shock (not value) carries into today.
MA(q):  Xt = μ + εt + θ1εt−1 + θ2εt−2 + … + θqεt−q

The crucial difference from AR is finite memory. In an MA(q), today is correlated with the past only out to lag q — beyond that, the shocks share nothing and the correlation is exactly zero. An AR process, by contrast, has correlation that decays forever without ever hitting zero. This is the single most useful contrast in the whole lecture, because it lets the data tell us which family it came from:

AnalogyAn MA process is an echo in a small room. A single clap (the shock εt) reverberates for a moment — you hear it again, fainter, weighted by θ — and then it is gone completely. The room has no memory of claps from minutes ago. The AR pendulum's memory never fully ends; the MA echo always does, after exactly q bounces.

6.3ARMA, Stationarity & Invertibility

Real return and spread series rarely look purely AR or purely MA. Combining both gives the ARMA(p, q) model — p autoregressive terms capturing persistence, q moving-average terms capturing the lingering imprint of shocks:

ARMA(p, q):  Xt = c + Σi=1..p φiXt−i + εt + Σj=1..q θjεt−j

Two conditions keep an ARMA model sane.

Stationarity (the AR side)

A model is stationary if its mean, variance, and autocovariances do not drift over time — without this, "the past predicts the future" is meaningless because the rules keep changing. Stationarity is governed entirely by the AR coefficients. For an AR(1) the rule is intuitive:

Stationary AR(1) ⇔ |φ| < 1If |φ| ≥ 1 shocks never die — the series explodes or random-walks away, like an un-differenced price.

For higher orders the general rule is that all roots of the AR characteristic polynomial 1 − φ1z − … − φpzp = 0 must lie outside the unit circle. A price level fails this (φ ≈ 1, a random walk) — which is why next week we difference it into returns before modeling.

Invertibility (the MA side)

An MA model is invertible if it can be rewritten as an infinite AR — equivalently, the roots of the MA polynomial 1 + θ1z + … + θqzq = 0 lie outside the unit circle (for MA(1): |θ| < 1). Invertibility matters because two different θ values can produce the identical autocorrelation pattern; requiring |θ| < 1 picks the unique, well-behaved one so the fit is identifiable and forecasts make sense.

Common mistakeFitting ARMA to a raw price series. Prices are non-stationary (they trend and random-walk), so the AR side hits |φ| ≈ 1 and every diagnostic misbehaves. Always model a stationary transform — log-returns or a mean-reverting spread — not the level itself. That "make it stationary first" step is precisely the I (integrated) in next week's ARIMA.

6.4Choosing p and q: The ACF / PACF Fingerprints

You do not guess p and q — the data leaves fingerprints. Two diagnostic functions, computed before any fitting, reveal the order:

The autocorrelation function (ACF) measures the correlation between Xt and Xt−k for each lag k. The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) measures that same correlation but after removing the influence of all the shorter lags in between — it isolates the direct link from lag k. The classic reading rule:

MA(q): ACF cuts off after lag q  ·  PACF tails off
AR(p): PACF cuts off after lag p  ·  ACF tails off"Cuts off" = drops abruptly into the noise band; "tails off" = decays gradually. ARMA: both tail off — then lean on information criteria.

The mnemonic: the PACF counts your AR terms, the ACF counts your MA terms. A spike in the PACF at lags 1 and 2 then nothing means AR(2); a spike in the ACF at lag 1 then nothing means MA(1). When both tail off, you likely have a genuine ARMA, and you select orders by minimizing an information criterion — the AIC or BIC — which rewards fit but penalizes extra parameters to prevent overfitting.

AIC = 2k − 2·ln(L)    BIC = k·ln(n) − 2·ln(L)k = number of parameters, L = maximized likelihood, n = sample size. Lower is better; BIC penalizes complexity harder.
✦ Worked Example — forecasting a mean-reverting spread with AR(1)

A pairs-trading spread is modeled as AR(1): Xt = 0.4 + 0.6·Xt−1 + εt, with shock standard deviation σ = 2. The most recent observed spread is Xt = 5. Forecast one and two steps ahead and find the long-run mean.

  1. Confirm stationarity: |φ| = 0.6 < 1, so the model is stationary and mean-reverting — forecasts will pull back toward the mean.
  2. Long-run mean: μ = c/(1−φ) = 0.4 / (1 − 0.6) = 0.4 / 0.4 = 1.0. The spread is currently 5, far above its equilibrium of 1 — a short-the-spread setup.
  3. One-step forecast: the expected next shock is 0, so X̂t+1 = 0.4 + 0.6·(5) = 0.4 + 3.0 = 3.4.
  4. Two-step forecast: iterate using the forecast as the input: X̂t+2 = 0.4 + 0.6·(3.4) = 0.4 + 2.04 = 2.44.
  5. Sanity check: the path 5 → 3.4 → 2.44 → … is decaying geometrically toward the long-run mean of 1.0 — each step closes 60% of the remaining gap. That convergence to equilibrium is exactly the statistical-arbitrage edge an AR(1) spread model is built to capture.

🛠️ Hands-On: fit an ARMA model and read the diagnostics

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + NumPy + Matplotlib + Jupyter — the full install block is in C1 Lecture 1). This lecture adds statsmodels and yfinance — install them with the note below. We download daily prices, convert to stationary log-returns, eyeball the ACF/PACF, then fit an ARMA and read the summary.

⬇ New tools this week: statsmodels & yfinance

Activate your quant-env from Week 1, then add the two libraries this lecture introduces:

source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install statsmodels yfinance
# arma_demo.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, yfinance as yf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf
from statsmodels.tsa.arima.model import ARIMA

# 1. download prices and make a STATIONARY series: daily log-returns
px  = yf.download("SPY", start="2018-01-01", end="2023-01-01")["Close"]
ret = np.log(px).diff().dropna()           # prices are non-stationary; returns are not

# 2. read the fingerprints BEFORE fitting
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3))
plot_acf(ret,  lags=20, ax=ax[0])          # MA order q?  (where ACF cuts off)
plot_pacf(ret, lags=20, ax=ax[1])          # AR order p?  (where PACF cuts off)
plt.tight_layout(); plt.show()

# 3. fit ARMA(1,1)  (= ARIMA with d=0) and inspect coefficients
model = ARIMA(ret, order=(1, 0, 1))        # order = (p, d, q); d=0 -> pure ARMA
res   = model.fit()
print(res.summary())                       # coefs, std errors, AIC, BIC

# 4. compare candidate orders by AIC — lower is better
for p, q in [(1,0), (0,1), (1,1), (2,2)]:
    aic = ARIMA(ret, order=(p,0,q)).fit().aic
    print(f"ARMA({p},{q})  AIC = {aic:.1f}")

Daily equity returns are famously close to white noise, so you will typically see ACF/PACF bars mostly inside the band and small φ, θ estimates — a healthy reminder that returns are hard to predict from their own past. The summary() hands you each coefficient with a standard error and p-value; the AIC loop is your objective tie-breaker when the fingerprints are ambiguous.

💬 Discussion

The efficient-market hypothesis says past prices should not predict future prices — yet ARMA models are everywhere on trading desks. If daily equity returns really are close to white noise (no usable ACF/PACF structure), where and why do AR and MA models still earn their keep in quantitative finance? Be specific about the kind of series or horizon where you'd expect real predictable structure to survive.

Show a model response

Weak-form efficiency is about price levels / directional returns of liquid assets, and ARMA mostly confirms it there — the autocorrelations of daily large-cap returns are tiny and unstable, so an ARMA on raw returns rarely beats a no-change forecast after costs. But the same machinery pays off on series that are not simple returns:

  • Mean-reverting spreads. A market-neutral spread (e.g., a cointegrated pair) is engineered to be stationary and AR(1)-like; |φ|<1 gives a tradeable pull toward equilibrium — the statistical-arbitrage edge.
  • High-frequency / microstructure. At the tick scale, bid–ask bounce induces strong negative lag-1 autocorrelation — a textbook MA(1) signature exploited for execution and short-horizon prediction.
  • Transformed series. Returns are near-white, but their magnitudes (volatility) and order flow are strongly autocorrelated — ARMA on those drives risk and execution models (and motivates GARCH next).

So ARMA earns its keep not by beating efficiency on liquid directional returns, but by modeling the stationary, structured series that markets do leave behind — spreads, volatility, microstructure, and macro/fundamental data.

❓ Lecture 6 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. An AR(1) model is written Xt = c + φ·Xt−1 + εt. For the process to be stationary, φ must satisfy…
A. |φ| < 1
B. φ > 1
C. φ = 1 exactly
D. φ = 0
Show answer

A. Stationarity of an AR(1) requires |φ| < 1 so shocks decay over time. φ > 1 (B) explodes; φ = 1 (C) is a random walk — non-stationary, the very thing we difference away; φ = 0 (D) is stationary but is just white noise, an unnecessary special case, not the general condition.

Q2. A series' sample ACF shows one large spike at lag 1 and then drops inside the noise band for all later lags, while its PACF decays gradually. This is the fingerprint of…
A. an AR(1) process
B. an MA(1) process
C. a random walk
D. pure white noise
Show answer

B. ACF cutting off after lag q (here q = 1) with a tailing PACF is the MA(q) signature. An AR(1) (A) is the reverse — PACF cuts off, ACF tails off. A random walk (C) has an ACF that stays near 1 for many lags. White noise (D) shows no significant spikes at all.

Q3. Why must you convert a price series to log-returns (or another stationary transform) before fitting an ARMA model?
A. Returns are always normally distributed
B. ARMA cannot handle negative numbers
C. Prices are non-stationary, so the AR side hits |φ| ≈ 1 and the model misbehaves
D. It makes the dataset smaller and faster to fit
Show answer

C. Prices trend and random-walk (non-stationary), violating the AR stationarity condition. Returns are approximately stationary, so the model is well-posed. Returns are not exactly normal (A — they have fat tails); ARMA handles negative values fine (B); speed (D) is irrelevant to correctness.

Q4. When you have several candidate ARMA orders that all fit reasonably, what does the AIC (or BIC) help you do?
A. Test whether the residuals are normal
B. Guarantee the model will forecast perfectly out of sample
C. Force the series to become stationary
D. Trade off goodness-of-fit against the number of parameters, penalizing overfitting
Show answer

D. Information criteria reward higher likelihood but add a penalty per parameter, so they pick the model that fits well without overfitting; lower is better. They are not a normality test (A), they make no out-of-sample guarantee (B), and they do nothing to the stationarity of the data (C — that is differencing's job).

Q5. What is the key structural difference between an AR(p) and an MA(q) process?
A. AR is built from past values of the series (infinite memory); MA is built from past shocks (finite memory of exactly q lags)
B. AR can only model upward trends; MA only models downward trends
C. MA processes are never stationary; AR processes always are
D. They are mathematically identical and the names are interchangeable
Show answer

A. AR regresses on the series' own lagged values and has an infinitely-decaying memory; MA is a weighted sum of recent white-noise shocks and has memory of exactly q lags. Neither is tied to a trend direction (B). MA processes are stationary by construction, while AR needs |φ|<1 — so C is backwards. They are distinct families (D is false).
