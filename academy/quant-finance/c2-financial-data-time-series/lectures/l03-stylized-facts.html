Stylized Facts of Returns

Asset returns aren't a tidy bell curve sprayed by a coin-flipping machine — they cluster, they ambush, and they remember. Here are the fingerprints every market leaves behind.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the stylized facts of returns and explain why they break the simple normal-iid model from Course 1.
  • Diagnose heavy tails using excess kurtosis and connect them to crash risk.
  • Reconcile the near-zero autocorrelation of returns with the strong autocorrelation of absolute / squared returns — the signature of volatility clustering.
  • Explain volatility persistence, the leverage effect, and aggregational Gaussianity in plain terms.
  • Measure all of these on real price data with pandas, statsmodels, and yfinance.

3.1What Is a "Stylized Fact"?

In Course 1 we modeled returns as iid normal — independent, identically distributed draws from a single bell curve. It is a beautiful starting point and it is wrong in specific, repeatable ways. A stylized fact is an empirical regularity that shows up across almost every liquid market, every era, and every asset class — stocks, currencies, commodities, crypto — robustly enough that any honest model must reproduce it.

These facts are not noise to be smoothed away; they are the structure. The whole edifice of modern risk management, option pricing corrections, and volatility trading exists because returns deviate from the normal-iid fairy tale in the same handful of ways every time. We'll meet five: heavy tails, the autocorrelation puzzle, volatility clustering and persistence, the leverage effect, and aggregational Gaussianity.

AnalogyThe iid-normal model is a weather forecast that says "every day, independently, draw the temperature from the same bell curve." Reality is more like real weather: hot spells cluster into heat waves (volatility clustering), record extremes are far more common than a bell curve predicts (heavy tails), and a cold snap tends to follow a storm, not a sunny day (the leverage effect). The average may match — but the texture is completely different.

3.2Heavy Tails & Excess Kurtosis

The first and most consequential fact: daily returns have fatter tails than a normal distribution. Moves of 5, 8, even 20 standard deviations happen far more often than the 68–95–99.7 rule allows. Under a true Gaussian, a 5σ daily move should occur roughly once every 14,000 years; in real equity indices something that large turns up every few years.

We quantify tail-heaviness with the fourth standardized moment, kurtosis. A normal distribution has kurtosis exactly 3, so we subtract that off and report excess kurtosis:

Kurt(R) = E[(R − μ)4] / σ4     ExcessKurt = Kurt(R) − 3Normal ⇒ excess = 0. Daily equity returns typically show excess kurtosis of 3 to 10 (or far higher) — "leptokurtic," meaning a sharper peak and fatter tails than the bell.

Why a sharper peak and fatter tails? On calm days returns hug zero more tightly than a Gaussian (the peak), while the rare violent days live way out in the tails. The mass has to come from somewhere — it is pulled out of the "shoulders" the normal would occupy. This is exactly why a value-at-risk (VaR) estimate built on a normal assumption systematically understates how bad a bad day can be: the model has no language for the tail.

Common mistakeHeavy tails are not the same as high variance. You can scale a normal up to any variance you like and its excess kurtosis is still exactly 0. Tail risk is about the shape of the distribution, not its width — which is why "we used a bigger σ" never fixes a model that ignores kurtosis.

3.3The Autocorrelation Puzzle & Volatility Clustering

Now a fact that looks like a contradiction. Compute the autocorrelation of returns — how today's return correlates with yesterday's, the day before, and so on. For liquid assets it is essentially zero at all lags. You cannot predict the sign or size of tomorrow's return from past returns; if you could, the trade would be arbitraged away. This is the weak-form efficiency that makes returns look "random."

ρR(k) = Corr(Rt, Rt−k) ≈ 0  for all k ≥ 1The autocorrelation function (ACF) of raw returns dies out almost immediately — returns are close to serially uncorrelated.

But now apply the same ACF to the absolute returns |Rt| or the squared returns Rt2 — both proxies for the size of the move, ignoring its direction. Suddenly the autocorrelation is strongly positive and decays only slowly, persisting for weeks or months:

ρ|R|(k) = Corr(|Rt|, |Rt−k|) > 0   and decays slowly in kThe magnitude of returns is highly predictable even though the returns themselves are not.

The reconciliation is volatility clustering: large moves tend to be followed by large moves (of either sign), and quiet periods by quiet periods. The market alternates between calm regimes and turbulent regimes. Direction is unpredictable; energy is highly predictable. This is the single most exploited fact in all of quant finance — it is why "predict tomorrow's volatility" is a tractable, profitable problem while "predict tomorrow's return" is nearly hopeless.

3.4Persistence, the Leverage Effect & Aggregational Gaussianity

Three more facts complete the portrait.

Volatility persistence (long memory)

The slow decay of the absolute-return ACF is so gradual that volatility is said to have long memory: a shock to volatility today still nudges volatility months later. Practically, this means a spike in turbulence does not evaporate overnight — risk stays elevated. Models from this fact (GARCH and its cousins, Week 12) treat tomorrow's variance as a persistent, mean-reverting function of recent shocks.

σ2t = ω + α R2t−1 + β σ2t−1,   α + β close to 1The GARCH(1,1) recipe (preview): a high α + β means a volatility shock decays slowly — that's persistence written as an equation.

The leverage effect (asymmetry)

Volatility responds asymmetrically to the sign of returns: a large negative return tends to raise future volatility more than an equally large positive return. Bad news scares the market more than good news soothes it. The classical story is that a price drop raises a firm's debt-to-equity ratio (its "leverage"), making the equity riskier — hence the name — but the effect is observed even where that mechanism is weak, so fear-and-panic dynamics matter too. The trader's shorthand: markets take the stairs up and the elevator down.

Aggregational Gaussianity

Here the normal sneaks back in. If you aggregate returns over longer horizons — daily → weekly → monthly → quarterly — the distribution looks progressively more Gaussian: excess kurtosis shrinks toward zero. Summing many daily returns is exactly the CLT setup from Course 1, and although daily returns are not independent, the dependence is weak enough that long-horizon sums still tame the tails.

Common mistakeAggregational Gaussianity does not license you to "just use the normal." It only holds at long horizons, and risk lives at the short ones — a daily VaR, a margin call, a stop-out all trigger on a single day. Worse, the very tail events you care about are when independence breaks and the CLT logic fails. Use the normal for monthly strategic planning, never for one-day tail risk.
AnalogyAggregational Gaussianity is like photographing a jagged coastline from higher and higher altitude. Up close the daily coastline is all sharp rocks and sudden cliffs (fat tails). Zoom out to monthly or yearly and the jaggedness averages into a smooth, gentle curve (a bell). The roughness never left — your resolution did.
✦ Worked Example — how rare is a normal "8σ" day, really?

An index has daily return std σ = 1.0%. One day it falls 8.0% — an 8σ move. We'll see how the normal model and a heavy-tailed view disagree, and read the kurtosis off a small sample.

  1. Normal verdict: P(Z < −8) ≈ 6.2 × 10−16. With ~252 trading days per year, the expected waiting time is 1 / (252 · 6.2e-16) ≈ 6.4 × 1012 years — about 460 times the age of the universe. The normal calls this impossible.
  2. Reality check: single-day drops of 8% or more have occurred multiple times in the last few decades across major indices. So the true frequency is "once in a couple of decades," not "once per universe." The normal is off by twelve orders of magnitude.
  3. Why: the culprit is excess kurtosis. Fat tails put orders of magnitude more probability into the −8σ region than a Gaussian does.
  4. Read kurtosis from data: take 5 standardized daily returns z = (−0.3, 0.2, 0.1, −0.2, 4.0) — four calm days and one shock. Mean ≈ 0.76; deviations4 are dominated by the 4.0 outlier. The fourth moment ≈ (Σ(z−z̄)4)/5 ≈ 27.0 and variance ≈ 2.78, so Kurt ≈ 27.0 / 2.782 ≈ 3.5, i.e. excess ≈ 0.5 from a single outlier in just five points.
  5. Takeaway: kurtosis is dominated by the largest few observations. One genuine outlier swings it hard — which is precisely why fat tails matter and why you never size risk off a Gaussian for short horizons.

🛠️ Hands-On: measure the stylized facts on real data

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + yfinance + Matplotlib — install block lives in Week 2). New this week: statsmodels for the autocorrelation function. We'll pull an index, compute log-returns, and verify heavy tails, the ACF puzzle, and aggregational Gaussianity in a few lines.

⬇ New tool this week: statsmodels

Activate your quant-env from Week 1, then add the one new package:

source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install statsmodels            # pandas, numpy, yfinance, matplotlib already installed
# stylized_facts.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, yfinance as yf
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import acf

# 1) Daily prices -> log returns
px  = yf.download("^GSPC", start="2005-01-01", end="2024-01-01")["Close"]
r   = np.log(px).diff().dropna()                 # daily log-returns

# 2) Heavy tails: excess kurtosis (0 for a normal)
print("excess kurtosis :", r.kurt())             # typically ~10-15 for daily equities

# 3) The ACF puzzle: returns vs. |returns|
acf_r   = acf(r,      nlags=20, fft=True)
acf_abs = acf(r.abs(), nlags=20, fft=True)
print("ACF of r    at lags 1-5 :", np.round(acf_r[1:6],   3))   # ~0  (no memory)
print("ACF of |r|  at lags 1-5 :", np.round(acf_abs[1:6], 3))   # big & positive

# 4) Aggregational Gaussianity: kurtosis falls as we aggregate
for rule in ["1D", "1W", "1ME"]:
    agg = r.resample(rule).sum()
    print(f"{rule:>3} excess kurtosis :", round(float(agg.kurt()), 2))  # shrinks toward 0

You'll see daily excess kurtosis in the double digits, an ACF of raw returns hovering near zero while the ACF of |r| stays clearly positive for many lags (volatility clustering), and the kurtosis dropping sharply from daily to weekly to monthly (aggregational Gaussianity). Three stylized facts confirmed in one screen.

💬 Discussion

Returns are essentially unpredictable in direction (zero autocorrelation) yet highly predictable in magnitude (volatility clustering). Pick a concrete trading or risk-management decision and argue: which of these two facts matters more for it, and how would a strategy that respects both differ from one built on the naive iid-normal model? Where might relying on volatility predictability quietly fail you?

Show a model response

Consider position sizing for a risk-controlled strategy (e.g. targeting constant volatility). Here the magnitude fact dominates. Because direction is unpredictable, I make no claim about whether tomorrow goes up or down — chasing that is the hopeless problem. But because volatility clusters and persists, I can forecast that tomorrow's risk resembles this week's. So I scale exposure inversely to a volatility estimate (e.g. an EWMA or GARCH forecast): cut size when turbulence rises, add it back as markets calm. Versus the naive iid-normal book — which uses one fixed σ forever — this avoids being maximally leveraged into the start of a turbulent cluster and de-risks before the worst of a drawdown compounds.

Where it quietly fails: (1) volatility clustering tells you a big move is likely, not its direction or size — and the leverage effect means down-shocks spike vol fastest, so a vol forecast updated on yesterday's close is always a step behind a gap-down open. (2) The forecast is most wrong exactly at regime transitions — the jump from calm to turbulent — which is when you most need it. (3) Heavy tails mean even a correctly-forecast σ leaves a fat tail of 5σ+ surprises that volatility targeting cannot size away. So I'd pair vol targeting with an explicit tail hedge or hard stop, not trust the vol model alone.

❓ Lecture 3 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Daily equity returns are described as "leptokurtic." This means their distribution has…
A. a sharper peak and fatter tails than a normal
B. a flatter peak and thinner tails than a normal
C. exactly the shape of a normal but a larger variance
D. negative variance
Show answer

A. Leptokurtic = excess kurtosis > 0 = a taller, narrower peak and heavier tails. B is platykurtic (the opposite). C is wrong because scaling a normal's variance leaves excess kurtosis at exactly 0 — a wider normal is still a normal. D is impossible; variance is never negative.

Q2. For real daily returns, which pattern of autocorrelation is observed?
A. Strong positive autocorrelation in the returns themselves
B. Strong autocorrelation in returns but none in absolute returns
C. Near-zero autocorrelation in returns, but strong slowly-decaying autocorrelation in absolute/squared returns
D. Zero autocorrelation in everything — returns, absolute returns, and squared returns alike
Show answer

C. This is the central puzzle: returns are nearly serially uncorrelated (you can't predict direction), yet |R| and R² are strongly autocorrelated (you can predict magnitude) — volatility clustering. A would imply trivially predictable prices (arbitraged away). B reverses the truth. D ignores clustering entirely.

Q3. The "leverage effect" in returns refers to the observation that…
A. leveraged ETFs always outperform in the long run
B. a large negative return tends to raise future volatility more than an equally large positive return
C. borrowing money increases your expected return
D. volatility is identical after up-moves and down-moves
Show answer

B. The leverage effect is the asymmetry: downside shocks spike future volatility more than upside ones ("stairs up, elevator down"). A and C are unrelated claims about leverage as borrowing/products. D describes symmetry — the exact opposite of the leverage effect.

Q4. "Aggregational Gaussianity" means that as you aggregate returns over longer horizons (daily → weekly → monthly), the return distribution…
A. becomes more heavy-tailed and less normal
B. stays exactly as fat-tailed as the daily distribution
C. becomes progressively more normal (excess kurtosis shrinks toward 0)
D. becomes perfectly uniform
Show answer

C. Summing many daily returns is a CLT-like operation, so long-horizon returns look progressively more Gaussian and their excess kurtosis falls. A is backwards. B ignores the aggregation effect. D is not what the CLT produces — the limit is normal, not uniform.

Q5. A risk model assumes daily returns are iid normal with σ = 1%. Why is this dangerous for estimating one-day tail risk?
A. Because the normal has fatter tails than real returns, so it overstates crash probability
B. Because returns are perfectly predictable, making any distribution irrelevant
C. Because aggregational Gaussianity guarantees the daily distribution is already normal
D. Because real returns have heavy tails and cluster, so the normal badly understates the probability of large one-day moves
Show answer

D. Real daily returns are leptokurtic and volatility-clustered, so a thin-tailed normal assigns far too little probability to extreme days — it understates tail risk by orders of magnitude (see the worked 8σ example). A reverses the tail comparison. B is false; direction is unpredictable. C misapplies aggregational Gaussianity, which holds at long horizons, not for one-day risk.

✓ What you learned

  • A stylized fact is a robust empirical regularity of returns that the simple iid-normal model fails to reproduce.
  • Heavy tails show up as positive excess kurtosis — a sharper peak and fatter tails — making extreme days vastly more likely than a normal predicts.
  • Returns have near-zero autocorrelation (direction unpredictable) but |R| and R² are strongly autocorrelated — the signature of volatility clustering and persistence.
  • The leverage effect is the asymmetry by which down-moves raise future volatility more than up-moves; aggregational Gaussianity is the return to normality at long horizons.
  • You measured kurtosis, the dual ACF, and aggregational Gaussianity on real index data with pandas + statsmodels.
