Market Microstructure & Statistical Signals

Zoom all the way in to where prices are actually born — the order book — then learn to mine that raw noise for a faint statistical edge without fooling yourself.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Describe the limit order book, the bid–ask spread, and how a market price is really two prices.
  • Explain bid–ask bounce and why it injects spurious negative autocorrelation into tick-level returns.
  • Turn a raw time series into a candidate trading signal and measure its information coefficient and signal-to-noise.
  • Diagnose the multiple-testing / overfitting trap and apply guardrails (out-of-sample, Bonferroni, deflated metrics).
  • Frame the capstone volatility-and-signal study and connect it forward to the Algorithmic Trading course.

10.1Inside the Price: the Order Book & the Spread

For nine weeks we treated "the price" as a single number arriving once per day. Zoom in to the millisecond scale and that fiction dissolves. At any instant there is no single price — there is a limit order book: a queue of resting buy orders (bids) below a queue of resting sell orders (asks, or offers). The highest bid and the lowest ask are the best bid and best ask; the gap between them is the bid–ask spread.

spread = ask − bid     mid = (ask + bid) / 2The mid-price is the usual "fair" reference; the spread is the round-trip cost of demanding immediacy.

If you want to buy right now you cross the spread and pay the ask; to sell now you hit the bid. You therefore pay the spread on every impatient round trip. The spread is the market-maker's compensation for two risks: holding inventory, and trading against someone who knows more than they do (adverse selection). A single recorded transaction — a tick — carries a price, a size, and a timestamp; a stream of ticks is the rawest data a quant ever sees.

AnalogyThe order book is a staircase of supply and demand. Bids are steps going down on the left, asks are steps going up on the right, and the spread is the empty landing in the middle where no one has yet agreed to trade. A big market order is someone sprinting across the landing and climbing several steps at once — that's why large trades "walk the book" and move the price.

10.2Bid–Ask Bounce: Noise That Looks Like Signal

Microstructure does not just add detail — it actively contaminates the statistics you learned to compute. The cleanest example is bid–ask bounce. Suppose the true (mid) price is dead flat, but trades arrive randomly as buyer-initiated (printing at the ask) or seller-initiated (printing at the bid). The transaction price then ricochets between bid and ask even though nothing fundamental moved.

That bounce stamps a tell-tale pattern onto tick returns: a print at the ask is likely followed by one at the bid, and vice-versa, so successive transaction-price changes are negatively correlated. Recall the autocorrelation tools from Weeks 3–4 — bounce manufactures a spurious negative lag-1 autocorrelation out of thin air.

Cov(Δpt, Δpt−1) = −s²/4  ⟹  Var(Δp) = σ²mid + s²/2Roll's model: with half-spread s/2 and a flat mid, transaction-price changes have lag-1 autocovariance −s²/4. This both biases autocorrelation negative and inflates measured volatility.

Two practical consequences. First, an analyst who measures volatility from raw trade prices will over-state it, because the bounce adds variance that is not real price movement. Second, the negative autocorrelation is not a tradable mean-reversion signal — you cannot harvest it, because capturing it would require paying the very spread that creates it. The classic fix is to work with the mid-price (or quote mid-points) rather than trade prices, which removes most of the bounce.

Common mistakeSeeing strong negative lag-1 autocorrelation in high-frequency trade prices and declaring "the asset mean-reverts — free money!" It is almost always bid–ask bounce, an artifact of which side traded, not of price direction. Re-run the analysis on mid-prices: the phantom signal usually vanishes, and with it the imaginary profit.

10.3From Data to Signal: Information Coefficient & Signal-to-Noise

A signal (or alpha) is any number computed from information available at time t that you hope predicts the future return rt+1. A momentum score, an order-book imbalance, a volatility regime flag — all are signals. The honest question is never "does it correlate?" but "how much, and is that more than luck?"

The standard yardstick is the information coefficient (IC): the correlation between the signal at time t and the realized forward return. The catch is that for any real predictor in liquid markets the IC is tiny — a single name's daily IC of 0.03–0.05 is already good. Predictive power lives in the third decimal place, swamped by noise.

IC = corr(signalt, rt+1)     Sharpe ≈ IC · √(breadth · periods/yr)The "fundamental law": a small edge (IC) compounds into a usable Sharpe ratio only by spreading it across many independent bets (breadth).

This is why signal-to-noise framing matters. A return is (predictable part) + (noise). If the predictable part has standard deviation σs and the noise has σn, the achievable IC is roughly σs/√(σs²+σn²). In equities the noise dominates so heavily that a 4% IC is a triumph — and crucially, an edge that small needs a lot of independent observations before you can even prove it exists. That sample-size requirement is the bridge to the next section.

AnalogyHunting for a signal in returns is like listening for one whispered word in a stadium roar. You cannot hear it in a single clip. But average thousands of synchronized recordings (many days, many names) and the random roar cancels while the whisper reinforces — the √n averaging from Week 2's CLT, now doing the work of separating alpha from noise.

10.4The Overfitting Trap & Framing the Capstone

If the edge is that small, the danger is obvious: test enough strategies and one will look brilliant by chance alone. This is the multiple-testing problem, and it is the single most expensive mistake in quantitative trading. A backtest is a hypothesis test, and running many of them silently inflates your false-discovery rate.

Concretely: at a 5% significance level, one in twenty random useless strategies will pass. Try 100 random rules on the same history and you expect roughly five "winners" that mean nothing. The more parameters you tune and the more variants you try, the more the in-sample Sharpe overstates the truth.

E[max of N null Sharpes] grows with √(2 ln N)  ⟹  trequired ↑ as you test moreThe best of N pure-noise backtests looks good purely from selection; the Bonferroni fix scales the required threshold up with the number of trials.

The guardrails are not exotic: (1) hold out a genuine out-of-sample period you touch exactly once; (2) penalize for the number of trials (Bonferroni, or a "deflated" Sharpe that discounts for how many strategies you searched); (3) prefer signals with an economic reason to exist over ones found by blind search; (4) demand robustness across names, regimes, and reasonable parameter ranges rather than a single knife-edge setting.

This is exactly the spirit of your capstone, due this week: a volatility-and-signal study. You will estimate volatility cleanly (mind the bounce of §10.2), construct one candidate signal, measure its IC honestly, and — most important — report it out-of-sample with an explicit count of how many variants you tried. A modest, honestly-measured edge beats a spectacular overfit one every time.

Common mistake"My backtest Sharpe is 3.0!" — after quietly trying 50 parameter settings and reporting only the best. That is selection bias, not skill. The headline number must come from data the strategy has never seen, and the threshold to call it real must rise with the number of things you tested.
✦ Worked Example — Roll's spread estimate & an honest IC

From a tick tape you measure the lag-1 autocovariance of transaction-price changes as −0.25 (in cents²). Separately, a momentum signal tested on 2,000 daily observations shows an IC of 0.045. Estimate the effective spread, and decide whether the IC is statistically real.

  1. Recover the spread (Roll's model): the model says Cov(Δpt, Δpt−1) = −s²/4. So s = 2·√(−Cov) = 2·√0.25 = 2·0.5 = 1.0 cent. The implied round-trip cost is one cent.
  2. Why the sign matters: the autocovariance had to be negative for Roll's model to apply — that negativity is the bid–ask bounce. A positive value would mean the model doesn't fit.
  3. Standard error of the IC: for n observations, an IC's standard error is ≈ 1/√n = 1/√2000 ≈ 0.0224.
  4. t-statistic: t = IC / SE = 0.045 / 0.0224 ≈ 2.01. On its own that just clears the usual ~2.0 bar — barely "significant" at 5%.
  5. The multiple-testing reality check: if you reached this signal after trying, say, 20 variants, the Bonferroni-adjusted bar is not t≈2 but closer to t≈2.9 (for 0.05/20). At t = 2.01 the "edge" fails the honest threshold — it is plausibly one of the lucky-noise winners from §10.4.
  6. Verdict: report the spread of 1 cent with confidence; treat the IC as unproven until it survives out-of-sample and a trial-count penalty. This is precisely the discipline the capstone grades you on.

🛠️ Hands-On: spread, bounce, and an out-of-sample IC

Reusing the course stack — pandas, NumPy, yfinance, statsmodels (install block is in the earlier Week-1/2 lectures if you skipped it). Daily bars hide the order book, so we'll (a) estimate an effective spread Roll-style from autocovariance, then (b) build a momentum signal and score its IC in-sample vs out-of-sample to feel the overfitting gap first-hand.

# microstructure_signal.py — Lecture 10
import numpy as np, pandas as pd, yfinance as yf

px = yf.download("SPY", start="2015-01-01", end="2024-01-01")["Close"].dropna()
ret = np.log(px).diff().dropna()                 # daily log returns

# (a) Roll's effective-spread estimate from lag-1 autocovariance of price changes
dp   = px.diff().dropna()
cov1 = dp.cov(dp.shift(1).reindex(dp.index))     # lag-1 autocovariance
spread = 2*np.sqrt(-cov1) if cov1 < 0 else np.nan
print("lag-1 autocov:", round(cov1, 4), " Roll spread est:", round(spread, 4))

# (b) a momentum signal: sign of trailing 10-day return predicts next day?
sig  = np.sign(ret.rolling(10).sum()).shift(1)   # known at t, predicts r_t
df   = pd.concat([sig, ret], axis=1).dropna()
df.columns = ["sig", "fwd"]

split  = len(df)//2                              # honest holdout
ic_in  = df.iloc[:split]["sig"].corr(df.iloc[:split]["fwd"])
ic_out = df.iloc[split:]["sig"].corr(df.iloc[split:]["fwd"])
print("IC in-sample :", round(ic_in, 4))
print("IC out-sample:", round(ic_out, 4))        # usually much smaller / flips sign

Two lessons fall straight out. The Roll spread will be a few cents — a real, mechanical cost the bounce reveals. And the in-sample IC will look more flattering than the out-of-sample one; if you loop over many lookback windows and keep only the best in-sample IC, the out-of-sample number collapses — overfitting, live. That gap is the capstone's whole point.

Q1. The bid–ask spread is best described as…
A. the difference between today's and yesterday's closing price
B. the gap between the best ask and the best bid — the cost of demanding immediacy
C. the standard deviation of intraday returns
D. the difference between the open and the close
Show answer

B. The spread is ask − bid: what an impatient round-trip trader pays. (A) and (D) are price changes over time, not the instantaneous quote gap; (C) is volatility, a separate concept.

Q2. You observe strong negative lag-1 autocorrelation in high-frequency transaction prices. The most likely explanation is…
A. a genuine, tradable mean-reversion edge
B. a data error that should be deleted
C. bid–ask bounce — trades alternating between bid and ask around a flat mid
D. the central limit theorem at work
Show answer

C. Bounce makes prints ricochet between bid and ask, producing spurious negative autocorrelation. It is not tradable (A) — capturing it means paying the spread that causes it. It's a real microstructure effect, not bad data (B), and unrelated to the CLT (D).

Q3. Measuring volatility from raw trade prices instead of mid-prices tends to…
A. overstate volatility, because the bounce adds variance that isn't real price movement
B. understate volatility, because trades happen less often than quotes
C. have no effect on the estimate
D. make the returns perfectly normal
Show answer

A. Roll's model gives Var(Δp) = σ²mid + s²/2: the bounce inflates measured variance. It does not shrink it (B), it is not negligible (C), and it has nothing to do with normality (D). Working with mid-prices removes most of the bias.

Q4. A realistic daily information coefficient (IC) for a single equity signal, and why it still matters, is closest to…
A. 0.9 — signals must explain most of returns to be useful
B. exactly 0 — no signal ever predicts returns
C. 0.04 — tiny, but it compounds into a usable Sharpe across many independent bets
D. 0.5 — half the variance is predictable
Show answer

C. Real ICs in liquid markets are a few percent; the fundamental law (Sharpe ≈ IC·√breadth) turns that small edge into profit via many bets. 0.9 and 0.5 (A, D) are wildly unrealistic; (B) is too defeatist — small but nonzero edges exist.

Q5. You tried 40 strategy variants and reported only the one with the best in-sample Sharpe of 2.8. The right way to judge whether it is real is to…
A. trust it — a Sharpe of 2.8 is high enough to be obviously real
B. try 40 more variants to find an even better one
C. add more parameters until the in-sample Sharpe exceeds 4
D. raise the significance bar for the number of trials and confirm it on untouched out-of-sample data
Show answer

D. With 40 trials, selection inflates the best in-sample number; the threshold must rise (Bonferroni / deflated Sharpe) and the verdict must come from data the strategy never saw. (A) ignores selection bias; (B) and (C) make the overfitting strictly worse.
