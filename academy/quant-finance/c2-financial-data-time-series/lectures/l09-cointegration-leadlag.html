Correlation, Cointegration & Lead-Lag

Two stocks can wander apart forever yet be tethered by an invisible rubber band — and finding that band, not their correlation, is where pairs trading lives.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the correlation of two return series and explain why it is unstable over time, motivating rolling correlation.
  • Distinguish correlation (a co-movement statistic on returns) from cointegration (a long-run equilibrium between price levels).
  • Explain how two non-stationary (I(1)) price series can combine into a stationary spread, and test it with Engle–Granger.
  • Describe lead-lag relationships and the intuition behind Granger causality — does one series help predict another?
  • See how a stationary spread and a hedge ratio set up a mean-reversion pairs trade you will build in Week 11.

9.1Correlation of Returns — and Why It Won't Sit Still

Correlation measures how two return series move together. If rX and rY are the daily returns of two assets, the Pearson correlation is the covariance scaled to live in [−1, +1]:

ρXY = Cov(rX, rY) / (σX σY)+1 = move in lockstep, 0 = no linear relation, −1 = perfect mirror image. It is computed on returns, not prices.

Correlation is the workhorse of diversification: a portfolio of two assets with correlation below 1 has lower variance than the weighted average of the parts (recall the Markowitz variance formula from the portfolio course). Risk managers live and die by the correlation matrix.

The catch is that ρ is not a constant of nature — it is a sample statistic estimated over a window, and it drifts. Two assets that look independent in calm markets can lurch to correlation near +1 in a crash, exactly when diversification was supposed to save you. So practitioners rarely trust a single number; they compute a rolling correlation over a moving window (say 60 days) and watch how it breathes.

ρt(60) = corr( rX, rY ) over the trailing 60 observations ending at tA time series of correlations, not a scalar — it exposes regime shifts the full-sample number hides.
Common mistakeCorrelation only captures the linear relationship, and a single full-sample ρ averages over wildly different regimes. A reported "correlation of 0.2" can hide a year at +0.8 and a year at −0.4. Always look at the rolling correlation before trusting one figure — and never assume yesterday's ρ will hold tomorrow.

9.2Correlation Is Not Cointegration

Here is the distinction that trips up most newcomers. Correlation is a short-term statement about returns moving together day to day. Cointegration is a long-term statement about price levels staying tethered. They are different questions, and one can hold without the other.

Recall from Week 4 that most price series are non-stationary — specifically integrated of order one, written I(1): the level wanders (a random walk with no fixed mean), but the first difference (the return) is stationary. Two such prices, PX and PY, are cointegrated if some linear combination of them is itself stationary — I(0):

spreadt = PY,t − β·PX,t  is stationary (mean-reverting)Each price alone wanders forever, but the spread is pinned to a stable mean. β is the hedge ratio.

This is remarkable: each series individually has no equilibrium level, yet a specific combination does. Economically it says the two assets share a common stochastic trend — they are driven by the same underlying force (two firms in one industry, a stock and its sector, a commodity and its futures) — so they can drift apart only temporarily before being pulled back.

AnalogyPicture a drunk walking her dog on a long leash. The drunk staggers randomly down the street — her position is a non-stationary random walk, with no "home" to return to. The dog also wanders unpredictably. But the distance between them — the leash — is bounded: it stretches, then snaps back. The two paths are cointegrated. Correlation asks "did they step the same way this second?"; cointegration asks "is the leash holding?"

Why does the difference matter for trading? Correlation gives you no entry signal — knowing two things move together tells you nothing about whether they are currently cheap or rich relative to each other. Cointegration does: a stationary spread has a known mean and variance, so when it stretches far from its mean you have a quantifiable bet that it will revert. That is the entire engine of pairs trading.

Common mistakeHigh correlation does not imply cointegration, and cointegration does not require high correlation. Two independent random walks will often show a large, completely spurious correlation over any given sample — regress one on the other and you get a deceptively significant t-stat with no real relationship. Test for a stationary spread, not for correlation, before you trust a pair.

9.3Testing for Cointegration: Engle–Granger

How do we decide a pair is genuinely cointegrated rather than two coincidentally-drifting walks? The simplest recipe is the two-step Engle–Granger procedure.

Step 1 — estimate the hedge ratio

Regress one price level on the other (with an intercept) by ordinary least squares to find the combination β that best ties them together:

PY,t = α + β·PX,t + εtThe fitted residual ε̂t = PY,t − α̂ − β̂·PX,t is the spread.

Step 2 — test the residual for stationarity

Run an Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test (Week 4) on that residual. The ADF null hypothesis is "has a unit root" — i.e. non-stationary. If the test statistic is sufficiently negative, you reject the null and conclude the residual (the spread) is stationary, which means X and Y are cointegrated.

ADF on ε̂t:  reject unit-root null ⇒ spread is stationary ⇒ cointegratedstatsmodels' coint() wraps both steps and returns a p-value directly.

A subtlety worth knowing: because the residual was estimated, you can't use the ordinary ADF critical values — the Engle–Granger test uses its own (more demanding) critical values, which statsmodels.tsa.stattools.coint applies for you. A small p-value (say < 0.05) is evidence of cointegration.

Common mistakeCointegration is not symmetric in finite samples: regressing Y on X can give a slightly different verdict than X on Y, because OLS minimizes errors in one variable only. Pick the economically sensible dependent variable, and remember the relationship can break — a structural change (a merger, a regime shift) can destroy a once-reliable cointegration. Re-test on a rolling basis; never assume it is permanent.

9.4Lead-Lag and Granger Causality

Co-movement is not always simultaneous. Sometimes one series moves first and the other follows with a delay — a lead-lag relationship. A liquid index future may lead the underlying cash index; a large-cap may lead its smaller peers as information propagates. If you can detect that X's move today helps predict Y's move tomorrow, you have a forecasting edge.

The tool for this is the cross-correlation function — correlation between X today and Y shifted by a lag k:

ρXY(k) = corr( rX,t,  rY,t+k )A peak at k > 0 means X leads Y by k periods; a peak at k = 0 is contemporaneous co-movement.

Granger causality formalizes this. X is said to "Granger-cause" Y if past values of X improve a forecast of Y beyond what Y's own past already provides. Operationally you fit two regressions and compare them with an F-test:

Yt = c + Σ ai Yt−i + Σ bj Xt−j + utIf the bj are jointly significant (adding X's lags lowers prediction error), X Granger-causes Y.
AnalogyGranger causality is "does the rooster's crow help me predict sunrise given I already know what time it crowed yesterday?" It is purely about predictive ordering, not real mechanism. The rooster does not cause the sun to rise — so a positive Granger result is a forecasting clue, never proof of a causal mechanism. Treat it as "X carries usable information about future Y," and verify the economics separately.
Common mistake"Granger-causes" is a notoriously misleading name — it does not establish true causation. A lurking third variable can drive both X and Y, producing a Granger result with no direct link. It also assumes the right lag length and (classically) stationary inputs, so difference your prices to returns before testing.
✦ Worked Example — building and trading a stationary spread

Two related stocks. A regression of price Y on price X over the past year gives intercept α = 4.0 and slope β = 1.5, with residuals (the spread) having mean 0 and standard deviation σ = 2.0. Today PX = 50 and PY = 86. The ADF p-value on the residual is 0.01. Is the pair tradeable, and what is the signal?

  1. Confirm cointegration: the Engle–Granger ADF p-value is 0.01 < 0.05, so we reject the unit-root null — the spread is stationary. The pair is cointegrated; mean reversion is justified.
  2. Compute today's spread: spread = PY − (α + β·PX) = 86 − (4.0 + 1.5·50) = 86 − 79 = +7.0.
  3. Express it in z-score units: the spread's mean is 0 and σ = 2.0, so z = (7.0 − 0)/2.0 = +3.5. The spread is 3.5 standard deviations above its equilibrium — Y is rich relative to X.
  4. Form the trade: bet on reversion — short 1 unit of Y and long β = 1.5 units of X (the hedge ratio makes the position roughly market-neutral). You profit as the spread falls back toward 0.
  5. Set the exit: a common rule is enter beyond |z| = 2, exit near z = 0. From z = +3.5 a reversion to the mean of 0 captures the full +7.0 of spread (×1 unit of Y) — minus costs. If instead the ADF p-value had been 0.40, the spread is not stationary and there is no reversion to bet on: stand aside.

🛠️ Hands-On: test cointegration and build a rolling spread z-score

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + yfinance + statsmodels — the full install block lives in Lecture 2). We download two related tickers, run the Engle–Granger cointegration test, estimate the hedge ratio, and turn the spread into a tradeable z-score.

# cointegration_demo.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import yfinance as yf
import statsmodels.api as sm
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import coint

# two economically related names (same sector / common driver)
tickers = ["KO", "PEP"]
px = yf.download(tickers, start="2018-01-01", end="2023-01-01")["Close"].dropna()
Y, X = px["KO"], px["PEP"]

# --- Step 1+2: Engle-Granger cointegration test (wraps OLS + ADF) ---
t_stat, p_value, crit = coint(Y, X)
print(f"coint p-value: {p_value:.4f}")     # < 0.05 ⇒ evidence of cointegration

# --- hedge ratio beta from OLS of Y on X (with intercept) ---
beta = sm.OLS(Y, sm.add_constant(X)).fit().params["PEP"]
spread = Y - beta * X                       # the stationary residual

# --- rolling z-score = the trade signal ---
win = 60
z = (spread - spread.rolling(win).mean()) / spread.rolling(win).std()
print(f"beta (hedge ratio): {beta:.3f}")
print(f"latest spread z-score: {z.iloc[-1]:.2f}")   # |z|>2 ⇒ stretched, expect reversion

# bonus: how unstable is plain return correlation? (rolling)
rets = px.pct_change().dropna()
roll_corr = rets["KO"].rolling(win).corr(rets["PEP"])
print(f"rolling corr range: {roll_corr.min():.2f} to {roll_corr.max():.2f}")

Read the output as a checklist: if p_value < 0.05 the spread is stationary and tradeable; the z-score is your entry/exit signal (enter beyond ±2, exit near 0); and the rolling-correlation range shows just how much plain correlation wanders — often a 0.3-wide band over five years. Try a clearly unrelated pair and watch the p-value jump well above 0.05.

💬 Discussion

A classic pairs trade is built on a pair that tested as cointegrated over the past three years, with a spread that has reverted dozens of times. One morning the spread blows out to z = +5 and keeps going — it does not come back. How would you decide, in real time, whether this is the best entry you have ever seen or the moment the relationship has permanently broken? What evidence would tip you each way, and how should that uncertainty shape your position sizing?

Show a model response

A z of +5 is genuinely ambiguous: a stationary spread should almost never reach it, so either the spread is extraordinarily stretched (a rare gift) or the cointegration that justified mean reversion has died. The honest answer is that the statistic alone cannot tell you which — so I would treat the move as a hypothesis test on the relationship itself, not just a deeper entry.

Evidence it's a structural break (stand aside / cut): a corporate event that changes the common driver (merger, spin-off, lawsuit, guidance cut on one name only); a re-run of the Engle–Granger test on a rolling window where the p-value has climbed above 0.05; the spread trending rather than oscillating; volume and news concentrated in one leg. Evidence it's a real opportunity (lean in, carefully): both legs moved on a broad sector/market shock with no name-specific news; the spread is still oscillating around a slowly-shifting mean; liquidity and fundamentals unchanged.

How uncertainty shapes sizing: because the break/opportunity odds are murky, I would not double down to the full Kelly-implied size. I would cap the position, define a hard stop in spread terms (not just z, since z's denominator is itself drifting), and size so a permanent break is survivable. Asymmetry matters: a broken cointegration has unbounded downside, while a true reversion has bounded upside — that argues for humility and a stop, not conviction.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) recognizes a stationary spread should rarely hit z = 5, making a break the leading suspect; (2) names concrete, testable evidence for both sides (re-test cointegration, look for name-specific news/events); (3) ties the resolution to risk management — a hard stop in spread units and reduced sizing under uncertainty — rather than just picking "in" or "out."

Q1. What is the key difference between correlation and cointegration?
A. Correlation is about long-run price levels; cointegration is about daily returns
B. Correlation describes short-term co-movement of returns; cointegration describes a long-run stationary relationship between price levels
C. They are two names for the same statistic
D. Cointegration can only be negative, correlation only positive
Show answer

B. Correlation is a returns co-movement statistic; cointegration says a linear combination of the price levels is stationary. A has the two backwards. C is false — they answer different questions and one can hold without the other. D is nonsense; both can take either sign.

Q2. Two price series are each I(1) (non-stationary random walks). They are cointegrated if…
A. their return correlation exceeds 0.9
B. both prices are trending upward
C. their volatilities are equal
D. some linear combination of the two price levels is stationary (I(0))
Show answer

D. Cointegration means a combination Y − βX is stationary even though each level wanders. A confuses cointegration with correlation (high correlation neither implies nor is required for cointegration). B and C are unrelated — common trends or equal vols guarantee nothing about a stationary spread.

Q3. In the Engle–Granger test, after regressing Y on X you run an ADF test on the residual. A very small ADF p-value means…
A. reject the unit-root null — the spread is stationary, so the pair is cointegrated
B. accept the unit-root null — the spread is a random walk
C. the two series are uncorrelated
D. the hedge ratio β must be exactly 1
Show answer

A. The ADF null is "has a unit root" (non-stationary). A small p-value rejects that null, so the residual/spread is stationary and the pair is cointegrated. B reverses the logic. C confuses cointegration with correlation. D is false — β is whatever OLS estimates, rarely exactly 1.

Q4. "X Granger-causes Y" most accurately means…
A. X physically causes Y through a known mechanism
B. X and Y are perfectly correlated at lag zero
C. past values of X improve the forecast of Y beyond Y's own past — predictive, not necessarily causal
D. Y always moves before X
Show answer

C. Granger causality is purely about predictive content: X's lags lower the forecast error for Y. A overclaims — it is explicitly not proof of true causation (a third variable can drive both). B describes contemporaneous correlation, not lead-lag. D is backwards (it would mean Y leads X).

Q5. A cointegrated spread has long-run mean 0 and σ = 2.0. Today the spread sits at +6.0. Expressed as a z-score and as a trade, this is…
A. z = +3.0; the spread is stretched high, so short Y and long βX, betting on reversion
B. z = +6.0; buy both legs
C. z = +3.0; the spread is stretched high, so go long Y and short βX
D. z = +1.5; do nothing, it is within one σ
Show answer

A. z = (6.0 − 0)/2.0 = +3.0, so the spread (Y − βX) is far above its mean — Y is rich relative to X. The mean-reversion bet is to short the rich leg (Y) and long βX. B miscomputes z (forgets to divide by σ) and ignores the hedge. C trades the wrong direction (it would profit only if the spread widens). D both miscomputes z and mislabels +3σ as inside one σ.
