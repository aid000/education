Volatility Modeling: ARCH & GARCH

Returns are nearly impossible to predict — but their turbulence is not, and GARCH is the model that turns yesterday's storm into a forecast of tomorrow's risk.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why financial volatility is time-varying and clusters, and why a single constant σ is the wrong model.
  • State the ARCH(q) idea: today's conditional variance depends on the size of past shocks.
  • Write down the GARCH(1,1) recursion and read each of its three parameters (ω, α, β).
  • Show how α + β controls persistence and compute the long-run (unconditional) variance.
  • Fit a GARCH(1,1) in Python and produce a multi-day volatility forecast for risk and option pricing.

8.1Volatility Is Not Constant

Through Weeks 5–7 we modeled the level of a series — random walks, AR and MA terms, ARIMA forecasts of the mean. But for an asset return the conditional mean is almost flat: tomorrow's expected return is roughly zero and stubbornly unpredictable. What is predictable is the spread. Calm weeks tend to follow calm weeks; once markets get violent they stay violent for a while. This is volatility clustering, and it is one of the most robust empirical facts in all of finance.

A simple way to see it: plot daily returns. You do not see uniform noise — you see long stretches of small wiggles punctuated by bursts of large up-and-down swings huddled together. Statistically, the returns themselves are nearly uncorrelated, but their squares (a proxy for variance) are strongly autocorrelated. That single observation is the seed of every model in this lecture.

rt = μ + εt,   εt = σt zt,   zt ~ iid Normal(0, 1)The return is a constant mean plus a shock εt. The shock is a fixed-shape noise zt scaled by a time-varying volatility σt. All the action is in modeling σt.

Here σt is the conditional volatility — the standard deviation of the return given everything we knew up to yesterday. It is a number that changes every day. The whole game is to write a sensible rule for how σt evolves.

AnalogyVolatility is the weather, not the climate. The long-run average temperature of a city (the unconditional variance) is a fixed climate fact. But whether tomorrow is stormy depends heavily on whether today was stormy — storms cluster. A constant-σ model is like quoting the annual average temperature when someone asks if they need an umbrella this afternoon.

8.2The ARCH Idea: Variance Has Memory

The breakthrough was to let the conditional variance depend on recent shocks. An ARCH(q) model — Autoregressive Conditional Heteroskedasticity — says today's variance is a base level plus a weighted sum of the last q squared shocks:

σt2 = ω + α1 εt−12 + α2 εt−22 + … + αq εt−q2ω > 0 is a floor; each αi ≥ 0 says "a big shock i days ago raises today's variance." A large εt−12 mechanically forces a large σt2.

Decode the jargon: heteroskedasticity just means "non-constant variance," and conditional means "given the past." The model is autoregressive in the squared shocks — a big move yesterday feeds directly into a bigger expected move today. That is exactly the clustering we saw in §8.1, now written as an equation.

ARCH works, but in practice it needs a long lag q (often 5–10 or more) to match how slowly real volatility decays, and every extra lag is another parameter to estimate. That fragility is what motivated the more economical model in the next section.

Common mistakeARCH models the variance of the shock εt, not the return level rt directly. The shocks themselves stay (nearly) unforecastable and zero-mean — ARCH never claims to predict direction. Confusing "I can forecast volatility" with "I can forecast returns" is the fastest way to blow up a trading account.

8.3GARCH(1,1): The Workhorse

The GARCH(1,1) model (Generalized ARCH) adds one elegant term: today's variance also depends on yesterday's variance. That single feedback term lets one lag do the work of many ARCH lags, because each day's variance already summarizes the entire past:

σt2 = ω + α εt−12 + β σt−12ω > 0, α ≥ 0, β ≥ 0. The "(1,1)" means one lag of the squared shock (α) and one lag of the variance (β).

Read the three knobs physically:

ParameterName / roleEffect
ω (omega)baseline / floorsets the long-run level of variance; keeps σ² from ever hitting zero
α (alpha)reaction / newshow violently variance jumps after a fresh shock (the "ARCH" effect)
β (beta)persistence / memoryhow long a burst of volatility lingers (the "GARCH" feedback)

The sum α + β is the persistence. As long as α + β < 1, shocks fade and the variance is mean-reverting — it always drifts back toward a constant long-run level. Setting σt2 = σt−12 = σ̄² and solving gives the unconditional (long-run) variance:

σ̄2 = ω / (1 − α − β)This only exists when α + β < 1. The closer α + β is to 1, the slower the mean reversion and the longer volatility shocks persist. Typical daily equity estimates put α + β near 0.95–0.99.

You can rewrite GARCH(1,1) to make the mean reversion explicit. Deviations of today's variance from the long-run level are a weighted echo of yesterday's deviations, decaying geometrically at rate (α + β):

σt2 − σ̄2 = α(εt−12 − σ̄2) + β(σt−12 − σ̄2)The variance is always being pulled back toward σ̄²; the pull is weaker (slower) the closer α + β is to 1.

8.4Fitting GARCH & Forecasting Volatility

GARCH parameters are estimated by maximum likelihood. The likelihood is built by assuming each shock εt is Normal(0, σt2), where σt2 is itself produced recursively from the parameters and the data. The optimizer searches over (ω, α, β) for the values that make the observed return series most probable. You never do this by hand — a library does it — but knowing it is MLE tells you why you need a few hundred observations and why the estimates carry standard errors.

Forecasting forward

Once fitted, GARCH gives a clean multi-step forecast. The one-step-ahead variance is known the moment today closes, because εt and σt are observed. Beyond that, you replace the unknown future squared shock with its expectation (which equals the variance), and the forecast decays geometrically toward the long-run level:

E[σt+h2] = σ̄2 + (α + β)h−1 (σt+12 − σ̄2)Each step out, the gap to σ̄² shrinks by a factor (α + β). High persistence (α + β near 1) ⇒ the forecast stays elevated for many days after a shock.

Why a risk desk cares

Volatility forecasts are the raw material of risk management and pricing. A one-day Value-at-Risk uses tomorrow's σt+1: the 99% VaR of a return is roughly 2.33·σt+1 (the normal quantile). Scaling to an h-day horizon means summing forecast variances, not naively applying the √h rule — because GARCH says volatility is mean-reverting, the term structure of volatility flattens out. Option traders read the same forecasts as a model-based estimate of future realized volatility, which they compare against the implied volatility baked into option prices to decide whether options look cheap or expensive.

AnalogyA GARCH forecast is a thermostat with momentum. After a heat spike (a shock) it predicts the room stays warm for a while, then drifts back to the set-point σ̄. The persistence α + β is how sluggish the thermostat is: near 1, the room cools agonizingly slowly; near 0, it snaps back to the set-point almost instantly.
✦ Worked Example — a GARCH(1,1) variance update and forecast

Suppose a fitted daily model has ω = 0.000002, α = 0.10, β = 0.88. Yesterday's variance was σt2 = 0.0004 (so σt = 2%/day) and today's shock was a large εt = −3% = −0.03. Find the update, the long-run level, and the 5-day-ahead forecast.

  1. Persistence: α + β = 0.10 + 0.88 = 0.98 < 1, so the model is stationary (mean-reverting) — good.
  2. Long-run variance: σ̄² = ω / (1 − α − β) = 0.000002 / 0.02 = 0.0001, i.e. σ̄ = 1%/day, annualizing to ≈ 1%·√252 ≈ 15.9%/yr.
  3. Update tomorrow's variance: σt+12 = ω + α·εt² + β·σt² = 0.000002 + 0.10·(0.0009) + 0.88·(0.0004) = 0.000002 + 0.00009 + 0.000352 = 0.000444, so σt+1 ≈ 2.11%/day. The −3% shock nudged forecast vol up from 2%.
  4. 5-day forecast: E[σ²t+5] = σ̄² + (α+β)4·(σt+1² − σ̄²) = 0.0001 + (0.98)4·(0.000444 − 0.0001). With 0.98⁴ ≈ 0.9224: = 0.0001 + 0.9224·0.000344 = 0.0001 + 0.000317 = 0.000417, so σt+5 ≈ 2.04%/day.
  5. Interpretation: volatility is still elevated five days out (2.04% vs the 1% long-run), reverting only slowly because α + β = 0.98 is high. A risk desk would set tomorrow's 99% one-day VaR ≈ 2.33·2.11% ≈ 4.9% of position value.

🛠️ Hands-On: fit GARCH(1,1) and forecast

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (pandas + yfinance + NumPy + Matplotlib — the full install block lives in Course 1, Lecture 1). GARCH fitting needs one new package, the arch library, so we add a one-line install note for just that tool.

⬇ One new package: the arch library

Activate your existing quant-env, then add the volatility-modeling package (statsmodels covers ARIMA, but the arch package is the standard for GARCH):

pip install arch yfinance pandas matplotlib
# garch_demo.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np, yfinance as yf
from arch import arch_model

# 1. daily log-returns in PERCENT (arch is happier with %-scaled data)
px  = yf.download("SPY", start="2015-01-01", end="2024-01-01")["Close"]
ret = 100 * np.log(px / px.shift(1)).dropna()

# 2. fit a GARCH(1,1) with a constant mean and Normal shocks
am  = arch_model(ret, mean="Constant", vol="GARCH", p=1, q=1, dist="normal")
res = am.fit(disp="off")
print(res.params)            # omega, alpha[1], beta[1]

a, b = res.params["alpha[1]"], res.params["beta[1]"]
print("persistence a+b :", a + b)                 # typically ~0.97-0.99
print("long-run daily vol %:",
      np.sqrt(res.params["omega"] / (1 - a - b)))  # sigma-bar

# 3. forecast variance 10 trading days ahead
fc = res.forecast(horizon=10, reindex=False)
print("10-day vol forecast %:", np.sqrt(fc.variance.values[-1]))

Read the output: alpha[1] (reaction) is usually small (~0.05–0.12) and beta[1] (persistence) large (~0.85–0.92), so α + β sits just under 1 — exactly the slow-decaying clustering we drew above. The 10-day forecast vector starts near the current elevated level and drifts toward the long-run vol. Swap dist="t" to use Student-t shocks for the fat tails real returns show.

❓ Lecture 8 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The empirical fact that motivates ARCH/GARCH — "calm periods follow calm periods, violent periods follow violent periods" — is called…
A. mean reversion of returns
B. volatility clustering
C. the random walk hypothesis
D. seasonality
Show answer

B. Volatility clustering is precisely the tendency of large (small) moves to be followed by large (small) moves. Mean reversion of returns (A) concerns the level, not its spread; the random walk hypothesis (C) is about unpredictable price levels; seasonality (D) is a fixed calendar pattern, not a variance-persistence effect.

Q2. In GARCH(1,1), σt2 = ω + α εt−12 + β σt−12, which term captures how long a volatility burst persists?
A. ω, the baseline floor
B. α, the reaction to the last shock
C. the noise zt
D. β, the coefficient on the lagged variance
Show answer

D. β feeds yesterday's variance into today's, so it is the memory/persistence term that makes a burst linger. ω (A) only sets the long-run level; α (B) governs the size of the jump after a fresh shock, not its duration; zt (C) is the standardized noise and carries no memory.

Q3. A fitted GARCH(1,1) has α = 0.08, β = 0.90, and ω = 0.000004. What is its long-run (unconditional) daily variance?
A. 0.0002
B. 0.000004
C. 0.00005
D. it does not exist
Show answer

A. α + β = 0.98 < 1, so σ̄² = ω/(1 − α − β) = 0.000004 / 0.02 = 0.0002. Choice B is just ω (forgetting the divisor); C uses the wrong divisor; D is wrong because α + β < 1 guarantees the long-run variance exists.

Q4. What does a GARCH model claim it can forecast?
A. Tomorrow's return direction (up or down)
B. Tomorrow's conditional variance (the size of the move), not its sign
C. The exact price level next week
D. The long-run mean return of the asset
Show answer

B. GARCH models the conditional variance of the shock; it predicts how big the move will be, not which way. It explicitly does not forecast direction (A) or price level (C). The mean return (D) is handled by the separate mean equation (often just a constant), not the GARCH variance recursion.

Q5. Compared with ARCH(q), the key advantage of GARCH(1,1) is that…
A. one lag of past variance summarizes many past shocks, so it needs far fewer parameters
B. it can forecast the direction of returns, which ARCH cannot
C. it removes the need to assume any noise distribution
D. it guarantees α + β is always greater than 1
Show answer

A. The β·σt−1² term recursively packs the whole shock history into one number, so GARCH(1,1) matches slow volatility decay with three parameters where ARCH would need many lags. B is false — neither model forecasts direction; C is false — you still assume a shock distribution (Normal or t) to build the likelihood; D is false and undesirable, since α + β > 1 would make variance explode (non-stationary).

✓ What you learned

  • Financial volatility is time-varying and clusters: returns are nearly unpredictable but their squared shocks are autocorrelated, so a constant σ is the wrong model.
  • The ARCH(q) idea makes conditional variance a weighted sum of recent squared shocks — but needs many lags to fit real decay.
  • GARCH(1,1), σt² = ω + α εt−1² + β σt−1², adds a lagged-variance feedback term; ω is the floor, α the reaction, β the persistence.
  • Persistence α + β (< 1 for stationarity) sets the decay rate; the long-run variance is σ̄² = ω/(1 − α − β), and forecasts revert toward it geometrically.
  • Fit by maximum likelihood in the arch library and turn the σt+1 forecast into VaR and option-pricing inputs — your Week-8 capstone risk module.
