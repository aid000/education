Working with Financial Data

Before you can model a market you have to wrangle it — pull the prices, line up the dates, patch the gaps, and turn raw quotes into clean returns.

  • Resample a series to a coarser frequency and handle missing data without silently fabricating prices.
  • Adjust for splits and dividends, and explain why unadjusted prices break a returns calculation.
  • Compute simple and log returns with pct_change and np.log, and produce a basic price/return plot.

2.1Prices Live in a pandas Series

A price history is the most basic object in quant finance: a list of numbers, each stamped with a date. The natural home for it in Python is a pandas Series — a one-dimensional array of values paired with an index of labels. For prices, that index should be the timestamps, not a default 0,1,2,… integer position. When the index is a DatetimeIndex, pandas knows the rows are points in time and unlocks date-aware slicing, alignment, and resampling.

Stack several Series side by side — one column per ticker — and you get a DataFrame: a table sharing a single date index. The magic is automatic alignment. When you combine two Series, pandas matches them on the index labels, not on row position, so a missing trading day in one asset doesn't shift everything below it out of register.

price Series:  index = dates  →  values = closesA DataFrame is just several such Series glued together on one shared DatetimeIndex.

This matters because financial data is famously misaligned: holidays differ across countries, some assets trade 24/7 and others 9:30–4:00, and history starts on different days. Indexing on the date — not the row number — is what keeps a portfolio's columns honestly lined up.

AnalogyThink of the DatetimeIndex as the spine of a ledger. Each asset is a column of entries; the dates are the ruled lines they must sit on. Align by row-number instead and it's like photocopying two ledgers and stacking them — one missing line and every figure below is attributed to the wrong day.

2.2Fetching Data with yfinance — and the Adjusted Close

You rarely type prices by hand. yfinance is a free library that downloads historical daily (or intraday) bars into a ready-made DataFrame indexed by date. A typical daily bar carries Open, High, Low, Close, Volume (OHLCV) plus an Adjusted Close. The last one is the column that matters most, and the one beginners get wrong.

The raw Close is the literal price the asset last traded at that day. But two events change the price without changing your wealth: a stock split (a 2-for-1 split halves the quoted price and doubles your share count) and a dividend (cash leaves the company, so the price drops by roughly the payout on the ex-dividend date). The Adjusted Close rewrites history so that these mechanical drops are removed — it answers "what would yesterday's price be, restated in today's share terms, with dividends reinvested?"

adjusted return = (Ptadj − Pt−1adj) / Pt−1adjUsing adjusted closes makes a split look like a non-event and folds dividends into the return — exactly the total return an investor actually earns.
Common mistakeComputing returns from the raw Close will show a fake −50% crash on a 2-for-1 split day, or a phantom −2% drop every ex-dividend date. Always build returns from the Adjusted Close unless you have a specific reason to want the raw quote. Note that yfinance's auto_adjust=True (now the default) overwrites the Close column with the adjusted figure — know which mode you asked for.

2.3Resampling & Missing Data

Raw data almost never arrives at the frequency you want to model. You might have daily bars but want monthly returns; you might have ragged intraday ticks but want a clean 1-minute grid. Resampling is pandas' tool for changing the time step. Downsampling (daily → monthly) groups many rows into one and you must say how to aggregate: for a price you usually take the last value in each period (the period-end close); for volume you sum.

df.resample('ME').last()   // month-end closing price'ME' = month-end, 'W' = weekly, 'QE' = quarter-end. Pair with .last() for prices, .sum() for volume, .ohlc() for bars.

Then there are holes. A market holiday simply has no row. A data error can leave a NaN (not-a-number) in an otherwise present row. You have three honest moves: drop the missing rows (dropna), forward-fill the last known price (ffill — defensible for a price that genuinely didn't change because the market was closed), or interpolate. What you must never do is back-fill a price from the future, which leaks information that wasn't available at the time.

Common mistakeForward-filling a price is often fine; forward-filling a return is a bug. A filled price simply repeats — the return across that gap is correctly 0. But if you fill returns directly you invent non-zero moves on days the market never traded, biasing volatility and any strategy built on it. Resample/clean the prices, then compute returns.

2.4From Prices to Returns: pct_change and np.log

Models live on returns, not price levels, because returns are roughly stationary and comparable across assets (Week 1). Two flavors, both one line in pandas. The simple return is the percentage change; pct_change() computes it directly:

rt = Pt / Pt−1 − 1  ≡   prices.pct_change()

The log return is the natural log of the price ratio. It has the prized property of time-additivity: log returns over consecutive days simply add up to the multi-day log return, which makes aggregating across time trivial.

ℓt = ln(Pt / Pt−1) = ln Pt − ln Pt−1  ≡   np.log(prices).diff()Σ of daily log returns over a window = the log return of the whole window. Simple returns compound (multiply), they don't add.

For small daily moves the two are nearly identical (ln(1+r) ≈ r when r is small), so the choice rarely changes a chart. It matters for the math: log returns are what you sum, scale by √t, and feed into the Gaussian models of later courses. The very first row of any return series is NaN — there is no "day before" the first day — so dropna() before you compute statistics.

Plotting closes the loop. A quick prices.plot() shows the level; (1 + returns).cumprod() rebases every asset to a common starting wealth of 1 so you can compare growth on one axis regardless of nominal price.

✦ Worked Example — adjusted vs. raw return across a split

A stock closes at $200 on Monday. After the close it does a 2-for-1 split; Tuesday it opens around $100 and closes at $102. You held 5 shares. What return should your data show, and what does the raw close wrongly show?

  1. Your real wealth: Monday you held 5 × $200 = $1,000. The split gives you 10 shares; Tuesday they close at $102 each → 10 × $102 = $1,020. Your true one-day return is 1020/1000 − 1 = +2.0%.
  2. Raw close return (wrong): 102 / 200 − 1 = −49%. The naive calculation reports a near-halving that never happened to your money.
  3. What adjustment does: the adjusted series scales every pre-split price by the split ratio (½). Monday's $200 becomes $100 in adjusted terms.
  4. Adjusted close return (right): 102 / 100 − 1 = +2.0% — matching your actual wealth change.
  5. Log-return check: ln(102/100) = ln(1.02) ≈ +0.0198, essentially the same +2% for a small move, and it would add cleanly to the next day's log return.

# financial_data.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import yfinance as yf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# 1) FETCH — daily bars, dividends/splits folded into Close (auto_adjust default)
data = yf.download("AAPL MSFT", start="2018-01-01", end="2024-01-01",
                   auto_adjust=True)        # adjusted prices
prices = data["Close"]                       # DataFrame: one column per ticker

print(type(prices.index))                    # <class 'pandas...DatetimeIndex'>
print(prices.head())
print("missing values per column:\n", prices.isna().sum())

# 2) CLEAN — align on the date index, forward-fill stray gaps in PRICES
prices = prices.ffill().dropna()             # fill holes, then drop any leading NaN

# 3) RESAMPLE — daily -> month-end closing price
monthly = prices.resample("ME").last()       # 'ME' = month end; .last() = period close

# 4) RETURNS — simple and log, on the cleaned daily prices
simple_ret = prices.pct_change().dropna()    # P_t / P_{t-1} - 1
log_ret    = np.log(prices).diff().dropna()  # ln(P_t) - ln(P_{t-1}), time-additive

# sanity: a window of log returns SUMS to the window's total log return
total = np.log(prices.iloc[-1] / prices.iloc[0])
print("sum of daily log returns:\n", log_ret.sum())
print("direct window log return:\n", total)   # matches log_ret.sum()

# 5) PLOT — rebase to growth of $1 so different price levels compare
growth = (1 + simple_ret).cumprod()
growth.plot(title="Growth of $1 (adjusted, total return)")
plt.ylabel("multiple of starting wealth"); plt.show()

Notice the workflow order: fetch → clean the prices → resample → compute returns → plot. The log_ret.sum() check should equal the single direct window log return, demonstrating time-additivity. Try swapping auto_adjust=False and re-running across a known split or dividend date — you'll see the phantom jump the warning above describes.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why should a price history use a DatetimeIndex rather than a default integer index?
A. It lets pandas align and resample by date, so misaligned assets stay correctly matched
B. It makes the file smaller on disk
C. It is required for pandas to store floating-point numbers
D. It automatically removes all missing values
Show answer

A. A DatetimeIndex tells pandas the rows are points in time, enabling date-aware alignment, slicing, and resampling. It has no effect on file size (B) or float storage (C), and it does not clean missing data on its own (D).

Q2. You compute returns from the raw Close and see a sudden −50% drop on one day with no news. The most likely explanation is…
A. the market was closed that day
B. a 2-for-1 stock split that the raw price reflects but adjustment would remove
C. log returns were used instead of simple returns
D. the DatetimeIndex was sorted incorrectly
Show answer

B. A 2-for-1 split halves the quoted price; on the raw close that looks like a −50% return even though wealth is unchanged. The adjusted close removes it. A closed market just has no row (A); log vs. simple returns barely differ for real moves (C); index sorting wouldn't create a clean −50% (D).

Q3. When downsampling a daily price series to month-end, the correct aggregation is generally…
A. .sum() of the prices in the month
B. .mean() of the prices in the month
C. .last() — the period-end closing price
D. .count() of trading days
Show answer

C. A month-end price series takes the last close of each month. Summing prices (A) is meaningless; the mean (B) is a different (smoothed) quantity, not the close; count (D) gives trading-day counts, not a price. (Volume, by contrast, is the case where you'd sum.)

Q4. Which property makes log returns especially convenient for aggregating over time?
A. They are always positive
B. They are time-additive: daily log returns sum to the multi-day log return
C. They never produce a NaN on the first row
D. They are identical to simple returns for large moves
Show answer

B. ln(P_t/P_{t-1}) summed over a window equals ln of the whole window's price ratio — they add instead of compounding. Returns can be negative (A); the first row is still NaN because there's no prior price (C); log and simple returns diverge for large moves, agreeing only for small ones (D).

Q5. Which data-cleaning step is a genuine bug that leaks future information or invents moves?
A. Forward-filling a missing price with the last known close
B. Dropping the first NaN row of a return series
C. Resampling daily prices to weekly with .last()
D. Back-filling today's price from tomorrow's value, or filling returns directly
Show answer

D. Back-filling uses future data that wasn't available at the time (look-ahead bias), and filling returns directly fabricates non-zero moves on non-trading days. Forward-filling a price (A) is a defensible choice, dropping the leading return NaN (B) is correct, and weekly resampling with .last() (C) is standard.
