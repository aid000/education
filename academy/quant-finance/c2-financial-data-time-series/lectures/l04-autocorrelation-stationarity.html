Autocorrelation & Stationarity

Before you can forecast a series, you must ask whether its statistical rules hold still long enough to be learned — and whether today secretly remembers yesterday.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the autocorrelation function (ACF) and read a correlogram to spot memory in a series.
  • Distinguish the partial autocorrelation function (PACF) from the ACF and explain what each isolates.
  • State weak vs strict stationarity and explain why stationarity is the precondition for forecasting.
  • Recognize a unit root and read the Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test as a hypothesis test for non-stationarity.
  • Apply differencing to turn a non-stationary price series into a stationary return series.

4.1Autocorrelation: Does Today Remember Yesterday?

A time series is a sequence of observations indexed by time: x1, x2, …, xT. Ordinary correlation (Course 1) measures the linear association between two different variables. Autocorrelation measures the correlation of a series with a time-shifted copy of itself. Shift the series back by k periods — the lag — line it up against the original, and compute the correlation. Do that for every lag and you get the autocorrelation function (ACF).

ρ(k) = Cov(xt, xt−k) / Var(xt) = γ(k) / γ(0)γ(k) is the autocovariance at lag k; dividing by γ(0)=Var(xt) rescales it to a correlation in [−1, 1]. By construction ρ(0) = 1.

Why a quant cares: autocorrelation is predictability. If ρ(1) is meaningfully positive, an up-move today raises the odds of an up-move tomorrow — a momentum or trend signal. If ρ(1) is negative, moves tend to reverse — a mean-reversion signal that pairs-trading and market-making strategies live on. A series whose ACF is essentially zero at every lag is white noise: unforecastable from its own past, which is roughly what efficient-market theory predicts for raw asset returns.

AnalogyAutocorrelation is an echo test. Clap once in a canyon: if you hear a strong echo half a second later, the environment "remembers" your clap at lag = 0.5s. A price series with positive ρ(1) echoes its last move into the next one; white noise is an anechoic chamber — the past leaves no audible trace.

We estimate ρ(k) from data with the sample autocorrelation, summing lagged products of the de-meaned series and normalizing by the total variation:

r(k) = Σt=k+1T (xt − x̄)(xt−k − x̄) ⁄ Σt=1T (xt − x̄)²

Plotting r(k) against k gives the correlogram. For white noise, each bar is approximately Normal(0, 1/T), so the band ±1.96/√T marks the 95% "indistinguishable-from-zero" zone. Bars poking outside it flag real, exploitable memory.

4.2Partial Autocorrelation: Direct vs Inherited Memory

The ACF has a blind spot. Suppose today depends on yesterday and yesterday depended on the day before. Then today is correlated with two days ago — but only indirectly, through yesterday. The ACF at lag 2 reports that inherited correlation as if it were real direct memory.

The partial autocorrelation function (PACF) strips out the middlemen. The PACF at lag k is the correlation between xt and xt−k after removing the linear effect of all the lags in between (1 through k−1). It answers: once I already know the recent past, does going one step further back tell me anything new?

PACF(k) = Corr( xt, xt−k | xt−1, …, xt−k+1 )The correlation that remains after the intermediate lags are regressed out. PACF(1) = ρ(1) always, since there is nothing in between to control for.

This pair of plots is the practitioner's fingerprint kit for choosing a model (you will fit these in Weeks 5–6). The classic reading:

PatternACFPACFSuggests
AR(p) — autoregressivetails off (decays gradually)cuts off after lag porder p from the PACF
MA(q) — moving averagecuts off after lag qtails offorder q from the ACF
White noiseall bars inside the bandall bars inside the bandnothing to model
Common mistakeReading a large ACF at lag 2 as "two-day memory" when the PACF at lag 2 is near zero. That lag-2 correlation is inherited through lag 1, not direct. Always read ACF and PACF together — the ACF tells you that there is memory; the PACF tells you how far back the direct memory truly reaches.

4.3Stationarity: Why the Rules Must Hold Still

Forecasting rests on a quiet assumption: that the statistical behavior you learned from the past still applies to the future. If the mean, variance, and correlation structure drift over time, then yesterday's fitted model describes a world that no longer exists. Stationarity is the formal name for "the rules hold still."

There are two grades. Strict stationarity demands that the entire joint distribution of any block of observations be unchanged by a time shift — a very strong, rarely-testable condition. Weak (covariance) stationarity asks only for the first two moments to be stable, which is all most models need:

E[xt] = μ   (constant)    Var(xt) = σ²   (constant)    Cov(xt, xt−k) = γ(k)   (depends only on k, not on t)Constant mean, constant variance, and an autocovariance that depends only on the gap k between two points — never on when they occur.

That third condition is what makes the ACF meaningful at all: only under stationarity is "the correlation at lag k" a single well-defined number rather than something that changes depending on which slice of history you measure. Raw asset prices are almost never stationary — they trend, and their variance grows over time. Returns, by contrast, are much closer to stationary, which is the deep reason Lecture 3's stylized facts were all stated about returns, not prices.

AnalogyStationarity is a level playing field. Imagine measuring a high-jumper's success rate, but the bar is silently being raised every round. Any "average success rate" you compute is meaningless — it mixes easy early rounds with impossible late ones. Differencing (§4.4) is like re-measuring jumps relative to the current bar height: a stable, comparable quantity you can actually model.

4.4Unit Roots, the ADF Test & Differencing

The canonical non-stationary process is the random walk (Course 1, Week 5): tomorrow equals today plus an unpredictable shock. Write it as an AR(1) with coefficient φ:

xt = φ·xt−1 + εt,   εt ~ white noiseIf |φ| < 1 the series is stationary and pulls back toward its mean. If φ = 1 it is a random walk — a "unit root" — and wanders without bound.

When φ = 1 the process has a unit root: shocks never decay, the series has no fixed level to return to, and its variance grows linearly with time. A unit root is the mathematical signature of non-stationarity. The Augmented Dickey–Fuller (ADF) test formalizes the check. It rewrites the equation in differenced form and tests whether the level term truly pulls the series back:

Δxt = α + β·t + γ·xt−1 + Σ δiΔxt−i + εtγ = φ − 1. The "augmented" part is the lagged-difference Σ δiΔxt−i terms, which soak up short-run autocorrelation so the test isn't fooled by it.

The hypotheses are framed around γ:

H0: γ = 0 (unit root, non-stationary)   vs   H1: γ < 0 (stationary)

A strongly negative γ means the level term actively drags the series back toward equilibrium — evidence against a unit root. The ADF statistic is that estimate divided by its standard error, but it does not follow a normal distribution, so it uses special Dickey–Fuller critical values. In practice you read the p-value: small p (say < 0.05) ⟹ reject H0 ⟹ call the series stationary.

Common mistakeGetting the null backwards. ADF's null hypothesis is "non-stationary (has a unit root)." A large p-value does not confirm stationarity — it merely fails to reject non-stationarity. To claim a series is stationary you need a small p-value. Treating "p = 0.40" as a green light is a classic way to fit a model on a series that is still wandering.

Differencing to achieve stationarity

If a series fails the test, the standard fix is differencing: model the change instead of the level.

Δxt = xt − xt−1One difference removes a single unit root. A series needing d differences to become stationary is "integrated of order d," written I(d); models built on it are the "I" in ARIMA (Week 6).

For prices this is exactly the move you already know: differencing the (log) price gives the return. A trending, unit-root price series I(1) becomes a roughly stationary I(0) return series — finally something whose ACF, mean, and variance you can trust enough to model. The art is differencing enough to reach stationarity but no more, since over-differencing injects artificial negative autocorrelation that corrupts the ACF you just worked to clean.

✦ Worked Example — ACF of a short return series, by hand

A tiny daily-return series (in %) over five days: x = [+2, −1, +2, −1, +2]. Compute the lag-1 sample autocorrelation r(1) and interpret it.

  1. Sample mean: x̄ = (2 − 1 + 2 − 1 + 2) / 5 = 4/5 = 0.8.
  2. De-mean the series: deviations dt = xt − 0.8 are [+1.2, −1.8, +1.2, −1.8, +1.2].
  3. Denominator Σdt² = 1.2² + 1.8² + 1.2² + 1.8² + 1.2² = 1.44 + 3.24 + 1.44 + 3.24 + 1.44 = 10.8.
  4. Lag-1 cross products dt·dt−1 for t = 2..5: (−1.8)(1.2) + (1.2)(−1.8) + (−1.8)(1.2) + (1.2)(−1.8) = −2.16 − 2.16 − 2.16 − 2.16 = −8.64.
  5. Lag-1 autocorrelation: r(1) = −8.64 / 10.8 = −0.80.
  6. Interpret: a strongly negative r(1) means the series alternates — an up-day is followed by a down-day. That is textbook mean reversion; a contrarian rule (fade today's move) would have predicted tomorrow's sign perfectly in this toy data. (With only T = 5 the white-noise band ±1.96/√5 ≈ ±0.88 is wide, so even this striking pattern is borderline-significant — a reminder that short samples lie.)

🛠️ Hands-On: ACF, PACF, and an ADF test on real prices

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + pandas + Matplotlib — install block is in C1 Lecture 1). This lecture adds two new tools — yfinance for data and statsmodels for the ACF/PACF/ADF machinery — so install just those.

⬇ New this lecture: yfinance + statsmodels

Activate your existing quant-env, then add the two new packages:

source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install yfinance statsmodels

Verify: python -c "import statsmodels, yfinance; print('ok')" should print ok.

# acf_stationarity.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np, yfinance as yf
from statsmodels.tsa.stattools import adfuller, acf
from statsmodels.graphics.tsaplots import plot_acf, plot_pacf
import matplotlib.pyplot as plt

# 1. Daily closing prices for a broad equity index ETF
px = yf.download("SPY", start="2015-01-01", end="2023-01-01")["Close"].dropna()
ret = np.log(px).diff().dropna()        # log-returns = first difference of log-price

# 2. ADF test on the PRICE level vs the RETURNS
def report(name, s):
    stat, p = adfuller(s.values)[:2]
    verdict = "STATIONARY (reject unit root)" if p < 0.05 else "non-stationary"
    print(f"{name:8s}  ADF={stat:7.3f}  p={p:6.3f}  -> {verdict}")

report("price", px)     # large p  -> non-stationary (unit root, as expected)
report("return", ret)   # tiny p   -> stationary, safe to model

# 3. Correlogram of returns: is there exploitable memory?
print("first 5 ACF lags of returns:", np.round(acf(ret, nlags=5)[1:], 3))
fig, ax = plt.subplots(1, 2, figsize=(11, 3))
plot_acf(ret, lags=20, ax=ax[0]);  plot_pacf(ret, lags=20, ax=ax[1])
plt.tight_layout();  plt.savefig("correlogram.png", dpi=120)

You will see the price fail the ADF test (large p-value, wandering) while the returns pass it decisively (tiny p-value). The return correlogram bars mostly sit inside the ±1.96/√T band — daily equity returns are close to white noise, exactly the efficient-market prediction. The handful of bars that poke out (often a small negative lag-1, or seasonal lags) are precisely where systematic strategies go hunting for edge.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The autocorrelation function ρ(k) of a time series measures…
A. the correlation between two different assets at the same time
B. the correlation of the series with a copy of itself shifted by k periods
C. the variance of the series at time k
D. the probability that the series is stationary
Show answer

B. Autocorrelation is the correlation of a series with its own lag-k copy. Correlation between two different variables (A) is ordinary cross-correlation. Variance (C) is a single-point spread, not a lagged relationship. Stationarity is a property tested separately, not a probability the ACF returns (D).

Q2. A series has an ACF that decays slowly across many lags but a PACF that shows one large spike at lag 1 and near-zero bars afterward. The lag-2 ACF being non-zero is best explained by…
A. direct two-day memory in the series
B. a unit root that must be differenced away
C. measurement error in the data
D. correlation inherited through lag 1, not a direct lag-2 link
Show answer

D. A PACF that cuts off after lag 1 says the only direct memory is one step; the lag-2 ACF is inherited through lag 1. Direct two-day memory (A) would show up as a PACF spike at lag 2, which is absent. A unit root (B) would show an ACF that barely decays at all, not the clean AR(1) decay described, and it would not be diagnosed from this pattern alone. Measurement error (C) is unrelated.

Q3. Which set of conditions defines weak (covariance) stationarity?
A. constant mean, constant variance, and an autocovariance that depends only on the lag k
B. the full joint distribution is unchanged by any time shift
C. the series has zero mean and is normally distributed
D. all autocorrelations are exactly zero
Show answer

A. Weak stationarity constrains only the first two moments: constant mean, constant variance, and a lag-only autocovariance. An unchanged full joint distribution (B) is the stronger strict stationarity. Zero-mean normality (C) is neither required nor sufficient. Zero autocorrelation everywhere (D) describes white noise — a special stationary case, not the definition.

Q4. You run an Augmented Dickey–Fuller test on a price series and get a p-value of 0.62. The correct conclusion is…
A. the series is definitely stationary
B. the test is invalid and should be discarded
C. you fail to reject the null of a unit root — the series is treated as non-stationary
D. the series has exactly one unit root and needs no differencing
Show answer

C. ADF's null is "has a unit root (non-stationary)." A large p-value (0.62) fails to reject that null, so you treat the series as non-stationary. It does not prove stationarity (A) — that needs a small p-value. The test is perfectly valid (B). And a non-rejection signals you likely do need to difference, contradicting D's "needs no differencing."

Q5. Why do quants almost always model asset returns rather than raw prices?
A. Returns are always normally distributed
B. Prices typically have a unit root and are non-stationary, whereas differencing into returns yields a roughly stationary series
C. Returns have no autocorrelation by definition
D. Prices cannot be downloaded reliably
Show answer

B. Prices trend and their variance grows (a unit root); differencing the log-price into returns removes that root and gives a stationary series whose mean, variance, and ACF are stable enough to model. Returns are not always normal (A) — Lecture 3 showed fat tails. They can carry small autocorrelation (C). And data availability (D) has nothing to do with it.
