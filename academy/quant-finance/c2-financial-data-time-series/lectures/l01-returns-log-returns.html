Prices, Returns & Log-Returns

A price tells you where an asset is; a return tells you how it got there — and choosing the right kind of return decides whether your math adds up or quietly breaks.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why quants analyze returns rather than raw prices, and define gross vs. net returns.
  • Compute the simple (arithmetic) return and the log (continuously-compounded) return from a price series.
  • Show why log-returns add across time while simple returns multiply, and why log-returns are roughly symmetric.
  • Aggregate returns over time and annualize a daily return correctly.
  • Load a price series and compute both return types in pandas with yfinance.

1.1From Prices to Returns

A market hands you a price series: a sequence of numbers P0, P1, P2, … recorded at regular times — a closing price each day, say. The price is the raw observable, but it is a poor object to model directly. Two assets at $5 and $500 are not comparable in dollars, prices drift upward over decades so their statistics keep shifting, and what an investor actually earns is the change, not the level.

So we work with returns: the fractional change in value over one period. The simple (or arithmetic) return from time t−1 to time t is the gain divided by what you started with:

Rt = (Pt − Pt−1) / Pt−1 = Pt / Pt−1 − 1If a stock goes from $100 to $105, R = 5/100 = 0.05 = 5%. This is the net return.

It's useful to name two flavors. The net return Rt is the percentage gain (e.g. +5%). The gross return is one plus the net, 1 + Rt = Pt/Pt−1 — the multiplier that turns last period's wealth into this period's (1.05, here). Gross returns are the natural objects when you compound, because wealth multiplies; net returns are what you quote to a client.

AnalogyThink of a price as the odometer of a car and a return as the speedometer. The odometer just keeps climbing and tells you little about how the trip is going right now; the speedometer is bounded, comparable trip-to-trip, and is what you actually react to. Quants drive by the speedometer.

Returns make assets comparable (a 2% day means the same thing for a $5 stock and a $500 stock), and — crucially for the rest of this course — they are far closer to stationary: their statistical character stays roughly constant over time, which is the precondition for almost every time-series model we'll build.

1.2Log-Returns: the Quant's Default

Alongside the simple return sits the log-return (also called the continuously-compounded return). It is the natural logarithm of the gross return:

rt = ln(Pt / Pt−1) = ln Pt − ln Pt−1 = ln(1 + Rt)Lower-case r for log-return, upper-case R for simple return — a convention we keep all course.

Why introduce a second definition at all? Because the logarithm converts the awkward multiplicative world of compounding into a tidy additive one. The connection to continuous compounding is exact: if you compound a return continuously at rate r over one period, your money grows by er, so the gross return is er and the log of it is just r. The log-return is the rate that, compounded continuously, reproduces the observed price move.

For the small moves typical of daily data, the two returns are nearly identical. The Taylor expansion of ln(1+R) explains why:

r = ln(1 + R) ≈ R − R²/2 + R³/3 − …For R = 0.01 (a 1% day), r ≈ 0.00995 — they differ in the fourth decimal. The gap only matters for large moves.

Because r ≈ R for small R, you lose almost nothing by switching to log-returns day to day — but you gain two properties (time-additivity and symmetry) that make every downstream calculation cleaner. That trade is why log-returns are the quant's default.

Common mistakeLog-returns are not "percentages" you can read off naively. A log-return of 0.05 corresponds to a simple return of e0.05 − 1 ≈ 5.13%, not 5%. The two coincide only in the small-R limit; never report a log-return to a client as if it were the percentage gain.

1.3Why Log-Returns Add — and Are Symmetric

Here is the property that earns log-returns their place. Suppose you hold an asset for two periods, with simple returns R1 then R2. Your wealth multiplies:

P2 / P0 = (P1/P0)(P2/P1) = (1 + R1)(1 + R2)Two-period gross return is the product of one-period gross returns — multiplicative, and ugly to sum over.

Take the logarithm of both sides and the product becomes a sum:

r0→2 = ln(P2/P0) = r1 + r2The total log-return over any span is just the sum of the single-period log-returns. This generalizes to n periods.

This time-additivity is enormous. To get a stock's return over a quarter you simply add its daily log-returns; the mean and variance of a multi-day return follow from adding daily means and (for independent days) variances — the same √n scaling you met in Course 1. Try that with simple returns and you must multiply gross factors, which doesn't play nicely with sums, expectations, or linear regression.

The second gift is symmetry. A simple return is bounded below by −100% (you can't lose more than everything) but unbounded above, so its distribution is skewed. A +50% gain followed by a −50% loss does not get you back to even: 1.5 × 0.5 = 0.75, a 25% loss. In log space the picture is symmetric: a log-return of +x exactly undoes a log-return of −x, because ln is the mirror of exp. That makes log-returns far more plausibly modeled by a symmetric bell curve.

AnalogyLog-returns are like decibels or musical octaves: a quantity built so that ratios turn into distances you can add. Going up an octave then down an octave lands you exactly where you started — just as +x then −x in log-return space returns you to the original price. Multiplicative reality, additive bookkeeping.

1.4Time-Aggregation & Annualizing

Because log-returns add, aggregating them over a window is trivial: the n-period log-return is the sum of the single-period log-returns, and its mean over a horizon of n periods is n times the per-period mean. If daily log-returns are independent with mean μ and variance σ², then over n trading days:

E[r1→n] = n·μ     Var(r1→n) = n·σ²     SD(r1→n) = σ·√nMean scales with time n; volatility scales with √n — the "square-root-of-time" rule.

Annualizing is the most common application. With about 252 trading days in a year, you scale a daily mean log-return by 252 and the daily volatility by √252 ≈ 15.87:

μannual = 252·μdaily     σannual = σdaily·√252A daily σ of 1% annualizes to ≈ 15.9% — the headline volatility number quoted everywhere.

To turn an annualized log-return back into the actual factor your money grows by, exponentiate: a year's gross return is eμ_annual, and the simple annual return is eμ_annual − 1. This is the clean round-trip that simple returns can't offer, because you'd have to compound 252 daily gross factors instead of adding 252 logs.

Common mistakeVolatility scales with √time, not time. Multiplying a daily σ by 252 (instead of √252) overstates annual risk by a factor of ~16. The √t rule also assumes returns are roughly independent across days; if they're autocorrelated (a theme later this course), the naive √n scaling is wrong.
✦ Worked Example — five days of a stock

A stock closes at these prices on five consecutive days: 100, 102, 101, 105, 104. Compute the daily simple and log-returns, verify additivity, and annualize.

  1. Daily simple returns Rt = Pt/Pt−1 − 1:   102/100−1 = +2.000%; 101/102−1 = −0.980%; 105/101−1 = +3.960%; 104/105−1 = −0.952%.
  2. Daily log-returns rt = ln(Pt/Pt−1):   ln(1.02) = +1.980%; ln(0.9902) = −0.985%; ln(1.0396) = +3.883%; ln(0.9905) = −0.957%. (Each r is close to its R, slightly below for gains.)
  3. Sum the log-returns: 0.01980 − 0.00985 + 0.03883 − 0.00957 = 0.03922.
  4. Check against the direct total: ln(104/100) = ln(1.04) = 0.03922. The daily logs add exactly to the whole-period log-return. ✓
  5. Total simple return: e0.03922 − 1 = 1.04 − 1 = +4.00% over the four days — matching 104/100 directly.
  6. Annualize the volatility: the standard deviation of the four daily log-returns is σ ≈ 0.0219 (2.19%/day). Annualized: 0.0219·√252 ≈ 0.348 = 34.8% — a very volatile stock (tiny five-day sample, so treat as illustrative).

🛠️ Hands-On: prices → returns in pandas

This course's free stack is pandas + yfinance + statsmodels (with NumPy and Matplotlib). Week 1 setup: install it once below, then reuse it every lecture. We'll pull a real price series and compute both return types.

⬇ Install the C2 stack (one-time, Week 1 setup)

Get Python 3 from python.org (macOS/Windows) or your package manager. Then create an isolated environment and install the data stack:

python3 -m venv quant-env
# activate it:
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux
quant-env\Scripts\activate         # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install pandas yfinance statsmodels numpy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import pandas, yfinance, statsmodels; print('ok')" — printing ok means you're set. Launch notebooks anytime with jupyter lab. (yfinance downloads free public market data; no API key needed.)

# returns_demo.py — C2 Lecture 1
import numpy as np
import pandas as pd
import yfinance as yf

# download ~1 year of daily prices for a broad market ETF
px = yf.download("SPY", start="2023-01-01", end="2024-01-01")["Close"]

# simple (arithmetic) returns: pct_change is P_t / P_{t-1} - 1
R = px.pct_change().dropna()

# log-returns: difference of the log price series
r = np.log(px).diff().dropna()

print("mean daily log-return :", r.mean())            # ~ tiny, e.g. 0.0008
print("daily volatility (std):", r.std())             # ~ 0.008  (0.8%/day)
print("annualized vol        :", r.std() * np.sqrt(252))   # ~ 0.13  (13%)
print("max |R - r| gap       :", float((R - r).abs().max()))  # small, grows with big moves

# additivity check: summed daily logs == total log-return
print("sum of log-returns    :", r.sum())
print("direct ln(P_end/P_0)  :", np.log(px.iloc[-1] / px.iloc[0]))

The last two prints should match to machine precision — that's time-additivity in code. Note pct_change() gives simple returns and np.log(px).diff() gives log-returns; the gap between them is tiny day to day but widens on the largest moves, exactly as the Taylor expansion predicted.

🔗 Where this is heading

Returns are the raw material for everything that follows. Next week you'll automate pulling and cleaning real price data with pandas and yfinance. From there the course models the statistics of these log-return series — stationarity and autocorrelation (the ACF/PACF), then ARMA and GARCH models that forecast the mean and the time-varying volatility you annualized here. The reason quants model log-returns rather than prices threads through all of it: log-returns add over time, are roughly symmetric and stationary, and feed directly into the random-walk and Brownian-motion models behind Black–Scholes pricing in Course 3 — where the continuously-compounded return you met today becomes the drift of the price process.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A stock rises from $80 to $84 in one day. Its simple (net) return is…
A. 4%
B. 5%
C. 1.05
D. $4
Show answer

B. R = (84 − 80)/80 = 4/80 = 0.05 = 5%. Choice C (1.05) is the gross return Pt/Pt−1, not the net return. D is the dollar change, not a return. A (4%) wrongly divides the $4 gain by the new price 84 instead of the starting price 80 — return is always change over the starting value.

Q2. Why do quants usually model log-returns instead of simple returns?
A. Log-returns are always larger, so profits look better
B. Log-returns add across time and are roughly symmetric, making the math and modeling cleaner
C. Simple returns cannot be computed from daily prices
D. Log-returns remove all risk from the data
Show answer

B. The logarithm turns multiplicative compounding into addition (time-additivity) and makes the distribution roughly symmetric. A is false — for gains the log-return is slightly smaller than the simple return. C is false (pct_change computes them easily). D is nonsense — no transformation removes risk.

Q3. Daily log-returns have mean μ and standard deviation σ and are independent. Over n trading days, the standard deviation of the total log-return is…
A. n·σ
B. σ / √n
C. σ
D. σ·√n
Show answer

D. Variances of independent terms add, so Var(total) = n·σ² and SD = σ·√n — the square-root-of-time rule. A (n·σ) overstates risk by scaling linearly. B (σ/√n) is the shrinking spread of an average, not a sum. C ignores aggregation entirely.

Q4. A daily log-return volatility of 1% annualizes (252 trading days) to approximately…
A. 252%
B. 1%
C. 16%
D. 50%
Show answer

C. σannual = σdaily·√252 = 1% × 15.87 ≈ 15.9% ≈ 16%. A wrongly multiplies by 252 (linear scaling — that's the classic error). B forgets to scale at all. D has no basis.

Q5. An asset gains 50% one period then loses 50% the next. Your cumulative simple return over the two periods is…
A. −25% (you end below where you started)
B. 0% (the gain and loss cancel)
C. +25%
D. +100%
Show answer

A. Gross returns multiply: 1.50 × 0.50 = 0.75, a cumulative −25%. B is the tempting trap — simple returns do not add, so +50% and −50% don't cancel. This asymmetry is exactly why log-returns (where +x and −x truly cancel) are preferred. C and D have the wrong sign or magnitude.

✓ What you learned

  • Why quants model returns, not prices — returns are comparable across assets and roughly stationary; gross = 1 + net.
  • The simple return R = Pt/Pt−1 − 1 and the log-return r = ln(1+R), the continuously-compounded rate, with r ≈ R for small moves.
  • Log-returns add across time (logs of products are sums) and are roughly symmetric, unlike skewed, multiplicative simple returns.
  • Time-aggregation: mean scales with n, volatility with √n — annualize a daily σ by √252 ≈ 15.87.
  • Computed both return types from a real price series in pandas + yfinance and verified additivity in code.
