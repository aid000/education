Moving Averages & Smoothing

Price is noise wrapped around a signal — a moving average is the simplest lens that lets you see the trend through the static, at the cost of always looking slightly into the past.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define a simple moving average (SMA) and explain why averaging a window reduces noise.
  • Define an exponentially-weighted moving average (EWMA) and connect its decay factor to an effective window length.
  • Articulate the smoothing-vs-lag tradeoff: longer windows are smoother but react later.
  • Compute rolling and exponential averages in pandas with .rolling() and .ewm().
  • Use moving averages as trend filters and build a moving-average crossover signal — a first taste of algorithmic trading.

5.1Signal, Noise & the Idea of Smoothing

A price series wiggles. Some of that wiggle is signal — a genuine drift in value that persists — and some is noise: bid-ask bounce, one-off orders, microstructure jitter that means nothing tomorrow. Last week (Lecture 4) you learned that returns are nearly uncorrelated white noise, while prices carry a slow-moving level. Smoothing is any operation that suppresses the fast noise so the slow level becomes visible.

The cleanest way to suppress noise is to average. If a price each day is its true level plus an independent zero-mean error of standard deviation σ, then averaging w of those days leaves the true level roughly intact while shrinking the error to σ/√w — the same √n law from the Central Limit Theorem (Course 1, Week 2). Average more days and the line gets calmer.

pricet = levelt + noiset,   noise ~ mean 0, sd σAveraging w independent noise terms cuts their standard deviation from σ to σ/√w — smoothing buys noise reduction.

That single trade is the whole story of this lecture: more averaging = less noise, but also slower to notice when the level genuinely changes. Everything below is a way of choosing how to average.

5.2The Simple Moving Average (SMA)

The simple moving average of window length w is the unweighted mean of the most recent w observations. At time t you look back w steps and average:

SMAt(w) = (1/w) · Σi=0w−1 Pt−i = (Pt + Pt−1 + … + Pt−w+1) / wEvery one of the w points gets the identical weight 1/w; points older than w steps get weight 0.

As t advances by one day, the window slides: the newest price enters and the oldest drops out. This is why it is called a moving (or rolling) average. Two structural facts matter for trading. First, the SMA is causal — it uses only past and present data, never the future, so you could actually have computed it in real time (critical when you later backtest a strategy). Second, it has a built-in lag: the average of the last w days "sits" at roughly the center of that window, about (w−1)/2 days behind the latest price.

AnalogyAn SMA is a convoy of w cars moving down a road, and you report the position of the middle car. When the lead car suddenly speeds up, the reported position barely moves at first — the whole convoy has to catch up. A long convoy (large w) is steadier but reacts to the lead car much later. That delay is the lag.

Because every point inside the window has equal weight and everything outside has zero weight, the SMA reacts in a jerky way: a single big day stays in the average for exactly w days and then abruptly falls out, sometimes causing a visible kink. The EWMA fixes that.

5.3The Exponentially-Weighted Moving Average (EWMA)

The EWMA keeps all the history but lets old data fade smoothly instead of dropping off a cliff. Pick a smoothing factor α between 0 and 1. The new average is a blend of today's price and yesterday's average:

EWMAt = α·Pt + (1 − α)·EWMAt−1A one-line recursion: weight α on the fresh price, weight (1−α) on everything that came before.

Unrolling that recursion shows the weights decay geometrically into the past: today gets α, yesterday gets α(1−α), the day before α(1−α)², and so on. The weights never reach zero but shrink fast, and they sum to 1.

EWMAt = α · Σi=0∞ (1−α)i · Pt−iGeometrically decaying weights: recent data dominates, ancient data is almost forgotten.

A large α (close to 1) makes the EWMA hug the latest price — fast, but noisy. A small α makes it sluggish and smooth. It is convenient to translate α into an effective window or "span" so you can compare it to an SMA. pandas uses the span convention:

α = 2 / (span + 1)  ⟺   span = 2/α − 1An EWMA with span N has roughly the same lag as an SMA of window N. A span-20 EWMA ≈ a 20-day SMA in responsiveness.

The payoffs: the EWMA updates in O(1) time and O(1) memory (no window to store), it has no abrupt drop-off artifact, and it is the exact engine behind RiskMetrics-style volatility estimates you will meet in Week 9. It is the workhorse smoother of quant finance.

Common mistakeDon't confuse the two "knobs." For an SMA, a bigger window w means more smoothing. For an EWMA, a bigger α means LESS smoothing (it tracks faster), while a bigger span means more smoothing. Mixing up α and span — or assuming "bigger parameter = smoother" for both — is the classic slip. Sanity check: α → 1 reduces the EWMA to just the latest price, i.e. no smoothing at all.

5.4The Smoothing–Lag Tradeoff & Crossover Signals

Every smoother faces the same dilemma. Smooth more and the line is calm but lags further behind reality, so you spot a turn late. Smooth less and you react quickly but chase noise, generating false alarms. There is no free lunch: responsiveness and smoothness pull in opposite directions.

long window → low noise, high lag   |   short window → high noise, low lag

Traders exploit this tension deliberately with a crossover rule. Run two SMAs — a fast one (short window, low lag) and a slow one (long window, smooth). When the fast line crosses above the slow line, recent prices have pulled ahead of the longer-run level: a candidate buy / go-long signal. When it crosses below, a sell / go-short signal. The slow line acts as the trend; the fast line as the trigger.

Why crossovers are trend-following

A crossover fires only after a move is already underway, so it is a momentum or trend-following tool, not a top/bottom picker. It earns money in sustained trends and bleeds money in choppy, sideways markets where the fast line whips back and forth across the slow one — producing many small losing "whipsaw" trades. Choosing the two windows is choosing where you sit on the smoothing-lag curve.

Common mistakeThe deadliest backtesting error here is look-ahead bias: acting on a crossover at the same bar's close that you used to detect it. In reality you only know today's average after today closes, so you can trade no earlier than the next bar. Shift signals forward by one period before computing returns, or your backtest will look brilliant and your live account will not.
✦ Worked Example — SMA vs EWMA on seven closes

A stock's daily closes (in $) for seven days are: 100, 102, 101, 105, 104, 108, 107. Compute the 3-day SMA and a span-3 EWMA at day 7, and compare their lag.

  1. 3-day SMA at day 7: average the last three closes (104, 108, 107): SMA = (104 + 108 + 107)/3 = 319/3 ≈ $106.33.
  2. EWMA factor: span = 3 ⟹ α = 2/(3+1) = 0.5. So each day the EWMA is half the new close plus half the prior EWMA.
  3. Seed & iterate (seed EWMA₁ = first close = 100): EWMA₂ = .5·102 + .5·100 = 101.00; EWMA₃ = .5·101 + .5·101.00 = 101.00; EWMA₄ = .5·105 + .5·101.00 = 103.00; EWMA₅ = .5·104 + .5·103.00 = 103.50; EWMA₆ = .5·108 + .5·103.50 = 105.75; EWMA₇ = .5·107 + .5·105.75 = $106.38.
  4. Compare: the SMA (106.33) and EWMA (106.38) land within a nickel of each other — by design, since span 3 matches window 3 in responsiveness. Both sit below the latest close of 107, visibly lagging the uptrend.
  5. Read the lag: price climbed from 100 to 107 (+7), but the smoothed lines only reached ≈106.3 — still "catching up" to a trend that is itself still rising. That ~0.7 gap below the latest close is the lag.

🛠️ Hands-On: rolling & exponential averages with a crossover

We reuse the Week-1/2 stack (pandas + yfinance + NumPy + Matplotlib — the one-time install block lives in Course 1, Lecture 1 and the data-loading recipe in this course's Week 1). Below: pull real daily closes, compute an SMA and an EWMA, and build a no-look-ahead crossover signal.

# moving_averages.py — C2 Lecture 5
import yfinance as yf

# 1) daily closes (any liquid ticker; uses the free yfinance loader from Week 1)
px = yf.download("SPY", start="2018-01-01", end="2023-01-01")["Close"]

# 2) two smoothers
sma_fast = px.rolling(window=20).mean()           # 20-day simple MA (fast)
sma_slow = px.rolling(window=100).mean()          # 100-day simple MA (slow)
ewma     = px.ewm(span=20, adjust=False).mean()   # span-20 EWMA, exact recursion

# 3) crossover signal: 1 when fast above slow, else 0 (long-only)
raw_signal = (sma_fast > sma_slow).astype(int)

# 4) AVOID look-ahead: trade on the NEXT bar after the signal appears
position = raw_signal.shift(1).fillna(0)

# 5) strategy return = yesterday's position * today's return
ret       = px.pct_change()
strat_ret = position * ret
print("buy&hold total :", (1 + ret).prod().item() - 1)
print("crossover total:", (1 + strat_ret).prod().item() - 1)

Note the two defenses: adjust=False gives the exact recursion EWMAₜ = α·Pₜ + (1−α)·EWMAₜ₋₁ from §5.3, and .shift(1) enforces that you can only trade after a signal closes. Drop the shift and the "strategy" will print an impossibly good number — that is look-ahead bias, not alpha. Swap span=20 for span=5, or use windows of 50/200, and watch the smoothness, lag, and trade count all change.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In a simple moving average of window w, how much weight does each of the w most recent observations receive?
A. Exponentially decaying weights summing to 1
B. An equal weight of 1/w each
C. More weight on the oldest point
D. A weight of 1 on the newest point only
Show answer

B. The SMA is the unweighted mean of the window, so every one of the w points gets exactly 1/w and everything older gets 0. Exponentially decaying weights (A) describe the EWMA, not the SMA. Nothing favors the oldest point (C), and weighting only the newest (D) would just return the latest price — no averaging at all.

Q2. For the EWMA recursion EWMAₜ = α·Pₜ + (1−α)·EWMAₜ₋₁, what happens as α approaches 1?
A. The EWMA becomes perfectly smooth and constant
B. Older data receives more weight
C. The EWMA collapses to just the latest price (no smoothing)
D. The recursion becomes undefined
Show answer

C. With α = 1 the term (1−α)·EWMAₜ₋₁ vanishes and EWMAₜ = Pₜ — the average just tracks today's price, the least possible smoothing. Maximum smoothing happens at small α, not large (A). Larger α puts more weight on recent data, not old (B). The recursion is perfectly well-defined for any α in [0,1] (D).

Q3. You lengthen a moving average's window from 20 days to 200 days. The most accurate description of the effect is…
A. smoother line, but it reacts to turning points later
B. noisier line that reacts faster
C. smoother line that also reacts faster
D. no change in either smoothness or lag
Show answer

A. A longer window averages more noise away (smoother) but pushes the average's center further into the past (more lag), so it turns later. You cannot get smoother and faster at once (C) — that is exactly the tradeoff this lecture is about. A longer window is not noisier (B), and it certainly changes both properties, so D is wrong.

Q4. A pandas EWMA is created with .ewm(span=19). Using α = 2/(span+1), the smoothing factor α is…
A. 0.05
B. 0.19
C. 0.50
D. 0.10
Show answer

D. α = 2/(19+1) = 2/20 = 0.10. Choice A (0.05) would need span 39; C (0.50) corresponds to span 3; B just confuses α with the span number itself. The span-to-α map is the standard pandas convention you'll use constantly.

Q5. In a fast/slow moving-average crossover backtest, why must you act on a signal only on the next bar rather than the same bar?
A. Because moving averages cannot be computed in real time
B. Because the EWMA is more accurate than the SMA
C. To reduce the number of trades and save on commissions
D. To avoid look-ahead bias — a close-based average is only known after the close
Show answer

D. A close-based average is only known once the bar closes, so trading at that same close uses information you wouldn't actually have — look-ahead bias that inflates backtest results. Moving averages can be computed in real time (A is false). SMA-vs-EWMA accuracy (B) is irrelevant to timing, and while shifting may change trade count, commissions (C) are not the reason for the shift.

✓ What you learned

  • Smoothing averages out zero-mean noise, shrinking it like σ/√w to reveal the slow-moving level inside a price series.
  • The simple moving average weights the last w points equally (1/w each), is causal, and lags the price by about (w−1)/2.
  • The EWMA blends today's price with yesterday's average via α, giving geometrically decaying weights; α = 2/(span+1) links it to an SMA window.
  • The smoothing–lag tradeoff: longer windows / smaller α are smoother but react later — responsiveness and calmness pull opposite ways.
  • A fast/slow crossover is a trend-following signal; computed in pandas with .rolling()/.ewm() and a .shift(1) to defeat look-ahead bias.
