Market-Making: Spread, Inventory & Adverse Selection

A market maker rents out liquidity for a sliver of the spread — and spends every second praying the trader hitting their quote knows less than they do.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the market maker's core business: quoting a two-sided market and earning the bid–ask spread.
  • Describe inventory risk — the danger of being left holding a one-sided position after fills.
  • Describe adverse selection — the cost of trading against better-informed counterparties.
  • Decompose a market maker's P&L into spread capture minus inventory and adverse-selection costs.
  • Argue from first principles why the quoted spread must be wide enough to cover both risks.

8.1The Business: Quote Both Sides, Earn the Spread

A market maker (MM) is a trader who continuously posts both a price to buy (the bid) and a price to sell (the ask) for the same asset, and stands ready to trade either side. The MM does not try to predict where the price is going. Instead they sell the most basic service a market needs: immediacy. Anyone who wants to trade right now can, because the MM is always there on the other side.

The compensation for providing that service is the bid–ask spread — the gap between the ask and the bid. If the MM buys at the bid and later sells at the ask, the difference is pure profit:

spread S = ask − bid     half-spread = S / 2   relative to the midThe mid-price m = (ask + bid) / 2 is the MM's best guess of fair value; the quotes sit a half-spread on either side.

The dream is a "round trip": buy one unit at the bid, sell one unit at the ask, pocket the full spread S, and end the day flat (zero position). Do this thousands of times a day on a high-volume name and tiny spreads compound into a real business. The catch — the entire rest of this lecture — is that the round trip rarely closes so cleanly, and two specific risks eat into that spread.

AnalogyA market maker is a currency kiosk at an airport. It posts a price to buy your dollars and a (worse) price to sell you euros; it makes the spread on travelers who need cash now and doesn't care which way the exchange rate drifts — as long as roughly equal numbers of people buy and sell. Its nightmare is a day when everyone only buys euros: it ends up short euros (inventory risk), right before the euro jumps (adverse selection).

8.2Inventory Risk: Stuck Holding a Position

Fills do not arrive in tidy buy–sell pairs. Over any short window the MM might get hit on the bid five times and lifted on the ask only once, leaving them long four units they never wanted. The leftover position is called inventory, and holding it exposes the MM to the very price risk they were trying to avoid.

If the MM holds inventory q (signed: positive = long, negative = short) and the mid-price moves by Δm before they can offload it, the mark-to-market P&L on that inventory is:

inventory P&L = q · ΔmA long book (q > 0) profits when the mid rises and bleeds when it falls; a short book is the mirror image. Either way it is undesired, undirectional exposure.

Because the MM has no view on direction, this term is pure noise with zero expected value but real variance. The risk scales with the size of the inventory and the volatility of the asset. Over a holding time t, the standard deviation of the price move grows like σ√t (the square-root-of-time law from the stochastic-calculus weeks), so the inventory risk is roughly:

inventory risk ≈ |q| · σ · √tThe longer you sit on a position and the more volatile the name, the more it can hurt. This is why MMs frantically try to flatten.

The defense is skewing quotes. If the MM is uncomfortably long, they shade both quotes down — lowering the ask to sell off inventory faster and lowering the bid to discourage buying more. They sacrifice a little spread to pull q back toward zero. Managing this trade-off optimally is exactly the subject of Week 9.

Common mistakeInventory risk is not a directional bet gone wrong — it has zero expected return by assumption. The danger is its variance: a large accidental position can produce a catastrophic loss (or gain) from a move the MM never had a view on. Confusing "expected P&L is zero" with "this is harmless" is how desks blow up in a fast market.

8.3Adverse Selection: Trading Against the Informed

Here is the subtler, deadlier risk. The MM quotes a fixed bid and ask, but not all counterparties are equal. Some are uninformed ("noise" traders) trading for liquidity reasons — they're as likely to buy as sell, and the MM happily makes the spread off them. Others are informed: they know something about where the price is headed, and they only trade when the MM's quote is stale in their favor.

The result is a brutal asymmetry. When good news is about to push the price up, informed traders lift the MM's ask just before it rises — the MM sells low and watches the asset climb. When bad news looms, they hit the MM's bid just before it drops — the MM buys high and watches it fall. This selective trading is adverse selection: the trades you get filled on are systematically the ones you'd rather not have.

expected loss to informed flow = π · (expected adverse move per informed trade)π = probability a given counterparty is informed. The MM loses, on average, every time the informed side trades.

The cruel part: a market maker cannot tell an informed trade from an uninformed one at the moment of execution. They only learn they were "picked off" afterward, when the price moves against the position the fill just gave them. The information is revealed by the trade itself — a fill is a small piece of bad news about fair value.

AnalogyAdverse selection is the used-car "lemons" problem in reverse. A dealer offering one fixed buy price finds that the people most eager to sell are the ones whose cars are secretly junk. The MM offering one fixed quote finds that the eager counterparties are the ones who know the quote is wrong. To survive, the dealer (and the MM) must widen their margin to cover the hidden lemons.

8.4P&L Decomposition: Why the Spread Must Be Wide Enough

Put the pieces together. Over a trading session, decompose the MM's profit into three additive terms:

MM P&L = (spread capture) − (adverse-selection cost) − (inventory cost)The first term is the gross edge; the next two are the leaks. The desk is profitable only when the first beats the sum of the other two.

Walk through each term. Spread capture is the half-spread earned on every fill: if you trade volume V at a half-spread of S/2, gross edge is V · (S/2). Adverse-selection cost is the share of those fills that came from informed traders who picked you off — proportional to π and the size of the adverse move. Inventory cost is a risk charge for the undesired positions you accumulate, scaling with σ and how long you hold.

Now the central insight. The MM chooses the spread S. Set it too tight and the spread capture per trade is too small to cover the inevitable losses to informed flow and the volatility of inventory — the desk bleeds. Set it too wide and uninformed traders go elsewhere, volume V collapses, and the desk earns nothing. The optimal spread balances these:

half-spread S/2 ≳ adverse-selection cost per trade + inventory risk premium per tradeThe spread is not arbitrary markup — it is the minimum compensation that makes providing liquidity break even against both risks.

This is the single most important takeaway of the lecture: the bid–ask spread is the price of two risks, not a fee. Wider spreads appear precisely where adverse selection and volatility are highest — illiquid names, the moments around news, the open and the close. When you see a spread blow out, the market is telling you that the cost of being the liquidity provider just went up.

Common mistakeDo not read a wide spread as "the market maker is greedy." A wide spread usually signals high adverse-selection or inventory risk — thin volume, an imminent announcement, or extreme volatility. Quoting a tight spread into that environment is not generous, it is a fast way to get picked off and accumulate toxic inventory.
✦ Worked Example — does this quote make money?

An MM quotes a stock with a $0.10 spread (bid $49.95, ask $50.05, mid $50.00). On a typical day they trade 10,000 shares. Suppose 20% of the volume comes from informed traders, and each informed fill costs the MM an average adverse move of $0.18. Inventory risk costs them an estimated $90 for the day. Is the desk profitable?

  1. Half-spread captured per share: S/2 = $0.10 / 2 = $0.05. This is the gross edge on every share traded.
  2. Gross spread capture: 10,000 shares × $0.05 = $500.
  3. Informed volume: π = 20%, so 0.20 × 10,000 = 2,000 shares come from informed traders.
  4. Adverse-selection cost: 2,000 shares × $0.18 adverse move = $360. (The half-spread earned on these is already in the $500; the $0.18 is the extra loss beyond it.)
  5. Inventory cost: given as $90 for the day.
  6. Net P&L: $500 − $360 − $90 = +$50. Thinly profitable — but notice how fragile it is.
  7. Stress it: if informed share rises to 30% (3,000 shares), adverse cost becomes 3,000 × $0.18 = $540, and net P&L = $500 − $540 − $90 = −$130. To stay profitable the MM must widen the spread — e.g. a $0.16 spread captures $0.08 × 10,000 = $800, comfortably covering the higher toxicity.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate a market-making session

Reusing the course stack — NumPy + Matplotlib (the Week-1/2 setup; the install block lives in C1 Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We'll run a toy session: a noisy mid-price, a stream of fills split into uninformed and informed flow, and a running P&L that splits cleanly into the three terms from §8.4.

# mm_session.py — C5 Lecture 8
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
n_steps   = 5_000        # discrete time steps in the session
half_sp   = 0.05         # half-spread we quote ($0.05 -> $0.10 spread)
sigma     = 0.02         # per-step mid volatility ($)
p_trade   = 0.30         # prob a fill arrives this step
p_inform  = 0.20         # prob a given fill is informed

mid = 50.0
q   = 0                  # signed inventory (shares)
spread_pnl = 0.0         # gross half-spread captured
adverse    = 0.0         # losses to informed flow

for _ in range(n_steps):
    drift = 0.0
    if rng.random() < p_trade:
        informed = rng.random() < p_inform
        if informed:
            # informed: trades in the direction the mid is about to move
            side = rng.choice([-1, 1])         # -1 = they sell to us (we go long)
            drift = -side * abs(rng.normal(0.09, 0.03))  # mid moves against us
        else:
            side = rng.choice([-1, 1])         # uninformed: symmetric
        q += -side                              # we take the opposite side of their trade
        spread_pnl += half_sp                   # we earn the half-spread on the fill
        if informed:
            adverse += abs(drift)               # extra loss on the picked-off fill
    # mid evolves: random noise + any informed drift
    old_mid = mid
    mid += drift + rng.normal(0, sigma)
    # inventory is marked to the new mid
    inv_pnl_step = q * (mid - old_mid)
    if _ == 0:
        inventory_pnl = 0.0
    inventory_pnl = locals().get('inventory_pnl', 0.0) + inv_pnl_step

total = spread_pnl - adverse + inventory_pnl
print(f"gross spread capture : ${spread_pnl:8.2f}")
print(f"adverse-selection cost: -${adverse:8.2f}")
print(f"inventory P&L (signed): ${inventory_pnl:8.2f}")
print(f"ending inventory      : {q:+d} shares")
print(f"TOTAL P&L             : ${total:8.2f}")

Run it, then sweep the knobs. Raise p_inform toward 0.5 and watch adverse-selection swamp the spread capture — the total goes negative. Push sigma up and the inventory P&L term gets wild and lossy whenever q drifts away from zero. Then try widening half_sp to rescue profitability: you'll feel, in code, exactly why the spread must compensate for both risks.

Q1. What is the fundamental service a market maker sells, and how are they paid for it?
A. Price prediction; paid by a management fee
B. Immediacy / liquidity; paid by the bid–ask spread
C. Leverage; paid by interest on margin
D. Insurance against default; paid by a premium
Show answer

B. The MM provides immediacy — someone always on the other side — and earns the spread between their bid and ask. They explicitly do not predict direction (A); they are not lending leverage (C) or writing default protection (D).

Q2. A market maker is accidentally left long 500 shares of a volatile stock. The danger of this inventory is best described as…
A. a positive expected return that distorts the spread
B. a guaranteed loss equal to the half-spread
C. undirectional exposure with zero expected return but real variance
D. nothing, because inventory is always immediately hedged for free
Show answer

C. The MM has no directional view, so the position's expected return is zero — but its variance (≈ |q|·σ·√t) can produce a large loss or gain. It is not a positive-edge bet (A), not a guaranteed loss (B), and hedging is neither free nor instant (D).

Q3. Adverse selection refers to the cost arising because…
A. the exchange charges higher fees to liquidity providers
B. informed traders selectively trade against stale quotes, so fills are systematically unfavorable
C. the market maker's own orders move the price
D. spreads are always too narrow on liquid stocks
Show answer

B. Adverse selection is the asymmetry that the counterparties who trade against a fixed quote tend to be the informed ones who know it's mispriced. It is not an exchange fee (A), not market impact from the MM's own orders (C), and unrelated to spreads being "too narrow" as a rule (D).

Q4. A desk captures $700 in gross spread, loses $300 to informed flow, and carries a $150 inventory risk cost over a session. Its net P&L is…
A. +$250
B. +$700
C. +$1,150
D. −$250
Show answer

A. Net = spread capture − adverse-selection − inventory = 700 − 300 − 150 = +$250. $700 (B) ignores both cost terms; $1,150 (C) wrongly adds the costs; −$250 (D) flips a sign.

Q5. You observe a market maker suddenly widening its spread right before a scheduled earnings announcement. The best interpretation is…
A. the MM is being greedy because it has no competition
B. the MM expects the price to rise and is positioning long
C. a software bug, since spreads should never change intraday
D. the MM is pricing in higher adverse-selection and inventory risk around the news
Show answer

D. A spread is compensation for risk; around news both adverse selection (informed flow) and volatility (inventory risk) spike, so the break-even spread widens. It is not greed (A) — a directional view (B) contradicts the MM's role — and spreads change continuously with conditions (C).

✓ What you learned

  • A market maker quotes both a bid and an ask, selling immediacy and earning the bid–ask spread rather than predicting direction.
  • Inventory risk is undirectional exposure (≈ |q|·σ·√t) from accumulating a one-sided position — zero expected return, dangerous variance — defended by skewing quotes.
  • Adverse selection is the systematic cost of being picked off by informed traders who only trade when the quote is stale in their favor.
  • The MM's P&L decomposes as spread capture − adverse-selection cost − inventory cost; profit requires the first to beat the other two.
  • The spread is the price of those two risks: hump-shaped profit vs. spread means there is an optimal width S*, and wide spreads signal high risk, not greed.
