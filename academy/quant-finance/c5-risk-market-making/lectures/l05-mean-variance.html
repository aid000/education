Mean-Variance Portfolio Optimization

Markowitz turned "don't put all your eggs in one basket" into geometry — a curve that tells you the most return any level of risk can buy.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Express a portfolio's expected return and variance in terms of weights, the mean vector, and the covariance matrix.
  • Explain how diversification lowers variance — and why correlation, not just individual volatility, drives the benefit.
  • Trace the efficient frontier and locate the minimum-variance and tangency (maximum-Sharpe) portfolios on it.
  • Set up the mean-variance problem as a constrained quadratic program and solve it numerically with CVXPY.
  • Recognize the practical fragility of the optimizer and why estimation error makes raw weights dangerous.

5.1A Portfolio Is a Weighted Bet

You hold several assets. The fraction of your capital in each is a weight wi, and the weights sum to 1 (you've invested all your money). The portfolio's return is just the weighted average of the individual returns — so its expected return is the weighted average of the expected returns. Nothing surprising yet: doubling the weight on a high-return asset doubles its contribution.

μp = wTμ = Σi wi μiw is the vector of weights, μ the vector of each asset's expected return. Expected return is linear in the weights.

Risk is where it gets interesting. Variance does not add like return does, because assets move together. The variance of the portfolio depends on every pairwise covariance σij — how asset i and asset j tend to move in tandem. Collect all of these into the covariance matrix Σ, a symmetric n×n grid with variances σi2 on the diagonal and covariances off it. Then portfolio variance is a single tidy quadratic form:

σp2 = wTΣw = Σi Σj wi wj σijThe diagonal terms are each asset's own variance; the off-diagonal terms are the cross-effects. Return is linear; risk is quadratic.

That asymmetry — linear reward, quadratic risk — is the entire engine of the theory. Because the cross-terms can be negative when assets are anticorrelated, the combined risk of a portfolio can fall below the risk of any single holding. That free lunch has a name: diversification.

AnalogyThink of two market-makers covering opposite sides of a trade. On any given day one tends to win when the other loses. Held alone, each book swings wildly; held together, the swings partly cancel and the desk's daily P&L is far steadier — even though neither book got any safer on its own. The covariance, not the individual volatility, did the work.

5.2How Diversification Lowers Variance

Take the simplest case: two assets with equal volatility σ, split 50/50. The portfolio variance works out to

σp2 = ¼σ2 + ¼σ2 + 2·(½)(½)·ρσ2 = ½σ2(1 + ρ)ρ is the correlation between the two assets. The whole story lives in that (1 + ρ) factor.

Read off the three regimes. If the assets are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1), σp2 = σ2 — no benefit at all; you've just bought two copies of the same risk. If they are uncorrelated (ρ = 0), variance halves to ½σ2, so volatility drops by a factor of √2. And if they are perfectly anticorrelated (ρ = −1), the variance collapses to zero: a perfect hedge.

The lesson generalizes. Spreading capital across n assets with the same volatility and zero pairwise correlation drives the portfolio variance down like σ2/n — the same √n law from the Central Limit Theorem, now working in your favor. But real assets are positively correlated, so risk never falls to zero; it bottoms out at the systematic (undiversifiable) floor. Diversification kills the idiosyncratic risk and leaves the market risk standing.

Common mistakeDiversification is not about owning more names — it is about owning uncorrelated names. Twenty tech stocks that all crash together give you almost no more protection than one. What lowers variance is low (ideally negative) correlation, not the headcount of positions.

5.3The Efficient Frontier

Now plot every possible portfolio on a chart with risk (σp) on the horizontal axis and expected return (μp) on the vertical. As you vary the weights, the achievable points fill a curved region. Its left boundary — the bullet-shaped curve hugging the lowest-risk edge — is the efficient frontier: for each level of risk it shows the maximum attainable return. Anything inside the region is dominated; you could get more return for the same risk by moving to the boundary.

Two landmark portfolios live on it. The leftmost tip is the minimum-variance portfolio (MVP) — the single least-risky combination, found by minimizing wTΣw subject to the weights summing to one. It ignores expected returns entirely, which is part of its appeal: expected returns are notoriously hard to estimate, but covariances are comparatively stable.

MVP:  minimize  wTΣw   subject to  1Tw = 1Closed form: wMVP = Σ−11 / (1TΣ−11). No expected returns appear.

Introduce a risk-free asset earning rf and the picture sharpens. Draw a line from rf on the return axis tangent to the frontier. The point where it touches is the tangency portfolio — the risky mix with the highest Sharpe ratio, the slope (μp − rf)/σp of return earned per unit of risk. That tangent line, the capital allocation line, is the best risk-return trade-off available: every investor should hold the same tangency portfolio and simply dial their exposure up or down by mixing it with the risk-free asset.

Tangency:  maximize  (wTμ − rf) / √(wTΣw)   ⟹  w ∝ Σ−1(μ − rf1)The max-Sharpe weights are proportional to Σ−1 times the excess-return vector, then renormalized to sum to one.

5.4Solving It as an Optimization Problem

For two assets you can do the algebra by hand. For fifty, you reach for a solver. The mean-variance problem is a quadratic program: a quadratic objective (variance) with linear constraints (weights sum to one, perhaps no short-selling, perhaps a target return). Quadratic programs with a positive-semidefinite Σ are convex — they have a single global optimum that solvers find reliably and fast.

The most common formulation maximizes a risk-adjusted objective: expected return minus a risk-aversion penalty γ times variance. Sliding γ from 0 (return-hungry) to ∞ (risk-phobic) traces out the entire efficient frontier.

maximize  wTμ − γ·wTΣw   subject to  1Tw = 1,  w ≥ 0γ is the risk-aversion coefficient. The w ≥ 0 constraint forbids short positions; drop it to allow shorting.

The covariance matrix Σ is the workhorse of all of this — it must be symmetric and positive-semidefinite (no portfolio can have negative variance). When Σ is estimated from limited data it can be ill-conditioned or even non-invertible, which is why the closed-form formulas involving Σ−1 are numerically delicate and a robust solver is preferred in practice.

Common mistakeTreating the optimizer's output as truth. Mean-variance optimization is an "error maximizer": it piles weight onto whatever asset's expected return was overestimated by noise. Tiny changes in the input μ produce wildly different, often extreme, weights. Practitioners shrink the estimates, add constraints (caps, no-shorting), or lean on the minimum-variance portfolio precisely because it never touches the unreliable μ.
✦ Worked Example — the minimum-variance mix of two assets

Asset A has volatility σA = 20%, asset B has σB = 10%, and their correlation is ρ = 0.2. With short-selling forbidden, what split minimizes portfolio variance, and how much risk does it remove?

  1. Covariances. σA2 = 0.04, σB2 = 0.01, and σAB = ρσAσB = 0.2·0.20·0.10 = 0.004.
  2. The MVP weight formula (two assets): wA = (σB2 − σAB) / (σA2 + σB2 − 2σAB).
  3. Plug in: numerator = 0.01 − 0.004 = 0.006; denominator = 0.04 + 0.01 − 2·0.004 = 0.042. So wA = 0.006 / 0.042 ≈ 0.143, wB ≈ 0.857.
  4. Portfolio variance: σp2 = 0.1432·0.04 + 0.8572·0.01 + 2·0.143·0.857·0.004 ≈ 0.000817 + 0.007344 + 0.000980 ≈ 0.00914, so σp ≈ 9.56%.
  5. Interpret: the minimum-variance volatility (9.56%) is below even the safer asset B's 10%. Mixing in a little of the riskier A actually reduced total risk — because A's modest positive correlation still lets some of its swings offset B's. That is diversification turning a quadratic crank in your favor.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve the frontier with CVXPY

We move from hand-algebra to a real convex solver. CVXPY lets you write the optimization in math-like syntax and hands it to a backend solver — perfect for the quadratic program above. It is free and pip-installable; the broader NumPy/Matplotlib stack is the Week-1/2 setup.

⬇ New tool this week: CVXPY

Activate your Week-1 quant-env, then add the one new package:

pip install cvxpy           # convex-optimization modeling layer (ships with a free solver)
# frontier.py — Lecture 5: minimum-variance & max-Sharpe via CVXPY
import numpy as np
import cvxpy as cp

# expected annual returns and covariance matrix for 3 assets
mu    = np.array([0.10, 0.07, 0.04])
Sigma = np.array([[0.040, 0.004, 0.002],
                  [0.004, 0.010, 0.001],
                  [0.002, 0.001, 0.009]])
rf = 0.02

w = cp.Variable(3)
ret  = mu @ w
risk = cp.quad_form(w, Sigma)         # wᵀ Σ w  — portfolio variance

# --- minimum-variance portfolio (long-only) ---
mvp = cp.Problem(cp.Minimize(risk), [cp.sum(w) == 1, w >= 0])
mvp.solve()
print("MVP weights :", w.value.round(3))
print("MVP vol     :", np.sqrt(risk.value).round(4))

# --- trace the frontier by sweeping the risk-aversion gamma ---
for gamma in [0.0, 2.0, 10.0, 50.0]:
    p = cp.Problem(cp.Maximize(ret - gamma * risk), [cp.sum(w) == 1, w >= 0])
    p.solve()
    vol = float(np.sqrt(w.value @ Sigma @ w.value))
    sharpe = (float(mu @ w.value) - rf) / vol
    print(f"gamma={gamma:5.1f}  ret={float(mu @ w.value):.3f}  "
          f"vol={vol:.3f}  Sharpe={sharpe:.3f}")

Small γ chases return and concentrates in the high-μ asset; large γ converges to the minimum-variance mix. The γ that yields the highest printed Sharpe is your tangency portfolio. Swap in real estimates — sample mean and np.cov of historical returns — and you have a working (if fragile) optimizer.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A portfolio's expected return and its variance behave differently in the weights. Which statement is correct?
A. Both are linear in the weights
B. Expected return is linear in the weights; variance is quadratic and depends on covariances
C. Both are quadratic in the weights
D. Variance is linear; expected return is quadratic
Show answer

B. μp = wTμ is linear (a weighted average), while σp2 = wTΣw is a quadratic form that includes every pairwise covariance. A and C mis-state one of the two; D reverses them. This linear-vs-quadratic asymmetry is exactly what makes diversification possible.

Q2. Two assets each have volatility σ and are combined 50/50. For which correlation ρ is the diversification benefit greatest (lowest portfolio variance)?
A. ρ = +1
B. ρ = 0
C. ρ = +0.5
D. ρ = −1
Show answer

D. σp2 = ½σ2(1 + ρ) is smallest when ρ is most negative. At ρ = −1 the variance collapses to zero — a perfect hedge. ρ = +1 (A) gives no benefit at all; ρ = 0 (B) and ρ = 0.5 (C) help, but less than −1.

Q3. On the risk-return plane, the tangency portfolio is best described as the portfolio that…
A. maximizes the Sharpe ratio — where a line from the risk-free rate just touches the efficient frontier
B. has the lowest possible variance of any portfolio
C. puts equal weight in every asset
D. maximizes expected return regardless of risk
Show answer

A. The tangency portfolio is the point where the capital allocation line from rf is tangent to the frontier, which is exactly the maximum-Sharpe risky mix. Lowest variance (B) describes the minimum-variance portfolio, a different point. Equal weighting (C) is a naive heuristic, not the tangency point. Maximizing return alone (D) ignores risk and sits at the frontier's top corner.

Q4. Why is the minimum-variance portfolio often preferred in practice over the full mean-variance optimum?
A. It always has the highest expected return
B. It requires no covariance matrix
C. It does not use the hard-to-estimate expected-return vector, so it is far more robust to estimation error
D. It is the only portfolio that allows short-selling
Show answer

C. The MVP minimizes wTΣw subject only to the weights summing to one — expected returns never enter. Since μ is the noisiest input, dropping it sidesteps the "error-maximizer" problem. It does not maximize return (A); it absolutely needs Σ (B); and short-selling rules are a separate constraint (D).

Q5. You set up mean-variance optimization as "maximize wTμ − γ·wTΣw subject to the weights summing to 1." What does sweeping the parameter γ from small to large accomplish?
A. It changes the number of assets in the universe
B. It traces out the efficient frontier, from return-seeking to risk-minimizing portfolios
C. It makes the problem non-convex and unsolvable
D. It has no effect because the constraint fixes the answer
Show answer

B. γ is the risk-aversion knob: γ → 0 maximizes return (the frontier's high-risk end), γ → ∞ approaches the minimum-variance portfolio. Sweeping it walks the whole efficient frontier. The asset count is fixed (A); a PSD Σ keeps the quadratic program convex for all γ (C); and the budget constraint leaves a whole family of solutions, one per γ (D).
