Value at Risk (VaR)

One number that tries to answer the question every risk manager dreads — "on a bad day, how much could we lose?" — and three very different ways to compute it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define Value at Risk as a quantile of the loss distribution at a chosen confidence level and read it in plain English.
  • Estimate VaR three ways — historical simulation, parametric (variance–covariance), and Monte-Carlo — and know what each assumes.
  • Use the closed-form parametric formula VaR = (μ − z·σ)·V and locate the correct z for 95% and 99%.
  • Scale a one-day VaR to longer horizons with the square-root-of-time rule, and state when that rule is valid.
  • Weigh the pros and cons of each method, and compute all three from the same data in Python.

2.1VaR Is a Quantile of the Loss Distribution

Last week you learned to measure risk with volatility — the standard deviation of returns. Volatility is symmetric: it treats a 3% gain and a 3% loss as equally "risky." But a trading desk doesn't lie awake worrying about gains. Value at Risk turns the conversation toward the downside and compresses it into a single, reportable dollar figure.

Start with the loss over a fixed horizon — say one trading day. Loss is just the negative of profit-and-loss: a loss of +$1m means you are down a million; a "loss" of −$0.5m means you actually made half a million. The loss is random, so it has a distribution. VaR picks one point off that distribution.

VaRα = the smallest loss L such that  P(Loss ≤ L) ≥ αFormally the α-quantile of the loss distribution. With α = 0.99 it is the loss level the actual loss stays below on 99% of days.

Read it as a sentence: "The one-day 99% VaR is $4 million" means we expect to lose no more than $4m on 99 out of 100 days; on the worst 1 day in 100, we lose more — possibly far more. Two knobs define every VaR figure: the horizon (1 day, 10 days) and the confidence level α (95%, 99%). Quote one without the other and the number is meaningless.

AnalogyVaR is the flood line painted on a riverbank. A "99% line" marks the level the water stays below in 99 years out of 100. It tells you nothing about how catastrophic the 1-in-100 flood will be — only that water reaches the line that often. A town that builds to the line is protected against ordinary years and silent about the disaster. That blind spot to the tail is exactly what Lecture 3 (Expected Shortfall) fixes.

2.2Three Roads to the Same Quantile

VaR is a definition, not a recipe. To produce a number you need the loss distribution, and there are three standard ways to get it. They differ in what they assume about the world.

Method 1 — Historical simulation

The most direct idea: the future will look like the recent past. Take the last (say) 500 daily returns of the portfolio, apply each one to today's positions to get 500 hypothetical P&Ls, sort them, and read off the loss at the α percentile. No distributional assumption at all — if the past data had fat tails or a skew, they are carried straight into the answer.

VaRα = − ( the (1−α)-quantile of the sorted historical P&L )For 99% VaR on 500 days, that is roughly the 5th-worst P&L (the 1% worst tail = 5 of 500).

Method 2 — Parametric (variance–covariance / normal)

Assume returns are normally distributed with mean μ and standard deviation σ (you estimated σ last week). Then the loss quantile is a closed form — no sorting, no simulation, just a z-score from the standard normal:

VaRα = ( zα·σ − μ ) · VV = portfolio value; σ, μ = return vol & mean over the horizon; z0.95 = 1.645, z0.99 = 2.326. Often μ is set to 0 for short horizons.

The name "variance–covariance" comes from the multi-asset case: portfolio σ is built from the asset volatilities and their covariance matrix (Course 2's Markowitz machinery), so the whole book collapses to a single σ and one z-lookup. Fast, but it inherits the normal's thin tails — the very flaw Lecture 2 of Course 1 warned about.

Method 3 — Monte-Carlo simulation

Don't trust history's exact path and don't want to be chained to the normal? Simulate. Pick a model for the risk factors (it can be normal, fat-tailed, or a full multi-factor stochastic model), draw tens of thousands of random scenarios, revalue the portfolio in each, and read the α quantile off the simulated loss distribution — exactly as in historical simulation, but on synthetic data you control.

Common mistakeConfusing the confidence level with the tail probability. A 99% VaR uses the 1% worst tail, so z0.99 = 2.326 — not 0.99 or 1%. And a higher confidence level gives a larger VaR (you reach further into the tail). Quoting "95% VaR" while plugging in z = 1.96 (a two-sided 95% number) is a classic error: VaR is one-sided, so the 95% z is 1.645.

2.3Scaling VaR Across Horizons: the √t Rule

You computed a one-day VaR, but the regulator wants a ten-day figure. Re-running everything on overlapping ten-day returns wastes data. Instead, recall the Central Limit insight from Course 1: if daily returns are independent and identically distributed with mean ≈ 0, then over t days the variance adds, so the standard deviation grows like √t.

σt-day = σ1-day · √t  ⟹   VaRt-day ≈ VaR1-day · √t10-day VaR ≈ 1-day VaR × √10 ≈ 3.16 × the daily number. The same √t that scales Brownian-motion volatility.

This is the engine behind the regulatory practice of computing a one-day VaR and scaling it to ten days. It is clean, but it rests on two assumptions that markets routinely break.

Common mistakeThe √t rule assumes returns are independent across days and have zero drift. If returns are autocorrelated (momentum or mean-reversion), or the horizon is long enough that the drift μ·t matters, √t scaling is wrong — it can badly understate risk when losses cluster (volatility comes in bursts). Never √t-scale across a horizon long enough for compounding or trends to bite.

2.4Choosing a Method: Pros and Cons

No method is "correct" — each trades realism for cost. A risk desk usually runs more than one and watches where they disagree, because divergence between them is itself a warning sign.

MethodStrengthsWeaknesses
HistoricalNo distribution assumed; captures real fat tails, skew, and observed correlations; trivial to explainAssumes the past repeats; blind to events absent from the window; ragged tail estimates from few data points
ParametricFast, closed-form, scales to thousands of assets via the covariance matrix; easy √t scalingNormal assumption understates tail risk; breaks for options and other nonlinear payoffs
Monte-CarloMost flexible: any model, fat tails, nonlinear instruments; full distribution, not one numberComputationally heavy; only as good as the model fed in ("garbage in, garbage out"); slow √n convergence

A useful rule of thumb: parametric for a quick, linear, multi-asset book; historical when you trust your data window more than any model; Monte-Carlo when the portfolio holds options or other nonlinear payoffs whose loss is not a simple multiple of the underlying move.

AnalogyThink of forecasting tomorrow's weather. Historical is "what happened on this date in past years." Parametric is a tidy formula — "temperature is normal around the seasonal average." Monte-Carlo is running a full atmospheric simulation thousands of times. The simulation is richest but only as trustworthy as its physics; the formula is instant but assumes the weather is well-behaved.
✦ Worked Example — parametric & √t-scaled VaR on a $10m book

A portfolio worth V = $10,000,000 has a daily return standard deviation σ = 1.5% and (we assume) zero mean. Find the one-day 99% VaR, then scale it to a 10-day horizon.

  1. Pick the z-score: 99% confidence is the 1% tail, so z0.99 = 2.326 (the standard-normal quantile leaving 1% in the left tail).
  2. Daily loss in return space: with μ = 0, the 99% worst daily return is −z·σ = −2.326 × 0.015 = −0.0349, i.e. a −3.49% day.
  3. Convert to dollars: VaR1-day = z·σ·V = 2.326 × 0.015 × $10,000,000 = $348,900 ≈ $349k.
  4. Scale to 10 days with √t: VaR10-day = $348,900 × √10 = $348,900 × 3.162 ≈ $1,103,000.
  5. Say it in English: "On 99 out of 100 days we expect to lose no more than ~$349k; over a 10-day window, no more than ~$1.10m — assuming returns are normal, independent, and drift-free." Drop the normal assumption (e.g. historical fat tails) and the real 99% loss is typically larger.

🛠️ Hands-On: all three VaRs from one return series

This is an early week in C5 — set the environment up once below. We simulate a year of daily returns (with deliberately fat tails via a Student-t), then compute the 99% one-day VaR three ways and compare. Watch the historical and Monte-Carlo numbers beat the parametric one because they see the tails.

⬇ One-time setup — the C5 Python stack

Create an isolated environment and install the free, pip-installable stack used across this course:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy cvxpy matplotlib pandas

You only do this once; later lectures reuse quant-env.

# var_three_ways.py — Lecture 2 (C5)
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
V     = 10_000_000        # portfolio value, $
alpha = 0.99              # confidence level
sigma = 0.015             # true daily vol (1.5%)

# --- a year of fat-tailed daily returns: Student-t, df=4, scaled to sigma ---
df    = 4
raw   = rng.standard_t(df, size=252)
rets  = raw * sigma / np.sqrt(df / (df - 2))   # rescale so std == sigma
pnl   = rets * V                               # daily P&L in $

# 1) HISTORICAL: empirical quantile of the loss tail
var_hist = -np.quantile(pnl, 1 - alpha)

# 2) PARAMETRIC (normal): z * sigma * V, mu assumed 0
z        = stats.norm.ppf(alpha)               # 2.326 for 0.99
var_param = z * sigma * V

# 3) MONTE-CARLO: draw 200k normal scenarios, take the quantile
sims      = rng.normal(0.0, sigma, size=200_000) * V
var_mc    = -np.quantile(sims, 1 - alpha)

print(f"Historical  99% 1-day VaR: ${var_hist:,.0f}")
print(f"Parametric  99% 1-day VaR: ${var_param:,.0f}")
print(f"Monte-Carlo 99% 1-day VaR: ${var_mc:,.0f}")

# scale parametric to 10 days with the sqrt-of-time rule
print(f"Parametric 10-day VaR    : ${var_param * np.sqrt(10):,.0f}")

The parametric and Monte-Carlo numbers (both normal) land near $349k. The historical VaR is noticeably larger — the Student-t tails put more mass in the 1% region than a normal allows. That gap is the price of the normal assumption, and the whole motivation for the tail-aware measures coming next week.

🔗 Where this is heading

VaR answers "where does the tail begin?" but stays silent on "how bad is it once we're in the tail?" — and it isn't even a coherent risk measure (it can punish diversification). Lecture 3 introduces Expected Shortfall, the average loss beyond VaR, which fixes both flaws and is now the regulatory standard. The covariance matrix behind parametric VaR is the same Markowitz object from Course 2, and the Monte-Carlo path connects to the Brownian-motion engines of Course 1. Later in C5 these risk numbers become live constraints on a market-making book — how much inventory you dare hold before VaR limits force you to quote wider or hedge.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. A desk reports a "one-day 95% VaR of $2 million." What does this mean?
A. The desk will lose exactly $2 million tomorrow
B. On about 5 days in 100, the one-day loss is expected to exceed $2 million
C. The maximum possible loss is $2 million
D. The average daily loss is $2 million
Show answer

B. 95% VaR is the loss the desk stays under on 95% of days, so it is exceeded on roughly the worst 5%. VaR is never an exact prediction (A) nor a cap on losses (C) — the loss beyond VaR can be far larger. It is a quantile, not an average (D).

Q2. Which method makes no assumption about the shape of the return distribution, instead replaying actual past returns?
A. Historical simulation
B. Parametric (variance–covariance)
C. Monte-Carlo simulation
D. The square-root-of-time rule
Show answer

A. Historical simulation sorts realized past P&Ls and reads off the quantile — distribution-free. Parametric (B) assumes a normal; Monte-Carlo (C) assumes whatever model you simulate from. The √t rule (D) is a scaling trick, not a way to build the distribution.

Q3. A portfolio is worth $5,000,000 with a daily return std of 2% and zero mean. Using the parametric method, the one-day 99% VaR (z0.99 = 2.326) is closest to…
A. $100,000
B. $1,163,000
C. $232,600
D. $11,630
Show answer

C. VaR = z·σ·V = 2.326 × 0.02 × $5,000,000 = $232,600. Option A forgets the z (it is just 1σ × V = $100k). B is 10× too large; D is 100× too small.

Q4. Your one-day VaR is $400,000. Under the square-root-of-time rule (zero drift, i.i.d. returns), the 9-day VaR is approximately…
A. $400,000
B. $3,600,000
C. $44,400
D. $1,200,000
Show answer

D. Scale by √t = √9 = 3, so $400,000 × 3 = $1,200,000. Multiplying by t directly (×9 = $3.6m, B) is the classic error — variance scales with t, but VaR scales with the standard deviation, i.e. √t. A ignores scaling; C divides instead of multiplying.

Q5. Why does parametric (normal) VaR tend to understate the true 99% loss for real assets?
A. Because it uses too much historical data
B. Because the normal distribution has thinner tails than real returns, so it puts too little probability on extreme losses
C. Because it always sets the mean return too high
D. Because z0.99 is too small a number
Show answer

B. Real return distributions are leptokurtic — fatter tails than a Gaussian — so the normal assigns too little mass to extreme moves and the quantile it returns is too small. Parametric VaR uses little or no raw history (A is false); the mean is usually set to 0, not too high (C); and z0.99 = 2.326 is the correct quantile (D) — the flaw is the assumed shape, not the z.
