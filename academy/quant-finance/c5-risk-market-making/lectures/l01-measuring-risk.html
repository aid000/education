Measuring Risk: Volatility & the Loss Distribution

Before you can manage risk you have to name it — and the whole discipline begins with one object: the distribution of how much you could lose.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define financial risk as uncertainty about future profit-and-loss, and place the loss distribution at the center of risk measurement.
  • Use volatility (the standard deviation of returns) as a risk measure, and scale it across horizons with the square-root-of-time rule.
  • Explain three limitations of volatility: it is symmetric, it assumes finite variance, and it says nothing about the shape of the tail.
  • Distinguish total risk from downside risk, and read a quantile of the loss distribution as a tail probability.
  • Estimate volatility and a loss quantile from real return data in NumPy/SciPy — the building blocks of VaR and expected shortfall.

1.1What Is Financial Risk?

Everyday speech treats "risk" as the chance of something bad. In finance we sharpen this into something measurable: risk is uncertainty about future profit and loss (P&L). If you hold a position whose value tomorrow is unknown, the amount you might gain or lose is a random variable. Risk management is the discipline of describing that random variable, putting numbers on its bad outcomes, and deciding whether you can live with them.

Concretely, take a position worth V0 today. Tomorrow it will be worth V1, which is random. The loss over the period is defined as the negative of the change in value:

L = −(V1 − V0) = V0 − V1A positive L is money lost; a negative L is a gain. Risk managers flip the sign on purpose so that "large" always means "bad."

Notice the sign convention: we work with losses, not profits, so that the dangerous outcomes sit in the right tail where they are easy to talk about. Every number in this course — volatility, Value at Risk, expected shortfall — is ultimately a summary statistic of the distribution of L. Get that distribution right and the rest is arithmetic; get it wrong and no formula will save you.

AnalogyThink of a weather forecaster. Saying "it might rain" is useless to a city planning flood defenses. What they need is the full distribution of possible rainfall — the median, and crucially the once-in-a-century deluge. The loss distribution is the risk manager's rainfall forecast: the median loss is unremarkable, but the design of the levees is decided entirely by the tail.

1.2The Loss Distribution & Returns

Because positions come in wildly different sizes, we usually work with returns rather than dollar losses. The simple return over a period is the fractional change in value:

R = (V1 − V0) / V0So the dollar loss is L = −V0·R. A −2% return on a $10M book is a $200,000 loss.

The loss distribution is simply the probability distribution of L (equivalently, of −R scaled by V0). It is the central object of risk management. Once you have it — whether from a fitted model, from historical data, or from a Monte-Carlo simulation — every risk number is just a question you ask of it: What is the average loss? How wide is the spread? How bad is the worst 1% of days?

A first, crude summary uses just two numbers from Course 1: the mean μ and the standard deviation σ of returns. Over a single day the mean is usually tiny and dominated by noise, so risk work focuses overwhelmingly on the spread. That spread, σ, is what markets call volatility.

1.3Volatility & the Square-Root-of-Time Rule

Volatility is the standard deviation of returns — the single most quoted risk number in markets. A daily volatility of 1% means a typical day's move is about ±1%; a volatility of 4% signals a far more dangerous instrument even if both have the same average return.

σ = √Var(R) = √( E[(R − μ)²] )The same σ from Course 1, now interpreted as risk. Bigger σ ⇒ a wider loss distribution ⇒ more risk.

Volatility is almost always quoted on an annual basis, but estimated from daily data. To convert between horizons we use the square-root-of-time rule. If daily returns are independent with the same variance, variances add over days, so the standard deviation scales with the square root of the number of periods:

σT = σ1 · √TWith ~252 trading days a year, σannual = σdaily · √252 ≈ 15.9 · σdaily.

This is the same √n / √t scaling that ran through Course 1's Central Limit Theorem and will return in the Brownian-motion models of the option courses. It is why a 1% daily volatility corresponds to roughly 16% annual volatility — a number you should be able to produce instantly.

Common mistakeVolatility scales with √T, not with T. A common error is to multiply daily volatility by the number of days (e.g. 1% × 252 = 252%) to "annualize" it. That confuses standard deviation with variance: it is variance that adds linearly in time, so the standard deviation grows only as √T. And the rule itself leans on independence — in trending or autocorrelated markets it under- or over-states the true multi-day risk.

1.4Beyond Volatility: Downside Risk & Quantiles

Volatility is indispensable but blunt. It has three limitations every risk manager must internalize.

1. It is symmetric. Variance squares deviations on both sides of the mean, so a 5% gain and a 5% loss contribute equally. But investors do not fear gains. A strategy that grinds out small profits then occasionally blows up can have modest volatility yet ruinous risk.

2. It assumes the spread is meaningful. Volatility is only a complete description of risk when returns are (close to) normal. Real returns have fat tails: extreme moves happen far more often than a normal distribution predicts, and for some heavy-tailed models the variance is barely finite. The same σ can sit atop very different tails.

3. It says nothing about a specific bad outcome. A trading desk needs to answer "how much could we lose on a bad day, and how often?" — a statement about a point in the tail, which σ alone cannot give.

The fix is to read the loss distribution directly through its quantiles. The α-quantile qα of the loss L is the level such that the probability of losing more than qα equals 1 − α:

P(L ≤ qα) = α,   equivalently   P(L > qα) = 1 − αExample: the 99% quantile q0.99 is the loss exceeded on only 1% of days. This is exactly the Value-at-Risk of next week.

Downside risk measures focus only on the unfavorable side. The simplest is semi-deviation — like volatility, but averaging only the squared shortfalls below the mean — and quantile-based measures take this further by reading a precise tail probability. These are the building blocks: a single quantile of L is Value at Risk, and the average loss beyond that quantile is expected shortfall, the two pillars of the rest of this course.

AnalogyVolatility is a car's average speed; the quantile is the reading on the speedometer at the scariest moment of the trip. Two drivers can average the same speed, but one crawls steadily while the other alternates idling and 120-mph bursts. The tail quantile — not the average — is what tells you which car is about to crash.
✦ Worked Example — volatility and a Gaussian loss quantile

A desk holds a $20,000,000 position. Daily returns have mean ≈ 0 and a daily volatility of 1.5%. Assuming returns are approximately normal, find (a) the annualized volatility, (b) the 99% loss quantile in dollars over one day.

  1. Annualize the volatility: σannual = σdaily·√252 = 0.015 × 15.87 ≈ 0.238, i.e. about 23.8% per year. (Multiplying by 252 instead would give a nonsensical 378%.)
  2. Find the return quantile: for a standard normal, the 99% point is z0.99 ≈ 2.326. With mean 0, the 1%-worst daily return is −2.326 × 1.5% = −3.49%.
  3. Convert the return to a loss: L = −V0·R, so the loss quantile is q0.99 = 0.0349 × $20,000,000 ≈ $698,000.
  4. Interpret it: on roughly 1 trading day in 100, this book is expected to lose more than about $698k. That single number is precisely the 1-day 99% Value at Risk we formalize next week.
  5. Reality check: because real returns are fat-tailed, the true 1% loss is usually worse than the normal estimate — a caveat that motivates expected shortfall and the heavy-tailed models later in the course.

🛠️ Hands-On: estimate volatility and a loss quantile

The free stack for this course is NumPy, SciPy, CVXPY, Matplotlib, and pandas — all pip-installable. Set it up once now (block below) and reuse it for every lecture and the course project. Here we simulate a year of daily returns, then estimate volatility and the empirical 99% loss quantile.

# measuring_risk.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

rng = np.random.default_rng(5)
V0   = 20_000_000.0          # position value in dollars
mu_d = 0.0                   # daily mean return
sig_d = 0.015                # daily volatility (1.5%)

# one year (252 trading days) of simulated daily returns
R = rng.normal(mu_d, sig_d, size=252)
L = -V0 * R                  # dollar loss series (sign flipped)

# 1) volatility, annualized via square-root-of-time
vol_d = R.std(ddof=1)
print("daily vol     :", round(vol_d, 5))           # ~0.015
print("annual vol    :", round(vol_d * np.sqrt(252), 4))  # ~0.238

# 2) empirical 99% loss quantile (the worst 1% of days)
q99_emp = np.quantile(L, 0.99)
print("empirical q99 :", round(q99_emp))            # ~ $700k, noisy at n=252

# 3) Gaussian (parametric) quantile for comparison
z = stats.norm.ppf(0.99)                             # 2.326
q99_gauss = z * sig_d * V0
print("gaussian q99  :", round(q99_gauss))           # ~ $698k

The empirical and Gaussian quantiles roughly agree here because we drew from a normal. Re-run after replacing the draw with a fat-tailed Student-t (R = sig_d * rng.standard_t(3, size=252) / np.sqrt(3)): the empirical 99% loss now jumps well above the Gaussian estimate — your first direct look at why thin-tailed assumptions under-state real risk.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. In risk management the loss over a period is conventionally defined as…
A. the change in value, V1 − V0
B. the negative of the change in value, V0 − V1
C. the absolute value of the return
D. the variance of the return
Show answer

B. Risk managers flip the sign so a positive L means money lost and the bad outcomes sit in the right tail. A (the raw change) makes losses negative; C discards the direction that matters; D is a measure of spread, not a loss.

Q2. A stock has a daily volatility of 2%. Assuming independent daily returns, its approximate annual volatility (252 trading days) is…
A. about 5%
B. about 504%
C. about 32%
D. about 2%
Show answer

C. σ scales with √T: 0.02 × √252 ≈ 0.02 × 15.87 ≈ 0.317, i.e. ~32%. Choice B (2% × 252) wrongly scales linearly with time; D ignores scaling entirely; A is too small.

Q3. Which is a genuine limitation of volatility as a risk measure?
A. It can only be computed for bonds, not equities
B. It is undefined whenever the mean return is positive
C. It always overstates the probability of large losses
D. It treats upside and downside moves symmetrically and ignores the shape of the tail
Show answer

D. Variance squares deviations on both sides, so gains and losses count equally, and a single σ is blind to how heavy the tail is. A and B are simply false; C is backwards — under fat tails a normal-volatility view tends to understate large-loss probability.

Q4. The 99% quantile q0.99 of the loss distribution is the loss level such that…
A. losses exceed it on about 1% of days
B. losses exceed it on about 99% of days
C. the average loss equals 99
D. volatility equals 99% of the mean
Show answer

A. By definition P(L ≤ q0.99) = 0.99, so P(L > q0.99) = 0.01 — exceeded only ~1% of the time. B inverts the probability; C and D confuse a quantile with a mean or a ratio.

Q5. Why do risk managers care about downside risk measures rather than relying on volatility alone?
A. Because volatility cannot be estimated from historical data
B. Because investors fear losses, not gains, and symmetric volatility hides the asymmetry and tail of the loss distribution
C. Because downside measures are always smaller and look better in reports
D. Because volatility requires the returns to be exactly uniform
Show answer

B. The harm in finance is one-sided, and volatility, being symmetric and tail-blind, can rate a "grind-then-blow-up" strategy as low-risk. A is false (volatility is routinely estimated from data); C misstates the goal (honesty, not flattery); D is nonsense — volatility does not require uniform returns.
