Risk Parity & Factor Models

Mean-variance trusts your forecasts; risk parity trusts only your risks — and in markets where returns are nearly unforecastable, that humility is an edge.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Contrast three ways to weight a portfolio — cap-weighting, mean-variance, and risk parity — and what each one actually optimizes.
  • Decompose total portfolio variance into each asset's marginal and total risk contribution.
  • State the risk-parity condition: every asset contributes an equal share of portfolio risk.
  • Explain factor models (market, size, value), how they shrink a huge covariance matrix, and what "factor risk" means.
  • Articulate why risk parity sidesteps the unreliable expected-return estimates that make mean-variance fragile.

6.1Three Ways to Weight a Portfolio

You have a pile of capital and a menu of assets. How much of each should you hold? Last week's mean-variance optimizer gave one famous answer; this week we meet a rival that has quietly become a workhorse of institutional allocation. To appreciate it, line up the three dominant philosophies side by side.

Cap-weighting is the laziest: hold each asset in proportion to its market value, exactly like a broad index. It requires no estimates at all — but it lets whatever is currently largest dominate your risk. When one sector inflates into a bubble, a cap-weighted book is most exposed precisely when that asset is most dangerous.

Mean-variance (Week 5) is the most ambitious: feed it expected returns μ and a covariance matrix Σ, and it hands back the weights with the best risk-adjusted return. Beautiful in theory — but it leans hard on μ, and expected returns are notoriously hard to estimate. A tiny change in a return forecast can swing the optimizer to a wildly different, often absurdly concentrated, portfolio.

Risk parity takes a third stance: forget forecasting returns altogether and instead equalize how much risk each asset contributes. The bet is that we can measure risk far more reliably than we can predict return — so a portfolio built only from risk estimates will be more stable out of sample. The rest of this lecture makes "risk contribution" precise.

AnalogyThink of a rowing boat with several rowers. Cap-weighting seats people by body weight — the heaviest rower dominates the stroke. Mean-variance tries to predict exactly how strong each rower will be today and seats them accordingly — brilliant when the forecast is right, capsizing when it's wrong. Risk parity simply makes sure no single rower is pulling the whole boat: every seat applies an equal share of effort, so one tired rower can't throw the whole crew off balance.

6.2Decomposing Portfolio Risk

Risk parity needs a precise notion of "how much risk does asset i contribute?" Start from the portfolio variance you already know. With weights w and covariance matrix Σ, portfolio variance is the quadratic form below, and volatility is its square root.

σp² = wTΣw     σp = √(wTΣw)The cross terms in wTΣw are why diversification works: correlations below 1 shave variance.

Here is the key trick. Portfolio volatility σp is a homogeneous function of degree 1 in the weights — scale every weight by 2 and risk doubles. Euler's theorem for such functions says the whole splits cleanly into per-asset pieces that sum exactly to the total. We define the marginal risk contribution of asset i as the partial derivative of σp, and its total risk contribution (RC) as that derivative times its weight:

MRCi = ∂σp/∂wi = (Σw)i / σp     RCi = wi · MRCiBy Euler's theorem the parts add up: RC1 + RC2 + … + RCn = σp. Each RCi is "asset i's slice of the total volatility."

The fraction RCi / σp is asset i's percent risk contribution — what share of the portfolio's wobble traces back to position i. This single number reframes the whole allocation problem: instead of asking "what fraction of my money is in each asset?" we ask "what fraction of my risk is in each asset?" Those two answers can be wildly different. A 10% allocation to a volatile, correlated asset can easily account for 40% of total risk.

6.3The Risk-Parity Condition

Risk parity is now a one-line goal: choose weights so that every asset contributes the same amount of risk. With n assets, each should account for 1/n of the total.

RCi = σp / n   for all i   ⟺   wi·(Σw)i = wj·(Σw)j   for all i, j"Equal risk contribution" (ERC). No expected returns appear anywhere — only the covariance matrix Σ.

Notice what is absent: μ. The risk-parity weights depend only on Σ. That is the whole point — and the reason it survives where mean-variance flails. In the simplest case of a single asset class, this reduces to inverse-volatility weighting: when assets are uncorrelated, wi ∝ 1/σi, so you hold less of the jumpier asset until each name pulls its equal weight in risk. Correlations complicate the algebra (there is no closed form in general), so we solve it numerically — which is exactly what the hands-on section does.

Why this beats inverse-variance intuition

A naive "just scale down the volatile stuff" rule ignores correlation: two assets that are 0.9-correlated barely diversify each other, so loading both still concentrates risk. The full ERC condition uses the marginal contribution (Σw)i, which already accounts for how asset i co-moves with the rest of the book. That is why we need Σ, not just the diagonal of variances.

Common mistakeEqual risk contribution is not equal dollar weight, and it is not the minimum-variance portfolio. Risk parity deliberately holds more of low-volatility assets (like bonds) than mean-variance would — which is why levered risk-parity strategies borrow to lift expected return back up. Confusing "equal risk" with "equal weight" or "lowest risk" is the classic error.

6.4Factor Models: Shrinking the Risk Picture

There is a deeper problem lurking behind every weighting scheme: where does Σ come from? With 500 assets, Σ has ~125,000 distinct entries, but a few years of data give only a few hundred return observations. Estimating that many correlations from so little data produces a noisy, unstable matrix — and any optimizer fed garbage covariance returns garbage weights. Factor models are the cure.

The idea: most of what drives any asset's return is a handful of common factors, plus an idiosyncratic wiggle unique to that asset. Write each asset's return as a linear combination of factor returns:

ri = αi + βi,1F1 + βi,2F2 + … + βi,kFk + εiFj = factor returns, βi,j = asset i's exposure (loading) to factor j, εi = idiosyncratic noise, uncorrelated across assets.

The classic factors are economically interpretable. The market factor is the broad return of the whole market — the single biggest common driver. Size captures the historical tendency of smaller companies to behave differently from large ones. Value captures the spread between cheap (high book-to-price) and expensive stocks. A handful of such factors explains a surprising fraction of the comovement among thousands of assets.

Why factors shrink the covariance matrix

If returns really are driven by k factors, the covariance matrix has a tidy structure: a low-rank "common" part from the shared factors, plus a diagonal "specific" part from the independent noise. Now we estimate only the n×k loadings, the k×k factor covariance, and n specific variances — a few thousand numbers instead of 125,000.

Σ ≈ B Ω BT + DB = loadings (n×k), Ω = factor covariance (k×k), D = diagonal of idiosyncratic variances. Far fewer parameters ⟹ a far more stable Σ.

Factor risk is the portion of portfolio variance that traces to factor exposures (B Ω BT) rather than to diversifiable name-specific noise (D). A portfolio can look diversified across hundreds of tickers yet carry enormous factor risk if every name loads the same way on, say, the value factor. Managers therefore monitor and sometimes deliberately neutralize unwanted factor exposures — and risk parity itself can be run across factors rather than assets, equalizing the risk contributed by market, size, and value.

✦ Worked Example — two-asset risk parity by hand

Stocks have volatility σS = 16%, bonds σB = 6%, correlation ρ = 0. Find the risk-parity weights and verify the contributions are equal. Compare against a 60/40 book.

  1. Uncorrelated ⟹ inverse-vol weighting: with ρ = 0 the ERC solution is wi ∝ 1/σi. So wS ∝ 1/16 = 0.0625 and wB ∝ 1/6 = 0.1667.
  2. Normalize: total = 0.0625 + 0.1667 = 0.2292. wS = 0.0625/0.2292 ≈ 0.273, wB ≈ 0.727. Risk parity holds far more bonds than stocks.
  3. Portfolio volatility (ρ = 0): σp = √(wS²σS² + wB²σB²) = √(0.273²·0.16² + 0.727²·0.06²) = √(0.001907 + 0.001903) ≈ √0.003810 ≈ 0.0617 (6.17%).
  4. Check the contributions: with ρ = 0, RCi = wi²σi² / σp. RCS = 0.001907/0.0617 ≈ 0.0309 and RCB = 0.001903/0.0617 ≈ 0.0308 — equal (the tiny gap is rounding). Each contributes ~50% of σp. ✓
  5. Contrast 60/40: σp = √(0.6²·0.16² + 0.4²·0.06²) = √(0.009216 + 0.000576) ≈ 0.0990. Stock RC share = 0.009216/0.009792 ≈ 94%. The "balanced" 60/40 is almost pure stock risk — exactly the imbalance risk parity removes.

🛠️ Hands-On: solve risk parity numerically

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + SciPy — the install block lives in the early lectures if you skipped it). With correlation present there is no closed form, so we minimize a convex objective whose minimum is the equal-risk-contribution portfolio.

# risk_parity.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np
from scipy.optimize import minimize

# covariance from vols + correlation matrix (3 assets)
vol  = np.array([0.16, 0.06, 0.20])          # stocks, bonds, commodities
corr = np.array([[1.0, 0.1, 0.3],
                 [0.1, 1.0, 0.0],
                 [0.3, 0.0, 1.0]])
Sigma = np.outer(vol, vol) * corr

def risk_contributions(w, Sigma):
    sig = np.sqrt(w @ Sigma @ w)              # portfolio vol
    mrc = (Sigma @ w) / sig                   # marginal contributions
    return w * mrc                            # total RC_i, sums to sig

def objective(w, Sigma):
    rc = risk_contributions(w, Sigma)
    target = rc.mean()                        # equal share = sig / n
    return np.sum((rc - target) ** 2)         # 0 when all RC are equal

n = len(vol)
cons = ({'type': 'eq', 'fun': lambda w: w.sum() - 1},)   # fully invested
bnds = [(0, 1)] * n                                       # long only
w0   = np.repeat(1 / n, n)

res = minimize(objective, w0, args=(Sigma,),
               method='SLSQP', bounds=bnds, constraints=cons)

w = res.x
rc = risk_contributions(w, Sigma)
print("weights      :", np.round(w, 3))
print("risk contrib :", np.round(rc, 4))           # all ~ equal
print("% risk each  :", np.round(rc / rc.sum(), 3))# ~ [0.333, 0.333, 0.333]

The optimizer returns weights tilted heavily toward the low-vol bond, and the printed % risk each lands near [0.333, 0.333, 0.333] — equal risk contribution achieved. Swap in your own Sigma (ideally a factor-model B Ω Bᵀ + D estimate from §6.4) and the same routine scales to hundreds of assets.

💬 Discussion

Risk parity throws away expected-return forecasts on the argument that they are too unreliable to trust. But a portfolio that ignores returns entirely will, by construction, never tilt toward the assets it expects to do best — and it tends to hold a lot of low-return bonds, often using leverage to compensate. When is deliberately ignoring information (here, μ) the smarter move, and when does it become an excuse for a worse portfolio? Argue both sides, then state where you would draw the line between mean-variance and risk parity for a long-horizon allocator.

Show a model response

Ignoring μ is smart precisely when the estimation error in μ swamps any signal it carries. Mean-variance is an error-maximizer: it puts the most weight where expected return looks highest, which is often just where the estimate is most overstated by noise. Since return estimates have enormous standard errors over any practical sample, while covariances are comparatively stable, risk parity trades a small loss of "true" signal for a large reduction in estimation-driven instability — a good trade when forecasts are weak and the horizon is long.

It becomes an excuse for a worse portfolio when you genuinely do have a defensible return view (e.g., a clear valuation gap or a structural risk premium) and discard it anyway, or when the resulting bond-heavy book must be levered so aggressively that funding and tail risk overwhelm the diversification benefit. Risk parity also implicitly assumes risk-adjusted returns are roughly equal across assets — itself a (hidden) return assumption that can be wrong.

My line: use risk parity as the default neutral prior — the portfolio you hold when you have no confident view — and tilt away from it via something like a shrinkage/Bayesian blend (Black–Litterman-style) only to the extent your return views are genuinely informative and the leverage stays prudent. The strength of your forecast, not dogma, sets how far you move off the equal-risk anchor.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) names estimation error in μ as the core reason to discount it, (2) acknowledges risk parity's costs — bond tilt, leverage, hidden equal-Sharpe assumption, (3) takes a concrete stance rather than hedging, and (4) ties the decision to forecast confidence and horizon rather than declaring one method universally superior.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The defining feature of a risk-parity (ERC) portfolio is that…
A. every asset has the same dollar weight
B. every asset contributes an equal share of total portfolio risk
C. total portfolio variance is minimized
D. the Sharpe ratio is maximized
Show answer

B. Risk parity equalizes risk contributions, not dollar weights (A). It is generally neither the minimum-variance portfolio (C) nor the max-Sharpe / tangency portfolio (D — that's mean-variance); in fact it deliberately overweights low-vol assets relative to those.

Q2. The total risk contribution of asset i is defined as…
A. wi · (Σw)i / σp
B. σi · wi
C. wi² · σp
D. (Σw)i only
Show answer

A. RCi = wi · MRCi = wi·(Σw)i/σp, and by Euler's theorem these sum to σp. (Σw)i alone (D) is unnormalized; B uses standalone vol and ignores correlation; C has the wrong form and units.

Q3. Why is risk parity considered more robust than mean-variance optimization?
A. It always produces higher returns
B. It avoids using the covariance matrix entirely
C. It depends only on Σ, not on the hard-to-estimate expected returns μ
D. It only works for two assets
Show answer

C. Mean-variance is unstable because tiny errors in μ swing the weights wildly; risk parity drops μ and uses only Σ, which is estimated more reliably. It does not avoid Σ (B — it needs it), makes no guarantee of higher return (A), and scales to many assets (D).

Q4. In a factor model ri = αi + Σ βi,jFj + εi, the term βi,j represents…
A. the idiosyncratic return unique to asset i
B. asset i's exposure (loading) to factor j
C. the return of factor j
D. the total portfolio variance
Show answer

B. βi,j is the loading — how strongly asset i moves with factor j. The idiosyncratic term is εi (A), the factor return is Fj (C), and portfolio variance (D) is a separate quantity built from the whole model.

Q5. A factor model writes Σ ≈ BΩBT + D. The main practical reason to prefer this to estimating Σ directly is…
A. it adds more parameters, capturing every nuance of the data
B. it guarantees all assets become uncorrelated
C. it removes the need for any historical return data
D. it uses far fewer parameters, giving a more stable, less noisy covariance estimate
Show answer

D. A full Σ for n assets needs ~n²/2 entries estimated from scarce data; the factor form needs only the loadings, factor covariance, and diagonal specifics — far fewer parameters, hence more stable. It uses fewer parameters, not more (A); assets remain correlated through shared factors (B); and it still requires return data to estimate the loadings (C).
