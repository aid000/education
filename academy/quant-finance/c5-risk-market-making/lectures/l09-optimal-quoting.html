Optimal Quoting & Inventory Control

A market maker who never adjusts for the position piling up on the books is a casino that forgot to set a house edge — here is how to quote so inventory drifts safely back to zero.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why a market maker's quotes must depend on current inventory, not just the mid-price.
  • Skew bid/ask quotes to mean-revert a long or short position back toward flat.
  • Define the reservation price and show how it shifts away from the mid as inventory grows.
  • Derive how the optimal half-spread widens with risk aversion γ, volatility σ, and remaining time T − t.
  • Connect the pieces into a generic continuous-time optimal market-making model and simulate it in Python.

9.1Why Inventory Changes the Quote

In Week 8 a market maker posted a symmetric bid and ask around the mid-price and collected the spread on each round-trip. That works in a frictionless thought-experiment, but it ignores the single largest risk in the business: inventory. Every fill leaves you holding a position. Buy when someone sells to you, and you accumulate a long book; the moment the market gaps down, that inventory is a loss, not a fee earned.

The core tension is this. A market maker earns money from the spread — a steady stream of small edges — but is exposed to inventory risk, the chance that the price moves against the position accumulated while providing liquidity. The spread income is roughly linear in how much you trade; the inventory risk grows with the variance of what you are holding. Quoting symmetrically lets inventory wander, and a wandering inventory has a variance that grows without bound over time.

The fix is to make quotes inventory-aware. If you are already long, you should be less eager to buy more and more eager to sell — so you raise both your bid and your ask, making your sells more attractive and your buys less so. If you are short, you do the reverse. The quote is no longer pinned to the mid; it is pinned to a price that accounts for the position you are carrying.

AnalogyThink of a grocer with a fridge full of milk nearing its sell-by date. They don't keep pricing milk at the "fair" shelf price — they mark it down to clear the shelf, accepting a thinner margin to avoid the bigger loss of spoilage. A market maker carrying a large long inventory does the same: shade prices to encourage the market to take the position off their hands before the next adverse move "spoils" it.

9.2The Reservation Price: Quoting Around Where You'd Be Indifferent

The central idea of optimal quoting is the reservation price r — the price at which the maker is indifferent between holding their current inventory and trading one more unit. It is the maker's own private "fair value," adjusted for the risk of the position they already carry. Quotes get centered on r, not on the public mid s.

With inventory q (positive = long, negative = short), risk aversion γ, return volatility σ, and time remaining T − t until the end of the trading horizon, the reservation price is the mid shifted by an inventory-risk penalty:

r(s, q, t) = s − q · γ · σ² · (T − t)Long inventory (q > 0) pulls r below the mid; short inventory (q < 0) pushes it above. The pull scales with γ, σ², and the time left to be exposed.

Read the sign carefully. If you are long (q > 0), r sits below the mid, so when you quote symmetrically around r, both your bid and your ask end up lower than they would around the mid — your ask becomes a more attractive sell, helping you offload the long. The size of the shift grows with how risk-averse you are (γ), how volatile the asset is (σ²), and how much time you still have to be caught holding it (T − t). As the horizon closes, T − t → 0 and the skew vanishes: there is no future left in which the inventory can hurt you.

Common mistakeThe reservation price is not your forecast of where the price is going. It contains no alpha or directional view. A long position lowering r does not mean you predict the price will fall — it means you are willing to sell cheaper to shed risk you'd rather not hold. Confusing inventory skew with a price forecast leads to chasing the market and doubling down on losers.

9.3The Optimal Spread: How Wide to Quote

Centering quotes on r tells you where to put them; it does not tell you how far apart. That is the job of the optimal spread. Two forces pull in opposite directions. A wider spread earns more per fill but gets fewer fills. A tighter spread wins more volume but at razor-thin margins. The optimal half-spread balances the expected profit per trade against the rate at which orders arrive.

Order arrival is the missing ingredient. Quote a distance δ away from the reference price and the probability of getting filled falls off as you move away. A standard model uses an exponentially decaying fill intensity, λ(δ) = A·e−κδ: post closer to the touch (small δ) and fills rain in; post far away (large δ) and they dry up. The parameter κ measures how quickly liquidity-takers walk away as you widen.

Solving the trade-off — maximize spread capture minus inventory risk, subject to that arrival rate — gives an optimal total spread that is the sum of an inventory-risk term and a market-structure term:

spreadopt = γ · σ² · (T − t)  +  (2 / γ) · ln(1 + γ / κ)First term: charge more when risk aversion γ, volatility σ², or remaining exposure (T − t) is high. Second term: charge more when takers are price-insensitive (small κ) and less when γ is large relative to κ.

The first term is the same γσ²(T−t) that appeared in the reservation price — it is the price of carrying risk through time. The second term is set by the shape of the order flow: if liquidity-takers tolerate wide quotes (small κ), you can afford to charge more. Quotes are then placed at r ± spread/2, so the bid is r − spread/2 and the ask is r + spread/2.

Notice the two levers act on different things. The reservation-price skew (§9.2) decides which side of the mid your quotes lean toward — it manages direction. The spread width decides how aggressive you are overall — it manages volume versus margin. Together they fully specify the bid and the ask at every instant.

9.4A Generic Optimal Market-Making Model

Put the pieces together and you have a complete continuous-time recipe — the canonical inventory-control model studied across the quantitative-finance literature. The maker watches the mid-price st evolve as a random walk (Brownian motion, from Course 1), tracks inventory qt as fills arrive according to the intensity λ(δ), and at every instant solves for the quote pair that maximizes expected terminal wealth penalized for inventory risk.

The objective is a mean–variance-style utility on terminal P&L: maximize expected wealth minus a risk penalty proportional to the variance contributed by holding inventory. The control variables are the two quote distances, on the bid and the ask side. The solution is exactly the two rules we built:

bidt = rt − spreadopt/2     askt = rt + spreadopt/2with rt = st − qt γ σ² (T−t) and spreadopt = γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ) ln(1 + γ/κ).

Controlling the variance of inventory

The reason this works is that the inventory skew creates mean reversion in qt. When q drifts positive, r drops, the ask becomes more attractive, sells outpace buys, and q is pulled back toward zero — and symmetrically when q goes negative. A symmetric quoter's inventory follows an unbounded random walk whose variance grows linearly with time; the inventory-aware quoter's position behaves like a mean-reverting process whose variance stays bounded. Bounding the variance of inventory is the whole point: it caps the worst-case loss from an adverse price move.

AnalogyA symmetric quoter steers like a car with the wheel locked straight — fine on a straight road, but any bump sends it drifting off course with no correction. The inventory skew is power steering that constantly nudges the wheel back toward center. The car still wanders with the bumps, but it never drives off the road, because every deviation generates a restoring push proportional to how far off-center it has gone.

The single knob that tunes the whole system is risk aversion γ. Crank γ up and both the skew and the spread widen: inventory snaps back to zero hard and quotes are defensive, sacrificing volume for safety. Dial γ toward zero and the maker becomes a pure spread-harvester, quoting tight and symmetric and letting inventory run — maximal volume, maximal risk. Choosing γ is choosing where to sit on that spectrum.

✦ Worked Example — quoting a long book mid-session

You make markets in a stock. The mid is s = 100.00. Per-step volatility σ = 0.20 (price units), risk aversion γ = 0.5, fill-decay κ = 1.5, and you are long q = 3 units with a fraction (T − t) = 0.4 of the session left. Compute the reservation price, the optimal spread, and your two quotes.

  1. Inventory-risk unit: γσ²(T−t) = 0.5 · (0.20)² · 0.4 = 0.5 · 0.04 · 0.4 = 0.008.
  2. Reservation price: r = s − q·γσ²(T−t) = 100.00 − 3 · 0.008 = 100.00 − 0.024 = 99.976. Long inventory pulls r below the mid, as expected.
  3. Optimal spread: spread = γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)·ln(1 + γ/κ) = 0.008 + (2/0.5)·ln(1 + 0.5/1.5) = 0.008 + 4·ln(1.3333) = 0.008 + 4·0.28768 = 0.008 + 1.1507 ≈ 1.159.
  4. Place the quotes: half-spread = 1.159 / 2 ≈ 0.579. Bid = r − 0.579 = 99.976 − 0.579 = 99.397; ask = r + 0.579 = 99.976 + 0.579 = 100.555.
  5. Read the result: both quotes sit below where a symmetric quoter (centered on 100.00) would place them — the bid is 0.603 below the mid while the ask is only 0.555 above it. The ask is the more competitive of the two relative to the mid, so it is more likely to fill, selling off the long position and pulling q back toward zero.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate an inventory-aware quoter

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Matplotlib — the install block lives in Course 1, Lecture 1). We simulate a mid-price as a random walk, post inventory-skewed quotes, fill them with an exponential arrival model, and compare the inventory path against a naive symmetric quoter.

# optimal_quoting.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
T, steps = 1.0, 1000
dt   = T / steps
sigma_step = 0.20            # vol per sqrt(dt) of the mid
gamma, kappa, A = 0.5, 1.5, 140.0

def simulate(skew):
    s, q, cash = 100.0, 0.0, 0.0
    inv = np.empty(steps)
    for i in range(steps):
        t_left = T - i * dt
        # reservation price (no skew -> symmetric quoter)
        r = s - (q * gamma * sigma_step**2 * t_left if skew else 0.0)
        spread = gamma * sigma_step**2 * t_left + (2/gamma) * np.log(1 + gamma/kappa)
        bid, ask = r - spread/2, r + spread/2
        # fill probabilities fall off as quotes move from the mid
        p_buy  = A * np.exp(-kappa * (s - bid)) * dt   # someone sells to us
        p_sell = A * np.exp(-kappa * (ask - s)) * dt   # someone buys from us
        if rng.random() < p_buy:  q += 1; cash -= bid
        if rng.random() < p_sell: q -= 1; cash += ask
        s += sigma_step * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.standard_normal()  # mid random walk
        inv[i] = q
    pnl = cash + q * s          # mark remaining inventory to market
    return inv, pnl

inv_skew, pnl_skew = simulate(skew=True)
inv_sym,  pnl_sym  = simulate(skew=False)
print("skewed  : final PnL %.2f, inventory std %.2f" % (pnl_skew, inv_skew.std()))
print("symmetric: final PnL %.2f, inventory std %.2f" % (pnl_sym,  inv_sym.std()))

Across many seeds the skewed quoter shows a markedly smaller inventory standard deviation — its position hugs zero while the symmetric quoter's wanders — and a steadier P&L with far thinner tails. You are not earning more on average per trade; you are removing the inventory-driven variance that occasionally produces a catastrophic day. Try sweeping gamma from 0.1 to 2.0 and plotting inventory-std against mean P&L to see the risk–reward frontier directly.

💬 Discussion

It is the final 30 minutes of the trading session and you are sitting on a large long inventory you have not been able to clear. The model says r drops toward the mid and the spread tightens as T − t → 0, telling you to quote more aggressively to flatten before the close. But a piece of news just hit and volatility is spiking. How should the conflict between "flatten before the close" and "volatility is rising" resolve in your quotes — and what does it reveal about the model's blind spots? Argue a position.

Show a model response

The two effects push the spread in opposite directions and you must weigh them rather than trust either term alone. The closing-time effect (T − t → 0) shrinks the inventory-risk term and tightens the spread; the volatility spike inflates σ² and widens it. In the model these multiply inside the same γσ²(T−t) term, so the net effect on the spread depends on which moves faster — and a genuine vol spike usually dominates a small T − t, so the spread should actually widen, not tighten.

But the more important point is what to do with the skew, which is separate from the spread. The long inventory still needs to come down, and an aggressive ask (low relative to the mid) is the right tool to attract sellers-to-you off the book — the skew should stay strong. The resolution: keep, or even increase, the inventory skew to keep flattening, while widening the overall spread so you are not handing out free options into a volatile, possibly adversely-selected market. You flatten by leaning your quotes, not by quoting tight.

The blind spots this exposes: (1) the model treats σ as a known constant, but real volatility is stochastic and clusters — a single constant σ cannot represent "calm then sudden spike"; (2) it ignores adverse selection — right after news, the counterparties hitting your quotes are likelier to be informed, so naive fills are toxic; (3) it has no notion of a hard end-of-day risk limit, only a smooth penalty, so it may not flatten urgently enough when a real position cap looms.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) distinguishes the skew decision from the spread-width decision rather than treating "quote aggressively" as one knob; (2) recognizes that a vol spike widens the spread while still keeping the inventory skew to flatten; and (3) names at least one structural blind spot — constant/known σ, adverse selection, or the absence of a hard risk limit.

Q1. A market maker is currently long inventory (q > 0). Relative to the mid-price, the reservation price r is…
A. above the mid, so the maker quotes a higher bid to buy more
B. below the mid, biasing quotes so the ask is more likely to fill and reduce the long
C. exactly at the mid, because r ignores inventory
D. below the mid because the maker forecasts the price will fall
Show answer

B. With q > 0 the term −q·γσ²(T−t) is negative, pulling r below the mid; quoting around r makes the ask more competitive so the long gets sold off. A and C have the sign/dependence wrong. D is the classic trap: the skew is risk management, not a price forecast — r has no directional view in it.

Q2. As the end of the trading session approaches (T − t → 0), the inventory-risk part of the optimal spread…
A. grows without bound
B. stays constant
C. shrinks toward zero, leaving only the market-structure floor
D. becomes negative
Show answer

C. The term γσ²(T−t) → 0 as T − t → 0, so the spread relaxes toward the structural floor (2/γ)ln(1+γ/κ) > 0. A and B contradict the (T−t) factor. D is impossible: a spread is a non-negative quantity and the floor term is strictly positive.

Q3. Which change makes the optimal spread wider?
A. Lower volatility σ
B. Lower risk aversion γ
C. Inventory already at exactly zero
D. A longer time remaining T − t with higher risk aversion γ
Show answer

D. The inventory-risk term γσ²(T−t) rises with both γ and the time remaining, widening the spread. Lower σ (A) and lower γ (B) shrink it. The spread width does not depend on the current inventory level (C) — inventory drives the skew of the quotes, not their width.

Q4. What is the main benefit of skewing quotes to mean-revert inventory toward zero, compared with a symmetric quoter?
A. It guarantees a profit on every single round-trip trade
B. It keeps the variance of inventory bounded instead of growing without limit over time
C. It eliminates the bid–ask spread entirely
D. It lets the maker forecast the direction of the mid-price
Show answer

B. The skew turns inventory into a mean-reverting process with bounded variance, capping worst-case inventory loss; a symmetric quoter's inventory random-walks and its variance grows linearly with time. A is false (no strategy guarantees per-trade profit). C is false — the spread is the maker's revenue, never eliminated. D confuses inventory control with forecasting.

Q5. In the fill-intensity model λ(δ) = A·e−κδ, what does a larger κ mean for how the maker should quote?
A. Takers walk away quickly as quotes widen, so the maker should keep the structure component of the spread tighter
B. Fills are independent of quote distance, so quote width is irrelevant
C. The maker should always quote at the maximum possible distance
D. Volatility no longer affects the spread
Show answer

A. A larger κ means fill probability decays faster as δ grows — takers are price-sensitive — so widening costs a lot of volume; the structure term (2/γ)ln(1+γ/κ) shrinks as κ rises, telling the maker to quote tighter. B contradicts the exponential dependence. C ignores that wide quotes barely fill when κ is large. D is wrong — the γσ²(T−t) volatility term is unaffected by κ and still matters.

✓ What you learned

  • A market maker faces a tension between spread income (linear in volume) and inventory risk (grows with the variance of the position), so quotes must be inventory-aware.
  • The reservation price r = s − q·γσ²(T−t) shifts below the mid when long and above when short, skewing quotes to flatten inventory — it carries no directional forecast.
  • The optimal spread = γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)ln(1+γ/κ) widens with risk aversion, volatility, and remaining time, and tightens toward a structural floor as the close nears.
  • Skewing quotes makes inventory mean-revert, keeping its variance bounded instead of letting it random-walk — capping worst-case loss.
  • A generic continuous-time model combines a random-walk mid, exponential fill intensity, and a mean–variance objective; risk aversion γ is the single dial trading volume against safety.
