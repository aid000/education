Market Microstructure & the Limit Order Book

Underneath every quoted price sits a living queue of orders — and learning to read that queue is the difference between trading the market and being traded by it.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain what market microstructure studies and why the mechanics of how a trade happens matter as much as what you trade.
  • Read a limit order book — its bid and ask queues, depth at each level, and the price–time priority rule that orders them.
  • Distinguish a market order (demand liquidity, pay the spread) from a limit order (supply liquidity, earn the spread).
  • Define the bid–ask spread, mid price, and tick size, and explain why the spread is a cost to takers and a reward to makers.
  • Trace how an incoming marketable order "walks the book," consumes depth, and creates slippage and price impact.

7.1What Is Market Microstructure?

Most of this specialization treats a price as a single number that drifts and diffuses — a clean P in a stochastic differential equation. Market microstructure zooms all the way in and asks the uncomfortable question that number hides: how does a price actually come to exist? There is no auctioneer shouting one fair value. Instead there are thousands of separate buy and sell intentions, each with its own price and size, sitting in an electronic queue and getting matched by a deterministic set of rules.

That distinction has teeth. A portfolio model might say "buy 100,000 shares at the screen price." Microstructure says: there is no single screen price — there is a best offer with limited size, then a worse offer, then a worse one still. The moment you try to buy that much, you move the very price you wanted. The gap between the idealized price and what you actually pay is born entirely in the order book. Microstructure is the study of how prices and liquidity are formed by the matching of individual orders, and it governs your real-world transaction costs.

AnalogyThink of an airline seat map, not a single "ticket price." A few cheap seats are left, then a tier of pricier ones, then business class. If you need ten seats right now, you sweep up the cheap ones and spill into the expensive tiers — your average price is worse than the headline fare. The order book is that seat map for an asset, refreshed many times per second.

Two roles recur throughout the lecture. A liquidity taker wants to trade immediately and accepts whatever prices are resting in the book. A liquidity provider (or maker) posts patient orders and waits to be filled. Every trade is a handshake between exactly one of each — a structure we will build on next week when you become the maker.

7.2The Limit Order Book: Bids, Asks, Depth & Priority

The limit order book (LOB) is the central data structure of a modern electronic market. It is two sorted lists of resting limit orders — promises to trade a stated quantity at no worse than a stated price:

  • The bid side: buyers, each willing to pay up to some price. Sorted high-to-low — the highest bid is the best bid.
  • The ask (offer) side: sellers, each willing to sell down to some price. Sorted low-to-high — the lowest ask is the best ask.

The best bid always sits below the best ask. Why? If a buyer were ever willing to pay as much as a seller demanded, they would simply trade and both orders would vanish from the book. So the book's two sides never overlap; the visible gap between them is the spread (§7.3). The quantity resting at each price level is the depth, and the stack of several levels is the shape of the book — a thick book absorbs large orders cheaply, a thin one does not.

When two orders compete, the exchange needs a tie-breaker. The near-universal rule is price–time priority: better-priced orders fill first; among orders at the same price, the one that arrived earliest fills first.

priority rank  =  (better price)  then  (earlier timestamp)A buy order at \$100.02 beats one at \$100.01. Two buys both at \$100.02 fill in arrival order — first in, first out.
Common mistakeThe "price" on a ticker is usually the last traded price or the mid — neither is a price you can actually transact at for size. You buy at the ask and sell at the bid. Treating the mid as your fill price quietly ignores half the spread on every trade, which for a high-turnover strategy can dwarf the alpha you were chasing.

7.3Orders, the Spread & Tick Size

There are two atomic order types, and their difference is the heart of microstructure.

Limit order — supply liquidity

A limit order states a price you will not cross. A buy limit at 100.02 means "buy, but pay no more than 100.02." If no one is selling that cheaply, it does not trade — it rests in the book, adding depth and waiting its turn under price–time priority. You control your price but not whether (or when) you fill. A resting limit order provides liquidity.

Market order — demand liquidity

A market order states a size and says "fill me now, at whatever prices are resting." It crosses the spread immediately, matching against the best opposing limit orders. You control execution certainty but not price. A market order consumes liquidity.

The bid–ask spread is the gap between best ask and best bid, and the mid price is their average:

spread = best ask − best bid     mid = (best ask + best bid) / 2A taker who buys at the ask and immediately sells at the bid loses the full spread — the maker's profit and the taker's round-trip cost.

The spread is not arbitrary noise; it is the price of immediacy. The taker pays it to trade now; the maker earns it as compensation for the risk of posting a passive quote (it might get filled just as the market moves against them — the adverse selection we tackle next week). Half the spread, measured from the mid, is the natural per-trade cost estimate:

taker cost per share ≈ spread / 2  (relative to mid)

The smallest allowed price increment is the tick size. It quantizes the book: with a one-cent tick, prices live on a 100.01, 100.02, 100.03 grid and the spread can never be smaller than one tick. Tick size shapes behavior — too large and the spread is forced wide (costly for takers); too small and queues fragment across many near-identical prices, weakening price–time priority's protection of patient orders.

AnalogyA limit order is listing your used bike for \$200 and waiting for a buyer; a market order is walking into a pawn shop and taking whatever they offer right now. The first lets you name your price but might never sell; the second guarantees a sale but at the worse, immediate price. The pawn shop's markup is the spread.

7.4Walking the Book: Slippage & Price Impact

A market order larger than the depth at the best level cannot fill at one price. It walks the book: it takes all the size at the best level, then the next level, then the next, until its quantity is satisfied. Each successive level is a worse price, so the order's average execution price is worse than the best quote it started at.

Two related costs emerge. Slippage is the difference between the price you expected (say, the mid or the best quote) and the volume-weighted average price you actually got. Price impact is the fact that after your order clears those levels, the new best quote has moved — you have shifted the price by consuming liquidity.

VWAPfill = ( Σlevels pricei × qtyi ) / ( Σlevels qtyi )The fill price is the depth-weighted average of every level the order ate. The bigger the order relative to depth, the further it climbs and the worse the VWAP.

This is why a thick, deep book is precious: it lets large orders trade near the touch with little impact. A thin book means even a modest order skips several ticks, and the realized cost can swamp a strategy's edge. Estimating impact before sending an order — and slicing a big order into small child orders over time to limit it — is a core execution discipline.

Common mistakeAssuming an entire large order fills at the best quote. The displayed best ask only has the depth shown at that level. Sizing trades off the top-of-book price — rather than off cumulative depth — systematically underestimates execution cost, and the error grows precisely when liquidity is thinnest and you can least afford it.
✦ Worked Example — cost of a 200-share market buy

Use the ask side from the figure. Best bid is 100.02, so mid = (100.03 + 100.02)/2 = 100.025. You send a market buy for 200 shares. Find the VWAP, the slippage vs. mid, and the new best ask.

  1. List the depth you'll consume (price–time priority, cheapest asks first): 90 @ 100.03, then 50 @ 100.04, then 60 @ 100.05. That is 90 + 50 + 60 = 200 shares — exactly your order.
  2. Dollar cost of each slice: 90 × 100.03 = 9 002.70; 50 × 100.04 = 5 002.00; 60 × 100.05 = 6 003.00. Total = \$20 007.70.
  3. VWAP = total cost / shares: 20 007.70 / 200 = 100.0385 per share.
  4. Slippage vs. mid: 100.0385 − 100.025 = 0.0135 per share, i.e. 200 × 0.0135 = \$2.70 worse than trading the whole lot at the mid.
  5. New top of book: the 100.03 and 100.04 levels are fully gone and 100.05 is partially eaten (60 of its 80 taken). The best ask is now 100.05 with 20 shares left — you moved the price up two ticks. A patient trader who had posted a limit buy at 100.02 instead would have paid the mid-or-better and earned, not paid, the spread — but might never have filled.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate walking the book

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + Jupyter — the install block lives in the early lectures if you skipped it). We'll represent an ask book as price/size levels and write a function that walks it for a market buy, returning the VWAP, slippage, and the resulting best ask.

⬇ Need the environment? (reuse the early-week setup)

If you already built quant-env, just activate it. Otherwise:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib pandas jupyter
# walk_book.py — Lecture 7
import numpy as np

# ask side, cheapest first: (price, size). Best bid given separately.
asks = [(100.03, 90), (100.04, 50), (100.05, 80), (100.06, 60)]
best_bid = 100.02
mid = (asks[0][0] + best_bid) / 2          # 100.025

def market_buy(asks, qty):
    cost, filled, remaining = 0.0, 0, list(asks)
    while qty > 0 and remaining:
        price, size = remaining[0]
        take = min(qty, size)              # can't take more than the level holds
        cost += take * price
        qty  -= take
        filled += take
        if take == size:
            remaining.pop(0)               # level fully consumed
        else:
            remaining[0] = (price, size - take)
    vwap = cost / filled
    new_best_ask = remaining[0][0] if remaining else None
    return vwap, cost, new_best_ask

vwap, cost, new_ask = market_buy(asks, 200)
print(f"VWAP fill     : {vwap:.4f}")        # 100.0385
print(f"total cost    : {cost:.2f}")        # 20007.70
print(f"slippage/share: {vwap - mid:+.4f}") # +0.0135 vs mid
print(f"new best ask  : {new_ask}")         # 100.05 (20 left)

Sweep the order size — for q in [50, 200, 500]: print(q, market_buy(asks, q)[0]) — and watch the VWAP climb as the order eats deeper into the book. Plotting VWAP vs. order size with Matplotlib traces an empirical price-impact curve, the exact object an execution algorithm tries to minimize.

Q1. An order book shows a best bid of 50.10 and a best ask of 50.14. What is the bid–ask spread and the mid price?
A. spread 0.04, mid 50.12
B. spread 0.02, mid 50.12
C. spread 0.04, mid 50.14
D. spread 100.24, mid 0.04
Show answer

A. Spread = ask − bid = 50.14 − 50.10 = 0.04; mid = (50.14 + 50.10)/2 = 50.12. B halves the spread incorrectly; C uses the ask as the mid; D adds the two prices instead of subtracting/averaging.

Q2. You want to be filled immediately and are willing to accept whatever price is resting. Which order type, and what is its effect on liquidity?
A. A limit order; it provides liquidity
B. A market order; it provides liquidity
C. A market order; it consumes liquidity
D. A limit order; it consumes liquidity
Show answer

C. A market order crosses the spread for immediate execution and removes resting depth, so it consumes (takes) liquidity. A limit order rests and provides liquidity, so A is the right description of the wrong tool here, and D mislabels a limit order. B mislabels a market order's liquidity effect.

Q3. Two buy limit orders both rest at 100.02; order X arrived at 9:30:01 and order Y at 9:30:02. A matching sell arrives. Under price–time priority, who fills first?
A. Y, because later orders are prioritized
B. X, because at equal price the earlier order has priority
C. Whichever is larger in size
D. They split the fill equally regardless of time
Show answer

B. Price–time priority breaks ties at the same price by arrival time — first in, first out — so the earlier order X fills first. A reverses the rule; C and D invent size-based or pro-rata rules that the standard price–time book does not use.

Q4. The ask side is 100 @ 25.00, then 80 @ 25.02, then 200 @ 25.05. You send a market buy for 150 shares. What is your approximate VWAP?
A. 25.00, since you start at the best ask
B. 25.05, the deepest level
C. 25.0107
D. 25.0233
Show answer

C. Take 100 @ 25.00 = 2500.00 and 50 @ 25.02 = 1251.00; total 3751.00 over 150 shares = 25.0107. A ignores that 150 > 100 so you walk past the top level; B uses a level you never reach; D over-weights the 25.02 level (e.g. wrongly taking 100 there).

Q5. Why does a market maker posting a passive limit order earn the spread as compensation?
A. Because limit orders always fill instantly
B. Because exchanges forbid market orders
C. Because the mid price is the true value, so resting orders are riskless
D. Because they bear the risk of being filled just as the market moves against them (adverse selection)
Show answer

D. The maker supplies immediacy and is exposed to adverse selection — getting filled precisely when informed flow pushes the price against the quote — so the spread is their compensation. A is false (limit orders may never fill); B is false (market orders are central); C is false (resting orders are not riskless, which is the whole point).

✓ What you learned

  • Market microstructure explains how a price is formed by matching individual orders — and why your real cost differs from the screen price.
  • The limit order book stacks bids (high-to-low) against asks (low-to-high), ordered by price–time priority, with depth at each level.
  • A limit order supplies liquidity and earns the spread; a market order consumes liquidity and pays it for immediacy.
  • The spread = ask − bid, the mid is their average, and tick size quantizes the price grid and floors the spread.
  • A large market order walks the book, fills at a worse VWAP, and creates slippage and price impact that scale with order size over depth.
