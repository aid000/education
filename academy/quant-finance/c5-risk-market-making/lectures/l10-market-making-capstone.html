Capstone: A Market-Making Simulator

Everything you built this course — risk metrics, optimal quotes, and inventory control — collides inside one loop that quotes, fills, and books P&L tick by tick.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Architect a market-making simulator as a discrete-time loop over quoting, order flow, fills, inventory, and P&L.
  • Simulate stochastic order arrivals and fills whose probability depends on how far your quote sits from the mid.
  • Decompose the market-maker's P&L into spread capture versus inventory (mark-to-market) risk.
  • Measure the Value-at-Risk and Expected Shortfall of the inventory's P&L from the simulated distribution.
  • Tie risk measurement, optimal quoting, and inventory control into one deliverable — the course capstone.

10.1The Capstone: One Loop That Ties It All Together

Across this course you built a toolkit one piece at a time: volatility and risk metrics (Weeks 1–3), position sizing and portfolio construction (Weeks 4–6), and the quoting-and-inventory machinery of a market maker (Weeks 7–9). The capstone is where those pieces stop being separate notebooks and become a single living system — a market-making simulator that you can run, instrument, and stress.

A market maker continuously posts a two-sided quote: a bid it will buy at and an ask it will sell at, straddling the current mid-price. It earns the spread when buyers and sellers trade against both sides, but every fill leaves it holding inventory it did not choose, exposed to the next price move. The whole job is a tug-of-war: quote wide enough to be paid for risk, tight enough to actually get filled, and skew your quotes to bleed off inventory before it bites.

The simulator is just a clock. At each tick we (1) observe the mid, (2) quote a bid and ask, (3) draw random order flow and decide which side fills, (4) update inventory and cash, and (5) mark the book to the new mid. Run that loop thousands of times and you get a full distribution of outcomes — exactly the raw material your risk metrics need.

tick t:  midt → quote (bt, at) → fills → inventory qt, cash → PnLtEach tick is deterministic given the random draws; the simulation is the loop repeated over t = 1 … T, then over many independent paths.
AnalogyThink of the market maker as a fruit stall owner who must post a buy price and a sell price for apples all day. The gap between them is the profit per round-trip apple. But every apple bought and not yet sold is inventory sitting in the crate, rotting (or ripening) as the wholesale price drifts. A good stall owner shades prices down when the crate is overflowing and up when it's bare — that price-shading is inventory control.

10.2Simulating Order Flow, Fills & Inventory

The heart of the simulator is the fill model: when you post a quote at a distance δ from the mid, what is the chance someone trades against it? The further out you quote, the wider your edge per trade — but the lower your chance of being hit. A standard, tractable choice (from Week 9) makes the arrival intensity of a fill decay exponentially in the quote distance:

λ(δ) = A · e−κδA is the base arrival rate at the mid; κ controls how fast fills dry up as you quote away from the mid. Over a short tick of length Δt, P(fill) ≈ λ(δ)·Δt.

So a tight quote (small δ) fills often but earns little per fill; a wide quote earns a lot per fill but rarely trades. Each side — bid and ask — has its own distance δb, δa and its own independent fill draw each tick. The mid-price itself wanders as a random walk (Course 1's arithmetic Brownian motion), independent of your quoting:

midt+1 = midt + σ·√Δt · Zt,   Zt ~ Normal(0, 1)

When the bid fills you buy one lot: inventory q goes up by 1, cash falls by the bid price. When the ask fills you sell: q falls by 1, cash rises by the ask price. Inventory is therefore the running net of buy-fills minus sell-fills — a path that random-walks toward dangerous extremes unless your quotes push back:

qt+1 = qt + 𝟙(bid fills) − 𝟙(ask fills)

Total wealth at any tick is cash plus inventory marked at the current mid. That second term is where the risk lives: a market maker who is long 50 lots into a falling mid is losing money on every downtick, spread capture be damned.

Common mistakeDo not book P&L only when you trade. A flat-cash position is not flat risk: your inventory must be marked to market every tick. Beginners who compute P&L as "cash received minus cash paid" miss the unrealized loss on the inventory they're still holding — and are blindsided when the close-out reveals it. PnLt = casht + qt·midt.

10.3Measuring VaR and Expected Shortfall of the Inventory

Once the loop runs over many independent paths, the terminal P&L is no longer a single number — it is a distribution. The left tail of that distribution is the inventory risk you've been quoting against all course. We measure it with the two metrics from Weeks 2–3.

Value-at-Risk (VaR) at confidence level α is the loss threshold the P&L breaches only a (1−α) fraction of the time. With simulated outcomes, it is simply a quantile: sort the P&L outcomes and read off the cutoff.

VaRα = −quantile1−α(PnL)e.g. the 95% VaR is the negative of the 5th-percentile P&L — losses worse than this occur on 5% of paths.

Expected Shortfall (ES), also called Conditional VaR, answers the sharper question: given that we are in that bad 5% tail, how bad is it on average? It is the mean loss conditional on breaching the VaR — a coherent risk measure that, unlike VaR, sees the shape of the tail beyond the cutoff.

ESα = −E[ PnL │ PnL ≤ quantile1−α(PnL) ]The average of all P&L outcomes in the worst (1−α) fraction. Always ES ≥ VaR.

For a market maker, this distribution is shaped by quoting choices. Quote tight and symmetric and you capture spread on average but accumulate large inventory swings — a fat left tail. Skew your quotes against inventory and you trade some expected spread to shrink that tail. The capstone makes this tradeoff visible: change κ, the skew, or the inventory cap, re-run, and watch VaR and ES move.

Common mistakeVaR is not the worst case. A 95% VaR says nothing about the size of losses in the worst 5% — two strategies can share an identical VaR while one has a far heavier tail. That blind spot is exactly why ES exists, and why regulators shifted toward it. Report both, and never call VaR a "maximum loss."

10.4Tying Risk, Quoting & Inventory Control Into One Project

The capstone deliverable is not three disconnected scripts — it is one simulator with three knobs you can turn, plus the risk lens to judge each setting. This is the payoff of the whole course.

The optimal-quoting knob

From Week 9, the inventory-aware quote does two things: it sets a half-spread that compensates for risk over the remaining horizon, and it skews the mid you quote around by an amount proportional to your inventory. Long inventory? Lower both quotes so you're more likely to sell and less likely to buy. The reservation price r and the optimal half-spread δ* capture this:

r = mid − q·γ·σ²·(T−t),   δ* ≈ ½·γ·σ²·(T−t) + (1/γ)·ln(1 + γ/κ)q = inventory, γ = risk aversion, σ = volatility, κ = fill-decay. Bigger q pulls the reservation price r away from the mid; bigger γ widens the spread and steepens the skew.

The risk-control knob

Optimal quoting nudges inventory back toward zero, but a hard position limit guarantees it: when |q| hits a cap, pull the offending side entirely. This bounds the worst case the math alone won't.

The measurement lens

Every parameter choice produces a P&L distribution, and you judge it not by mean P&L alone but by risk-adjusted performance — Sharpe ratio (Week 1) and the VaR/ES of §10.3. The deliverable is a sweep: vary γ, show how mean P&L, Sharpe, and ES trade off, and argue for a chosen operating point.

AnalogyRisk aversion γ is the thermostat of the whole system. Turn it up and the maker quotes timidly — wide spreads, aggressive skew, small inventory, a thin but very stable P&L. Turn it down and it quotes greedily — tight spreads, fat average profit, but an inventory that can run hot and a left tail that can burn you. The capstone is finding the temperature you can live with.
✦ Worked Example — VaR and ES from eight simulated paths

A toy run of the simulator produces eight terminal-P&L outcomes (in dollars). Compute the 75% VaR and 75% Expected Shortfall, and the mean P&L.

  1. The outcomes: +40, +22, +18, +12, +5, −10, −28, −55. (n = 8 independent paths.)
  2. Sort ascending: −55, −28, −10, +5, +12, +18, +22, +40. Already done above in reverse.
  3. Find the 1−α = 25% lower tail: 25% of 8 paths = the worst 2 outcomes: −55 and −28.
  4. VaR75%: the cutoff is the boundary of the tail — the 25th-percentile P&L is −28, so VaR = −(−28) = $28. Losses worse than $28 happen on 25% of paths.
  5. ES75%: the average P&L inside the tail = mean(−55, −28) = −41.5, so ES = −(−41.5) = $41.50. As required, ES ($41.50) ≥ VaR ($28).
  6. Mean P&L: (40+22+18+12+5−10−28−55)/8 = 4/8 = +$0.50. The desk is profitable on average, but the tail dwarfs the mean — a classic market-making signature, and exactly why we size with risk metrics, not the mean alone.

🛠️ Hands-On: the capstone simulator in ~40 lines

Reusing the course stack — NumPy for the loop, plus Matplotlib/pandas for the report (install block is in Course 1, Lecture 1). This is the skeleton you extend for the deliverable: it quotes with inventory skew, draws fills, marks to market, and reports VaR/ES over many paths.

# mm_simulator.py — Capstone (C5 · Lecture 10)
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(0)

# --- parameters ---
T, dt   = 1.0, 0.005          # horizon and tick size
steps   = int(T / dt)
sigma   = 2.0                 # mid-price volatility
A, kappa = 140.0, 1.5         # fill intensity: lambda = A*exp(-kappa*delta)
gamma   = 0.1                 # risk aversion (the thermostat)
N_paths = 20_000

def run_path():
    mid, q, cash = 100.0, 0, 0.0
    for t in range(steps):
        tau = T - t * dt                          # time remaining
        # inventory-skewed reservation price + optimal half-spread (Week 9)
        r      = mid - q * gamma * sigma**2 * tau
        spread = gamma * sigma**2 * tau + (2/gamma) * np.log(1 + gamma/kappa)
        bid, ask = r - spread/2, r + spread/2
        d_b, d_a = mid - bid, ask - mid           # quote distances
        # fill probabilities this tick (clipped to [0,1])
        p_b = np.clip(A * np.exp(-kappa * d_b) * dt, 0, 1)
        p_a = np.clip(A * np.exp(-kappa * d_a) * dt, 0, 1)
        if rng.random() < p_b: q += 1; cash -= bid    # bid hit -> we buy
        if rng.random() < p_a: q -= 1; cash += ask    # ask hit -> we sell
        mid += sigma * np.sqrt(dt) * rng.standard_normal()
    return cash + q * mid                          # mark-to-market P&L

pnl = np.array([run_path() for _ in range(N_paths)])

alpha = 0.95
cut   = np.quantile(pnl, 1 - alpha)                # 5th-percentile P&L
VaR   = -cut
ES    = -pnl[pnl <= cut].mean()                    # mean of the left tail
print(f"mean PnL : {pnl.mean():6.2f}")
print(f"Sharpe   : {pnl.mean()/pnl.std():6.3f}")
print(f"95% VaR  : {VaR:6.2f}")
print(f"95% ES   : {ES:6.2f}")

Run it, then sweep gamma over, say, [0.02, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3] and tabulate mean P&L, Sharpe, and ES with pandas. You'll see the thermostat in action: tiny γ maximizes mean P&L but blows out ES; large γ crushes the tail but starves the average. Plotting the inventory path of a single run alongside the P&L distribution is the figure your capstone write-up wants.

🔗 Where this is heading

This simulator is a scale model of a real electronic market-making desk, and the same loop generalizes far beyond it. Swap the random-walk mid for a model with jumps and stochastic volatility and you stress-test against crashes the normal tail underrates. Replace the single instrument with a basket and the scalar inventory becomes a vector hedged with the covariance and factor tools from Week 6. Make the fill model react to your own trading and you've stepped into market impact and adversarial flow — the frontier where market making meets optimal execution and reinforcement learning. Q1. In the fill model λ(δ) = A·e−κδ, what happens as you quote further from the mid (larger δ)?
A. The fill probability falls while the edge per fill rises
B. Both the fill probability and the edge per fill rise
C. The fill probability rises while the edge per fill falls
D. Neither changes — δ only affects inventory
Show answer

A. The exponential makes λ(δ) decrease as δ grows, so you fill less often; but each fill is further from the mid, so you earn a larger edge. That tension is the core quoting tradeoff. (B) and (C) get the direction backward; (D) is false — δ directly drives the fill rate.

Q2. A market maker holds long inventory and the mid drifts down all afternoon while it makes no trades. How should its P&L be recorded?
A. Unchanged — P&L only moves when a trade happens
B. It improves, because holding inventory earns the spread
C. It falls, because the inventory must be marked to the new, lower mid
D. It is undefined until the position is closed
Show answer

C. Total P&L = cash + q·mid. With q > 0 and the mid falling, the mark-to-market term shrinks, so P&L drops even with zero trades. (A) is the classic mistake of booking only realized cash; (B) confuses spread capture with holding; (D) is wrong — marking to market is well-defined every tick.

Q3. From the simulated terminal-P&L distribution, the 95% Value-at-Risk is computed as…
A. the mean of all P&L outcomes
B. the single worst P&L outcome observed
C. the negative of the 5th-percentile (lowest 5%) P&L
D. the standard deviation of the P&L
Show answer

C. 95% VaR is the loss at the 1−0.95 = 5% lower quantile: sort the P&L and negate the 5th-percentile value. The mean (A) ignores the tail; the single worst case (B) is not VaR (and is closer to what ES probes); the standard deviation (D) is a symmetric spread measure, not a tail loss.

Q4. Two quoting strategies have an identical 95% VaR, but strategy X loses far more than strategy Y in its very worst 1% of paths. Which statement is correct?
A. Their Expected Shortfall is also identical
B. Strategy X has the higher Expected Shortfall, since ES averages losses beyond the VaR cutoff
C. VaR fully captures the difference, so ES adds nothing
D. Strategy Y is riskier because lower VaR means lower ES
Show answer

B. ES averages all losses beyond the VaR cutoff, so it sees the heavier deep tail of X even when the cutoff itself matches. That's precisely VaR's blind spot. (A) and (C) deny that blind spot; (D) is incoherent — their VaRs are equal, not lower for Y.

Q5. In the capstone simulator, you increase the risk-aversion parameter γ. Compared to a low-γ setting, you should expect…
A. tighter spreads, larger inventory swings, and a fatter left tail
B. no change to quotes, since γ affects only the risk report
C. a higher mean P&L with unchanged tail risk
D. wider spreads, stronger inventory skew, a smaller mean P&L but a thinner left tail (lower ES)
Show answer

D. Larger γ widens the optimal half-spread and steepens the inventory skew, so the maker trades less and holds smaller inventory: average profit falls but the left tail (ES) shrinks — the thermostat turned up. (A) describes low γ; (B) is false since γ enters the quote formulas directly; (C) wrongly claims a free lunch.
