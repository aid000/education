Expected Shortfall & Coherent Risk

VaR tells you where the cliff edge is; Expected Shortfall tells you how far you fall once you go over — and why that distinction can decide whether diversification looks like a virtue or a vice.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain the two structural flaws of Value at Risk: it is blind to the shape of the tail beyond the cutoff, and it is not subadditive.
  • Construct Expected Shortfall (ES / CVaR) as the average loss conditional on being in the worst tail, and relate it to VaR.
  • State the four axioms of a coherent risk measure and check each one against VaR and ES.
  • Show, with a concrete example, how VaR can penalize diversification while ES rewards it.
  • Estimate VaR and ES from a return sample in NumPy and explain why regulators moved their capital rules toward ES.

3.1Where VaR Stops Looking

In Lecture 2 we built Value at Risk: the loss threshold L such that, over a chosen horizon, the probability of losing more than L is only some small α (say 1%). Formally, the 99% VaR is the 99th percentile of the loss distribution. It is a single, communicable number — "we won't lose more than $10M on 99 days out of 100" — and that clarity is exactly why it took over risk desks and regulation.

But a percentile only marks a point on the loss curve. It says nothing about what lies beyond it. Two portfolios can have an identical 99% VaR of $10M while one's worst 1% of days average a $12M loss and the other's average a $40M loss. VaR cannot tell them apart, because it stops looking at the cliff edge instead of measuring the drop.

VaRα(L) = inf { ℓ : P(L > ℓ) ≤ 1 − α }The smallest loss level ℓ that the loss L exceeds with probability at most 1 − α. With α = 0.99 this is just the 99th percentile of losses.

This tail-blindness is the first flaw. A trading book stuffed with sold deep out-of-the-money options can show a beautifully small VaR — the bad outcomes are rare enough to fall past the percentile — while hiding a catastrophic tail. VaR rewards the strategy of "picking up pennies in front of a steamroller" precisely because it never weighs the steamroller.

AnalogyVaR is a flood wall marked "protects against all but the worst 1% of floods." Useful — but it tells you nothing about how deep the water gets when a once-in-a-century flood actually arrives. Expected Shortfall is the answer to "and when it overtops, how bad is it on average?" You want both the wall height and the depth behind it.

3.2Expected Shortfall: Averaging the Tail

The fix is natural once you see the flaw. Instead of reading off the percentile and stopping, keep going and average every loss in the bad tail. That average is the Expected Shortfall (ES), also called Conditional Value at Risk (CVaR) or expected tail loss. At the 97.5% level, ES is the mean loss given that the loss already lands in the worst 2.5% of days.

ESα(L) = E[ L | L ≥ VaRα(L) ](continuous case) — the conditional average of losses in the tail beyond the VaR cutoff. Equivalently, ES is the average of VaR over all confidence levels deeper than α.

Because ES averages over the whole tail rather than reading one point, it is sensitive to the shape of the tail. Make the worst outcomes worse and ES rises immediately; VaR at the same level does not budge. This is exactly the information a risk manager needs to distinguish the $12M-tail book from the $40M-tail book that share a VaR.

Two facts to anchor: ES is always at least as large as VaR at the same level (you are averaging numbers that are all ≥ the cutoff), and the gap between them widens as the tail gets heavier. For a thin-tailed normal the gap is modest; for a fat-tailed crash distribution the gap is enormous — which is precisely when you most want to know it.

ESα ≥ VaRαFor a normal loss with mean μ and σ: VaRα = μ + σ·zα while ESα = μ + σ·φ(zα)/(1 − α), where φ is the standard-normal density. The ratio φ(z)/(1−α) > z for all α, so ES > VaR.

3.3The Four Axioms of a Coherent Risk Measure

Why prefer ES on principle rather than just taste? Because risk theory wrote down what a sensible risk measure must do. A risk measure ρ that assigns a capital number to a portfolio is called coherent if it satisfies four common-sense axioms. They sound abstract; each is a property a trader would demand without being told.

AxiomStatementPlain meaning
MonotonicityIf X always loses more than Y, then ρ(X) ≥ ρ(Y)A strictly worse position cannot be measured as safer.
Translation invarianceρ(X + c) = ρ(X) − cAdding c of risk-free cash reduces required capital by exactly c.
Positive homogeneityρ(λX) = λρ(X) for λ ≥ 0Doubling every position doubles the risk.
Subadditivityρ(X + Y) ≤ ρ(X) + ρ(Y)Merging two books is never riskier than the sum — diversification can only help.

The fourth axiom, subadditivity, is the crucial one. It is the mathematical encoding of "don't put all your eggs in one basket." If a risk measure can charge more capital for a combined, diversified book than for its two pieces held separately, it is punishing diversification — and it would tempt a firm to split itself into legal silos purely to game the risk number. That is a deep defect.

Expected Shortfall satisfies all four axioms — it is coherent. Value at Risk satisfies the first three but violates subadditivity in general. VaR is only guaranteed subadditive when losses are elliptically distributed (e.g. jointly normal); the moment tails are skewed or discrete — exactly the realistic case — VaR can break it.

Common mistakeDon't assume VaR is "usually fine" on subadditivity. For thin-tailed, jointly-normal portfolios it does hold — which lulls people into trusting it. The violations appear precisely with the lumpy, fat-tailed, optionality-laden books that matter most for tail risk. Treating VaR as coherent is dangerous exactly where danger lives.

3.4When VaR Punishes Diversification

An example makes the subadditivity failure concrete. Consider two corporate bonds, each with face value $100. Each independently defaults with probability 4%, in which case you lose the full $100; otherwise you lose nothing. Look at the 95% VaR (α = 0.95, so the worst 5% of outcomes count as the tail).

Each bond alone: a single bond keeps its value with probability 96% and loses $100 with probability 4%. Since that 4% chance of loss is smaller than the 5% tail, the loss event falls just outside the worst-5% boundary — so the 95th percentile of the loss is $0. Thus VaR0.95 of one bond = $0.

The diversified pair: hold both bonds. The probability that both survive is 0.96² = 92.16%, so P(loss = $0) = 92.16%, which is now below 95%. That means $0 is no longer enough to cover the best 95% of outcomes — the 95th-percentile loss has been pushed up. The next loss level is $100 (one default), and P(loss ≤ $100) = P(at most one default) = 0.9216 + 2 × 0.04 × 0.96 = 99.84% ≥ 95%. So the 95th-percentile loss is $100, giving VaR0.95 of the pair = $100.

Put the two facts side by side and the contradiction is stark:

VaR(bond A) + VaR(bond B) = $0 + $0 = $0  <  VaR(A + B) = $100Subadditivity demands ρ(A + B) ≤ ρ(A) + ρ(B). Here VaR does the opposite: the diversified book is measured as riskier than the sum of its parts. VaR has punished diversification.

This is not a contrived edge case — it is the generic behavior of VaR for portfolios of independent, large-loss events (credit books, insurance, sold-tail options). Expected Shortfall does not have this problem. ES at 95% averages the actual losses in the worst 5%, so it sees the real $100 exposures in each bond individually and adds them sensibly; the merged book's ES comes out no larger than the sum, honoring diversification. The axiom isn't decoration — it is the difference between a risk number you can build a firm on and one you can't.

✦ Worked Example — VaR and ES for a normal P&L

A desk's daily P&L is approximately normal with mean μ = $0 and daily volatility σ = $2.0M. Compute the 99% VaR and the 99% Expected Shortfall, and compare the cushion each implies.

  1. Set the tail: 99% confidence means α = 0.99, so we look at the worst 1% of days. The standard-normal 99th percentile is z0.99 ≈ 2.326.
  2. VaR: VaR0.99 = μ + σ·zα = 0 + 2.0M × 2.326 = $4.65M. On 99 days out of 100 the loss stays under this.
  3. ES via the normal formula: ES0.99 = μ + σ·φ(zα)/(1 − α). Here φ(2.326) ≈ 0.0267 and 1 − α = 0.01, so the multiplier is 0.0267/0.01 = 2.665.
  4. ES: ES0.99 = 0 + 2.0M × 2.665 = $5.33M — the average loss on the worst 1% of days.
  5. Interpret: ES exceeds VaR by 2.0M × (2.665 − 2.326) ≈ $0.68M, about 15% more capital. For a thin-tailed normal that gap is modest; swap in a fat-tailed return model and the same ES calculation would balloon while VaR barely moves — which is the whole reason to track ES.

🛠️ Hands-On: estimate VaR and ES from a sample

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + SciPy — the install block lives in Lecture 2). We simulate a fat-tailed daily-loss sample, then read VaR off as a percentile and ES as the mean of the losses beyond it. Watch how far ES sits above VaR once the tail is heavy.

# es_var.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
alpha = 0.99                       # 99% confidence level

# fat-tailed daily losses: Student-t, scaled to ~2% daily vol
nu = 4                             # 4 degrees of freedom = heavy tails
losses = rng.standard_t(nu, size=1_000_000) * 0.02

# VaR = the alpha-quantile of the loss distribution
var = np.quantile(losses, alpha)

# ES = average of all losses at or beyond the VaR cutoff
es = losses[losses >= var].mean()

print(f"99% VaR : {var:.4f}")          # the cliff edge
print(f"99% ES  : {es:.4f}")           # the average drop beyond it
print(f"ES / VaR ratio : {es/var:.2f}")  # > 1; grows as tails fatten

With heavy Student-t tails (nu = 4) the ES/VaR ratio comes out well above 1 — far higher than the 1.15 a normal would give. Re-run with rng.standard_normal instead and the ratio collapses toward 1.15: same VaR machinery, but ES is the metric that actually reports the heaviness of the tail. That difference is what regulators bought into.

🔗 Where this is heading

The move from VaR to Expected Shortfall is the headline change in modern bank capital rules: the regulatory framework that governs trading-book capital replaced 99% VaR with 97.5% ES precisely because ES is coherent and tail-aware. Later in this course we'll use ES inside portfolio optimization — minimizing CVaR turns out to be a clean linear program solvable with CVXPY, which is why a coherent measure matters for allocation, not just reporting. It also threads back to the fat-tailed distributions of Course 1 and forward to the market-making inventory risk we'll manage in the back half of this course, where the cost of a rare adverse fill is exactly a tail you must price.

Q1. Two portfolios have an identical 99% VaR of $10M. What can Expected Shortfall reveal that VaR cannot?
A. Which portfolio has the higher expected return
B. How severe the losses are on average in the worst 1% of days
C. The exact probability of any given loss
D. Which portfolio has lower transaction costs
Show answer

B. ES averages the losses beyond the VaR cutoff, so it distinguishes a mild tail from a catastrophic one even when VaR is identical. VaR and ES are both loss measures, not return (A) or cost (D) measures, and neither gives the full probability of every loss (C) — they summarize the tail.

Q2. Which axiom of a coherent risk measure does Value at Risk violate in general?
A. Monotonicity
B. Translation invariance
C. Positive homogeneity
D. Subadditivity
Show answer

D. VaR can charge more for a merged book than for its parts, breaking subadditivity — so it can penalize diversification. VaR does satisfy the other three (monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity), which is why it's so close to coherent yet fails the one axiom that matters most.

Q3. At the same confidence level α, how does Expected Shortfall compare to VaR?
A. ES is always ≥ VaR, with a larger gap for heavier tails
B. ES is always ≤ VaR
C. ES equals VaR for every distribution
D. ES and VaR are unrelated quantities
Show answer

A. ES averages losses that are all at or beyond the VaR cutoff, so it can't be smaller (B is backwards). They're equal only in a degenerate limit, not for every distribution (C), and they're tightly related — ES is literally an average of VaR over deeper levels (D is wrong). The fatter the tail, the wider the ES − VaR gap.

Q4. Two bonds each lose $100 with probability 2% (independently), else $0. Why can their combined 95% VaR exceed the sum of the individual 95% VaRs?
A. Because combining the bonds raises their expected returns
B. Because each bond's 2% loss falls inside the best 95%, giving each a VaR of $0, but the pair's tail probability pushes the percentile loss to $100
C. Because VaR is computed with the wrong volatility
D. Because Expected Shortfall was used instead
Show answer

B. Each single bond's loss event (2%) sits below the 5% tail boundary, so its 95th-percentile loss is $0; combining them shifts the percentile so the pair's 95% VaR is $100 > $0 + $0. That's a direct subadditivity violation. It has nothing to do with returns (A) or volatility inputs (C), and ES (D) would not show this — ES is subadditive.

Q5. A key reason regulators shifted trading-book capital from 99% VaR toward 97.5% Expected Shortfall is that ES…
A. is always a smaller, more lenient number
B. requires no distributional assumptions at all
C. is coherent and captures the severity of losses in the tail, not just the cutoff
D. can be computed without any historical data
Show answer

C. ES is subadditive (coherent) and averages the whole tail, so it rewards diversification and reflects tail severity. It is generally larger, not smaller, than VaR (A is false); it still depends on the loss distribution you assume (B false); and like VaR it needs data or a model to estimate (D false).

✓ What you learned

  • VaR is just a tail percentile — communicable but blind to the shape of losses beyond the cutoff and not subadditive.
  • Expected Shortfall (CVaR) is the average loss in the worst tail; it is always ≥ VaR and widens with heavier tails.
  • A coherent risk measure obeys four axioms — monotonicity, translation invariance, positive homogeneity, and subadditivity.
  • ES is coherent; VaR violates subadditivity, so VaR can penalize diversification (the two-bond example: $0 + $0 < $100).
  • You computed VaR and ES from a sample in NumPy and saw why regulators moved toward 97.5% ES for tail-aware capital.
