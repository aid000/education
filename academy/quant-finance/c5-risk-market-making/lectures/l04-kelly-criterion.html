The Kelly Criterion & Optimal Bet Sizing

Having an edge is half the battle — how much of your capital you stake on it decides whether that edge compounds into a fortune or gets vaporized by a single bad streak.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why long-run growth is governed by the geometric mean of returns, not the arithmetic mean.
  • Derive and apply the Kelly fraction f* = edge/odds for a discrete bet, and f* = μ/σ² for a continuous investment.
  • Show that maximizing expected log-wealth is equivalent to maximizing the long-run growth rate of capital.
  • Describe the sharply asymmetric penalty for over-betting past Kelly, and why growth turns negative beyond 2f*.
  • Justify why practitioners deploy fractional Kelly (typically ¼ to ½) given parameter uncertainty and drawdown tolerance.

4.1Growth, Not Average: Why Sizing Is Its Own Problem

Suppose you have found a genuine edge — a bet, a trade, a strategy with positive expected value. Course 4 taught you to find the edge; this lecture answers the question that decides whether you survive to enjoy it: how much do you bet? Bet too little and your edge barely compounds. Bet too much and a normal losing streak wipes you out — even when every individual wager was favorable.

The trap is that maximizing the arithmetic expected value of each bet does not maximize the growth of your capital, because returns multiply rather than add. If you bet your whole bankroll on a coin that pays +100% on heads and −100% on tails, your expected wealth per round grows — yet a single tail takes you to zero, forever. Wealth is a product of gross returns, so what matters is the geometric mean.

Wn = W0 · (1+r1)(1+r2)···(1+rn)Capital compounds multiplicatively; one factor of zero destroys the entire product no matter how large the others were.

Take logarithms and the product becomes a sum, so the law of large numbers applies to log-returns. The long-run growth rate g is the expected log-return per round:

g = (1/n) ln(Wn/W0) = (1/n) Σ ln(1+ri) ⟶ E[ ln(1+r) ]Maximizing g — the growth-optimal objective — means maximizing expected log-wealth. This is the entire idea behind Kelly.
AnalogyTwo climbers ascend the same mountain. One takes long, aggressive strides and occasionally slips all the way back to base camp; the other takes steady steps sized so a slip never costs more than a little ground. Even if the aggressive climber's average step is bigger, the steady climber reaches the summit first — because altitude, like wealth, is something you keep multiplying on, and you can't recover from falling to zero.

4.2The Kelly Fraction for a Discrete Bet

Consider the cleanest case: a favorable bet you can repeat. With probability p you win and receive b times your stake (net odds b-to-1); with probability q = 1−p you lose the stake. You bet a fraction f of your current bankroll each round. After a win your wealth multiplies by (1+bf); after a loss, by (1−f). The expected growth rate is:

g(f) = p·ln(1 + bf) + q·ln(1 − f)

To find the growth-maximizing f, differentiate with respect to f and set it to zero:

g′(f) = pb/(1+bf) − q/(1−f) = 0  ⟹   f* = (pb − q) / b = p − q/b

Read the formula as edge over odds. The numerator pb − q is exactly the bet's expected net profit per unit staked — its edge. Divide by the payout odds b and you get the optimal fraction. A bigger edge → bet more; longer odds (bigger b, more dangerous payoff profile) → bet less. If the edge is zero or negative, f* ≤ 0: don't bet at all.

f* = edge / odds    (bet nothing if edge ≤ 0)For an even-money bet (b = 1), this simplifies to f* = p − q = 2p − 1 — just your probability edge over a coin flip.
Common mistakeDo not confuse the net odds b with gross payout. If a winning $1 bet returns $1.80 total, your profit is $0.80, so b = 0.8, not 1.8. Plugging the gross figure into f* = p − q/b inflates the recommended stake and pushes you toward over-betting — the single most expensive error in this lecture.

4.3Continuous Kelly: f* = μ/σ² and the Growth Curve

Markets rarely offer clean yes/no bets. A trade has a continuous return with mean μ (excess of the risk-free rate) and variance σ² per period. Allocate a fraction f of capital to it; the rest earns roughly zero excess. Your per-period log-growth is approximately:

g(f) ≈ f·μ − ½ f²σ²A second-order (Taylor) approximation of E[ln(1+f·r)] valid for small returns — the workhorse form used across continuous finance.

This is a downward-opening parabola in f. The linear term f·μ is the reward from your edge; the quadratic term −½f²σ² is the volatility tax that compounding levies — and it grows with the square of your bet size. Maximize by setting the derivative μ − f σ² to zero:

f* = μ / σ²The continuous Kelly fraction. Note μ/σ² = (μ/σ)·(1/σ) = Sharpe ratio divided by volatility — Kelly scales your bet by edge and shrinks it by risk.

Plug f* back in and the maximum achievable growth rate is g(f*) = μ²/(2σ²) = ½·Sharpe² — the squared Sharpe ratio is, quite literally, the compounding speed limit of a strategy. The figure below shows the parabola: growth rises to a peak at f*, then falls, crossing back to zero growth at f = 2f* and turning negative — outright wealth destruction — beyond that.

AnalogyThink of f* like the redline on a car's tachometer. Driving right at the redline (full Kelly) extracts maximum power but is brutal on the engine and unforgiving of a pothole. Cruising at half the redline (½ Kelly) gets you almost the same speed with a far smoother, more durable ride — and you never blow the engine on a single bad bump.

4.4Over-Betting and Why Practitioners Use Fractional Kelly

The parabola is steep and dangerously asymmetric. Underbetting at ½f* still earns ~75% of peak growth; overbetting at 1.5f* earns the same as ½f* but with vastly more volatility, and at 2f* your edge and your volatility tax exactly cancel — zero growth despite a real advantage. Past 2f*, growth goes negative: you are guaranteed, in the long run, to go broke with a winning strategy. Because the cost of an error is so lopsided, you always want to err on the side of under-betting.

The three reasons for fractional Kelly

1. Parameter uncertainty. Full Kelly assumes you know p, b, μ, σ exactly. You never do — you estimate them, and estimates of μ are notoriously noisy. Over-estimating your edge by a factor of two and betting full Kelly puts you at 2f* — the zero-growth cliff. Betting half Kelly leaves a cushion against this estimation error.

2. Drawdown tolerance. Full Kelly is wild: the theory implies you'll spend a large fraction of time at half your peak wealth or below. Real allocators, traders, and risk committees cannot stomach 50% drawdowns. Half-Kelly roughly quarters the variance of growth while giving up only ~25% of the growth rate.

3. Multiple simultaneous edges. A trading book runs many positions at once; their risks add, and correlated drawdowns can stack. Sizing each at full Kelly ignores this overlap and over-levers the whole book. Fractional Kelly is a simple, robust haircut.

fused = c · f*  (0 < c ≤ 1, typically c ≈ ¼ to ½)Growth at fraction c of Kelly is g = (c − ½c²)·g(f*) → e.g. c = ½ keeps 75% of max growth; c = ¼ keeps 43.75%, at a quarter of the risk.
Common mistakeBelieving "Kelly is the optimal bet, so bet full Kelly." Kelly maximizes asymptotic growth under perfectly known probabilities and an infinite horizon — neither holds in practice. Given noisy estimates and finite drawdown tolerance, full Kelly is almost always too aggressive. Treat f* as a ceiling, not a target.
✦ Worked Example — sizing a favorable repeated bet

A pricing model says a tradeable event hits 55% of the time. A winning $1 stake returns $1 net profit (even money, b = 1); a loss costs the $1. You can recycle capital into this bet repeatedly. How much of your bankroll should you risk per round?

  1. Identify the inputs: p = 0.55, q = 0.45, net odds b = 1.
  2. Full Kelly fraction: f* = (pb − q)/b = (0.55·1 − 0.45)/1 = 0.10. Stake 10% of the bankroll per round.
  3. Peak growth rate: g(f*) = p·ln(1+f*) + q·ln(1−f*) = 0.55·ln(1.10) + 0.45·ln(0.90) = 0.55(0.09531) + 0.45(−0.10536) = 0.05242 − 0.04741 = 0.00501 per round (≈ 0.50% compounded growth each bet).
  4. Check the over-betting cliff: 2f* = 0.20. Betting 20% per round gives g = 0.55·ln(1.20) + 0.45·ln(0.80) = 0.55(0.18232) + 0.45(−0.22314) = 0.10028 − 0.10041 ≈ −0.0001 ≈ 0 — confirming zero growth at twice Kelly, despite the 55% edge.
  5. Apply fractional Kelly: a desk wary of estimation error bets c = ½, so fused = 0.05 (5% per round). Growth = 0.55·ln(1.05) + 0.45·ln(0.95) = 0.026824 − 0.023082 = 0.00374 — about 75% of the peak 0.00501, with roughly a quarter of the variance. A far more comfortable ride for almost the same compounding.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate Kelly, half-Kelly, and over-betting

Reusing the Week-1/2 environment (NumPy + Matplotlib — the install block lives in the Course 1, Lecture 1 setup if you skipped it). We'll race four bet sizes through 1,000 rounds of the 55%/even-money bet above and watch full Kelly win on median growth while over-betting collapses.

# kelly_race.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(11)
p, b = 0.55, 1.0                       # 55% win, even-money net odds
f_star = (p*b - (1-p)) / b             # = 0.10  (full Kelly)

rounds, paths = 1_000, 5_000
fractions = {"half (0.05)": 0.5*f_star,
             "full (0.10)": f_star,
             "1.5x (0.15)": 1.5*f_star,
             "2x  (0.20)": 2.0*f_star}  # the zero-growth cliff

for name, f in fractions.items():
    wins = rng.random((paths, rounds)) < p          # True = win
    step = np.where(wins, 1 + b*f, 1 - f)           # gross return per round
    wealth = step.prod(axis=1)                      # final wealth, W0 = 1
    g = np.log(wealth).mean() / rounds              # realized growth rate
    print(f"{name:>12}:  growth/round = {g:+.5f}   median W = {np.median(wealth):8.3f}")

# Theory check: g(f*) should be ~0.00501; the 2x line should sit near 0.0

You'll see full Kelly post the highest growth rate, half-Kelly trail slightly with a dramatically tighter spread of outcomes, and the 2× line hover at zero growth — proof that doubling the optimal stake throws away the entire edge. Swap in f = 2.5*f_star to watch median wealth fall below 1: a winning bet that loses money through pure over-sizing.

🔗 Where this is heading

Kelly is the single-asset seed of portfolio construction. Next week's Mean-Variance Optimization generalizes f* = μ/σ² to many correlated assets: the optimal weight vector becomes Σ⁻¹μ — Kelly with a covariance matrix in place of a scalar variance. The same μ²/(2σ²) growth becomes ½·(Sharpe of the whole book)², linking sizing directly to the Sharpe ratio you'll optimize in mean-variance and risk-parity frameworks. Fractional Kelly reappears as shrinkage and volatility targeting in production risk systems. Capstone checkpoint (Week 4): add a Kelly-based position sizer to your market-making engine — compute f* from your model's edge and quoted spread, cap it at ½f*, and log realized vs. theoretical growth across a backtest. That sizer becomes the bridge to the portfolio-level allocator you build in Week 5.

Q1. The Kelly criterion maximizes long-run wealth growth by maximizing which quantity each round?
A. The arithmetic expected return, E[r]
B. The probability of winning, p
C. The expected log-wealth, E[ln(1+r)]
D. The variance of returns, σ²
Show answer

C. Because wealth compounds multiplicatively, the long-run growth rate is the expected log-return, so Kelly maximizes E[ln(1+r)]. Maximizing arithmetic E[r] (A) leads to over-betting and ruin; maximizing p (B) ignores payoffs and stake size; minimizing or maximizing variance (D) is not the objective at all.

Q2. A bet wins with probability p = 0.6 and pays net odds b = 2 (win $2 profit per $1 staked). What is the full Kelly fraction?
A. 0.40
B. 0.20
C. 0.60
D. 0.80
Show answer

A. f* = p − q/b = 0.6 − 0.4/2 = 0.6 − 0.2 = 0.40. Answer B is p − q (the even-money formula, ignoring b = 2); C is just p; D over-states the edge. Always divide the edge by the net odds.

Q3. For a continuous investment with excess mean μ and variance σ², what happens to the long-run growth rate if you bet exactly twice the Kelly fraction, 2f* = 2μ/σ²?
A. Growth doubles relative to full Kelly
B. Growth returns to zero
C. Growth equals the peak μ²/(2σ²)
D. Growth becomes infinite
Show answer

B. Plug f = 2μ/σ² into g = fμ − ½f²σ² = 2μ²/σ² − ½·(4μ²/σ⁴)·σ² = 2μ²/σ² − 2μ²/σ² = 0. The reward and the volatility tax cancel exactly. Growth never doubles (A) — the parabola falls past the peak; the peak (C) occurs at f*, not 2f*; nothing here is infinite (D).

Q4. The continuous Kelly fraction is f* = μ/σ². If you double your estimate of the edge μ while σ stays fixed, the recommended bet size…
A. stays the same
B. is cut in half
C. doubles
D. quadruples
Show answer

C. f* is linear in μ, so doubling μ doubles f*. This is exactly why over-estimating μ is dangerous: a 2× error in the edge pushes you to 2f*, the zero-growth cliff. Halving (B) would happen if you doubled σ²-related risk; quadrupling (D) would require a quadratic dependence, which Kelly does not have in μ.

Q5. Which is the best reason most professional traders bet a fraction of full Kelly (e.g. ¼ to ½) rather than full Kelly?
A. Fractional Kelly produces a higher long-run growth rate than full Kelly
B. Full Kelly is illegal for institutional accounts
C. Fractional Kelly guarantees you can never lose money
D. Parameter estimates are noisy and full-Kelly drawdowns are intolerable, so the asymmetric over-betting risk favors a cushion
Show answer

D. Because the growth parabola is steep and asymmetric, the cost of over-betting (from over-estimating a noisy edge) far exceeds the cost of under-betting, and full-Kelly drawdowns are too violent — so a fraction adds a safety cushion. A is false: full Kelly has the highest growth by construction; B is fiction; C is false — fractional Kelly still loses in bad streaks, it just survives them better.

✓ What you learned

  • Long-run wealth is driven by the geometric mean, so growth-optimal sizing maximizes E[ln(1+r)], not arithmetic E[r].
  • For a discrete bet, the Kelly fraction is f* = (pb − q)/b = edge/odds; bet nothing when the edge is non-positive.
  • For a continuous investment, f* = μ/σ², giving maximum growth μ²/(2σ²) = ½·Sharpe².
  • Growth peaks at f*, returns to zero at 2f*, and turns negative beyond — over-betting destroys capital even with a real edge.
  • Practitioners use fractional Kelly (¼–½) for parameter noise, drawdown tolerance, and correlated multi-position risk — and you scoped a Kelly sizer for the capstone engine.
