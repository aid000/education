Measuring Risk, VaR & Expected Shortfall · Weeks 1–3
1. A stock has a daily volatility of 1%. Assuming independent daily returns, its approximate annual volatility (252 trading days) is…
Answer: D. Volatility scales with √T, not T: 0.01 × √252 ≈ 0.01 × 15.87 ≈ 0.159. Multiplying by 252 instead (B) confuses variance scaling with standard-deviation scaling.
2. In risk management, the loss over a period is conventionally defined as…
Answer: A. Risk managers flip the sign on purpose so that money lost is a positive number and the dangerous outcomes sit in the right tail. B leaves losses negative; C and D discard information or confuse a loss with a measure of spread.
3. A desk reports a "one-day 99% VaR of $3 million." What does this mean?
Answer: C. VaR is the quantile the loss stays under on 99% of days, so it is exceeded on roughly the worst 1%. It is never an exact prediction (A), an average (B), nor a cap on the worst case (D) — the loss beyond VaR can run far larger.
4. A portfolio worth $8,000,000 has a daily return std σ = 1.25% and zero mean. Using the parametric method, the one-day 99% VaR (z₀.₉₉ = 2.326) is closest to…
Answer: B. VaR = z·σ·V = 2.326 × 0.0125 × $8,000,000 ≈ $232,600. A is just 1σ×V (forgets the z-score); C is roughly 5× too large; D is 10× too small.
5. Your one-day 95% VaR is $500,000. Under the square-root-of-time rule (i.i.d., zero-drift returns), the 4-day VaR is approximately…
Answer: D. Scale by √t = √4 = 2, so $500,000 × 2 = $1,000,000. Multiplying by t directly (×4 = $2,000,000, B) is the classic error — variance scales with t, VaR scales with √t.
6. At the same confidence level α, how does Expected Shortfall (ES) compare to Value at Risk (VaR)?
Answer: B. ES averages every loss at or beyond the VaR cutoff, so it can't be smaller (A is backwards), and the gap widens as the tail gets heavier — they are tightly related (D is wrong), not equal in general (C).
7. Which axiom of a coherent risk measure does Value at Risk violate in general?
Answer: A. VaR can charge more for a merged book than for its parts, breaking subadditivity and penalizing diversification. VaR satisfies the other three axioms, which is why it is so close to coherent yet fails the one that matters most.
8. Two corporate bonds each independently default with probability 3% (losing the full $100 face value; otherwise losing $0). Using 95% VaR (the worst 5% as the tail), the VaR of a single bond held alone is…
Answer: D. Since 3% < 5%, the default event falls just outside the worst-5% tail boundary, so the 95th-percentile loss is $0. (Pooling two such bonds pushes the combined VaR above $0 — VaR's classic subadditivity failure.)
The Kelly Criterion & Bet Sizing · Week 4
9. The Kelly criterion maximizes long-run wealth growth by maximizing which quantity each round?
Answer: C. Wealth compounds multiplicatively, so the long-run growth rate is the expected log-return. Maximizing arithmetic E[r] (A) leads to over-betting and ruin; p alone (B) ignores payoff size; variance (D) is not the objective.
10. A bet wins with probability p = 0.7 and pays net odds b = 3 (profit $3 per $1 staked on a win). The full Kelly fraction f* is…
Answer: A. f* = p − q/b = 0.7 − 0.3/3 = 0.7 − 0.1 = 0.60. B is just p (ignoring odds); C is just q; D over-states the edge.
11. For a continuous investment with excess mean μ and variance σ², betting exactly twice the Kelly fraction (2f* = 2μ/σ²) produces a long-run growth rate of…
Answer: B. Plugging f = 2μ/σ² into g = fμ − ½f²σ² gives 2μ²/σ² − 2μ²/σ² = 0 — the edge and the volatility tax cancel exactly, despite a genuinely favorable bet.
Mean-Variance Optimization & Risk Parity · Weeks 5–6
12. Two assets, each with volatility σ, are combined 50/50, giving portfolio variance ½σ²(1 + ρ). For which correlation ρ is the diversification benefit greatest?
Answer: D. ½σ²(1+ρ) is smallest when ρ is most negative; at ρ = −1 the variance collapses to zero (a perfect hedge). ρ = +1 (A) gives no benefit at all.
13. On the risk-return plane, the tangency portfolio is best described as the portfolio that…
Answer: C. The tangency portfolio is exactly the maximum-Sharpe risky mix. Lowest variance (A) describes the minimum-variance portfolio, a different point; equal weighting (B) is a naive heuristic; maximizing return alone (D) ignores risk.
14. Why is the minimum-variance portfolio often preferred in practice over the full mean-variance optimum?
Answer: B. The MVP minimizes wᵗΣw subject only to the weights summing to one — expected returns never enter, sidestepping the "error-maximizer" problem. It does not maximize return (A) and absolutely needs Σ (C).
15. The defining feature of a risk-parity (equal-risk-contribution) portfolio is that…
Answer: A. Risk parity equalizes risk contributions, not dollar weights (B). It is generally neither the minimum-variance portfolio (C) nor the max-Sharpe tangency portfolio (D) — it deliberately overweights low-volatility assets relative to both.
16. Why is risk parity considered more robust than mean-variance optimization out of sample?
Answer: D. Mean-variance is unstable because tiny errors in μ swing the weights wildly; risk parity drops μ entirely and uses only Σ, which is estimated far more reliably. It still needs Σ (B is false) and scales to many assets (C is false).
17. A factor model writes Σ ≈ BΩBᵗ + D. The main practical reason to prefer this over estimating Σ directly is that it…
Answer: A. A full Σ for n assets needs ~n²/2 noisy entries; the factor form needs only loadings, a small factor covariance, and a diagonal — far fewer parameters, hence more stable. It uses fewer parameters, not more (D), and assets remain correlated through shared factors (B is false).
Market Microstructure & Market-Making Basics · Weeks 7–8
18. An order book shows a best bid of 75.20 and a best ask of 75.26. The bid–ask spread and the mid price are…
Answer: C. Spread = ask − bid = 75.26 − 75.20 = 0.06; mid = (75.26 + 75.20)/2 = 75.23. B uses the ask itself as the mid; D adds the two prices instead of subtracting and averaging.
19. Two sell limit orders rest at the same best ask price; order X arrived first, order Y arrived second. Under price–time priority, when a matching buy order arrives…
Answer: D. Price–time priority breaks ties at the same price by arrival time — first in, first out — so X fills first. A and B invent size-based or pro-rata rules the standard book does not use; C reverses the rule.
20. A market maker posting a passive limit order earns the spread as compensation mainly because…
Answer: B. The maker supplies immediacy and is exposed to adverse selection — getting filled precisely when informed flow pushes the price against the quote — so the spread compensates for that. A and C are false, and market orders are central to any book (D is false).
21. A market maker is accidentally left long 400 shares of a volatile stock. This inventory's danger is best described as…
Answer: C. The maker has no directional view, so the position's expected return is zero — but its variance (≈ |q|·σ·√t) can produce a large loss or gain. It is not a guaranteed loss (A) or a positive-edge bet (B), and hedging is neither free nor instant (D).
Optimal Quoting & the Capstone · Weeks 9–10
22. A market maker is currently long inventory (q > 0). Relative to the mid-price, the reservation price r is…
Answer: A. With q > 0, the term −q·γσ²(T−t) is negative, pulling r below the mid; quoting around r makes the ask more competitive so the long gets sold off. D is the classic trap — the skew is risk management, not a price forecast.
23. Which change makes the Avellaneda–Stoikov optimal spread, γσ²(T−t) + (2/γ)ln(1+γ/κ), wider?
Answer: D. The inventory-risk term γσ²(T−t) rises with both γ and the time remaining, widening the spread. Lower σ (A) and lower γ (B) shrink it; the spread's width does not depend on the current inventory level (C) — inventory drives the quotes' skew, not their width.
24. A market maker holds long inventory while the mid drifts down all afternoon with no new trades. How should its P&L be recorded?
Answer: B. Total P&L = cash + q·mid. With q > 0 and the mid falling, the mark-to-market term shrinks, so P&L drops even with zero trades. A is the classic mistake of booking only realized cash; D is wrong — marking to market is well-defined every tick.
25. Two quoting strategies from the capstone simulator share an identical 95% VaR, but strategy X loses far more than strategy Y in its worst 1% of paths. Which statement is correct?
Answer: C. ES averages all losses beyond the VaR cutoff, so it sees the heavier deep tail of X even when the cutoff itself matches — exactly VaR's blind spot. D is incoherent: the two strategies' VaRs are equal, not lower for Y.
