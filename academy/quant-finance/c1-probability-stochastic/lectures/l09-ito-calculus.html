Week 9 · Lecture 9

Intro to Itô Calculus

Brownian paths are too jagged for ordinary calculus — so we build a new one, where the square of a tiny random wiggle stops being negligible and rewrites the chain rule itself.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Explain why ordinary calculus breaks down on Brownian paths — they are continuous everywhere yet differentiable nowhere.
  • Describe the Itô integral as a non-anticipating Riemann-style sum that evaluates the integrand at the left endpoint of each step.
  • Internalize the central rule of stochastic calculus: (dW)² = dt, with dt·dt and dt·dW vanishing.
  • Apply Itô's lemma to transform a function of a stochastic process, including the extra second-derivative term.
  • Derive the log dynamics of geometric Brownian motion and explain the −½σ² drift correction in finance terms.

9.1Why Ordinary Calculus Fails

In Week 8 you met Brownian motion Wt: a continuous random path whose increments over disjoint intervals are independent and Normal, with Wt+Δt − Wt ~ Normal(0, Δt). That last fact hides something violent. The size of a typical increment over a window Δt is its standard deviation, √Δt — not Δt. Over a tiny step, √Δt is enormous compared to Δt itself.

Ordinary calculus rests on the idea that over a small step a smooth function looks like a straight line: Δf ≈ f′(x)·Δx, and the leftover is order (Δx)², which you happily throw away. The slope f′ exists because Δf/Δx settles down to a limit. For a Brownian path that ratio is ΔW/Δt ≈ √Δt/Δt = 1/√Δt, which blows up as Δt → 0. The path is continuous but has no derivative at any point — it is infinitely jagged at every scale.

ΔW ~ √Δt  ⟹   ΔW/Δt ~ 1/√Δt → ∞A Brownian path is continuous everywhere and differentiable nowhere — dW/dt simply does not exist.

So we cannot write dW/dt and integrate it as a normal velocity. The escape is to stop chasing slopes and work directly with increments dW, defining a brand-new integral and a brand-new chain rule. That machinery is Itô calculus, and it is the language every continuous-time pricing model is written in.

AnalogyZoom into a stock's tick-by-tick price chart and it never smooths out — each magnification reveals fresh zig-zags, like a coastline that looks equally crinkly from orbit or from the beach. A smooth road has a well-defined direction at every point; a Brownian coastline does not. You cannot ask "which way is the price heading right now?" — only "how far did it travel over this interval?"

9.2The Itô Integral: Summing Bets, Not Slopes

If we cannot differentiate W, can we still integrate against it? We want to make sense of ∫ f(t) dWt — the running total of a quantity f multiplied by the random kicks dW. The finance picture makes the definition obvious: imagine you hold ft units of an asset whose price moves by dWt. Your gain over a step is (position) × (price change). Sum those gains.

We chop [0, T] into steps t0 < t1 < … < tn and add up the increments. The crucial choice is when we read the integrand. Itô insists you evaluate f at the left endpoint of each step — the start, before the random move happens:

∫₀ᵀ ft dWt = lim Σ fti · ( Wti+1 − Wti )The integrand f is sampled at the start of each interval — you commit your position before the dice are thrown.

This non-anticipating rule is not a technicality; it is the entire financial meaning. You must size your position using only information available now; you cannot peek at the price change you are about to earn. A consequence we leaned on in the martingale week (Week 6): an Itô integral of a non-anticipating integrand is a martingale — it has zero expected gain, E[∫ f dW] = 0, because each step multiplies a known position by a fair, mean-zero kick.

Common mistakeEvaluating the integrand at the midpoint or right endpoint gives a different answer (the Stratonovich integral), and it quietly lets you trade on the future. In finance always use the Itô (left-point) convention — otherwise your "strategy" earns money by knowing the next price move, which is not a strategy, it is cheating.

9.3The Rule That Changes Everything: (dW)² = dt

Here is the single fact that separates stochastic calculus from the ordinary kind. Over a step Δt, the Brownian increment ΔW has mean 0 and variance Δt. Square it: (ΔW)² has mean Δt. The remarkable part — provable from the Normal distribution — is that as the step shrinks, (ΔW)² does not just average Δt, it becomes essentially equal to Δt, with vanishing randomness. The squared wiggles, summed up, are deterministic. We write this as:

(dW)² = dt,    dt·dt = 0,    dt·dW = 0The "multiplication table" of stochastic calculus. The square of noise survives; everything with two or more dt-factors vanishes.

Why does this break ordinary calculus? Recall the Taylor expansion of a smooth f around a step in its argument: Δf ≈ f′·Δx + ½f″·(Δx)² + …. In normal calculus (Δx)² is order (Δt)² and is discarded. But when x is Brownian, (ΔW)² is order Δt — the same order as the term we keep. The second-derivative term refuses to die. That surviving ½f″ term is the whole of Itô's lemma.

9.4Itô's Lemma and the GBM Drift Correction

Suppose a process Xt follows a stochastic differential equation (SDE) with a drift a (the predictable trend per unit time) and a diffusion b (the size of the random kicks):

dXt = a(Xt, t) dt + b(Xt, t) dWt

Now take a smooth function f(Xt, t) — for instance the log of a price, or an option payoff. Itô's lemma is the chain rule for this world. Expand f to second order and apply the table from §9.3: keep f′·dX and ½f″·(dX)², then substitute (dW)² = dt and drop everything with dt·dt or dt·dW. Since (dX)² = b²(dW)² = b²dt, you get:

df = ( ∂f/∂t + a ∂f/∂x + ½ b² ∂²f/∂x² ) dt + b ∂f/∂x dWThe boxed-in extra piece, ½ b² ∂²f/∂x², is the Itô correction — absent from ordinary calculus, present whenever f is nonlinear.

The headline application is geometric Brownian motion (GBM), the standard model for a stock price St with constant expected return μ and volatility σ:

dSt = μ St dt + σ St dWt

Here a = μS and b = σS. Take f = ln S (so ∂f/∂t = 0, ∂f/∂x = 1/S, ∂²f/∂x² = −1/S²). Plug into Itô's lemma:

d(ln S) = ( μ − ½σ² ) dt + σ dWtNaïve calculus would have given just μ dt + σ dW. The −½σ² is the Itô correction — and it is real money.

So the log price is an ordinary Brownian motion with drift (μ − ½σ²) and volatility σ. Two finance lessons fall out. First, the log-price grows linearly with the corrected drift, so log ST is Normal — meaning ST itself is lognormal, never negative, exactly as a price should be. Second, the median compound growth rate of the stock is μ − ½σ², not μ. Volatility is a literal drag on geometric returns: the more a price bounces around, the more its compounded growth lags its average return.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the arithmetic expected return E[ST]/S0 = eμT (still governed by μ) with the typical / median growth e(μ−½σ²)T (governed by the corrected drift). A volatile asset can have a healthy average return μ yet a negative median log-growth — the average is dragged up by a few huge winners while most paths underperform. Forgetting the −½σ² is one of the most expensive mistakes in finance.
AnalogyVolatility drag is the "up-10%, down-10%" trap. Gain 10% then lose 10% and you are not flat — you are at 0.99, down a percent. The arithmetic average move is 0%, but the compounded result is negative, and the gap grows with the size of the swings. That gap is the −½σ² term, made concrete.
✦ Worked Example — median vs. mean growth of a GBM stock

A stock follows GBM with expected return μ = 12% per year and volatility σ = 40% per year, starting at S0 = $100. Over T = 1 year, compare the expected price with the median price.

  1. Apply Itô to ln S: the corrected log-drift is μ − ½σ² = 0.12 − ½(0.40)² = 0.12 − 0.08 = 0.04 per year. Volatility alone eats 8 percentage points of geometric growth.
  2. Distribution of log return: ln(ST/S0) ~ Normal(mean = 0.04, variance = σ²T = 0.16), so std = 0.40.
  3. Median price: the median of a Normal log is its mean, so median ST = 100·e0.04 ≈ 100·1.0408 = $104.08.
  4. Expected (mean) price: for a lognormal, E[ST] = S0·eμT = 100·e0.12 ≈ 100·1.1275 = $112.75. The ½σ² added back inside the exponential cancels the correction, recovering the raw μ.
  5. Read the gap: the average path lands near $112.75, but a typical (median) path only reaches $104.08. The $8.67 gap is volatility drag — most outcomes trail the mean, which is pulled up by rare large winners. Cut σ to 20% and the median jumps to 100·e0.10 ≈ $110.52: lower volatility, higher typical growth, same μ.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate GBM and confirm the −½σ² drift

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Jupyter — install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We simulate many GBM paths by stepping the log-price with Itô's exact log dynamics, then check that the simulated mean log return matches μ − ½σ² and the mean price matches eμT.

# ito_gbm.py — Lecture 9
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(9)
mu, sigma, S0, T = 0.12, 0.40, 100.0, 1.0
n_paths, n_steps = 200_000, 252        # one trading year, daily steps
dt = T / n_steps

# Step the LOG-price: Ito gives d(lnS) = (mu - 0.5*sigma**2) dt + sigma dW
drift = (mu - 0.5 * sigma**2) * dt
dW    = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(dt), size=(n_paths, n_steps))
logS  = np.log(S0) + np.cumsum(drift + sigma * dW, axis=1)
ST    = np.exp(logS[:, -1])            # terminal prices

print("mean log return  :", np.mean(np.log(ST / S0)))   # ~ mu - 0.5*sigma^2 = 0.040
print("theory (corrected):", mu - 0.5 * sigma**2)        # 0.040
print("median price     :", np.median(ST))              # ~ 100*e^0.04  = 104.08
print("mean price       :", np.mean(ST))                # ~ 100*e^0.12  = 112.75

The mean log return lands on 0.040 (the corrected drift), while the mean price lands on ≈112.75 (governed by raw μ). Seeing both numbers come out of one simulation is the −½σ² correction made tangible: log-space and price-space have different drifts, and Itô's lemma is the bridge between them.

🔗 Where this is heading

Itô's lemma is the engine of continuous-time finance. Next week (Week 10) you'll write full SDEs and simulate them by Monte Carlo, turning the discretization above into a general-purpose pricer. In Course 3, applying Itô to a derivative's value V(S, t) and hedging away the dW term yields the Black–Scholes partial differential equation — the −½σ² and the surviving second-derivative term you met here become the famous "gamma" and the diffusion term of that PDE. Interest-rate models (Vasicek, CIR) and stochastic-volatility models (Heston) are all just different choices of drift a and diffusion b fed through the very same lemma.

💬 Discussion

A colleague argues: "If a stock's expected return μ is 12%, then over the long run I should expect my money to compound at roughly 12% per year — the volatility just adds noise around that trend." Using what you now know about Itô's lemma and the −½σ² correction, is your colleague right? Reframe their claim precisely, and explain to them — in plain language a non-quant could follow — what volatility actually does to long-run typical wealth versus average wealth.

Show a model response

The colleague is conflating two different averages. The number μ = 12% is the arithmetic expected return: it governs the mean ending wealth, E[ST] = S0eμT. But "what my money compounds at" over the long run is a geometric growth rate, and Itô's lemma tells us the log-price drifts at μ − ½σ², not μ. With σ = 40%, that correction is 8 points — so a typical (median) path compounds at only about 4% per year, not 12%.

In plain language: gaining and losing the same percentage doesn't leave you flat — "+10% then −10%" lands you at 0.99, a net loss. Volatility creates a structural drag on compounding, and the drag grows like σ². The 12% mean isn't a lie, but it's propped up by a handful of spectacular winning paths; most individual paths fall short of it. So the right summary is: average wealth grows near μ, but typical wealth grows near μ − ½σ². For someone living one realized path, the median is the honest expectation, and reducing volatility (without lowering μ) genuinely raises long-run typical wealth.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Why can't we use ordinary calculus to write dW/dt for a Brownian path?
A. Because Brownian paths are discontinuous, so no limit exists
B. Because the increment ΔW scales like √Δt, so ΔW/Δt blows up as Δt → 0
C. Because W is always negative
D. Because dt is not a real number
Show answer

B. A typical increment has size √Δt (its standard deviation), so ΔW/Δt ~ 1/√Δt → ∞ — no finite derivative exists. The path is actually continuous everywhere (so A is wrong), it is not sign-restricted (C), and dt is a perfectly ordinary infinitesimal (D); the problem is purely the √Δt scaling.

Q2. In the definition of the Itô integral ∫ ft dWt, the integrand f is evaluated at…
A. the right endpoint of each step
B. the midpoint of each step
C. the left endpoint of each step, before the random move
D. the maximum of W over each step
Show answer

C. Itô uses the left (start-of-interval) value — the non-anticipating convention, so your position is set before the price moves. The right endpoint or midpoint (A, B) is the Stratonovich integral and would let you trade on the future; the running maximum (D) is not used at all.

Q3. What is the central rule of stochastic calculus that makes the second-order Taylor term survive?
A. (dW)² = dt
B. (dW)² = (dt)²
C. dW = dt
D. (dt)² = dW
Show answer

A. (dW)² = dt: the square of the random increment is of order dt, the same order as the time term, so ½f″(dW)² does not vanish. (dt)² (B) is much smaller and is discarded; dW ≠ dt since dW is random with size √dt (C); and (D) is dimensionally nonsense.

Q4. For geometric Brownian motion dS = μS dt + σS dW, applying Itô's lemma to ln S gives a drift of…
A. μ
B. μ + ½σ²
C. μ − ½σ²
D. ½σ² − μ
Show answer

C. With f = ln S, ∂²f/∂x² = −1/S², so the Itô term ½b²f″ = ½σ²S²(−1/S²) = −½σ² subtracts from μ, giving drift μ − ½σ². Naïve calculus would give just μ (A); adding the term (B) gets the sign backwards; and (D) is the negative of the answer.

Q5. A stock has GBM expected return μ = 10% and volatility σ = 30%. Which statement is correct?
A. Both the mean and median terminal price grow at 10%
B. The mean price grows at eμT (10%), while the median grows at the lower corrected rate μ − ½σ² = 5.5%
C. The median grows faster than the mean
D. Volatility has no effect on long-run growth
Show answer

B. The mean price is S0eμT (driven by μ = 10%), but the log-price drifts at μ − ½σ² = 0.10 − ½(0.09) = 5.5%, so the median is lower. The two rates differ (A wrong); the mean always meets or exceeds the median for a lognormal, never the reverse (C wrong); and volatility clearly drags down typical growth (D wrong).

✓ What you learned

  • Ordinary calculus fails on Brownian paths: increments scale like √Δt, so dW/dt blows up — the path is continuous but nowhere differentiable.
  • The Itô integral sums position × increment using the left endpoint (non-anticipating) rule, which makes it a zero-mean martingale.
  • The defining rule (dW)² = dt keeps the second-order Taylor term alive — everything with dt·dt or dt·dW vanishes.
  • Itô's lemma adds a ½ b² ∂²f/∂x² correction to the ordinary chain rule whenever the transformation f is nonlinear.
  • For GBM, the log price drifts at μ − ½σ², making prices lognormal and explaining volatility drag on typical (median) growth.
