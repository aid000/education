Martingales & Fair Games

A martingale is the mathematics of a perfectly fair game — and once you grasp it you understand why no clever bet beats one, and why a priced market looks like one.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Understand a filtration as the growing flow of information and conditional expectation as "best forecast given what you know."
  • State the martingale property E[Xn+1 | Fn] = Xn and read it as the definition of a fair game.
  • Explain why no betting strategy can turn a fair game into a winning one (the optional-stopping intuition).
  • Distinguish martingales from sub- and supermartingales, and match each to favorable, fair, and unfavorable games.
  • See why discounted prices are martingales under the risk-neutral measure — the backbone of arbitrage-free pricing.

6.1Filtrations & Conditional Expectation

Every model so far in this course quietly assumed we knew everything at once. Real trading is a story that unfolds in time: at each moment you know a little more than you did before, and you have to make decisions on that partial information. To talk about "fairness over time" we first need a clean way to say what is known when.

That bookkeeping device is a filtration Fn — the collection of everything observable up through time n. It only ever grows: F0 ⊆ F1 ⊆ F2 ⊆ … because information is never un-learned. Think of Fn as the full trading record up to today's close — every price you have seen so far.

Given that information, the natural forecast of a future quantity is the conditional expectation E[X | Fn] — the probability-weighted average of X using only what is known at time n. It is itself a random variable, because the forecast changes as the record changes.

E[ E[X | Fn] ] = E[X]The "tower" / law of iterated expectations: averaging today's best forecast over all the ways today could turn out recovers the plain average.

Two facts about conditional expectation do almost all the work this week. First, what is already known comes out: if Y is known given Fn, then E[Y | Fn] = Y. Second, the tower property above lets you collapse a forecast-of-a-forecast into a single, longer-horizon forecast — exactly what you do when you reason about where a price might be many steps from now.

AnalogyA filtration is a sealed dossier that gains a page each day and never loses one. E[X | Fn] is your best guess about tomorrow's headline given only the pages written so far. As more pages arrive the guess sharpens — but you can never tear a page out, which is why Fn only grows.

6.2The Martingale Property — a Fair Game

Now we can define the central object. A sequence X0, X1, X2, … adapted to a filtration Fn is a martingale if your best forecast of the next value, given everything known so far, is simply the current value:

E[ Xn+1 | Fn ] = Xnplus two housekeeping conditions: each Xn is known at time n (adapted) and has finite expectation (E|Xn| < ∞).

Read it in plain English: knowing the entire past tells you nothing about the direction of the next move. The expected change is zero, no matter what has happened. The word comes from an old French betting term, and the connection is exact — a martingale is the precise mathematical statement of a fair game: a game where, on average, you neither gain nor lose with each play.

The classic example is the running total of a fair coin game. Win +$1 on heads, lose −$1 on tails; let Xn be your cumulative wealth after n flips. Because the next flip averages to zero, E[Xn+1 | Fn] = Xn + 0 = Xn. Your current pile is the best prediction of your future pile — that is a martingale, and it is precisely the symmetric random walk from Week 5.

Xn+1 = Xn + ξn+1,   E[ξn+1 | Fn] = 0   ⟹   Xn is a martingale

One immediate consequence by the tower property: a martingale has a flat expected path. Apply the property repeatedly and E[Xn] = E[X0] for every n. The value wanders — sometimes far — but it never drifts. Average over enough parallel universes and you are back where you started.

6.3Sub-, Super-, and Why No Strategy Beats a Fair Game

Loosen the equality and you get the two cousins of the martingale. If the game tilts in your favor each step, the forecast of the next value is at least the current one — a submartingale. If it tilts against you, the forecast is at most the current value — a supermartingale.

TypePropertyGameExpected path
SubmartingaleE[Xn+1 | Fn] ≥ Xnfavorabledrifts up
MartingaleE[Xn+1 | Fn] = Xnfairflat
SupermartingaleE[Xn+1 | Fn] ≤ Xnunfavorabledrifts down

The mnemonic feels backwards: a supermartingale is the one that sinks. Remember it from the gambler's seat — the casino runs a supermartingale for you and a submartingale for itself. A bet with a house edge makes your wealth a supermartingale.

Now the deep result. Surely a clever betting scheme — double after every loss, or bet bigger when you "feel due" — can squeeze a profit out of a fair game? It cannot. Any strategy that decides how much to stake on the next play using only past information is called a predictable strategy. The wealth it produces, the so-called martingale transform, is still a martingale when the underlying game is fair:

Wn = W0 + Σk≤n Hk · (Xk − Xk−1)   ⟹   E[Wn] = W0Hk is your stake chosen from Fk−1 (the past); since E[Xk−Xk−1|Fk−1]=0, every term has zero expected contribution.

This is the discrete heart of the optional-stopping theorem: if you stop a fair game by any rule based only on what you have seen (and you cannot bet infinitely or play forever), your expected wealth at the stopping time equals what you started with. No information-respecting strategy changes the expectation of a fair game. The only way to "beat" it is to break a hidden assumption — bet unlimited amounts, or play infinitely long with infinite credit.

Common mistakeThe famous "double your bet until you win" (the martingale system, confusingly named) is not a counterexample. It appears to guarantee a profit only because it secretly assumes a bankroll and number of plays that are both unbounded. With any real finite bankroll, the rare catastrophic losing streak exactly cancels the steady small wins — expectation stays zero, just as optional stopping says. A fair game cannot be gamed.

6.4Markets as Martingales: Efficiency & the Risk-Neutral Measure

Why does an abstract idea about coin games sit at the center of quantitative finance? Because a well-functioning market looks like a fair game. The efficient-market intuition says all known information is already baked into the price, so the price is the market's best forecast of itself. That is the martingale property, almost word for word:

E[ pricen+1 | Fn ] ≈ pricenIf tomorrow's price were predictably higher given today's info, traders would buy now until that edge disappeared.

Strictly, raw prices drift upward — investors demand compensation for risk and for the time value of money, so a stock is closer to a submartingale than a martingale. The fix is two adjustments. First, discount by the risk-free rate to strip out the time value of money. Second — and this is the conceptual leap of the whole field — switch from the real-world probabilities to a specially chosen risk-neutral measure Q, under which every asset is expected to grow at exactly the risk-free rate.

Under Q, the discounted price is an honest martingale:

EQ[ e−r(n+1) Sn+1 | Fn ] = e−rn SnDiscount at the risk-free rate r and re-weight outcomes under Q, and the price becomes drift-free — a fair game.

This is not a modeling convenience; it is forced by the absence of arbitrage. The fundamental theorem of asset pricing says: no arbitrage exists if and only if there is a risk-neutral measure making discounted prices martingales. Once prices are martingales, the optional-stopping logic of §6.3 hands you the master pricing rule — the fair value of a derivative today is its discounted expected payoff under Q:

price0 = EQ[ e−rT · payoff ]The single formula behind the Black–Scholes model and every Monte-Carlo pricer you will build in Course 3.
AnalogySwitching to the risk-neutral measure is like changing to a frame of reference where everyone is risk-blind. In that imaginary world nobody demands extra return for danger, so every asset coasts at the risk-free rate and the discounted price stops drifting. Prices computed in this convenient frame are still correct in the real world — because no-arbitrage is the bridge that makes the two agree.
✦ Worked Example — does the doubling strategy beat a fair coin?

A fair coin pays +1 (heads) or −1 (tails). You use the doubling system: bet $1; if you lose, bet $2; then $4, $8, … until your first win, then stop. With an unbounded bankroll the win nets +$1. But suppose your bankroll caps you at 4 plays ($1+$2+$4+$8 = $15 total risk). Show the expected profit is still 0.

  1. You win on play k (first heads at flip k) with probability (1/2)k. Winning at any point nets exactly +$1 (the geometric stakes are designed so one win recovers all prior losses plus a dollar).
  2. You win within 4 plays with probability 1 − (1/2)4 = 1 − 1/16 = 15/16, each such outcome giving +$1.
  3. You lose all 4 plays (TTTT) with probability (1/2)4 = 1/16, losing the whole staked 1+2+4+8 = −$15.
  4. Expected profit: E[profit] = (15/16)·(+1) + (1/16)·(−15) = 15/16 − 15/16 = $0.
  5. Lesson: the tiny 1/16 chance of a −$15 wipeout exactly offsets the comfortable 15/16 chance of a +$1 win. Wealth is a martingale; capping the plays cannot create a positive expectation. Optional stopping guaranteed this answer before any arithmetic — the result had to be zero.

🛠️ Hands-On: a fair game stays fair, whatever you bet

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy — setup is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We simulate the doubling strategy on a fair coin with a finite bankroll and confirm the average profit hugs zero — exactly as optional stopping predicts.

# martingale_strategy.py — Lecture 6
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(6)
N_games  = 500_000          # independent rounds of the strategy
max_plays = 4               # finite bankroll: stakes 1,2,4,8 (risk $15)

profit = np.empty(N_games)
for g in range(N_games):
    stake, pnl = 1.0, 0.0
    for _ in range(max_plays):
        if rng.random() < 0.5:      # heads = win this play
            pnl += stake            # win recovers losses +$1, then stop
            break
        pnl  -= stake               # tails = lose the stake
        stake *= 2                  # double the next bet
    profit[g] = pnl

print("mean profit :", profit.mean())   # ~ 0.0  (fair game!)
print("std  profit :", profit.std())     # big: rare -15 vs many +1
print("worst case  :", profit.min())     # -15.0 when all four are tails

The mean sits within a whisker of 0 no matter the seed, while the standard deviation is large — most rounds make a quiet +$1, but the occasional −$15 streak balances the books. Swap 0.5 for 0.48 (a house edge) and watch the mean turn reliably negative: now wealth is a supermartingale, and no staking rule can rescue it.

💬 Discussion

If markets are efficient and discounted prices behave like martingales — so that the expected future price equals today's price — then how can any trader or fund earn a consistent edge? Reconcile the martingale picture with the existence of strategies that genuinely seem to make money over time. Where, exactly, do the martingale assumptions bend in the real world, and what does a "real" edge have to come from?

Show a model response

A strong answer separates the idealized martingale from the messy market and locates the edge in the gaps:

1. The martingale is under a measure, not a free lunch. The clean statement "discounted price is a martingale" holds under the risk-neutral measure Q, which is a no-arbitrage construct. Under the real-world measure P, prices carry a risk premium: they are submartingales that drift up to pay investors for bearing risk. Earning that premium (e.g., simply holding equities) is not "beating" a fair game — it is being paid for risk, which the martingale picture never claimed away.

2. Real edges come from broken assumptions. The "no strategy wins" result needs (a) a genuinely fair game, (b) information available to all, and (c) no frictions. Real edges arise precisely where these fail: superior or faster information (the market isn't perfectly efficient), structural roles (liquidity provision, market-making earning the spread), or bearing risks others won't. Each is compensation for something real, not magic.

3. Survivorship and luck. Over many traders, some will show long winning streaks by chance alone — a martingale guarantees wandering paths, and the survivors are the ones we hear about. Distinguishing skill from a lucky path requires far more data than intuition suggests (the √n problem from Week 2).

✓ Self-check rubric — a complete answer should: (1) distinguish the risk-neutral Q from the real-world P measure and mention the risk premium; (2) name at least one concrete assumption (fairness, equal information, or no frictions) and how violating it creates an edge; (3) acknowledge that observed winners may reflect luck/survivorship, not a violated martingale.

Q1. A sequence Xn satisfies E[Xn+1 | Fn] = Xn for all n. This means that, given the entire past…
A. the next value is guaranteed to equal the current value
B. the expected next value equals the current value — zero expected change
C. the next value is expected to grow at the risk-free rate
D. the variance of the next value is zero
Show answer

B. The martingale property is about the expected next value, not the actual one — the path still moves randomly (so A and D are wrong; variance is generally positive). Growing at the risk-free rate (C) describes a price before discounting, not a martingale.

Q2. A gambler plays a game with a persistent house edge, so each bet loses money on average. Their cumulative wealth is best described as a…
A. submartingale
B. martingale
C. supermartingale
D. filtration
Show answer

C. An unfavorable game makes E[Xn+1|Fn] ≤ Xn — a supermartingale (the one that drifts down, despite the name). A submartingale (A) drifts up (favorable); a martingale (B) is fair; a filtration (D) is the information flow, not a wealth process.

Q3. What does a filtration Fn represent?
A. the variance of the process at time n
B. the information available up to and including time n
C. a betting strategy that doubles after each loss
D. the risk-free discount factor
Show answer

B. A filtration is the growing record of everything observable through time n (F0 ⊆ F1 ⊆ …). It is not a variance (A), not a strategy (C, that's the martingale system), and not a discount factor (D).

Q4. Why can't a predictable betting strategy (stakes chosen from the past) turn a fair game into a profitable one?
A. Because the resulting wealth process is itself a martingale, so its expected value never changes
B. Because casinos forbid changing your bet size
C. Because the law of large numbers makes all strategies converge to the same payout
D. Because variance always grows faster than the mean
Show answer

A. The martingale transform of a fair game is again a martingale, so E[Wn] = W0 for any predictable stakes — this is the optional-stopping intuition. B is false (rule, not math), C misstates the LLN, and D is irrelevant to the expectation.

Q5. In arbitrage-free pricing, the discounted price of a traded asset is a martingale under…
A. the real-world probability measure P
B. any measure you like — it makes no difference
C. the risk-neutral measure Q
D. the uniform distribution
Show answer

C. Discounted prices are martingales under the risk-neutral measure Q, which exists precisely when there is no arbitrage (the fundamental theorem of asset pricing). Under the real measure P (A) prices drift up by a risk premium, so they are submartingales, not martingales; B and D are simply false.
