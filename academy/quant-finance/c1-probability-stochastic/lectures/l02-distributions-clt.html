Distributions & the Central Limit Theorem

A handful of named distributions describe most of finance — and one theorem explains why the bell curve keeps showing up uninvited.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Recognize the Bernoulli, binomial, and Poisson distributions and recall their mean and variance.
  • Describe the uniform, exponential, and normal (Gaussian) distributions as densities, and use the 68–95–99.7 rule.
  • State the Central Limit Theorem and explain what it says about the sum and the average of many independent variables.
  • Standardize to a Z-score and use the normal approximation to estimate a tail probability.
  • Watch the CLT emerge in NumPy by averaging a deliberately non-normal distribution.

2.1Why Named Distributions?

In Lecture 1 a random variable was anything with a PMF or density. In practice the same few shapes appear over and over, so they get names. Knowing the name buys you its mean, variance, and behavior for free — you don't re-derive them every time. Think of them as a small vocabulary: master six words and you can describe most of the randomness in finance.

We split them into discrete (counts: how many defaults, how many up-days) and continuous (measurements: a return, a waiting time, a price).

2.2The Discrete Trio: Bernoulli, Binomial, Poisson

A Bernoulli(p) variable is a single yes/no trial — 1 with probability p, 0 otherwise. It is the atom of discrete probability: a coin flip, a single bet won or lost, one bond either defaulting or not.

Bernoulli:  E[X] = p,   Var(X) = p(1 − p)Variance is largest at p = 0.5 (maximum uncertainty) and zero at p = 0 or 1 (a sure thing).

Add up n independent Bernoulli(p) trials and you count the successes — that's a Binomial(n, p). Because expectation and variance both add over independent trials, the formulas are just n times the Bernoulli ones:

Binomial:  P(X = k) = C(n,k) pk(1−p)n−k,   E[X] = np,  Var(X) = np(1−p)

The Poisson(λ) distribution is the limit of a binomial when n is huge and p is tiny but the average count λ = np stays moderate — the natural model for rare events over a fixed window: trade arrivals in a second, jumps in a price, defaults in a large pool. Its single parameter is both its mean and its variance:

Poisson:  P(X = k) = e−λ λk / k!,   E[X] = λ,  Var(X) = λ
Common mistake"Mean equals variance" is special to the Poisson — do not assume it for other distributions. If a dataset's variance badly exceeds its mean ("overdispersion"), a Poisson model is the wrong tool.

2.3The Continuous Trio: Uniform, Exponential, Normal

Continuous variables use a density f(x); probabilities are areas under it. Three carry most of the load:

DistributionDensity / roleMeanVariance
Uniform(a, b)flat on [a, b] — "no value preferred"; the basis of every random-number generator(a + b)/2(b − a)² / 12
Exponential(λ)f(x) = λe−λx, x ≥ 0 — waiting time until the next Poisson event; memoryless1 / λ1 / λ²
Normal(μ, σ²)the bell curve — the limiting shape of sums (see §2.4)μσ²

The normal (Gaussian) distribution is the centerpiece. It is fully pinned down by its mean μ and standard deviation σ, and obeys the famous 68–95–99.7 rule: about 68% of the mass lies within ±1σ of the mean, 95% within ±2σ, and 99.7% within ±3σ.

AnalogyThe exponential's memorylessness is the "bus that never gets closer." If buses arrive on average every 10 minutes at random, having already waited 10 minutes tells you nothing — your expected remaining wait is still 10 minutes. Markets behave this way for the timing of the next trade or the next shock.

2.4The Central Limit Theorem

Here is the result that makes the normal distribution unavoidable. Take any distribution with a finite mean μ and variance σ² — it can be skewed, lumpy, discrete, whatever. Draw n independent samples and add them up. As n grows, the distribution of the sum (or equivalently the average) approaches a normal — regardless of the original shape.

Sn = X1 + … + Xn ≈ Normal(nμ,  nσ²)    X̄n ≈ Normal(μ,  σ²/n)The sum's spread grows like √n; the average's spread shrinks like 1/√n. That √n is the most important square root in finance.

Two consequences worth burning into memory:

The √n law of error

The standard deviation of the average is σ/√n. To halve your estimation error you need four times the data — which is exactly why Monte-Carlo simulations (Lecture 1) converge frustratingly slowly, and why doubling your sample never feels like enough.

Standardizing to a Z-score

To compute probabilities, subtract the mean and divide by the standard deviation. The result is a standard normal, Normal(0, 1), whose tail probabilities are tabulated (and one keystroke in NumPy/SciPy):

Z = (Sn − nμ) / (σ√n) ⟶ Normal(0, 1)
Common mistakeThe CLT needs finite variance and (roughly) independent samples. Real markets violate both at the worst moments: returns have fat tails and become highly correlated in a crash, so the normal approximation under-states the odds of extreme moves. Use it as a baseline, never as gospel for tail risk.
✦ Worked Example — sum of 100 dice via the CLT

Roll a fair die 100 times and add the results to get a total S. Use the CLT to estimate the probability the total exceeds 375.

  1. One die's moments (from Lecture 1): μ = 3.5 and Var = E[X²] − μ² = 91/6 − 12.25 ≈ 2.9167, so σ ≈ 1.708.
  2. Scale to the sum of n = 100: sums add means and (for independent draws) variances. E[S] = 100·3.5 = 350; Var(S) = 100·2.9167 = 291.67; σS = √291.67 ≈ 17.08.
  3. Standardize the threshold: Z = (375 − 350) / 17.08 ≈ 1.46.
  4. Read the tail: for a standard normal, P(Z > 1.46) ≈ 0.072 — about a 7% chance the total tops 375.
  5. Sanity check: 375 is 25 above the mean, and 25/17.08 ≈ 1.5σ out — comfortably inside the "plausible but uncommon" band the 68–95 rule predicts. The exact answer (computable by brute force) is ≈ 0.073; the CLT nailed it with a single Z-lookup.

🛠️ Hands-On: watch the CLT appear

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Jupyter — install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We'll average a deliberately non-normal distribution — the right-skewed exponential — and watch the averages turn into a bell.

⬇ Need the environment? (reuse Week-1 setup)

If you built quant-env in Lecture 1, just activate it. Otherwise:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter
# clt_demo.py — Lecture 2
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N_trials = 200_000          # how many averages we histogram
n        = 30               # sample size that gets averaged

# parent is Exponential(mean=1): strongly right-skewed, NOT a bell
draws = rng.exponential(scale=1.0, size=(N_trials, n))
means = draws.mean(axis=1)

print("parent mean / std :", 1.0, 1.0)            # Exp(1): mean=1, std=1
print("avg of means      :", means.mean())        # ~ 1.0   (unbiased)
print("std of means       :", means.std())         # ~ 1/sqrt(30) = 0.1826
print("CLT prediction     :", 1.0 / np.sqrt(n))    # 0.1826

The single exponential draw is lopsided, yet means is symmetric and bell-shaped, centered at 1.0 with spread ≈ 0.183 — exactly σ/√n. Re-run with n = 2, 5, 30, 200: the histogram of means looks more Gaussian and narrower each time. That shrinking 1/√n width is the same √n that governs Monte-Carlo accuracy.

🔗 Where this is heading

The CLT is the bridge from this week to the rest of the specialization. Sum many small independent price moves and the total move is approximately normal — that's the modeling assumption behind a random walk (Week 5) and its continuous limit, Brownian motion (Weeks 7–8), where the √n becomes the √t "square-root-of-time" scaling of volatility. The Black–Scholes model in Course 3 assumes log-returns are exactly normal. Knowing why normals appear — and the fat-tail caveat where the assumption breaks — is what separates using a model from trusting it blindly.

❓ Lecture 2 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. You count the number of successes in 20 independent trials, each succeeding with probability 0.3. The right distribution is…
A. Poisson(0.3)
B. Binomial(20, 0.3)
C. Bernoulli(0.3)
D. Normal(6, 4.2)
Show answer

B. A fixed number n = 20 of independent yes/no trials with the same p is exactly a binomial. A single trial would be Bernoulli (C); Poisson (A) is the rare-event limit; the normal (D) is only an approximation to this binomial, not the exact model.

Q2. For a Poisson distribution with λ = 4, the variance is…
A. 2
B. 16
C. 4
D. impossible to know without n
Show answer

C. The Poisson's defining quirk is that its mean and variance are both equal to λ. So Var = 4. (There is no n — the Poisson is a one-parameter distribution.)

Q3. The Central Limit Theorem says that as n grows, the average of n independent samples has a distribution that is approximately…
A. normal, with standard deviation σ/√n
B. normal, with standard deviation σ·√n
C. the same shape as the parent distribution
D. uniform, regardless of the parent
Show answer

A. The average is approximately normal with spread σ/√n (it shrinks as n grows). σ·√n (B) is the spread of the sum, not the average. The whole point of the CLT is that the limiting shape does not depend on the parent (C, D).

Q4. A quantity is approximately Normal with mean 50 and standard deviation 5. Roughly what fraction of outcomes fall between 40 and 60?
A. 68%
B. 50%
C. 99.7%
D. 95%
Show answer

D. 40 and 60 are exactly μ ± 2σ (50 ± 10). By the 68–95–99.7 rule, ±2σ captures about 95% of the probability. ±1σ (45–55) would be 68% (A); ±3σ would be 99.7% (C).

Q5. Why does the normal approximation tend to understate the probability of extreme market crashes?
A. Because real returns have fatter tails and correlate during stress, violating the CLT's assumptions
B. Because the mean of returns is always negative
C. Because variance is undefined for any real asset
D. Because the CLT only applies to discrete variables
Show answer

A. The CLT assumes finite variance and roughly independent samples. Real returns have heavy tails and become strongly correlated in a crash, so a thin-tailed normal assigns too little probability to large moves. (B and C are false; the CLT applies to both discrete and continuous variables, so D is wrong.)

  • Named the continuous trio — uniform, exponential (memoryless waiting time), and the normal — with their means and variances.
  • Used the 68–95–99.7 rule to turn a mean and σ into a range of plausible outcomes.
  • Stated the Central Limit Theorem: sums → Normal(nμ, nσ²), averages → Normal(μ, σ²/n), and the √n / 1-over-√n scaling.
