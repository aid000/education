Week 7 · Lecture 7

From Random Walks to Brownian Motion

Take a coin-flip walk, shrink the steps to nothing, speed up the clock just right — and a jagged staircase melts into the smooth, unpredictable curve that prices the entire derivatives market.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Set up a symmetric random walk and recall that its variance grows linearly in the number of steps.
  • Apply the diffusive rescaling — divide space by √n and time by n — that keeps the walk from collapsing or exploding.
  • State Donsker's functional Central Limit Theorem in plain words: the whole rescaled path converges, not just its endpoint.
  • Explain why the limit has continuous paths, independent Gaussian increments, and a standard deviation that scales like √t.
  • Build a rescaled-walk simulation in NumPy and watch √t volatility scaling appear before your eyes.

7.1The Random Walk We Already Know

In Week 5 we met the simple symmetric random walk: start at zero, and at each tick take a step of +1 or −1, each with probability ½, independently. After n steps the position is the sum of the steps:

Sn = X1 + X2 + … + Xn,   Xi = ±1 with prob ½Each step has mean 0 and variance 1, so E[Sn] = 0 and — because the steps are independent — Var(Sn) = n.

That last fact is the whole game. The variance grows linearly in time, so the typical distance the walk wanders from the origin grows like the square root of the number of steps, √n. Doubling the time does not double how far you typically drift — it multiplies the spread by only √2 ≈ 1.41. This is the discrete ancestor of the "square-root-of-time" rule every risk desk uses to scale daily volatility up to a yearly figure.

A random walk is the canonical toy model of a price that has no predictable drift — a fair game (a martingale, from Week 6). Each tick is an unforecastable shock; the question this week is what happens when the ticks become infinitely frequent and infinitely small, as they effectively are for a price quoted to the microsecond.

AnalogyPicture a slightly drunk person leaving a lamppost, flipping a coin at every step to decide left or right. After 100 steps they are typically about 10 steps away (√100), not 100 — the back-and-forth keeps cancelling. After 400 steps, typically 20 away. To wander twice as far they need four times as long. That stubborn √n is the same square root that haunted Monte-Carlo accuracy in Lecture 2.

7.2The Diffusive Rescaling — Zooming Out Correctly

We want a continuous-time object. The naive move is to cram more and more steps into a fixed time interval [0, 1] and watch what the path does. Split the interval into n tiny time-steps of length Δt = 1/n, and let the walk take one ±1 step each. But there is a trap: if we leave the step size at ±1, then by time 1 the walk has variance n → ∞, and the path blows up. If instead we shrink the step size too aggressively, the path collapses to a flat line. We need the Goldilocks scaling.

The variance bookkeeping tells us exactly what to do. With n steps the total variance is n × (step variance). To pin the variance at time 1 to a finite value, each step must have variance 1/n — that is, a step size of 1/√n. So we rescale space by √n while we rescale time by n:

Wn(t) = S⌊nt⌋ / √nAt time t we have used about nt steps, so Var(Wn(t)) = (nt) × (1/√n)² = nt/n = t. The variance grows like t, not like nt — finite and clean.

Notice the asymmetry: time and space are not rescaled by the same factor. Time is divided by n; space only by √n. This unequal squashing — squeeze time hard, squeeze space gently — is the signature of diffusion, and it is precisely what produces the famous √t spread. Get the exponents wrong and you get either a frozen line or an explosion; only the ½-power on space survives.

7.3Donsker's Theorem — the Whole Path Converges

The ordinary Central Limit Theorem of Lecture 2 told us about a single number: the rescaled endpoint Wn(1) = Sn/√n converges in distribution to a Normal(0, 1). That is already suggestive, but a price is not one number — it is an entire trajectory in time. We want the whole function t ↦ Wn(t) to settle down to a limiting random function.

That stronger statement is Donsker's theorem, also called the functional Central Limit Theorem or the invariance principle. In plain words:

Wn(·) = S⌊n·⌋ / √n  ⟹  B(·)   as n → ∞The entire rescaled random-walk path converges to a limiting random path B, called standard Brownian motion (or the Wiener process).

Two words in that sentence carry enormous weight. Functional means we are taking a limit of whole paths, not just endpoints — the convergence holds for the maximum, the time of the maximum, the area under the path, every continuous feature at once. Invariance means the limit does not care about the microscopic details of the steps: ±1 coin flips, ±2 with some skip, draws from any distribution with mean 0 and finite variance — they all rescale to the same Brownian motion. Just as the plain CLT erased the shape of the parent distribution, Donsker erases the shape of the individual step.

AnalogyThe plain CLT is a single still photo of the finish line — where did the walker end up? Donsker is the entire film of the journey, and it says every walker's film, no matter how their individual footsteps differed, edits down to the same archetypal movie once you zoom out. That universality is why one model — Brownian motion — underlies the pricing of options on thousands of unrelated assets.

This is the deep reason Brownian motion is the default continuous-time model for an efficient price. You do not need to know the exact mechanism of every tick — the order-book microstructure, the queue jumps, the cancellations. As long as the increments are roughly independent with finite variance, the aggregate, viewed from far enough away, looks like Brownian motion.

7.4What the Limit Looks Like — Three Defining Features

The object Donsker hands us, standard Brownian motion B(t), inherits three properties directly from the random walk it came from. These will be the formal axioms of Week 8; here we see where they come from.

1. Gaussian increments with √t scaling

Over an interval of length t, the limiting position B(t) is the rescaled sum of about nt independent steps. By the CLT that sum is Gaussian, and from §7.2 its variance is t. So:

B(t) − B(s) ~ Normal(0,  t − s),   SD = √(t − s)The standard deviation of a Brownian increment is the square root of the elapsed time. Daily vol × √252 ≈ annual vol is exactly this rule.

2. Independent increments

Because the random-walk steps were independent, non-overlapping chunks of the path are independent. What the price does between Monday and Tuesday is independent of what it does between Thursday and Friday — the formal version of "the past is already priced in."

3. Continuous but nowhere-smooth paths

Each rescaled step has size 1/√n → 0, so no single jump survives in the limit: the path is continuous. Yet over any tiny interval Δt the wiggle has size √Δt, which is much larger than Δt itself. The ratio (wiggle)/(time) = √Δt / Δt = 1/√Δt → ∞, so the path has no well-defined slope anywhere — it is continuous but nowhere differentiable.

Common mistakeDo not say "Brownian motion has variance √t." Its variance is t (it grows linearly); the standard deviation is √t. Likewise, never scale volatility by adding it over time — variances add, so volatilities add in quadrature. Two independent daily moves of vol σ combine to σ√2, not 2σ. Forgetting this is the single most common √t error on a trading desk.

Because the path has no slope, the ordinary derivative dB/dt does not exist, and ordinary calculus breaks down on it. Repairing calculus for these jagged paths is the job of Itô calculus later in the specialization — the engine of the Black–Scholes equation.

✦ Worked Example — from a coin-flip walk to a √t volatility quote

A toy price model takes one ±1 tick every minute, independently, with no drift. The market is open 390 minutes a day. Treat the rescaled walk as Brownian motion and find the standard deviation of the daily move, then the implied move over a 4-hour window.

  1. One tick's moments: a ±1 step has mean 0 and variance 1, so its standard deviation is 1 "price unit" per minute of variance.
  2. Variance adds over independent ticks: over a full day of n = 390 ticks, Var(S390) = 390 × 1 = 390. The position itself has mean 0 — no drift.
  3. Standard deviation = √(variance): SD of the daily move = √390 ≈ 19.7 price units. Note we take the square root of time, not of variance-of-variance — this is the √t rule.
  4. Scale to a 4-hour window (240 minutes): Var = 240, so SD = √240 ≈ 15.5 units. Crucially, 4 hours is roughly ⅗ of the day, yet its SD is √(240/390) ≈ 0.78 of the daily SD — not 0.62. Spread scales with √time, so most of a day's "movement budget" is spent quickly.
  5. Sanity / quadrature check: two independent 4-hour windows back-to-back give Var = 240 + 240 = 480, SD = √480 ≈ 21.9 — which equals √2 × 15.5, the volatilities adding in quadrature, not 2 × 15.5 = 31. The √t scaling is internally consistent.

🛠️ Hands-On: rescale a walk and watch √t appear

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Matplotlib — install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We build the diffusive rescaling directly: take ±1 steps, divide space by √n, and confirm that the spread at time t scales like √t — the fingerprint of the Brownian limit.

⬇ Need the environment? (reuse Week-1 setup)

If you built quant-env in Lecture 1, just activate it. Otherwise:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter
# rw_to_bm.py — Lecture 7: diffusive rescaling of a random walk
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(7)
N_paths = 100_000           # how many walks we simulate
n       = 2_000             # steps per unit of time (the "n" we send to infinity)

# ±1 steps; cumulative sum is the walk S_k along each path
steps = rng.choice([-1.0, 1.0], size=(N_paths, n))
S     = np.cumsum(steps, axis=1)

# diffusive rescaling: W_n(t) = S_floor(n t) / sqrt(n)
W = S / np.sqrt(n)

# sample the rescaled path at t = 0.25, 0.50, 1.00
for t in (0.25, 0.50, 1.00):
    col = int(t * n) - 1
    sd  = W[:, col].std()
    print(f"t={t:.2f}  empirical SD={sd:.4f}   sqrt(t)={np.sqrt(t):.4f}")

The two columns line up: the standard deviation of the rescaled walk at time t is ≈ √t (0.50, 0.71, 1.00). The endpoint W[:,-1] is also bell-shaped and Normal(0, 1) — Donsker in miniature. Bump n to 20,000 and the path you'd plot looks visibly continuous; the √t fit only tightens. That is the entire content of this week in four printed numbers.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. For a simple symmetric ±1 random walk, after n independent steps the variance of the position Sn is…
A. √n
B. n
C. n²
D. 1, independent of n
Show answer

B. Each ±1 step has variance 1, and variances of independent steps add, so Var(Sn) = n. The standard deviation is √n (A), which is the typical distance, not the variance. n² (C) would require the steps to be perfectly correlated; (D) ignores that more steps mean more spread.

Q2. To turn the random walk into a sensible continuous-time limit on [0, 1] using n steps, how must you rescale space and time?
A. Divide both space and time by n
B. Divide space by n and time by √n
C. Divide space by √n and time by n
D. Leave space alone and divide time by n
Show answer

C. This is the diffusive scaling: Wn(t) = S⌊nt⌋/√n. Space is squeezed gently (√n) so the variance at time t lands at t. Equal scaling (A) makes the path collapse; reversing the exponents (B) makes it explode; (D) leaves variance growing like n and blowing up.

Q3. Donsker's theorem (the functional CLT) strengthens the ordinary CLT by guaranteeing convergence of…
A. only the rescaled endpoint Wn(1)
B. the entire rescaled path, not just the endpoint
C. the variance of the steps to zero
D. the parent distribution to a uniform
Show answer

B. The "functional" in functional CLT means the whole path t ↦ Wn(t) converges to Brownian motion, so functionals like the maximum or area converge too. The endpoint alone (A) is just the ordinary CLT. (C) and (D) describe nothing Donsker claims.

Q4. For standard Brownian motion, the standard deviation of the increment B(t) − B(s) over an interval of length t − s is…
A. t − s
B. (t − s)²
C. √(t − s)
D. 1, for any interval
Show answer

C. The increment is Normal(0, t − s), so its variance is t − s and its standard deviation is √(t − s) — the √t scaling. (A) is the variance, not the SD; (B) and (D) are not how diffusion spreads.

Q5. Why is a Brownian motion path continuous but nowhere differentiable?
A. Each rescaled step → 0 (so it's continuous), but the wiggle over Δt is of size √Δt ≫ Δt, so the slope blows up
B. Because the path has occasional finite jumps that break the derivative
C. Because the increments are correlated, creating sharp corners
D. Because variance is infinite at every point
Show answer

A. Steps shrink like 1/√n so no jump survives — the path is continuous. But over a tiny Δt the typical move is √Δt, and the slope (√Δt)/Δt = 1/√Δt → ∞, so no derivative exists. Brownian motion has no jumps (B is false); its increments are independent, not correlated (C); its variance at time t is finite and equal to t (D is false).

✓ What you learned

  • The simple symmetric random walk has Var(Sn) = n, so its typical drift grows like √n — the discrete root of square-root-of-time.
  • The diffusive rescaling divides space by √n and time by n, so Wn(t) = S⌊nt⌋/√n has variance t — finite, not exploding or collapsing.
  • Donsker's theorem (the functional CLT) says the whole rescaled path converges to standard Brownian motion, regardless of the step distribution.
  • The limit has Gaussian increments with SD = √(t − s), independent increments, and continuous but nowhere-differentiable paths.
  • You reproduced the √t scaling in NumPy by rescaling a coin-flip walk — Donsker in four printed numbers.
