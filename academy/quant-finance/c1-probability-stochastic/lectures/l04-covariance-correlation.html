Week 4 · Lecture 4

Covariance, Correlation & Independence

One number tells you whether two assets rise and fall together — and it is the single number that makes diversification, the only free lunch in finance, actually work.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Read a joint distribution and compute covariance Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y].
  • Standardize covariance into the correlation coefficient ρ and explain why it always lies in [−1, +1].
  • Distinguish independence from zero correlation — independence implies ρ = 0, but ρ = 0 does not imply independence.
  • Use the variance-of-a-sum formula, including the covariance term, to compute portfolio risk.
  • Build a correlation matrix in NumPy and see numerically why low correlation shrinks portfolio variance.

4.1Joint Distributions: Two Variables at Once

So far each lecture has lived with one random variable at a time. Markets are not so polite — a portfolio holds many assets, and what matters is how they move together. To describe two random variables jointly we need a joint distribution: for discrete X and Y it is the table of probabilities p(x, y) = P(X = x and Y = y), one entry for every pair of values.

From that joint table you can always recover each variable on its own by summing the other one out — the marginal distributions. The joint table holds strictly more information than the two marginals, because it also records how the variables co-move: knowing each asset's individual return distribution tells you nothing about whether they tend to crash on the same day.

pX(x) = Σy p(x, y),   pY(y) = Σx p(x, y)Marginals are the row and column sums of the joint table; they discard the co-movement information.

Two variables are independent when the joint factors into the product of the marginals for every pair — knowing one tells you nothing about the other:

X, Y independent  ⟺  p(x, y) = pX(x) · pY(y)  for all x, y
AnalogyA joint distribution is a weather map for two cities. The marginals tell you how often it rains in each city separately. But only the full map tells you whether the two cities tend to be rainy on the same days. In markets, the "same-day rain" is exactly what wipes out a portfolio that looked diversified on paper.

4.2Covariance: Do They Move Together?

The cleanest summary of co-movement is covariance. It asks: when X is above its mean, does Y also tend to be above its mean? Multiply each variable's deviation from its own mean and average the product:

Cov(X, Y) = E[(X − μX)(Y − μY)] = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]The right-hand form ("mean of the product minus product of the means") is the one you compute with.

The sign is what your intuition latches onto. If X and Y usually overshoot their means together, the products are mostly positive and covariance is positive — the variables move in the same direction. If one tends to be high when the other is low, the products are mostly negative and covariance is negative. If there is no systematic pattern, the positive and negative products cancel and covariance is near zero.

Notice that Cov(X, X) = E[(X − μ)²] = Var(X): a variable's covariance with itself is just its variance. Covariance is the natural generalization of variance to two variables.

Common mistakeCovariance's magnitude is almost meaningless on its own — it carries the units of X times Y and scales with them. Re-quote a return in basis points instead of percent and the covariance number changes by a factor of 10,000, though nothing about the relationship has changed. To compare relationships you must standardize, which is exactly what correlation does next.

4.3Correlation: Covariance on a Fixed Scale

The correlation coefficient ρ (rho) strips covariance of its units by dividing out each variable's standard deviation. The result is a pure, dimensionless number:

ρXY = Cov(X, Y) / (σX σY)Covariance normalized by the two standard deviations — same sign as covariance, but now comparable across any pair.

The decisive fact about ρ is that it always lies between −1 and +1. This is not an accident of definition; it falls straight out of the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, which guarantees |Cov(X, Y)| ≤ σXσY. The boundaries are reached only in the extreme cases:

  • ρ = +1: perfect positive linear relationship — Y is an increasing linear function of X.
  • ρ = −1: perfect negative linear relationship — Y moves exactly opposite to X.
  • ρ = 0: no linear relationship (the variables are "uncorrelated").

That fixed scale is why correlation, not covariance, is the number traders quote. "These two sectors are 0.8 correlated" is instantly meaningful; a covariance of 0.00031 is not.

4.4Independence, the Variance of a Sum & Diversification

Here is a subtlety that snares people in interviews and in production risk models alike. Independence is strictly stronger than zero correlation. If X and Y are independent then E[XY] = E[X]E[Y], so their covariance — and hence ρ — is exactly zero. But the arrow runs only one way:

independent  ⟹  ρ = 0,    but   ρ = 0  ⇏  independentCorrelation only detects the linear part of a relationship. A purely nonlinear dependence can hide behind ρ = 0.

The classic counterexample: let X be symmetric about 0 and set Y = X². Then Y is completely determined by X — as dependent as two variables can be — yet Cov(X, Y) = E[X³] − E[X]E[X²] = 0 − 0 = 0, so ρ = 0. The relationship is real but curved, and correlation is blind to curvature. In markets this is the danger of "uncorrelated" strategies that nonetheless blow up together when volatility spikes: the dependence was nonlinear all along.

Variance of a sum

Now we can finally complete the formula left dangling in Lecture 1. For any two random variables, the variance of their sum carries a covariance term:

Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2·Cov(X, Y)Variances add only when Cov = 0. For a weighted portfolio: Var(aX + bY) = a²Var(X) + b²Var(Y) + 2ab·Cov(X, Y).

This single line is the mathematical engine of diversification. Build a two-asset portfolio with equal weights and equal individual variances σ². If the assets are perfectly correlated (ρ = 1) the portfolio variance is the full σ² — you gained nothing. But if they are uncorrelated (ρ = 0) the covariance term vanishes and the portfolio variance drops to σ²/2; with negative correlation it drops further still. Combining imperfectly-correlated assets lowers risk without lowering expected return — the closest thing to a free lunch in finance, and the heart of Markowitz portfolio theory.

Scale this to many assets and the bookkeeping is captured by the correlation matrix: a symmetric grid with 1's on the diagonal (each variable is perfectly correlated with itself) and ρij in the off-diagonal cells. It is the central input to every modern risk model.

✦ Worked Example — two-asset portfolio variance

Two assets have annual return standard deviations σA = 20% and σB = 10%, with correlation ρ = 0.30. You hold 60% in A and 40% in B. Find the portfolio's standard deviation, and compare it to the naïve weighted average of the two volatilities.

  1. Covariance from correlation: Cov(A, B) = ρ·σA·σB = 0.30 · 0.20 · 0.10 = 0.006.
  2. Plug into the weighted variance-of-sum formula with a = 0.6, b = 0.4:
    Varp = a²σA² + b²σB² + 2ab·Cov(A, B).
  3. Term by term: a²σA² = 0.36·0.04 = 0.0144;   b²σB² = 0.16·0.01 = 0.0016;   2ab·Cov = 2·0.6·0.4·0.006 = 0.00288.
  4. Sum: Varp = 0.0144 + 0.0016 + 0.00288 = 0.01888, so σp = √0.01888 ≈ 13.7%.
  5. Compare to the naïve average: 0.6·20% + 0.4·10% = 16.0%. The portfolio's true risk (13.7%) is lower than the weighted-average volatility — the 2.3-point gap is the diversification benefit, and it exists precisely because ρ < 1.
  6. Sanity check: set ρ = 1 and the covariance term grows until σp = 16.0% exactly — at perfect correlation diversification vanishes, as the formula demands.

🛠️ Hands-On: covariance & the correlation matrix in NumPy

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Jupyter — install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We simulate three assets — two that share a market factor and one independent — then read their correlation matrix and confirm that low correlation shrinks portfolio variance.

# correlation_demo.py — Lecture 4
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(4)
N = 200_000

market = rng.normal(0, 1, N)              # a shared market factor
A = 0.8*market + rng.normal(0, 0.6, N)    # loads heavily on market
B = 0.7*market + rng.normal(0, 0.6, N)    # also loads on market -> A,B correlated
C = rng.normal(0, 1.0, N)                 # independent of the market

R = np.vstack([A, B, C])                  # 3 x N matrix of returns
corr = np.corrcoef(R)                     # 3 x 3 correlation matrix
print(np.round(corr, 2))
# [[1.   0.6  0.  ]      A-B correlated (shared factor)
#  [0.6  1.   0.  ]      A-C and B-C near zero
#  [0.   0.   1.  ]]     diagonal is always 1

# equal-weight portfolio variance vs. sum of individual variances
w = np.array([1/3, 1/3, 1/3])
cov = np.cov(R)                           # covariance matrix
port_var = w @ cov @ w                    # wᵀ Σ w  — the quadratic form
naive   = w**2 @ np.diag(cov)             # ignores covariances
print("portfolio var (full)  :", round(port_var, 4))
print("naive var (no cov term):", round(naive, 4))   # smaller? bigger? compare!

The off-diagonal 0.6 between A and B is the shared market factor; C is uncorrelated with both. The expression w @ cov @ w is the matrix form of the variance-of-sum formula — the quadratic form wᵀΣw that every portfolio optimizer evaluates. Swap in different weights and watch portfolio variance fall as you tilt toward the uncorrelated asset C.

🔗 Where this is heading

The correlation matrix Σ you just built is the load-bearing object for the rest of the specialization. Markowitz mean-variance optimization (Course 2) minimizes exactly the quadratic form wᵀΣw to find the efficient frontier; risk metrics like the Sharpe ratio and portfolio Value-at-Risk read straight off it. Project checkpoint (Week 4): as your capstone milestone, pull real daily returns for 3–5 tickers with the Week-1 stack and compute and visualize their correlation matrix — the same heatmap you will later feed into the optimizer. When correlated random steps accumulate over time (Weeks 5–8) this matrix becomes the covariance structure of correlated Brownian motions, the engine inside multi-asset Black–Scholes and Monte-Carlo pricers in Course 3.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. Cov(X, Y) is most directly computed as…
A. E[X]·E[Y] − E[XY]
B. E[XY] − E[X]·E[Y]
C. E[X²] − (E[X])²
D. E[XY] / (σXσY)
Show answer

B. Cov(X, Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y]. Option A has the terms reversed (wrong sign); C is the variance of a single variable; D is not correlation either (correlation divides the covariance, not E[XY], by σXσY).

Q2. The correlation coefficient ρ is always confined to the interval…
A. [0, 1]
B. [0, ∞)
C. [−1, +1]
D. (−∞, +∞)
Show answer

C. Dividing covariance by σXσY and applying Cauchy–Schwarz forces ρ into [−1, +1]. A excludes negative (hedging) correlations, which are real; B and D describe unbounded quantities like covariance or variance, not the standardized correlation.

Q3. Let X be symmetric about 0 and let Y = X². What is true of X and Y?
A. They are uncorrelated (ρ = 0) yet clearly dependent
B. They are independent because ρ = 0
C. They are perfectly correlated, ρ = +1
D. Their covariance is undefined
Show answer

A. Cov(X, X²) = E[X³] − E[X]E[X²] = 0 − 0 = 0, so ρ = 0 — yet Y is fully determined by X, the opposite of independent. B inverts the logic (ρ = 0 does not imply independence); C is false (the link is nonlinear, not linear); D is wrong (the covariance is well-defined and equals zero).

Q4. For two assets each with variance σ² and correlation ρ = 0, the variance of an equally weighted two-asset portfolio (weights ½, ½) is…
A. σ²
B. 2σ²
C. σ²/4
D. σ²/2
Show answer

D. Var = (½)²σ² + (½)²σ² + 2(½)(½)·0 = ¼σ² + ¼σ² = σ²/2. The covariance term vanishes at ρ = 0, halving the variance — diversification at work. A would require perfect correlation; B ignores the ½ weights; C drops one of the two ¼σ² terms.

Q5. A correlation matrix for a set of assets always has…
A. all entries equal to zero
B. 1's on the diagonal and is symmetric
C. 0's on the diagonal
D. entries that can exceed 1 when assets are very volatile
Show answer

B. Every variable is perfectly correlated with itself (ρ = 1 on the diagonal), and ρij = ρji makes it symmetric. A would mean all assets are mutually uncorrelated (a special case, not a rule); C confuses it with a deviation matrix; D is impossible since correlation is capped at 1 regardless of volatility.

✓ What you learned

  • Read a joint distribution, recovered the marginals, and defined independence as p(x,y) = pX(x)pY(y).
  • Computed covariance Cov(X,Y) = E[XY] − E[X]E[Y] and read its sign as the direction of co-movement.
  • Standardized to the correlation coefficient ρ = Cov/(σXσY), always in [−1, +1].
  • Learned that independence ⟹ ρ = 0 but ρ = 0 ⇏ independence (the Y = X² counterexample).
  • Used Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2Cov(X,Y) and the correlation matrix to explain why diversification lowers portfolio risk.
