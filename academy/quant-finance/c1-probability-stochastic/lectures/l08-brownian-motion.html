Week 8 · Lecture 8

Brownian Motion & Its Properties

The jagged, never-smooth, never-repeating path that a stock price seems to trace — turned into a precise mathematical object that the whole of modern finance is built on.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the three defining axioms of standard Brownian motion: W₀ = 0, independent Gaussian increments, and continuous paths.
  • Derive the mean E[Wₜ] = 0, the variance Var(Wₜ) = t, and the covariance Cov(Wₛ, Wₜ) = min(s, t).
  • Explain why a Brownian path is continuous everywhere yet differentiable nowhere — and why that means "velocity" is undefined.
  • Compute the quadratic variation of Brownian motion and see why it equals t (not zero).
  • Preview geometric Brownian motion as the standard model for a positive asset price.

8.1From the Random Walk to a Definition

Last week we took a symmetric random walk, shrank each step in size, took the steps faster and faster, and watched the jagged staircase converge to a single continuous curve. That limiting object is standard Brownian motion, written Wₜ (the "W" honors Wiener, who made it rigorous). It is the continuous-time cousin of the coin-flip walk, and it is the single most important stochastic process in quantitative finance.

Rather than re-derive it as a limit each time, we pin it down with three axioms. A process {Wₜ : t ≥ 0} is a standard Brownian motion if:

(1)  W₀ = 0
(2)  increments are independent and Wt − Ws ~ Normal(0, t − s)  for s < t
(3)  the path t ↦ Wt is continuousThree short rules generate the entire object — start at zero, move by independent Gaussian increments whose variance is the elapsed time, and never jump.

Unpack axiom (2). Over a window of length t − s, the displacement is normally distributed, centered at zero, with variance equal to the length of the window. So a one-second move has variance 1, a four-second move has variance 4, and its standard deviation is √4 = 2. The spread grows like the square root of time — the same √n from the Central Limit Theorem (Lecture 2), now wearing a √t costume. "Independent increments" means what happens on a disjoint future window is statistically unrelated to the past: the process has no memory of its direction.

AnalogyImagine a speck of pollen jittered by invisible water molecules — the original physical Brownian motion. Each collision nudges it a tiny random amount, independent of the last. Watch for one second and it has wandered some distance; watch for four seconds and it has wandered twice as far on average, not four times. A traded price is jostled the same way by a relentless stream of small, independent buy and sell orders.

8.2Mean, Variance, and Covariance

The three axioms hand us every moment we need. Since W₀ = 0 and Wₜ = Wₜ − W₀ ~ Normal(0, t), the first two moments are immediate:

E[Wt] = 0     Var(Wt) = t     SD(Wt) = √tOn average the process goes nowhere, but its typical distance from the start grows like √t — never linearly.

The zero mean says Brownian motion is driftless: it has no built-in tendency to rise or fall. (A real stock does drift; we add that back with geometric Brownian motion in §8.4.) The variance growing linearly in t is the statement that uncertainty about where the price will be compounds with the horizon.

Covariance: how two time-points are linked

How related are the values at two different times s and t? Take s < t and split Wₜ into the past piece plus a fresh, independent increment: Wₜ = Wₛ + (Wₜ − Wₛ). Then

Cov(Ws, Wt) = Cov(Ws, Ws) + Cov(Ws, Wt − Ws) = Var(Ws) + 0 = s = min(s, t)The future increment is independent of the past, so its covariance with Wₛ is zero — only the shared history remains.

The clean result Cov(Wₛ, Wₜ) = min(s, t) is worth memorizing: two times share exactly the variance accumulated up to the earlier of them. This is the algebra behind correlated returns over overlapping windows, and it is the kernel you feed into a covariance matrix when simulating many correlated price paths.

Common mistakeBrownian motion is not a sequence of independent values — the increments are independent, the levels are not. Wₜ and Wₛ are strongly correlated because they share the path up to time min(s, t). Confusing "independent increments" with "independent positions" leads to badly underestimating how persistent a price level is.

8.3Two Strange Properties: No Derivative, Finite Quadratic Variation

Brownian motion is continuous — you can draw it without lifting your pen — but it is nowhere differentiable. At no instant does the path have a well-defined slope, so the "velocity" dW/dt simply does not exist. The reason is the √t scaling. Over a tiny window Δt the typical move is of size √Δt, so the difference quotient is

ΔW / Δt  ~  √Δt / Δt  =  1 / √Δt  →  ∞  (as Δt → 0)The numerator shrinks like √Δt while the denominator shrinks like Δt — faster — so the slope blows up. No limit exists, in any direction.

This is why finance can't use ordinary calculus on prices and needs the special machinery of stochastic calculus (next week). You cannot write "rate of change of the price" — the path is infinitely wiggly at every scale, more like a coastline than a smooth trajectory.

Quadratic variation

Ordinary smooth functions have zero quadratic variation: chop [0, t] into n pieces, square each increment, sum them, and the total vanishes as the mesh shrinks. Brownian motion is different. Each squared increment (ΔW)² has expected value Δt, and there are t/Δt of them, so the squares sum to t and — remarkably — do not vanish but converge to t with certainty:

[W]t = limn→∞ Σ (Wtk+1 − Wtk)² = tWritten informally as (dW)² = dt — the rule that powers Itô's lemma. Squared Brownian increments behave like time itself.

The slogan (dW)² = dt is the single most useful identity in the entire course. It says that while the path's signed moves average out to zero, its squared moves accumulate deterministically — which is exactly why variance (and therefore risk and option value) builds up predictably even though direction does not.

AnalogyThink of "realized variance" that a trader computes by summing squared minute-by-minute returns over a day. As you sample more finely, the sum of squared moves settles down to a stable number (the day's variance budget) even though the direction of the price stays completely unpredictable. That stable number is quadratic variation in action — and it is what variance swaps are written on.

8.4From W to a Price: Geometric Brownian Motion

Standard Brownian motion can go negative and has no drift, so it cannot be a stock price directly — a share can't be worth −$3, and over the long run equities tend to rise. The fix is to make the log of the price a Brownian motion with drift. The result is geometric Brownian motion (GBM), the workhorse price model behind Black–Scholes:

St = S0 · exp[ (μ − ½σ²) t + σ Wt ]μ is the drift (expected growth rate), σ is the volatility. The exponential keeps Sₜ strictly positive; the σWₜ term injects the Brownian randomness.

Two design choices make GBM the natural model. First, the exponential guarantees Sₜ > 0 always. Second, returns are multiplicative: a 1% move means the same dollar fraction whether the price is $10 or $1000, which matches how markets actually behave. The peculiar −½σ² term is the fingerprint of the (dW)² = dt rule from §8.3 — it's the correction Itô's lemma forces on us when we exponentiate a Brownian motion, and we'll derive it properly next week.

Under GBM, log-returns over a window are exactly normal with variance σ²·(elapsed time) — so the √t scaling of Brownian motion becomes the √t scaling of volatility that lets a quant convert a daily volatility into an annual one by multiplying by √252.

Common mistakeThe drift of the price is μ, but the average growth rate of the log price is μ − ½σ². Forgetting that −½σ² "volatility drag" makes you overstate expected compounded returns: a high-volatility asset with the same μ compounds more slowly. This term is not optional bookkeeping — it falls straight out of quadratic variation.
✦ Worked Example — probabilities and overlap with Wₜ

A standard Brownian motion Wₜ models the cumulative random shock to a desk's P&L (in $ millions), with time measured in years. Answer three questions using only the axioms.

  1. Spread at t = 4: W₄ ~ Normal(0, 4), so SD(W₄) = √4 = 2. A one-year horizon had SD = 1; quadrupling the horizon only doubled the spread — the √t law.
  2. Tail probability: P(W₄ > 3) standardizes to Z = 3 / 2 = 1.5, so P(Z > 1.5) ≈ 0.067 — about a 6.7% chance the cumulative shock exceeds +$3M by year 4.
  3. Covariance of two horizons: Cov(W₂, W₄) = min(2, 4) = 2. The correlation is 2 / (√2·√4) = 2 / 2.828 ≈ 0.71 — the two times are strongly linked because year 4 contains all of year 2's path.
  4. Increment is fresh: the move from year 2 to year 4 is W₄ − W₂ ~ Normal(0, 2), and it is independent of W₂. So knowing the P&L at year 2 tells you nothing about the next two years' increment — only about the level it starts from.
  5. Sanity check: Var(W₄) = Var(W₂) + Var(W₄ − W₂) = 2 + 2 = 4 ✓ — variances of independent increments add, exactly as axiom (2) promises.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate Brownian motion and check (dW)² = dt

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Matplotlib — install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We build a path by accumulating Gaussian increments, then numerically confirm the variance grows like t and the quadratic variation lands on t.

⬇ Need the environment? (reuse Week-1 setup)

If you built quant-env in Lecture 1, just activate it. Otherwise:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter
# brownian.py — Lecture 8
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(8)
T, n = 1.0, 10_000            # one unit of time, 10k steps
dt = T / n                    # mesh size

# increments: each ~ Normal(0, dt)  ->  W_t - W_s ~ Normal(0, t-s)
dW = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(dt), size=n)
W  = np.concatenate(([0.0], np.cumsum(dW)))   # W_0 = 0

print("W_T  (should vary, mean 0):", round(W[-1], 4))
print("Var across many paths -> t:")          # build 50k endpoints
ends = rng.normal(0.0, np.sqrt(T), size=50_000)
print("   empirical Var(W_T) =", round(ends.var(), 4), " theory =", T)

# quadratic variation: sum of squared increments -> t
qv = np.sum(dW**2)
print("quadratic variation   =", round(qv, 4), " theory =", T)   # ~ 1.0

The empirical Var(W_T) sits at ≈ 1.0 (= T) and the quadratic variation np.sum(dW**2) also lands near 1.0 — not zero. Halve dt (double n) and the signed sum W[-1] stays wild, but the sum of squares tightens around T. That contrast — noisy direction, deterministic squared-variation — is the whole engine of Itô calculus.

🔗 Where this is heading

The identity (dW)² = dt you just verified numerically is the seed of Itô's lemma (Week 9) — the chain rule for functions of Brownian motion, and the step that turns S₀·exp[(μ−½σ²)t + σWₜ] into the stochastic differential equation dS = μS dt + σS dW. From there, replacing the drift μ with the risk-free rate gives the Black–Scholes option-pricing formula in Course 3, and the same Brownian engine drives interest-rate and credit models later in the program. Q1. For a standard Brownian motion Wₜ, what is the variance of the position at time t = 9?
A. 3
B. 9
C. 81
D. 0
Show answer

B. Axiom (2) gives Wₜ ~ Normal(0, t), so Var(W₉) = t = 9. The value 3 (A) is the standard deviation √9, not the variance; 81 (C) is t² (a common slip); 0 (D) is the mean E[W₉], not the variance.

Q2. What is Cov(W₃, W₇) for a standard Brownian motion?
A. 7
B. 10
C. 3
D. 21
Show answer

C. Cov(Wₛ, Wₜ) = min(s, t) = min(3, 7) = 3 — two times share the variance accumulated up to the earlier one. 7 (A) is max(s,t); 10 (B) is the sum s+t; 21 (D) is the product s·t. Only the minimum is correct.

Q3. Why is a Brownian path nowhere differentiable?
A. Because the path is discontinuous and jumps
B. Because over a window Δt the move scales like √Δt, so ΔW/Δt ~ 1/√Δt blows up as Δt → 0
C. Because E[Wₜ] = 0 forces a flat path
D. Because the increments are correlated
Show answer

B. The typical increment is of size √Δt, so the difference quotient is √Δt/Δt = 1/√Δt → ∞ and no slope exists. The path is in fact continuous, so (A) is false; a zero mean (C) does not flatten an individual path; the increments are independent, not correlated (D).

Q4. The quadratic variation of standard Brownian motion over [0, t] equals…
A. 0, just like a smooth function
B. t²
C. √t
D. t
Show answer

D. Summing squared increments gives Σ(ΔW)² → t, captured by the slogan (dW)² = dt. Smooth functions do have zero quadratic variation (A), but Brownian motion does not — that's exactly what makes it special. t² (B) and √t (C) are not the limit; the sum converges to t itself.

Q5. In geometric Brownian motion Sₜ = S₀·exp[(μ − ½σ²)t + σWₜ], why is the exponential used instead of modeling Sₜ as Brownian motion directly?
A. To keep the price strictly positive and make returns multiplicative
B. To make the path differentiable
C. To remove all randomness from the price
D. To force the variance to stop growing with time
Show answer

A. The exponential guarantees Sₜ > 0 (a price can't go negative) and makes returns proportional rather than additive, matching real markets. It does not smooth the path — exp of a jagged function is still jagged (B); the σWₜ term keeps the randomness (C); and uncertainty still grows with the horizon (D).
