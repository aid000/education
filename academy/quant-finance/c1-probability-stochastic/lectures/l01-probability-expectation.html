Probability, Expectation & Variance

The three numbers a quant reaches for first: how likely, how much on average, and how much it swings.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • State the probability axioms and define a random variable.
  • Compute the expectation E[X] of a discrete random variable and read it as a long-run average.
  • Compute variance and standard deviation, and explain what they measure.
  • Use linearity of expectation, and avoid the trap that E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X]).
  • Estimate E[X] and Var(X) by Monte-Carlo simulation in NumPy and watch the law of large numbers converge.

1.1Sample Spaces, Events & the Axioms

Probability starts with an experiment whose outcome is uncertain. The set of all possible outcomes is the sample space Ω; a subset of outcomes is an event. Rolling a die has Ω = {1,2,3,4,5,6}; "rolled even" is the event {2,4,6}.

A probability P assigns each event a number obeying three rules (Kolmogorov's axioms):

1) P(A) ≥ 0    2) P(Ω) = 1    3) P(A ∪ B) = P(A) + P(B) if A, B disjointProbabilities are non-negative, the certain event has probability 1, and disjoint events add.

Everything else — complements, unions, conditional probability — is derived from these three. For a fair die, each outcome has probability 1/6, so P(even) = 3/6 = 1/2.

Common mistake"Probability" and "odds" are not the same. A probability of 1/4 corresponds to odds of 1 to 3 (one favorable to three unfavorable), not "1 to 4". Quant interview puzzles routinely exploit this slip.

1.2Random Variables

A random variable (RV) X is a function that attaches a number to each outcome. The die roll itself is an RV; so is your profit on a bet that pays $10 on a six and −$2 otherwise.

A discrete RV takes countably many values, described by its probability mass function (PMF) p(x) = P(X = x). The masses are non-negative and sum to 1:

Σx p(x) = 1,   p(x) ≥ 0

A continuous RV (a stock return, say) is described instead by a probability density f(x), where probabilities are areas: P(a ≤ X ≤ b) = ∫ab f(x) dx. This course starts discrete, because the intuition transfers directly and the algebra is cleaner.

1.3Expectation — the Long-Run Average

The expectation (or mean) of a discrete RV is the probability-weighted average of its values:

E[X] = Σx x · p(x)Each value weighted by how likely it is. Also written μ.

For a fair die, E[X] = (1+2+3+4+5+6)/6 = 3.5. You can never roll a 3.5 — the expectation is not a typical outcome but the average you'd converge to over many rolls. That convergence is the law of large numbers (LLN), which we'll watch happen in code below.

AnalogyExpectation is the center of mass of the PMF. Picture the six die-bars as equal weights on a ruler at positions 1–6; the ruler balances at 3.5. Skew the weights toward high values and the balance point slides right — exactly what a positive-edge bet does to your expected payoff.

The single most useful property is linearity of expectation — it holds even when variables are dependent:

E[aX + bY] = a·E[X] + b·E[Y]

This is the quant's workhorse: the expected value of a portfolio is just the weighted sum of the expected values of its parts, no matter how correlated they are.

1.4Variance & Standard Deviation — the Spread

Expectation tells you the center; variance tells you how far outcomes typically stray from it. It's the expected squared deviation from the mean:

Var(X) = E[(X − μ)²] = E[X²] − (E[X])²The right-hand form ("mean of the square minus square of the mean") is almost always easier to compute.

Because variance is in squared units, we usually report its square root, the standard deviation σ = √Var(X), which is back in the original units. In finance, σ of returns is exactly what we call volatility — the headline measure of risk.

Common mistakeExpectation is linear, but variance is not. Var(aX) = a²·Var(X) (the constant comes out squared), and Var(X + Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) only when X and Y are uncorrelated. Forgetting the covariance term is how people badly under- or over-estimate portfolio risk.

One more warning that pays for itself in interviews: for a non-linear function g, E[g(X)] ≠ g(E[X]) in general. The average of the squares is not the square of the average — indeed E[X²] − (E[X])² = Var(X) ≥ 0 is precisely the gap. (This one-directional gap is Jensen's inequality.)

✦ Worked Example — a simple bet

You roll one fair die. A casino pays you $12 if it lands on a 6, and you pay $3 otherwise. Let X be your net profit. Find E[X], Var(X), and the standard deviation — then decide whether to play.

  1. List outcomes & probabilities: X = +12 with p = 1/6; X = −3 with p = 5/6.
  2. Expectation: E[X] = 12·(1/6) + (−3)·(5/6) = 2 − 2.5 = −$0.50. The game has a negative edge — over many plays you lose 50¢ per roll on average.
  3. Mean of the square: E[X²] = 12²·(1/6) + (−3)²·(5/6) = 144/6 + 9·5/6 = 24 + 7.5 = 31.5.
  4. Variance: Var(X) = E[X²] − (E[X])² = 31.5 − (−0.5)² = 31.5 − 0.25 = 31.25 (dollars²).
  5. Standard deviation: σ = √31.25 ≈ $5.59 — the swings dwarf the tiny negative mean, which is exactly why a single roll feels like a coin-flip even though the house edge is real.
  6. Decision: negative expectation ⟹ don't play for profit. The casino's edge only reveals itself over many rolls (the LLN again).

🛠️ Hands-On: estimate E and Var by Monte Carlo

Python + NumPy + Jupyter is the free stack we use all course. Week 1 setup: install it once (below), then you'll reuse it every lecture and for the course project.

⬇ Install Python + NumPy + Jupyter (one-time, Week 1 setup)

Get Python 3 from python.org (macOS/Windows) or your package manager. Then create an isolated environment and install the stack:

python3 -m venv quant-env
# activate it:
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux
quant-env\Scripts\activate         # Windows (PowerShell)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter

Verify: run python -c "import numpy; print(numpy.__version__)" — a version string means you're set. Launch notebooks anytime with jupyter lab.

Now simulate the casino bet from the worked example. We play it 1,000,000 times and let the sample mean and variance approach the exact values we computed by hand.

# montecarlo_bet.py — Lecture 1
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(42)        # reproducible randomness
N = 1_000_000
rolls  = rng.integers(1, 7, size=N)    # uniform on 1..6
profit = np.where(rolls == 6, 12.0, -3.0)

print("sample mean    :", profit.mean())   # ≈ -0.50  (E[X])
print("sample variance:", profit.var())    # ≈ 31.25  (Var(X))
print("sample std dev :", profit.std())     # ≈ 5.59   (σ)

Run it a few times with different N (say 100, then 10,000, then 1,000,000). Watch the estimates tighten around −0.50 and 31.25 as N grows — that is the law of large numbers, the bedrock of every Monte-Carlo pricer you'll build later in this specialization.

🔗 Where this is heading

Expectation and variance aren't just bookkeeping — they're the seeds of the whole course. Add up many independent steps and the distribution of the sum sharpens into a bell curve (Week 2's Central Limit Theorem). Let those steps accumulate over time and you get a random walk (Week 5), whose continuous limit is Brownian motion (Week 7) — the mathematical object underneath the Black–Scholes option-pricing model you'll build in Course 3. Every one of those models is ultimately an expectation you estimate by the Monte-Carlo method you just ran.

❓ Lecture 1 Quiz

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The expectation E[X] of a discrete random variable is best described as…
A. the most likely single outcome
B. the probability-weighted average of its values
C. the middle value when outcomes are sorted
D. the spread of outcomes around the center
Show answer

B. E[X] = Σ x·p(x), each value weighted by its probability. The most likely outcome is the mode (A); the middle value is the median (C); the spread is the variance (D).

Q2. A fair coin pays $1 for heads and $0 for tails. The variance of the payoff is…
A. 0.5
B. 0.25
C. 1.0
D. 0
Show answer

B. E[X] = 0.5, E[X²] = 1²·0.5 + 0²·0.5 = 0.5, so Var = 0.5 − 0.5² = 0.5 − 0.25 = 0.25. (This is the familiar p(1−p) for a Bernoulli variable.)

Q3. Linearity of expectation, E[X + Y] = E[X] + E[Y], requires that X and Y be…
A. independent
B. uncorrelated
C. identically distributed
D. nothing special — it always holds
Show answer

D. Linearity of expectation holds for any X and Y, dependent or not. (Independence is what you need to multiply expectations or to add variances — not to add expectations.)

Q4. If Var(X) = 9, then Var(2X) equals…
A. 18
B. 9
C. 36
D. 6
Show answer

C. Constants pull out of variance squared: Var(aX) = a²·Var(X) = 2²·9 = 36. (The standard deviation, by contrast, just doubles: 2·3 = 6.)

Q5. In a Monte-Carlo simulation, the sample mean approaches the true expectation as the number of samples grows. This is a statement of…
A. the central limit theorem
B. the law of large numbers
C. Bayes' rule
D. Jensen's inequality
Show answer

B. The law of large numbers says the sample average converges to E[X]. The CLT (A) is the related but distinct statement about the shape (normal) and scale (1/√N) of the error around that limit.
