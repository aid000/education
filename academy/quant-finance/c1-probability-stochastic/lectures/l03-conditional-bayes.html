Conditional Probability & Bayes

Every new tick, headline, and credit report is evidence — Bayes' rule is the machine that turns evidence into a revised probability.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define conditional probability P(A|B) as a renormalization on the world where B is known to have happened.
  • Apply the multiplication rule and the law of total probability to break a hard event into manageable cases.
  • State Bayes' theorem and use it to update a prior into a posterior as evidence arrives.
  • Distinguish independence from conditional independence, and explain why both matter for risk models.
  • Diagnose the base-rate fallacy and compute a correct posterior for a noisy default signal in NumPy.

3.1Conditioning: Shrinking the World

So far every probability lived in the full sample space Ω. But in markets you almost never start from scratch — you already know something. The Fed has spoken; the earnings number is out; the bond is in a recession-hit sector. Conditional probability answers: given that event B has occurred, how likely is event A now?

The definition is a renormalization. Once you know B happened, B becomes your new universe, so you only count the part of A that lives inside B, and you rescale by how big B is:

P(A | B) = P(A ∩ B) / P(B),   provided P(B) > 0"The fraction of B's probability that also lands in A." The conditioning event B is the new denominator.

Rearranging gives the multiplication rule, which is just the same statement read the other way — the probability that both happen is the chance of one times the chance of the other given the first:

P(A ∩ B) = P(B) · P(A | B) = P(A) · P(B | A)

That symmetry — two ways to write the same joint probability — is the entire seed of Bayes' theorem in §3.3. Hold onto it.

AnalogyConditioning is zooming in. The full sample space is a satellite map of a whole country; learning "B happened" crops the photo to one city. Probabilities don't disappear — they get rescaled so the cropped city sums back to 1. A 2%-of-the-country event can become a 40%-of-the-city event once you zoom in.

3.2The Law of Total Probability

Sometimes the event you care about is tangled, but it becomes simple once you split the world into exhaustive, non-overlapping cases. Suppose B1, …, Bn partition Ω — exactly one of them must occur. Then any event A can be reassembled from its pieces inside each case:

P(A) = Σi P(A | Bi) · P(Bi)Total probability = weighted average of the conditional probabilities, weighted by how likely each case is.

This is how a quant computes a headline number that depends on an unseen regime. Want the probability a bond defaults next year? Split on the macro state — expansion, slowdown, recession — estimate the default probability inside each, and weight by how likely each regime is:

P(default) = P(def | exp)·P(exp) + P(def | slow)·P(slow) + P(def | rec)·P(rec)

The figure below shows the two-case version, the workhorse you will reach for most often: a "signal" event A whose total probability is assembled from the two columns of a partition.

Common mistakeThe cases must be mutually exclusive and exhaustive — a true partition. If your regimes overlap, you double-count; if they leave a gap, you under-count. And remember to weight by P(Bi): averaging the conditional probabilities without the weights silently assumes every regime is equally likely.

3.3Bayes' Theorem & Posterior Updating

Now the payoff. Often you can easily measure P(evidence | cause) — how a test or signal behaves given the true state — but you actually want P(cause | evidence), the reverse. Bayes' theorem flips the conditioning. Set the two multiplication-rule expressions for P(A ∩ B) equal and solve:

P(H | E) = P(E | H) · P(H) / P(E)posterior = (likelihood × prior) / evidence. H = hypothesis, E = observed evidence.

Read it as an update. The prior P(H) is your belief before the data. The likelihood P(E|H) says how well the hypothesis explains the evidence. Their product, rescaled by the total chance of the evidence P(E) (computed via §3.2's law of total probability), is the posterior P(H|E) — your revised belief.

P(H | E) = P(E|H)·P(H) / [ P(E|H)·P(H) + P(E|¬H)·P(¬H) ]The fully expanded two-hypothesis form: the denominator is just total probability over H and not-H.

This is the formal engine of learning from data. A credit model starts with a prior default probability from the issuer's rating, then sees a missed payment (evidence) and outputs a higher posterior. A trader holds a prior that a stock will rise, sees an unusual volume spike (a noisy signal), and updates. Tomorrow's posterior becomes the next day's prior — Bayesian updating is sequential by design.

AnalogyBayes is a courtroom, not a crystal ball. The prior is the defendant's reputation before trial; each piece of evidence nudges belief up or down by how much more consistent it is with guilt than innocence (the likelihood ratio); the verdict is the posterior. Strong evidence against a wildly implausible prior still rarely convicts — exactly the base-rate effect in §3.5.

3.4Independence vs. Conditional Independence

Two events are independent when knowing one tells you nothing about the other — conditioning changes nothing:

A ⫫ B  ⟺  P(A | B) = P(A)  ⟺  P(A ∩ B) = P(A)·P(B)

Independence is what lets you multiply probabilities — the reason a sequence of fair coin flips, or i.i.d. samples in the CLT (Lecture 2), factorizes so cleanly.

Conditional independence is subtler and is where many risk models live or die. A and B can be dependent overall, yet become independent once you condition on a third variable C — a hidden common cause:

A ⫫ B | C  ⟺  P(A ∩ B | C) = P(A | C)·P(B | C)

Two firms' defaults look correlated because both respond to the economy. Condition on the macro state C, and within that regime their defaults may be (roughly) independent — the dependence was the shared driver, not a direct link. This is precisely the structure behind factor models of credit risk.

Common mistakeConditional independence does not imply unconditional independence, and vice versa. Assuming defaults are independent unconditionally — ignoring the shared macro factor — catastrophically understates the chance of many defaulting at once. That exact error, baking independence into pooled-credit models, was central to the 2008 mortgage-securities blowup.
✦ Worked Example — a noisy default-warning signal

A monitoring model flags bonds likely to default. Historically 2% of bonds in this pool default within a year (the base rate). The flag is decent but imperfect: it fires for 90% of bonds that truly default (sensitivity), but it also fires for 8% of healthy bonds (false-positive rate). A bond just got flagged. What is the probability it actually defaults?

  1. Name the events: H = "defaults", so P(H) = 0.02 and P(¬H) = 0.98. Evidence E = "flag fires", with likelihood P(E|H) = 0.90 and false alarm P(E|¬H) = 0.08.
  2. Numerator (true alarms): P(E|H)·P(H) = 0.90 × 0.02 = 0.018.
  3. False-alarm mass: P(E|¬H)·P(¬H) = 0.08 × 0.98 = 0.0784.
  4. Total probability of a flag: P(E) = 0.018 + 0.0784 = 0.0964.
  5. Bayes: P(H|E) = 0.018 / 0.0964 ≈ 0.187 — about an 18.7% chance of default despite the flag.
  6. Interpret: the flag raised the probability roughly 9-fold (from 2% to ~19%), yet most flagged bonds are still fine. Why? The healthy pool is so large that 8% of it (a flood of false alarms) swamps the true positives. The rare base rate dominates — the lesson of §3.5.

3.5The Base-Rate Fallacy

The worked example exposes the most expensive mistake in applied probability. Faced with a 90%-sensitive flag, intuition screams "90% chance of default!" — but the correct answer was 19%. The trap is ignoring the prior (the base rate) and reading the likelihood as if it were the posterior. This is the base-rate fallacy.

Mathematically, P(H|E) ≠ P(E|H). They are linked by Bayes, and the gap between them is set entirely by the prior. When the base rate P(H) is tiny, even a strong test produces mostly false positives, because the huge "negative" population manufactures false alarms faster than the small "positive" population produces true ones.

Common mistakeConfusing P(E|H) with P(H|E) — the "prosecutor's fallacy." A signal that is 95% accurate is not a 95%-confident verdict when the event it hunts is rare. In trading this shows up as overreacting to a flashy-but-rare pattern; in surveillance and fraud detection it shows up as drowning in false alarms. Always anchor on the base rate first.

🛠️ Hands-On: posterior updating & a Monte-Carlo check

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Jupyter — the install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We compute the posterior in closed form, then simulate a million bonds to confirm Bayes is just bookkeeping on counts.

# bayes_default.py — Lecture 3
import numpy as np

prior     = 0.02     # base rate: P(default)
sens      = 0.90     # P(flag | default)
fpr       = 0.08     # P(flag | healthy)

# --- closed-form Bayes ---
num   = sens * prior
evid  = sens * prior + fpr * (1 - prior)   # law of total probability
post  = num / evid
print("P(default | flag) =", round(post, 4))   # 0.1867

# --- Monte-Carlo sanity check ---
rng   = np.random.default_rng(3)
N     = 1_000_000
defaulted = rng.random(N) < prior                         # true state
p_flag    = np.where(defaulted, sens, fpr)                # flag prob per bond
flagged   = rng.random(N) < p_flag
post_mc   = defaulted[flagged].mean()                     # of flagged, frac that defaulted
print("Monte-Carlo estimate =", round(post_mc, 4))        # ~0.187

The simulation never uses Bayes' formula — it just counts how many flagged bonds truly defaulted — yet it lands on the same ~0.187. Now experiment: push prior to 0.20 and the posterior leaps past 70%; drop it to 0.005 and even this good flag becomes nearly worthless. That sensitivity to the base rate is the whole lesson, made tangible.

🔗 Where this is heading

Conditioning is the spine of stochastic processes. A Markov chain (Week 6) is defined entirely by conditional probabilities — tomorrow's state given today's. The conditional expectation E[X | information so far] becomes a martingale (Week 9), the "fair game" object behind no-arbitrage pricing: under the risk-neutral measure, an option's price is a conditional expectation of its payoff. And sequential Bayesian updating is the engine of the Kalman filter and modern signal-driven strategies in Course 4, where each new tick is evidence that refines an estimate of a hidden "true" price.

💬 Discussion — Week 3

A backtested pattern fires a "buy" signal that has correctly preceded a one-day rally 70% of the time in your historical sample. A colleague says, "70% win rate — we should bet big every time it fires." Using the language of priors, likelihoods, and base rates, explain what is missing from that reasoning. What would you need to know before sizing a trade on this signal, and how could the base-rate fallacy or a hidden conditioning variable make the real edge much smaller than 70%?

Show a model response

The "70%" is a likelihood-flavored statistic — it describes how often a rally was preceded by the signal-firing condition — and it is being read as if it were the posterior probability of profit, P(rally | signal). Three things are missing:

1. The base rate. If the asset rallies on 65% of all days anyway, a 70% conditional rate is almost no edge — the signal barely moves the prior. The relevant question is the lift P(rally | signal) − P(rally), not the raw 70%.

2. A hidden conditioning variable. The signal may only have fired during a single trending regime in the sample (a common cause C). Conditional on "bull market," its edge could be real; unconditionally, it may vanish or reverse. Defaults-and-macro all over again: correlation through a shared driver, not a direct link.

3. Payoff asymmetry and costs. Win rate is not expectation. A 70% win that gains a little but the 30% loss is large can have negative E[return] once transaction costs and slippage are subtracted (Lecture 1's edge vs. variance).

Before sizing, I'd want: the unconditional base rate, an out-of-sample test across regimes, the win/loss magnitudes (not just the rate), the sample size behind "70%," and costs. Only then does a Kelly-style sizing make sense.

✓ Self-check rubric: A strong answer (1) distinguishes P(rally|signal) from P(signal|rally) and from the unconditional base rate; (2) names a hidden conditioning variable / regime as a confound; (3) separates win-rate from expected payoff/edge; and (4) lists at least one concrete piece of missing data before sizing.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. The definition of conditional probability P(A|B) is…
A. P(A) · P(B)
B. P(A ∩ B) / P(B)
C. P(A ∩ B) / P(A)
D. P(A) + P(B) − P(A ∩ B)
Show answer

B. Conditioning renormalizes by the conditioning event B, so the denominator is P(B). P(A)·P(B) (A) is the joint only under independence; dividing by P(A) (C) would give P(B|A); and (D) is the union P(A ∪ B), not a conditional.

Q2. Bayes' theorem is most directly used to…
A. convert a likelihood P(E|H) into the reverse conditional P(H|E)
B. prove that two events are independent
C. compute the variance of a sum
D. guarantee the sample mean converges to the true mean
Show answer

A. Bayes flips the conditioning, turning the easy-to-measure P(E|H) and a prior P(H) into the posterior P(H|E). Independence tests (B) use P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B); variance of a sum (C) is Lecture 1; convergence of the mean (D) is the law of large numbers.

Q3. A flag fires for 90% of true defaulters but also for 8% of healthy bonds, and only 2% of bonds default. A flagged bond's probability of default is closest to…
A. 90%
B. 2%
C. 19%
D. 50%
Show answer

C. Bayes gives 0.9·0.02 / (0.9·0.02 + 0.08·0.98) = 0.018/0.0964 ≈ 0.187. 90% (A) is the sensitivity P(flag|default), not the posterior — the base-rate fallacy. 2% (B) ignores the evidence entirely; 50% (D) is just a guess.

Q4. Two firms' defaults are correlated overall, but become independent once you condition on the macro regime. This is an example of…
A. mutual exclusivity
B. conditional independence given a common factor
C. unconditional independence
D. the law of large numbers
Show answer

B. The shared macro factor is the common cause C; conditioning on it removes the dependence, so the defaults are independent given C. They are not unconditionally independent (C) — they are correlated — and they certainly aren't mutually exclusive (A), which would mean they can't both happen. (D) is unrelated.

Q5. The law of total probability lets you compute P(A) by…
A. multiplying P(A) by the number of cases
B. taking a simple unweighted average of P(A|Bi) across cases
C. averaging P(A|Bi) over a partition, weighted by each P(Bi)
D. adding P(A|Bi) without any weights
Show answer

C. P(A) = Σ P(A|Bi)·P(Bi) — a probability-weighted average over a partition. An unweighted average (B) or a bare sum (D) ignores how likely each case is, and (A) is meaningless. The weights P(Bi) are exactly what makes it correct.
