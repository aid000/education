Week 5 · Lecture 5

Discrete-Time Random Walks

Stack up coin flips one step at a time and you get the simplest model of a price — wandering nowhere on average, yet drifting ever wider as the √n clock ticks.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Define the simple symmetric random walk as a cumulative sum of independent ±1 steps.
  • Show that its expected position is 0 while its variance grows linearly in n, giving the √n spread.
  • Use symmetry and reflection to reason about where the walk can be and how often it crosses a level.
  • Build the gambler's-ruin intuition: a fair game still ends in ruin against a deep-pocketed house.
  • Simulate a price path as a cumulative sum of independent returns in NumPy and read the √t fan.

5.1The Simple Symmetric Random Walk

Weeks 1–4 gave us single random variables and how they combine. Now we let randomness accumulate over time. Start at position 0. At each tick, flip a fair coin: step +1 on heads, −1 on tails. Your position after n steps is the running total of those flips. That object — a cumulative sum of independent, identical ±1 steps — is the simple symmetric random walk, the atom of every dynamic model in this specialization.

Write the step at time i as Xi, with P(Xi = +1) = P(Xi = −1) = 1/2. The position after n steps is their sum Sn:

Sn = X1 + X2 + … + Xn,    S0 = 0Each Xi is ±1 with equal probability, independent of every other. "Symmetric" means up and down are equally likely; "simple" means steps are unit-size.

The word "discrete-time" means the clock advances in whole ticks — flip 1, flip 2, flip 3 — rather than flowing continuously. In Week 7 we shrink the step size and speed up the clock until the walk becomes the continuous Brownian motion that underlies modern option pricing. Everything there is foreshadowed here, in arithmetic a coin can do.

The finance translation is immediate. Replace each ±1 with a one-period return — the small gain or loss an asset earns in a day — and the cumulative sum Sn becomes a price path. A price is, to a first approximation, just the running total of the independent shocks that hit it. That single sentence is the seed of this entire week.

AnalogyPicture a slightly tipsy walker on a long sidewalk who, at each step, flips a coin to go one tile left or right. Watch for a moment and you'd swear they're "heading" somewhere — but they have no destination. Their average position stays right where they started; what grows is the range of places you might find them. A stock with no drift behaves the same way: no built-in direction, just widening uncertainty.

5.2Mean Zero, Variance That Grows Linearly

How does the walk behave? Two numbers answer almost everything: where it sits on average, and how far it scatters. Both follow from the rules of Lectures 1 and 4 — linearity of expectation, and that variances of independent variables add.

Each step has mean E[Xi] = (+1)(½) + (−1)(½) = 0. By linearity of expectation (which never needs independence), the expected position is a sum of zeros:

E[Sn] = E[X1] + … + E[Xn] = 0The walk has no drift: on average it goes nowhere, no matter how long you run it.

Now the spread. Each step has variance Var(Xi) = E[Xi²] − 0² = 1, because (±1)² = 1 always. The steps are independent, so their variances add with no covariance term (the lesson of Week 4):

Var(Sn) = Var(X1) + … + Var(Xn) = n  ⟹   SD(Sn) = √nVariance grows linearly in n; the standard deviation — the typical distance from start — grows like the square root of n.

This is the headline of the whole lecture. Variance piles up in proportion to time, so the typical displacement of the walk after n steps is on the order of √n, not n. After 100 steps you are typically about 10 tiles from the origin; after 400 steps, about 20 — quadruple the time for only double the spread. In markets this is the famous square-root-of-time scaling of volatility: risk over a horizon of T days is roughly σdaily·√T, which is exactly why a daily volatility is annualized by multiplying by √252.

Common mistakeIt is the variance that grows linearly in n, not the standard deviation. Students routinely say "the spread doubles when time doubles" — wrong. SD grows like √n, so doubling the horizon multiplies the typical spread by only √2 ≈ 1.41. Likewise, volatility scales with √T, never with T. Confusing the two is how horizons get mis-sized and risk gets badly misquoted.

5.3Symmetry & the Reflection Principle

Because up and down are equally likely, the walk has a powerful symmetry: any path is exactly as probable as its mirror image flipped about the time axis. Replace every +1 with −1 and you get an equally likely path ending at −Sn instead of +Sn. So the distribution of Sn is symmetric about 0 — P(Sn = +k) = P(Sn = −k) for every k.

The exact distribution is a relabeled binomial. If U of the n steps are up and the rest down, the position is Sn = U − (n − U) = 2U − n, where U ~ Binomial(n, ½). The walk can only land on values of the same parity as n (even n ⟹ even position), and the most likely spot is the start:

P(Sn = 2k − n) = C(n, k) · (½)nk up-steps out of n. The position is a stretched, recentred binomial — and by the CLT of Week 2, it is approximately Normal(0, n) for large n.

The deeper tool is the reflection principle: to count paths that touch some level a before time n, reflect the portion of the path after its first touch of a across the line y = a. This sets up a clean one-to-one correspondence between "paths that crossed a" and a simpler set of paths, turning hard first-passage questions into easy counting. It is the combinatorial engine behind barrier-option pricing — contracts that pay out only if the price ever reaches a level, not just where it finishes.

AnalogyThe reflection principle is a hall of mirrors for paths. Every trajectory that pokes above a ceiling has a mirror twin obtained by reflecting its tail in that ceiling. Counting the twins — which is easy — counts the originals. Casinos and exchanges both quietly rely on this trick to price "touch" bets that trigger the moment a level is hit.

5.4Gambler's Ruin & the Price-Path Picture

Symmetry says the walk drifts nowhere on average — so a fair game should be safe to play forever, right? Gambler's ruin says otherwise, and the gap is one of the most instructive results in all of probability.

You start with i dollars and bet $1 per round on a fair coin against a house holding the rest of N dollars. The game ends when you hit $0 (ruin) or $N (you've broken the bank). For a fair walk, the probability you reach N before 0 is simply your share of the total stake:

P(reach N before 0) = i / N,    P(ruin) = 1 − i / NLinear in your starting wealth. Expected number of rounds until the game ends is i·(N − i).

The sting is in the limit. Against an effectively infinite bankroll (N → ∞), your ruin probability i/N → 0 of winning means ruin is certain: a fair game played against a deep-pocketed opponent ends in your ruin with probability 1, even though your expected wealth at every instant is exactly your starting wealth. Zero is an absorbing wall; the unbounded side is not. The walk, given enough time, always finds the wall.

This is why position sizing and stop-out levels matter more than edge for survival. A trader with a genuine positive expectation can still be wiped out by a run of bad luck if bet sizes are large relative to capital — the discrete cousin of gambler's ruin, formalized later by the Kelly criterion. "The market can stay irrational longer than you can stay solvent" is gambler's ruin in plain English.

Common mistake"The game is fair, so I can't lose in the long run" is dangerously false. A zero-drift random walk still hits any fixed loss level with probability 1 if you play long enough and your capital is finite. Fair expectation does not imply safe; it is the variance, colliding with a hard floor at zero, that ruins you.

Tie it back to prices. A price path with no drift is a random walk; add a small upward step bias (a positive expected return) and you get a random walk with drift, the workhorse model for an asset that earns a risk premium. But the no-drift symmetric walk is the right null hypothesis: if you cannot beat it, your "strategy" is just paying fees to flip coins.

✦ Worked Example — where is the walk after 100 steps, and a fair bet's ruin odds

A symmetric ±1 walk runs for n = 100 steps. (a) Give its mean and standard deviation and a 95% range for its final position. (b) You start with $30 and bet $1 per fair round against a house with $70 more, so N = 100. Find your probability of breaking the bank and your expected number of rounds.

  1. Mean position: E[S100] = 0 — no drift, so the center is the origin.
  2. Spread: Var(S100) = n = 100, so SD = √100 = 10. The typical distance from start after 100 flips is about 10 tiles.
  3. 95% range (CLT, Week 2): S100 ≈ Normal(0, 100), so a ±2σ band is 0 ± 2·10 = [−20, +20]. Ending beyond ±20 happens only about 5% of the time.
  4. Gambler's ruin set-up: i = 30, N = 100. Probability of reaching $100 before $0 is i/N = 30/100 = 0.30.
  5. Ruin probability: 1 − 0.30 = 0.70 — despite a perfectly fair coin, you bust 70% of the time because you start with the smaller stake.
  6. Expected game length: i·(N − i) = 30·70 = 2,100 rounds. A "fair" game can run a long time before the absorbing wall is reached — but reached it will be.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate a price path as a cumulative sum

Reusing the Week-1 environment (NumPy + Matplotlib — the install block is in Lecture 1 if you skipped it). We build many random walks at once, confirm the variance-equals-n law, and view the √n fan that brackets the paths.

⬇ Need the environment? (reuse Week-1 setup)

If you built quant-env in Lecture 1, just activate it. Otherwise:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter
# random_walk.py — Lecture 5
import numpy as np

rng   = np.random.default_rng(5)
paths = 20_000          # number of independent walks
n     = 100             # steps per walk

# each step is +1 or -1 with prob 1/2; a price path is the cumulative sum
steps = rng.choice([-1, 1], size=(paths, n))
walks = np.cumsum(steps, axis=1)          # shape (paths, n)
final = walks[:, -1]                       # position after n steps

print("mean of S_n      :", final.mean())  # ~ 0      (no drift)
print("variance of S_n  :", final.var())   # ~ 100    (= n)
print("std dev of S_n   :", final.std())   # ~ 10     (= sqrt(n))

# the spread at each step t should track sqrt(t):
spread = walks.std(axis=0)                 # std across paths at each step
t      = np.arange(1, n + 1)
print("max |spread - sqrt(t)| :", np.abs(spread - np.sqrt(t)).max())  # small

The mean stays near 0, the variance lands near 100, and spread hugs sqrt(t) step by step — the linear-variance law made visible. Plot a handful of walks rows over ±2*np.sqrt(t) and you'll see most paths stay inside that widening parabolic fan. Swap rng.choice([-1,1]) for rng.normal(0, 1, ...) and the same code becomes a Gaussian-return price simulator — the bridge to Week 7's Brownian motion.

🔗 Where this is heading

The random walk is the discrete skeleton of nearly everything that follows. Next week (Week 6) we ask when a process is a martingale — a "fair game" with no predictable drift — and the symmetric walk is the canonical example. Shrink the step size and accelerate the clock and the walk converges to Brownian motion (Weeks 7–8), where the √n spread becomes the √t scaling of volatility and the binomial position becomes an exact Gaussian. That continuous walk is the engine inside the Black–Scholes option-pricing model of Course 3, and the gambler's-ruin lesson on survival reappears as the Kelly criterion for bet sizing. Master the coin-flip walk and you have already met, in miniature, the central object of quantitative finance.

5 questions · click "Show answer" to check yourself.

Q1. For a simple symmetric random walk after n steps, the variance of the position Sn is…
A. √n
B. n
C. n²
D. 0
Show answer

B. Each ±1 step has variance 1, and independent variances add, so Var(Sn) = n. √n (A) is the standard deviation, not the variance. n² (C) confuses linear growth with squaring. The variance is 0 only at n = 0 (D).

Q2. After 400 steps of a symmetric ±1 walk, the typical distance (standard deviation) from the start is about…
A. 400
B. 200
C. 20
D. 40
Show answer

C. SD = √n = √400 = 20. The full step count 400 (A) and half of it 200 (B) ignore the square root; 40 (D) is √400 doubled. Spread grows like √n, so quadrupling the steps only doubles the spread.

Q3. The expected position E[Sn] of a simple symmetric random walk is…
A. 0, because each step has mean 0 and expectation is linear
B. n/2, the average number of up-steps
C. √n, the same as its spread
D. undefined until n is known
Show answer

A. Each step averages (+1)(½)+(−1)(½)=0, and linearity of expectation sums those zeros to 0 for every n. n/2 (B) is the expected count of up-steps, not the position. √n (C) is the spread, not the mean. The mean is well-defined (0) for all n (D).

Q4. You start with $20 and bet $1 per fair round against a house, with the total stake N = $100. Your probability of reaching $100 before going broke is…
A. 0.50
B. 0.80
C. 0.20
D. 1.00
Show answer

C. For a fair walk the chance of reaching N before 0 is your share i/N = 20/100 = 0.20. It is not 0.50 (A) — a fair coin does not make a fair outcome when stakes are unequal; 0.80 (B) is the ruin probability; 1.00 (D) would require an infinite or larger stake.

Q5. Why does treating a price as a cumulative sum of independent returns lead to the "√t" scaling of volatility?
A. Because returns are always negative on average
B. Because variances of independent returns add, so total variance grows like t and the standard deviation like √t
C. Because expectation grows linearly, so volatility must too
D. Because the Poisson mean equals its variance
Show answer

B. Summing independent returns adds their variances, so variance ∝ t and volatility (the SD) ∝ √t. Returns are not always negative (A); expectation growing linearly describes drift, not the spread (C); the Poisson identity (D) is unrelated to summing returns.
