SDEs & Monte Carlo

The capstone payoff: turn ten weeks of probability into a working simulator that prices an option by rolling thousands of possible futures.

🎯 Learning Objectives

  • Write down geometric Brownian motion and its exact closed-form solution, and explain the −½σ² drift correction.
  • Discretize any SDE with the Euler–Maruyama scheme and know why the noise step scales as √Δt.
  • Simulate many GBM price paths in NumPy using vectorized cumulative sums.
  • Estimate an option-like expected payoff by Monte Carlo, and quantify its 1/√N error — the capstone of the course.

10.1From Brownian Motion to a Stochastic Differential Equation

Across the last three weeks we built Brownian motion Wt — the continuous limit of a random walk — and in Week 9 we learned to do calculus with it (Itô calculus). Now we put those tools to work. A stochastic differential equation (SDE) is the recipe for how a random quantity evolves over an instant of time:

dXt = μ(Xt, t) dt + σ(Xt, t) dWt"drift" × time  +  "diffusion" × Brownian increment

Read it literally as a description of one tiny step. Over an instant dt the quantity changes by two pieces. The drift term μ dt is the predictable, deterministic push — the average direction the process is heading. The diffusion term σ dW is the unpredictable kick, a fresh dose of Brownian noise scaled by the volatility σ. Everything in finance that we model continuously — a stock price, an interest rate, a volatility itself — is some choice of μ and σ in this template.

The crucial fact, inherited from Brownian motion, is that the random kick does not scale like the time step. Over an interval Δt the increment ΔW is normal with mean 0 and variance Δt — so its typical size is √Δt, not Δt. For small Δt the noise √Δt is far larger than the drift Δt. That is why, zoomed in, prices look jagged and unpredictable, while zoomed out the drift can still dominate.

AnalogyThink of a sailor walking the deck of a moving ship. The ship's heading is the drift μ dt — slow, steady, predictable. The deck's pitching tosses each footstep around randomly — that's the diffusion σ dW. Over one second the tossing dominates and the sailor staggers; over an hour the ship's course is what actually decides where the sailor ends up. Markets mix the same two ingredients on every time scale.

10.2Geometric Brownian Motion: the Model of a Price

A stock price can't follow plain Brownian motion — that would let it go negative, and it would mean a \$1 move matters as much for a \$2 stock as for a \$2000 one. Returns, not dollar changes, are what behave consistently. So we make the drift and diffusion proportional to the price itself. That is geometric Brownian motion (GBM), the engine under the Black–Scholes model:

dSt = μ St dt + σ St dWtμ = expected return (drift rate),  σ = volatility,  both per unit time

Here μ S dt says the price grows on average at rate μ (a 7%/year expected return is μ = 0.07), and σ S dW injects noise whose size is σ times the current price. Because both terms scale with S, GBM stays positive and treats percentage moves identically at every price level — exactly how real returns behave.

This SDE has an exact solution. Applying Itô's lemma to ln St (Week 9) turns the multiplicative equation into an additive one, and integrating gives:

St = S0 · exp( (μ − ½σ²) t + σ Wt )ln St is normal ⟹ St is log-normal with the √t scaling of volatility

The startling piece is the −½σ² correction to the drift. Naïvely you'd expect the exponent to grow at rate μ, but Itô's lemma adds the −½σ² term because of the convexity of the exponential: random up-and-down wiggles, when exponentiated, do not cancel — they drag the median path below the mean. This is the same gap between the arithmetic and geometric mean of returns that erodes a volatile portfolio's compounded growth.

Common mistakeDo not confuse the two growth rates. The expected price grows at μ: E[St] = S0eμt. But the expected log-return (the typical compounded growth) is only (μ − ½σ²)t. Writing the solution as S0eμt + σW without the −½σ² is the single most common GBM error — it silently inflates your simulated returns.

10.3Euler–Maruyama: Simulating an SDE Step by Step

For GBM we have a closed form, but most real SDEs (stochastic volatility, interest-rate models) do not. So we need a universal recipe to march any SDE forward numerically. The Euler–Maruyama scheme is exactly the SDE template with dt replaced by a small step Δt and dW replaced by a random draw:

Xk+1 = Xk + μ(Xk) Δt + σ(Xk) · √Δt · ZkZk ~ Normal(0, 1) i.i.d. — one fresh standard-normal per step

The one detail you must get right is the noise: the Brownian increment over Δt has variance Δt, so we write it as √Δt · Z with Z a standard normal. Multiply the standard normal by √Δt, never by Δt. Split a year into n = 252 trading-day steps and Δt = 1/252; the diffusion term gets √(1/252) ≈ 0.063 of the daily noise.

For GBM, plugging μ(X) = μX and σ(X) = σX gives the discrete update directly. In practice we often simulate the log price instead, because for GBM the log update is exact for any step size — there's no discretization error at all:

ln Sk+1 = ln Sk + (μ − ½σ²) Δt + σ √Δt · Zk
Common mistakeScaling the noise by Δt instead of √Δt. If you write sigma * dt * Z your simulated volatility collapses to almost nothing as you refine the grid, and every risk number comes out far too small. The square root is the whole signature of Brownian motion — it is not optional.

10.4Monte Carlo: Pricing by Averaging Many Futures

Here is where the entire course pays off. Many quantities we care about are expected values of a payoff at a future date — and an expectation, by the Law of Large Numbers (Week 1), is just a long-run average. So the Monte Carlo recipe is breathtakingly simple: simulate many possible futures, compute the payoff on each, and average.

Take a European call option that pays max(ST − K, 0) at expiry T — you collect the amount the price exceeds the strike K, or nothing. Its fair value (under the risk-neutral drift, Course 3) is the discounted expected payoff:

Price = e−rT · E[ max(ST − K, 0) ]  ≈  e−rT · (1/N) Σ max(ST(i) − K, 0)simulate N terminal prices ST(1)…ST(N), average the payoffs, discount back

The accuracy is governed by the very same √n law that has shadowed us since the Central Limit Theorem in Week 2. With N independent paths the standard error of the estimate shrinks like:

standard error ≈ s / √Ns = sample standard deviation of the payoffs — halving the error needs 4× the paths

This slow 1/√N convergence is Monte Carlo's blessing and curse. The blessing: the rate does not depend on the number of dimensions, so the method shrugs off problems (baskets of 50 assets, path-dependent options) that defeat every grid-based method. The curse: getting one more digit of accuracy costs 100× the computation. The entire research field of variance reduction exists to beat that constant factor.

AnalogyMonte Carlo is an opinion poll on the future. You can't survey every possible path the market might take, so you sample N of them at random and average their verdicts. Just like a political poll, your margin of error narrows as 1/√N — a poll of 10,000 is only ~3× sharper than one of 1,000, not 10× — and a biased sampler (wrong drift) gives a confidently wrong answer no matter how many paths you draw.
✦ Worked Example — price a call by hand-rolled Monte Carlo (tiny N)

S₀ = 100, strike K = 100, T = 1 year, volatility σ = 0.20, risk-free rate r = 0.05. We use the exact log-normal solution under the risk-neutral drift r, and (to keep it by hand) just N = 4 simulated terminal prices using four pre-drawn standard normals Z = {+1.0, −0.5, +0.3, −1.2}.

  1. Terminal-price formula: ST = S₀·exp((r − ½σ²)T + σ√T·Z). The drift term is (0.05 − ½·0.04)·1 = 0.03; the noise coefficient is σ√T = 0.20.
  2. Z = +1.0: exponent = 0.03 + 0.20·1.0 = 0.23 → ST = 100·e0.23 ≈ 125.86; payoff = max(125.86 − 100, 0) = 25.86.
  3. Z = −0.5: exponent = 0.03 − 0.10 = −0.07 → ST = 100·e−0.07 ≈ 93.24; payoff = max(93.24 − 100, 0) = 0.
  4. Z = +0.3: exponent = 0.03 + 0.06 = 0.09 → ST = 100·e0.09 ≈ 109.42; payoff = 9.42.
  5. Z = −1.2: exponent = 0.03 − 0.24 = −0.21 → ST = 100·e−0.21 ≈ 81.06; payoff = 0.
  6. Average & discount: mean payoff = (25.86 + 0 + 9.42 + 0)/4 = 8.82; price ≈ e−0.05·8.82 ≈ 8.39.
  7. Reality check: the exact Black–Scholes value here is ≈ 10.45. With only N = 4 paths our estimate is noisy — its standard error is huge. Run N = 1,000,000 paths (next panel) and the estimate locks onto 10.45, confirming the simulator and the formula agree.

🛠️ Hands-On: simulate GBM paths and price a call

Reusing the Week-1 stack (NumPy + Matplotlib — setup in Lecture 1). We vectorize the whole simulation: draw an entire matrix of normals, build log-paths with a cumulative sum, then exponentiate. No Python loop over time — this is the capstone pattern you'll reuse everywhere.

⬇ Need the environment? (reuse Week-1 setup)

If you built quant-env in Lecture 1, just activate it. Otherwise:

python3 -m venv quant-env
source quant-env/bin/activate      # macOS / Linux  (quant-env\Scripts\activate on Windows)
pip install numpy scipy matplotlib jupyter
# gbm_mc.py — Lecture 10 (capstone)
import numpy as np

rng = np.random.default_rng(10)
S0, K, T, mu, sigma, r = 100.0, 100.0, 1.0, 0.07, 0.20, 0.05
n_steps, n_paths       = 252, 1_000_000
dt = T / n_steps

# --- 1. simulate full GBM paths (Euler-Maruyama on the log price) ---
Z = rng.standard_normal((n_paths, n_steps))
log_incr = (mu - 0.5*sigma**2)*dt + sigma*np.sqrt(dt)*Z   # note the sqrt(dt)!
log_paths = np.cumsum(log_incr, axis=1)                   # sum increments over time
S = S0 * np.exp(log_paths)                                # (n_paths, n_steps) price matrix
ST = S[:, -1]                                             # terminal prices

print("E[S_T] sim vs theory:", ST.mean(), S0*np.exp(mu*T))

# --- 2. Monte-Carlo price of a European call (risk-neutral: drift = r) ---
ZT  = rng.standard_normal(n_paths)                        # 1-step exact draw for the option
STq = S0 * np.exp((r - 0.5*sigma**2)*T + sigma*np.sqrt(T)*ZT)
payoff = np.maximum(STq - K, 0.0)
price  = np.exp(-r*T) * payoff.mean()
stderr = np.exp(-r*T) * payoff.std() / np.sqrt(n_paths)   # the 1/sqrt(N) error
print(f"MC call price = {price:.3f} +/- {stderr:.3f}")    # ~ 10.45

The simulated E[ST] matches S₀eμT, and the Monte-Carlo call price converges to the Black–Scholes value ≈ 10.45 with a reported ± standard error. Cut n_paths to 10,000 and watch the error balloon by ≈ 10×; that visible 1/√N tradeoff is the same square root from the CLT in Week 2, now pricing a derivative. This is the course in one script.

Q1. In the SDE dX = μ dt + σ dW, over a small interval Δt the random term σ dW has a typical size that scales like…
A. Δt
B. √Δt
C. (Δt)²
D. 1/Δt
Show answer

B. The Brownian increment over Δt has variance Δt, so its standard deviation — its typical magnitude — is √Δt. Scaling it as Δt (A) is the classic error that kills your simulated volatility; (C) and (D) are not how Brownian noise behaves.

Q2. The exact solution of geometric Brownian motion dS = μS dt + σS dW is St = S₀·exp((μ − ½σ²)t + σWt). Where does the −½σ² term come from?
A. A rounding convention with no real effect
B. The risk-free rate being subtracted
C. Itô's lemma — the convexity correction when applying calculus to ln S
D. Discounting the price to present value
Show answer

C. Applying Itô's lemma to ln S adds a −½σ² term because the second-derivative (convexity) term in Itô calculus is non-zero for Brownian motion. It is not a rounding artifact (A), it is unrelated to the rate r (B) or to discounting (D).

Q3. Under geometric Brownian motion, the expected price E[St] grows at which rate?
A. (μ − ½σ²), the log-return drift
B. μ, so E[St] = S₀eμt
C. σ, the volatility
D. zero — GBM is a martingale
Show answer

B. Taking the expectation of the log-normal cancels the −½σ², leaving E[St] = S₀eμt. The (μ − ½σ²) rate (A) governs the median/log-return, not the mean; σ (C) is not a growth rate; GBM is only a martingale in the special case μ = 0 (D).

Q4. You estimate a call price by Monte Carlo with N = 10,000 paths and get a standard error of 0.20. Roughly how many paths do you need to cut the error to 0.05?
A. 40,000
B. 20,000
C. 160,000
D. 100,000
Show answer

C. Error scales as 1/√N. Cutting the error by a factor of 4 (0.20 → 0.05) needs 4² = 16× the paths: 16 × 10,000 = 160,000. Doubling to 20,000 (B) or even 100,000 (D) is nowhere near enough; 40,000 (A) only halves the error.

Q5. Why is Monte Carlo the method of choice for pricing an option on a basket of 50 assets, even though its 1/√N convergence is slow?
A. Its convergence rate is independent of the number of dimensions, unlike grid methods
B. It gives an exact answer with no error
C. It does not require simulating any random numbers
D. It only works for one-dimensional problems
Show answer

A. The 1/√N rate doesn't depend on dimension, so Monte Carlo handles 50-asset baskets that make grid (lattice) methods explode combinatorially. It is never exact (B), it is built on random draws (C), and high dimensions are precisely its strength, not a limitation (D).
