Probability, Expectation & Distributions · Weeks 1–2
1. The expectation E[X] of a discrete random variable is best described as…
Answer: B. E[X] = Σ x·p(x), each value weighted by its probability. The most likely outcome is the mode (A); the middle value is the median (C); the spread is the variance (D).
2. If Var(X) = 5, then Var(3X) equals…
Answer: D. Constants come out of variance squared: Var(aX) = a²Var(X) = 3²·5 = 45. Multiplying the constant through unsquared (A, 3·5=15) is the classic slip; (B) wrongly treats variance like expectation (unchanged); (C) is 5² with no connection to the scaling.
3. According to the Central Limit Theorem, to cut the standard error of a sample mean in half, you need roughly how many times the data?
Answer: A. The standard error scales as σ/√n, so halving it requires quadrupling n (√4 = 2). Doubling the data (B) only shrinks the error by √2 ≈ 1.41, not 2; 16× (C) would quarter the error, not halve it; 8× (D) is not a clean square relationship.
4. A Poisson-distributed count has λ = 9. Its variance is…
Answer: C. The Poisson's defining quirk is mean = variance = λ, so Var = 9. √9 = 3 (A) confuses variance with standard deviation; λ² = 81 (B) is not how the Poisson works; there is no separate n parameter for a Poisson (D).
Conditional Probability & Covariance · Weeks 3–4
5. Bayes' theorem is most directly used to…
Answer: D. Bayes flips the conditioning, turning the easy-to-measure likelihood and a prior into the posterior. Independence (A) uses P(A∩B)=P(A)P(B); variance of a sum (B) is covariance bookkeeping; convergence of the mean (C) is the law of large numbers.
6. A fraud-detection flag fires for 95% of true fraud cases but also for 10% of legitimate ones, and only 1% of all cases are actually fraud. A flagged case's probability of being fraud is closest to…
Answer: B. Bayes: (0.95·0.01) / (0.95·0.01 + 0.10·0.99) = 0.0095/0.1085 ≈ 0.088, about 9%. 95% (A) is the sensitivity P(flag|fraud), not the posterior — the base-rate fallacy; 1% (D) ignores the evidence entirely; 50% (C) is just a guess.
7. The variance of the sum of two random variables X and Y is given by…
Answer: A. Var(X+Y) = Var(X) + Var(Y) + 2Cov(X,Y); the covariance term vanishes only when X and Y are uncorrelated. The minus sign (B) belongs to Var(X−Y); multiplying variances (C) is meaningless; assuming no covariance term "always" (D) is the diversification trap of Week 4.
8. Let X be symmetric about 0 and let Y = X². What is true of X and Y?
Answer: C. Cov(X,X²) = E[X³] − E[X]E[X²] = 0 − 0 = 0, so ρ = 0 — yet Y is fully determined by X, the opposite of independent. (A) inverts the logic (ρ=0 does not imply independence); (B) is false since the link is nonlinear; (D) is wrong, the covariance is well-defined and equals zero.
Random Walks & Martingales · Weeks 5–6
9. For a simple symmetric random walk, after n = 256 steps, the standard deviation of the position is…
Answer: D. Var(Sn) = n = 256, so SD = √256 = 16. The full step count 256 (A) and half of it 128 (B) ignore the square root; 32 (C) is 2×16, not the correct value.
10. You start with $40 and bet $1 per fair round against a house, with total stake N = $200. Your probability of ruin (going broke before reaching $200) is…
Answer: A. For a fair walk, P(reach N before 0) = i/N = 40/200 = 0.20, so P(ruin) = 1 − 0.20 = 0.80. 0.20 (B) is the probability of winning, not losing; 0.50 (C) ignores the unequal stakes; 1.00 (D) would require an infinite house bankroll with you starting at zero.
11. A sequence Xn satisfies E[Xn+1 | Fn] = Xn. This defines…
Answer: B. Equality in the conditional-expectation property is exactly the definition of a martingale — a fair game whose expected next value is the current one. A submartingale (A) has ≥; a supermartingale (C) has ≤; the law of large numbers (D) is a separate statement about sample averages converging.
12. Why can't a betting strategy that chooses stakes using only past information turn a fair game into a profitable one?
Answer: C. The martingale transform of a fair game is again a martingale, so E[Wn] = W0 for any predictable stakes — the optional-stopping intuition. (A) is a rule, not math; (B) misstates the LLN; (D) is irrelevant to the expectation.
13. In arbitrage-free pricing, the discounted price of a traded asset is a martingale under…
Answer: D. Discounted prices are martingales under the risk-neutral measure Q, which exists precisely when there is no arbitrage. Under the real measure P (A), prices drift up by a risk premium, so they are submartingales, not martingales; (B) and (C) are simply false.
Brownian Motion · Weeks 7–8
14. To build a sensible continuous-time limit from an n-step random walk on [0, 1], you must rescale…
Answer: A. This is the diffusive scaling Wn(t) = S⌊nt⌋/√n, which keeps the variance at time t finite and equal to t. Reversing the exponents (B) makes the path explode; equal scaling (C) collapses it; leaving time alone (D) makes the variance grow without bound.
15. Donsker's theorem (the functional CLT) strengthens the ordinary CLT by guaranteeing convergence of…
Answer: C. "Functional" means the whole path t ↦ Wn(t) converges to Brownian motion, so functionals like the maximum or area converge too. The endpoint alone (A) is just the ordinary CLT; (B) and (D) describe nothing Donsker claims.
16. For standard Brownian motion Wt, the variance of W16 is…
Answer: B. Axiom (2) gives Wt ~ Normal(0, t), so Var(W16) = t = 16. 4 (A) is the standard deviation √16, not the variance; 256 (C) is t² (a common slip); 0 (D) is the mean, not the variance.
17. For standard Brownian motion, Cov(W5, W12) equals…
Answer: D. Cov(Ws, Wt) = min(s, t) = min(5, 12) = 5 — the two times share exactly the variance accumulated up to the earlier one. 12 (A) is max(s,t); 17 (B) is the sum s+t; 60 (C) is the product s·t.
18. The quadratic variation of standard Brownian motion over [0, t] equals…
Answer: A. Summing squared increments gives Σ(ΔW)² → t, the slogan (dW)² = dt. Smooth functions have zero quadratic variation (B), but Brownian motion does not — that is exactly what makes it special; t² (C) and √t (D) are not the limit.
Itô Calculus, SDEs & Monte Carlo · Weeks 9–10
19. Why does dW/dt fail to exist for a Brownian path?
Answer: C. A typical increment has size √Δt, so the difference quotient ΔW/Δt ~ 1/√Δt blows up as Δt → 0. Brownian paths are in fact continuous (A is false); increments are independent, not correlated (B); a zero mean (D) does not flatten any individual path.
20. In the Itô integral ∫ ft dWt, the integrand f is evaluated at…
Answer: B. Itô uses the non-anticipating left-endpoint convention, so a position is set before the price moves. The right endpoint or midpoint (A, C) is the Stratonovich integral and would let you trade on the future; the running maximum (D) is not used at all.
21. The central rule of stochastic calculus that keeps the second-order Taylor term from vanishing is…
Answer: D. (dW)² = dt: the square of the random increment is of the same order as the time term, so the ½f″(dW)² term does not vanish. (dt)² (C) is far smaller and is discarded; dW ≠ dt since dW is random with size √dt (B); (A) is dimensionally nonsense.
22. Applying Itô's lemma to ln S for geometric Brownian motion dS = μS dt + σS dW gives a drift of…
Answer: A. With f = ln S, the surviving Itô term ½σ²S²·(−1/S²) = −½σ² subtracts from μ, giving drift μ − ½σ². Naïve calculus would give just μ (C); adding the term (B) gets the sign backwards; (D) is the negative of the correct answer.
23. In the Euler–Maruyama scheme Xk+1 = Xk + μΔt + σ√Δt·Z, the noise term is scaled by √Δt rather than Δt because…
Answer: B. The increment over Δt is Normal(0, Δt), so its typical size — the standard deviation — is √Δt. Scaling the noise as Δt instead is the single most common Euler–Maruyama bug, collapsing simulated volatility. (A), (C), and (D) are not the reason.
24. You estimate an option price using N = 5,000 Monte-Carlo paths and get a standard error of 0.40. Roughly how many total paths do you need to cut the standard error to 0.10?
Answer: C. Standard error scales as 1/√N. Cutting it by a factor of 4 (0.40 → 0.10) needs 4² = 16× the paths: 16 × 5,000 = 80,000. 20,000 (A) only halves the error; 50,000 (B) and 160,000 (D) do not match the required 16× factor.
25. Why is Monte Carlo the preferred method for pricing an option on a basket of 50 correlated assets?
Answer: D. The 1/√N rate is dimension-independent, so Monte Carlo handles 50-asset baskets that make grid (lattice) methods explode combinatorially. It is never exact (A); it is built entirely on random draws (B); high dimensions are its strength, not a limitation (C).
