Overview

📋 Why this page exists

Bra-ket notation packs a lot of meaning into very few symbols, and it's easy to get lost in a wall of $\langle\,|\,\rangle$'s. This page is a standalone “decoder ring” for the notation used so far — it doesn't introduce new physics, it just slows down and re-explains the symbols themselves. For now this page focuses specifically on qubits and entanglement (Topic 01) — kets, bras, overlaps, superposition, and the Kronecker product — with extra practice problems that don't overlap the ones already in HW 01 and HW 02+03.

Use this page alongside, not instead of, the Topic 01 page — that page has the actual physics derivations; this page is purely about getting fluent at reading the symbols those derivations are written in.

Notation Decoder Ring

Every symbol that's shown up so far, in one table. When an equation looks scary, find the symbol here first — the physics is usually simpler than the notation makes it look.

Last column, in plain language: most of this notation is just ordinary vector/matrix math from linear algebra wearing a costume. If a symbol still feels new after reading that column, that's the one worth asking about — the rest is mostly relabeling.

👉 Swipe the table left to reach the highlighted “what it's for” column — that's the one that actually explains the symbol.

Symbol Name Read it as… What it is What it's for (engineer's-eye view)
$|\psi\rangle$ ket “the state $\psi$” a column vector Just a column vector — a list of numbers, same as any vector from linear algebra. The “$|\ \rangle$” is just decoration that says “this list of numbers is a quantum state,” the way you might label a vector $\mathbf v$ vs. $\mathbf F$ to say one's a velocity and one's a force.
$\langle\psi|$ bra “$\psi$, starred and laid on its side” a row vector, $\equiv|\psi\rangle^\dagger$ Same list of numbers as the ket, just written as a row instead of a column, with every number's sign flipped on the imaginary part. You make this version whenever you're about to do a dot product.
$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ inner product / overlap “how much $\phi$ is in $\psi$” a plain (complex) number It's a dot product. That's really it — the same dot product from your first linear algebra class, with one small extra step (conjugating one side) to handle complex numbers: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\sum_k\phi_k^*\psi_k$.
$|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$ outer product (“bowtie”) “a ket glued to a bra” a matrix / operator The opposite of a dot product: instead of squashing two vectors down into one number, this multiplies them out into a whole matrix (column vector times row vector). Same “column times row” multiplication rule from linear algebra, just usually skipped over in a first course.
$\dagger$ dagger (Hermitian conjugate) “star every entry, then flip rows$\leftrightarrow$columns” $A^\dagger\equiv A^{T*}\equiv A^{*T}$ Two familiar steps done back to back: transpose the matrix (rows become columns), and conjugate every entry (flip the sign on every imaginary part). If all the numbers happen to be real, dagger is just the plain transpose you already know.
$*$ complex conjugate “flip the sign in front of every $i$” $z=x+iy \Rightarrow z^*=x-iy$ Just flips the sign on the imaginary part of a number: $3+2i$ becomes $3-2i$. It shows up constantly because multiplying a number by its own conjugate ($z^*z$) always gives a real, non-negative result — which is exactly what you need to turn a number into a probability.
$T$ transpose “flip rows$\leftrightarrow$columns, no conjugating” column $\to$ row (or vice versa) The exact same transpose you already know — nothing new here.
$\delta_{j,k}$ Kronecker delta “1 if the indices match, 0 if they don't” a number, either 0 or 1 A simple on/off switch. It's mostly used inside a sum, where it deletes every term except the one where the two indices happen to match — a shortcut for “only keep this one term.”
$\sum_{k=1}^n$ summation “add up the thing to the right, once for every $k$ from 1 to $n$” notation for adding a list of terms Plain old sigma notation from math class — add up a list of numbers. Nothing quantum-specific about it. (In this course, “operator” means a matrix that acts on a state, so we don't call $\sum$ an operator.)
$I\equiv\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|$ completeness relation / identity “a fancy way to write the identity that you're always allowed to insert” the identity operator (matrix), $I|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle$ The identity matrix: it acts like multiplying by $1$, so $I|\psi\rangle=|\psi\rangle$ and $AI=A$. Because it doesn't change anything, you can drop this expansion into the middle of an equation for free, then use algebra tricks on it — that's the entire point of it.
$A_{j,k}\equiv\langle j|A|k\rangle$ matrix element “sandwich $A$ between bra $j$ and ket $k$ to pull out row-$j$, column-$k$ of its matrix” a number How you pull one single number out of a matrix — row $j$, column $k$. Same as looking up cell A[j][k] in a spreadsheet or array.
$\otimes$ Kronecker (tensor) product “glue two separate systems into one joint system” a bigger vector/matrix: $\binom{\alpha_1}{\alpha_2}\otimes\binom{\beta_1}{\beta_2}=\begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1\beta_1\\\alpha_1\beta_2\\\alpha_2\beta_1\\\alpha_2\beta_2\end{pmatrix}$ How you combine two separate systems into one bigger one. The sizes multiply instead of adding: a 2-level system plus a 3-level system needs $2\times3=6$ numbers together, not $2+3=5$. That “multiply, not add” behavior is the whole reason entanglement can happen at all.
$|jk\rangle\equiv|j\rangle\otimes|k\rangle$ two-mode (product) ket “mode $A$ is $j$, mode $B$ is $k$, written as one symbol” a bigger column vector (size multiplies) Shorthand for a Kronecker product of two basis states. The left label is always mode $A$, the right label is always mode $B$. So $|12\rangle$ and $|21\rangle$ are different states.
DOC Descending-Order Convention “the $1$ in the column vector moves down as the basis-state index goes up” a rule for turning kets into column vectors $|1\rangle\equiv\binom{1}{0}$, $|2\rangle\equiv\binom{0}{1}$ — the $1$ descends one row as the label increases. For two qubits the same rule plus Kronecker product produces the ordered basis $\{|11\rangle,|12\rangle,|21\rangle,|22\rangle\}$ (register counting: the rightmost index runs first). Everyone in the course uses this same stacking order; swapping it would scramble multi-qubit vectors.

Type-Checking an Expression

The single most useful trick: count the open ends

Before trying to simplify anything, ask: is this a number, a vector, or a matrix? You can answer that just by counting unmatched bras/kets, without doing any algebra:

  • A lone ket $|\psi\rangle$ (nothing to its left) $\Rightarrow$ column vector.
  • A lone bra $\langle\psi|$ (nothing to its right) $\Rightarrow$ row vector.
  • A bra directly touching a ket, $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ (both ends “closed”) $\Rightarrow$ number.
  • A ket directly touching a bra, $|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$ (both ends “open” — nothing between them) $\Rightarrow$ matrix/operator.
  • An operator sandwiched by a bra on the left and ket on the right, $\langle\phi|A|\psi\rangle$ (both ends closed) $\Rightarrow$ number.
  • An operator with only a ket on the right, $A|\psi\rangle$ (one end still open on the left) $\Rightarrow$ column vector.
If you can't tell whether something is a number or a matrix, you will not be able to tell whether you're allowed to move it around freely (numbers commute with everything; matrices generally don't) — so type-checking first prevents most algebra mistakes before they start.

Worked walkthrough: extracting a coefficient, $c_k=\langle b_k|\psi\rangle$

This is the exact same move used repeatedly in Topic 01 whenever a coefficient gets “pulled out” of a state. Start from a state written in a basis $\{|b_1\rangle,\dots,|b_n\rangle\}$:

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m=1}^n c_m|b_m\rangle$$

Step 1 — apply the bra $\langle b_k|$ to both sides. This is legal because $\langle b_k|$ is just a row vector — you can multiply both sides of any vector equation by the same thing.

$$\langle b_k|\psi\rangle = \langle b_k|\sum_{m=1}^n c_m|b_m\rangle$$

Step 2 — the sum doesn't care about $\langle b_k|$ (it's a sum over $m$, not $k$), so move $\langle b_k|$ inside the sum, and pull each scalar $c_m$ out front of its overlap (scalars can always be pulled out of a bra-ket sandwich).

$$\langle b_k|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m=1}^n c_m\langle b_k|b_m\rangle$$

Step 3 — use orthonormality, $\langle b_k|b_m\rangle=\delta_{k,m}$. Every term in the sum is multiplied by 0 except the one where $m=k$, which is multiplied by 1.

$$\langle b_k|\psi\rangle = \sum_{m=1}^n c_m\delta_{k,m} = c_k$$

Step 4 — so the whole sum collapses to a single surviving term, giving the boxed result. Three small, individually obvious steps — multiply both sides by a bra, distribute, use orthonormality to kill every term but one.

$$\boxed{c_k = \langle b_k|\psi\rangle}$$

Worked walkthrough: reading the entanglement factoring test

From HW 02+03 Problem 7c — this is the notation students most often gloss over instead of actually parsing. The question: can $|\Phi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle+|2\rangle\otimes|1\rangle\big)$ be written as a single Kronecker product $|\chi\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle$ of two one-mode states?

Step 1 — recall what a ket position means in $|\Phi\rangle$: the first factor in each term is mode $A$'s state, the second factor is mode $B$'s state. So $|\Phi\rangle$ says “mode $A$ is $|1\rangle$ while mode $B$ is $|2\rangle$” and “mode $A$ is $|2\rangle$ while mode $B$ is $|1\rangle$” — both at once.

Step 2 — try to factor anyway. $|\chi\rangle$ is allowed to be a superposition, say $\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|2\rangle$. Expanding $|\chi\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle=(\alpha|1\rangle+\beta|2\rangle)\otimes(\gamma|1\rangle+\delta|2\rangle)$ always produces all four coincidence terms $|11\rangle,|12\rangle,|21\rangle,|22\rangle$ (unless some of $\alpha,\beta,\gamma,\delta$ are zero), with coefficients satisfying $ad=bc$. $|\Phi\rangle$ has $|12\rangle$ and $|21\rangle$ but neither $|11\rangle$ nor $|22\rangle$, so the coefficients cannot come from any single pair $|\chi\rangle,|\xi\rangle$.

$$|\Phi\rangle \ne |\chi\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle \ \ \text{for any single } |\chi\rangle,|\xi\rangle$$

Step 3 — contrast this with a state that does factor, like $|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle$ alone: there, mode $A$'s ket is $|1\rangle$ in every term (there's only one term), so $|\chi\rangle=|1\rangle$, $|\xi\rangle=|2\rangle$ works fine. The same is true of a superposition that shares a factor, e.g. $\frac1{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)\otimes|2\rangle$.

The notation itself is the physics here: “can't factor into a single $\otimes$” is the definition of entanglement for a pure bipartite state — there's no separate formula to memorize on top of just reading the expression carefully.

This is also exactly what the concurrence formula $C(|\psi\rangle)=2|ad-bc|$ for $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$ is checking numerically — $C=0$ means it happens to factor, $C>0$ means it doesn't (see HW 02+03 Problem 8).

Common Notation Pitfalls

1. A bra is not just “the ket sideways”

Taking the bra also complex-conjugates every coefficient. For $|\psi\rangle=c_1|1\rangle+ic_2|2\rangle$, the bra is $\langle\psi|=c_1^*\langle1|-ic_2^*\langle2|$ (note the sign flip on the $i$ that was factored out) — not $c_1\langle1|+ic_2\langle2|$.

2. $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \ne \langle\psi|\phi\rangle$ in general

They're complex conjugates of each other: $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle=(\langle\phi|\psi\rangle)^*$. They're only equal when the overlap happens to be real. Always keep track of which bra came from which state.

3. $|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$ (outer product) and $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ (inner product) look almost identical but are completely different objects

One is a bra written before a ket ($\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$, a number); the other is a ket written before a bra ($|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$, a matrix). The only difference on the page is which one comes first — but the difference in meaning is enormous. Use the open-ends counting trick from the Type-Checking section above whenever you're unsure.

4. A Kronecker delta inside a sum just picks out one term

Seeing $\sum_k(\text{stuff depending on }k)\,\delta_{j,k}$ should immediately simplify in your head to just “(stuff depending on $k$), with every $k$ replaced by $j$” — every other term in the sum is multiplied by 0 and vanishes. This is exactly how $c_k=\langle b_k|\psi\rangle$ gets derived in the Type-Checking section above.

5. $A_{j,k}$'s index order: first index = bra (row), second = ket (column)

$A_{j,k}\equiv\langle j|A|k\rangle$ — $j$ comes from the bra on the left and picks the row; $k$ comes from the ket on the right and picks the column. Swapping the order of the subscripts swaps which matrix entry you're talking about (unless the matrix happens to be symmetric).

6. $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle \ne |\phi\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle$ — the Kronecker product is NOT commutative

Order matters. $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ means “mode $A$ in state $\psi$, mode $B$ in state $\phi$”; swapping the factors describes a physically different assignment (unless $|\psi\rangle=|\phi\rangle$). This is also why $|jk\rangle\equiv|j\rangle\otimes|k\rangle$ is not the same state as $|kj\rangle$.

7. In a composite ket, position tells you which subsystem — not just the label

In $|\Phi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle+|2\rangle\otimes|1\rangle)$, don't just read off “1 and 2 show up” — the first slot in every term is always mode $A$, the second is always mode $B$. Losing track of which slot is which is the single most common reason students misjudge whether a two-mode state is separable or entangled.

8. This course uses two labeling systems for a qubit

A single qubit is usually $|1\rangle,|2\rangle$ (DOC computational basis from HW 01 and HW 02+03). Two-qubit concurrence and the Bell states are written in $|0\rangle,|1\rangle$ labels: $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$. They are the same two-level system; $1\leftrightarrow0$, $2\leftrightarrow1$ is the translation. Mixing the two without translating is how $C=2|ad-bc|$ gets the wrong four numbers.

Qubits & Entanglement Extra Practice

📋 Extra practice — notation for qubits & entanglement

Based on Topic 01 (pure states, superposition, matrix representations, entanglement) and HW 01 / HW 02+03. Do those problems first if you haven't — the problems below are additional practice covering the same ideas from different angles, not a repeat.

Checklist

Close the laptop and try to write each of these from memory. If any of them don't come immediately, that's exactly what to re-drill:

  • General pure qubit: $|\psi\rangle=c_1|1\rangle+c_2|2\rangle$, $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2=1$
  • Extracting a coefficient from a state: $c_k=\langle b_k|\psi\rangle$
  • Orthonormality: $\langle j|k\rangle=\delta_{j,k}$
  • Transition probability: $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$
  • Bra of a ket: conjugate every coefficient, then write as a row: $\langle\psi|\equiv|\psi\rangle^\dagger$
  • Kronecker product of two kets, and why it's not commutative
  • Concurrence: $C(|\psi\rangle)=2|ad-bc|$ for $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$; $C=0\Rightarrow$ separable, $C=1\Rightarrow$ maximally entangled
  • The factoring test: can $|\Psi\rangle$ be written as $|\chi\rangle\otimes|\xi\rangle$?
This checklist is worth more than it looks like on the page. Closing the laptop and forcing yourself to write each line out cold — instead of just re-reading it — is called active recall, and it's one of the best-tested study techniques there is: the struggle to remember is what actually builds the memory. If a line won't come out of your head, that's not a sign to feel bad — it's the checklist doing its job by showing you exactly where to spend the next 10 minutes.
Extra Problem 1 — normalization with a missing coefficient

↩ Covered in Topic 01, Section 1 (Pure Quantum States & Superposition)

A qutrit is $|\psi\rangle=\frac12|1\rangle+\frac{i}{2}|2\rangle+c_3|3\rangle$, where $c_3$ is real and positive.
  1. Use normalization to solve for $c_3$.
  2. What is the probability of measuring the system in state $|2\rangle$?
Show solution

(a) $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2+|c_3|^2=1$: $\left|\frac12\right|^2+\left|\frac{i}2\right|^2+c_3^2=1 \Rightarrow \frac14+\frac14+c_3^2=1 \Rightarrow c_3^2=\frac12 \Rightarrow c_3=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ (taking the positive root, as given).

(b) $p_2=|c_2|^2=\left|\frac{i}2\right|^2=\frac14$ — note the factor of $i$ doesn't change the probability, since $|i|=1$.

Extra Problem 2 — overlap of two qutrit states, index by index

↩ Covered in Topic 01, Section 1c (Overlaps & Quantum States as Complex Vectors)

For $|\phi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}|1\rangle-\frac{i}{2}|2\rangle+\frac12|3\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt3}\big(|1\rangle+|2\rangle+i|3\rangle\big)$, compute $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ and the transition probability $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$.
Show solution

First form the bra: $\langle\phi|=\frac1{\sqrt2}\langle1|+\frac{i}{2}\langle2|+\frac12\langle3|$ (note $-\frac{i}2$ conjugates to $+\frac{i}2$). Then, term by term using orthonormality:

$$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3} + \frac{i}{2}\cdot\frac1{\sqrt3} + \frac12\cdot\frac{i}{\sqrt3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt6} + \frac{i}{2\sqrt3} + \frac{i}{2\sqrt3} = \frac{1}{\sqrt6} + \frac{i}{\sqrt3}$$

Transition probability: $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2=\left(\frac1{\sqrt6}\right)^2+\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)^2=\frac16+\frac13=\frac16+\frac26=\frac36=\frac12$.

Extra Problem 3 — Kronecker product and the separability check, new numbers

↩ Covered in Topic 01, Section 2b (How Multiple Quantum Objects Play Together) and Section 3a (Separability vs. Entanglement)

Let $|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$ and $|\phi\rangle=|2\rangle$ (single-qubit states).
  1. Write out $|\Xi\rangle\equiv|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ as a 4-component column vector.
  2. Confirm $|\Xi\rangle$ is separable by re-factoring it back into $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ from the vector alone (i.e. without just citing that you built it that way).
  3. Compute its concurrence directly from the vector components to double-check.
Show solution

(a) $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)\otimes|2\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle+|2\rangle\otimes|2\rangle)=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|12\rangle+|22\rangle)$, i.e. in the ordered basis $\{|11\rangle,|12\rangle,|21\rangle,|22\rangle\}$:

$$|\Xi\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}0\\ \frac1{\sqrt2}\\ 0\\ \frac1{\sqrt2}\end{pmatrix}$$

(b) Reading the vector as $a|11\rangle+b|12\rangle+c|21\rangle+d|22\rangle$ with $a=0,b=\frac1{\sqrt2},c=0,d=\frac1{\sqrt2}$: both surviving terms have mode $B$ in $|2\rangle$, so that common ket factors out: $\frac1{\sqrt2}|12\rangle+\frac1{\sqrt2}|22\rangle=\left(\frac1{\sqrt2}|1\rangle+\frac1{\sqrt2}|2\rangle\right)\otimes|2\rangle$, recovering $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ exactly.

(c) Concurrence formula uses $a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$ labeling (relabel $1\to0,2\to1$ to match): here $a=0,b=\frac1{\sqrt2},c=0,d=\frac1{\sqrt2}$, so $C=2|ad-bc|=2\left|0\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}-\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot0\right|=0$ — separable, confirming (b).

Extra Problem 4 — concurrence for new states

↩ Covered in Topic 01, Section 3a (Separability vs. Entanglement for Pure States)

Compute the concurrence of each:
  1. $|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}|00\rangle+\frac1{\sqrt2}|10\rangle$
  2. $|\psi\rangle=\frac1{2}|00\rangle+\frac{\sqrt3}{2}|11\rangle$
  3. $|\psi\rangle=\frac12|00\rangle+\frac12|01\rangle+\frac12|10\rangle-\frac12|11\rangle$
Show solution

Using $C=2|ad-bc|$ for $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$:

(a) $a=\frac1{\sqrt2},b=0,c=\frac1{\sqrt2},d=0$: $C=2\left|\frac1{\sqrt2}\cdot0-0\cdot\frac1{\sqrt2}\right|=0$ — separable (matches $|\psi\rangle=\frac1{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)\otimes|0\rangle$).

(b) $a=\frac12,b=0,c=0,d=\frac{\sqrt3}2$: $C=2\left|\frac12\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}2-0\right|=2\cdot\frac{\sqrt3}4=\frac{\sqrt3}2\approx0.866$ — partially entangled (not maximal, since $\frac{\sqrt3}2<1$).

(c) $a=b=c=\frac12,d=-\frac12$: $C=2\left|\frac12\left(-\frac12\right)-\frac12\cdot\frac12\right|=2\left|-\frac14-\frac14\right|=2\cdot\frac12=1$ — maximally entangled.

Extra Problem 5 — translate the symbols into a sentence

↩ Covered in Topic 01, Section 1c and Section 3a

Without doing any computation, write one plain-English sentence describing what each of the following is saying (not how to derive it):
  1. $|\psi\rangle=\sum_k c_k|b_k\rangle$
  2. $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\equiv0 \iff |\phi\rangle\perp|\psi\rangle$
  3. $C(|\psi\rangle)=2|ad-bc|$
Show solution

(a) “Any state can be written as a weighted sum of basis states, one term per basis state, weighted by that state's coefficient.”

(b) “Two states have zero overlap exactly when they're perpendicular (orthogonal) — measuring one gives zero probability of the other.”

(c) “A single number, built from a two-qubit state's four coefficients, that's zero exactly when the state factors into two separate one-qubit states, and grows toward 1 the more inseparably entangled the two qubits are.”

Being able to say the sentence before touching the algebra is a good sign you actually understand what a problem is asking, rather than pattern-matching to a memorized derivation.
