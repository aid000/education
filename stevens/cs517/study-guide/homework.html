Homework #01

Covers Topic 1 (Pure Quantum States & Superposition). See the Topic 01 study guide page for the underlying concepts.

Problem 1

(a) Write a general pure state of a qubit as a ket $|\psi\rangle$ expanded in the computational basis $\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$ with coefficients $c_1$ and $c_2$.

(b) Take the Hermitian conjugate of $|\psi\rangle$ from (a) to get its bra in terms of the same coefficients.

Show solution

(a) $|\psi\rangle = c_1|1\rangle+c_2|2\rangle$, where $c_1\equiv\langle1|\psi\rangle$, $c_2\equiv\langle2|\psi\rangle$, and $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2=1$.

(b) Using $|\psi\rangle^\dagger\equiv(|\psi\rangle)^{T*}\equiv\langle\psi|$, and the general rules $|\psi\rangle^\dagger=\langle\psi|$, $c^\dagger=c^*$:

$$\langle\psi| = c_1^*\langle1|+c_2^*\langle2|$$
Problem 2
For a 3-level system (a qutrit) in a pure state, apply the orthonormality condition between the 3 computational basis states $\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle,|3\rangle\}$. Using $\langle j|k\rangle=\delta_{j,k}$, compute all 9 possible overlaps to get each result as a number.
Show solution

The computational basis is orthonormal by definition, so it obeys $\langle j|k\rangle=\delta_{j,k}$. All 9 overlaps:

$$\langle1|1\rangle=1,\ \langle1|2\rangle=0,\ \langle1|3\rangle=0,\quad \langle2|1\rangle=0,\ \langle2|2\rangle=1,\ \langle2|3\rangle=0,\quad \langle3|1\rangle=0,\ \langle3|2\rangle=0,\ \langle3|3\rangle=1$$
Problem 3

(a) In a 3-level system, write out a general pure state as a ket with general coefficients $c_k$ in the computational basis $\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle,|3\rangle\}$.

(b) Use computational basis bras to extract each coefficient from the state separately, writing each coefficient as an overlap between a bra of the computational basis and the ket of the full state.

(c) Write out the 3-level ket's normalization condition in terms of the coefficients (leave them as letter variables — don't substitute in the results of (b)).

Show solution

(a) $|\psi\rangle=c_1|1\rangle+c_2|2\rangle+c_3|3\rangle$.

(b) Applying bra $\langle k|$ to $|\psi\rangle$ and using orthonormality:

$$\langle1|\psi\rangle=c_1,\qquad \langle2|\psi\rangle=c_2,\qquad \langle3|\psi\rangle=c_3$$

(c) Axiomatically: $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2+|c_3|^2=1$. Rigorously, starting from $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$, distributing, and using orthonormality to zero every cross term, gives the same result — using $c^*c\equiv|c|^2$.

Problem 4

(a) Show that the following two pure qubit states are orthogonal by computing their overlap (inner product): $|\psi_1\rangle\equiv a|1\rangle+b|2\rangle$, $|\psi_2\rangle\equiv -b^*|1\rangle+a^*|2\rangle$.

(b) Find the overlap of $|\psi\rangle\equiv a_1|1\rangle+a_2|2\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle\equiv b_1|1\rangle+b_2|2\rangle$ in terms of their coefficients.

Show solution

(a) $\langle\psi_2|\psi_1\rangle = (-b\langle1|+a\langle2|)(a|1\rangle+b|2\rangle) = -ba\langle1|1\rangle - b^2\langle1|2\rangle + a^2\langle2|1\rangle + ab\langle2|2\rangle = -ba+ab = 0$ — so $|\psi_1\rangle$ and $|\psi_2\rangle$ are orthogonal.

(b) $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle = (a_1^*\langle1|+a_2^*\langle2|)(b_1|1\rangle+b_2|2\rangle) = a_1^*b_1+a_2^*b_2$. Note there are, in general, two overlaps for any pair of states, and they're complex conjugates of each other: $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=(\langle\psi|\phi\rangle)^*=a_1b_1^*+a_2b_2^*$.

Problem 5
In the 5-level state $|\psi\rangle\equiv c_1|1\rangle+c_2|2\rangle+c_3|3\rangle+c_4|4\rangle+c_5|5\rangle$, what is the probability that it will collapse to $|3\rangle$ if measured?
Show solution
$$p_3 \equiv |c_3|^2$$
Problem 6
Complex numbers are defined as $z\equiv x+iy$ ($x,y$ real, $i\equiv\sqrt{-1}$) and $z^*\equiv x-iy$ (complex conjugate). The real part is $\operatorname{Re}(z)\equiv x$ and the imaginary part is $\operatorname{Im}(z)\equiv y$ (also real-valued!). Solve for $x$ and $y$ to get formulas for $\operatorname{Re}(z)$ and $\operatorname{Im}(z)$ in terms of only $z$ and $z^*$.
Show solution

Add $z=x+iy$ and $z^*=x-iy$: $z+z^*=2x \Rightarrow x=\dfrac{z+z^*}{2}$.

Subtract: $z-z^*=2iy \Rightarrow y=\dfrac{z-z^*}{2i}$.

$$\boxed{\operatorname{Re}(z)=\frac{z+z^*}{2}, \qquad \operatorname{Im}(z)=\frac{z-z^*}{2i}}$$
Problem 7

(a) Real and imaginary parts of $z$ act like perpendicular vector components, $z=x+iy$, with modulus $|z|\equiv\sqrt{z^*z}$. Plug $z=x+iy$ into this and use $i^2=-1$ to get $|z|$ in terms of $x$ and $y$.

(b) By $\cos(\phi)\equiv x/|z|$, $\sin(\phi)\equiv y/|z|$, solve for $x$ and $y$ each in terms of $|z|$ and $\phi$.

(c) Plug your expressions for $x,y$ from (b) into $z\equiv x+iy$, and use Euler's formula $e^{i\phi}=\cos(\phi)+i\sin(\phi)$ to get $z$ in terms of only $|z|$ and $\phi$ — the “polar form” of $z$.

Show solution

(a) $|z|=\sqrt{z^*z}=\sqrt{(x-iy)(x+iy)}=\sqrt{x^2+ixy-iyx-i^2y^2}=\sqrt{x^2-(-1)y^2}=\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$.

(b) Multiply both sides of each equation by $|z|$: $x=|z|\cos(\phi)$, $y=|z|\sin(\phi)$.

(c) $z=x+iy=|z|\cos(\phi)+i|z|\sin(\phi)=|z|\big(\cos(\phi)+i\sin(\phi)\big)$. Using Euler's formula, $\cos(\phi)+i\sin(\phi)=e^{i\phi}$, so:

$$\boxed{z=|z|e^{i\phi}}, \qquad |z|=\sqrt{x^2+y^2},\quad \phi=\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\equiv\arg(z)$$
Problem 8

For $|\psi\rangle\equiv\cos(\theta)|1\rangle+\sin(\theta)e^{i\phi}|2\rangle$, $\theta\in[0,\frac{\pi}{2}]$, $\phi\in[0,2\pi)$:

(a) What is the transition probability from $|\psi\rangle$ to the balanced-superposition state $|{+}\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle)$?

(b) What is the quantum interference term in the probability from (a), in terms of $\theta,\phi$?

(c) What are the maximum and minimum values of the interference term?

(d) What states $|\psi_{\max}\rangle,|\psi_{\min}\rangle$ correspond to the extremes from (c)?

(e) What conditions make the interference term 0? What states $|\psi\rangle$ correspond?

Show solution

Abbreviate $c_\theta\equiv\cos(\theta)$, $s_\theta\equiv\sin(\theta)$, so $|\psi\rangle=a_1|1\rangle+a_2|2\rangle$ with $a_1=c_\theta$, $a_2=s_\theta e^{i\phi}$.

(a) $\langle{+}|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(a_1+a_2)$, so $P_+\equiv|\langle{+}|\psi\rangle|^2=\frac12(a_1^*+a_2^*)(a_1+a_2)=\frac{|a_1|^2}{2}+\frac{|a_2|^2}{2}+\operatorname{Re}(a_1^*a_2)$. Substituting $a_1,a_2$ back in ($c_\theta s_\theta=\frac12\sin(2\theta)$):

$$P_+ = \tfrac12+\tfrac12\sin(2\theta)\cos(\phi)$$

(b) The interference term is $Q_I \equiv \tfrac12\sin(2\theta)\cos(\phi)$.

(c) Since $\sin(\cdot),\cos(\cdot)\in[-1,1]$: $\max(Q_I)=\tfrac12$, $\min(Q_I)=-\tfrac12$.

(d) Since $\theta\in[0,\frac\pi2]$, $\sin(2\theta)$ is nonnegative and maxes at $2\theta=\frac\pi2\Rightarrow\theta=\frac\pi4$; $\cos(\phi)$ then controls the sign, maxing at $\phi=0$ and mining at $\phi=\pi$:

$$|\psi_{\max}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle+|2\rangle) \ (\theta=\tfrac\pi4,\phi=0), \qquad |\psi_{\min}\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle-|2\rangle) \ (\theta=\tfrac\pi4,\phi=\pi)$$

(e) $Q_I=0$ requires $\sin(2\theta)=0$ (i.e. $\theta\in\{0,\frac\pi2\}$) or $\cos(\phi)=0$ (i.e. $\phi\in\{\frac\pi2,\frac{3\pi}2\}$). The corresponding states are the computational basis states (up to global phase) and states with relative phase $\pm i$:

$$|\psi_0\rangle \in \Big\{\,|1\rangle,\ e^{i\phi}|2\rangle,\ c_\theta|1\rangle+is_\theta|2\rangle,\ c_\theta|1\rangle-is_\theta|2\rangle\,\Big\}$$

So the transition probability from $|\psi\rangle$ to $|{+}\rangle$ is purely “classical Bayesian” (no interference term) exactly when $|\psi\rangle$ is a basis state or has relative phase $\pm i$.

Homework #02+03

Covers Topics 2–3 (Matrix Representations & Entanglement). See the Topic 01 study guide page.

Problem 1

(a) Given the general pure state of a qubit $|\psi\rangle=a|1\rangle+b|2\rangle$, write it as a column vector using the Descending-Order Convention (DOC) for the standard basis $\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle\}$.

(b) Use the Hermitian conjugate to write the bra of $|\psi\rangle$ from (a) as a row vector.

(c) Write out column vectors for both computational basis states separately, and find their bras as row vectors.

Show solution

(a) $|1\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $|2\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, so $|\psi\rangle=a\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}+b\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}$.

(b) $\langle\psi|\equiv|\psi\rangle^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}a\\b\end{pmatrix}^{T*}=(a\ \ b)^*=(a^*\ \ b^*)$.

(c) $|1\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $|2\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$; bras $\langle1|=(1\ \ 0)$, $\langle2|=(0\ \ 1)$ — the computational basis (and any state with purely real coefficients) is unaffected by complex conjugation. Note also $A^\dagger\equiv A^{T*}\equiv A^{*T}$ (order of $*$ and $T$ doesn't matter).

Problem 2
For pure states $|\psi\rangle\equiv a_1|1\rangle+a_2|2\rangle$ and $|\phi\rangle\equiv b_1|1\rangle+b_2|2\rangle$, the overlap $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=b_1^*a_1+b_2^*a_2$ can be written as a row-times-column matrix product $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle\equiv(b_1^*\ \ b_2^*)\binom{a_1}{a_2}$, giving the general rule $(v_1^*\ \ v_2^*)\binom{w_1}{w_2}\equiv v_1^*w_1+v_2^*w_2$. Using this, find the overlap $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ of $|\psi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt5}(|1\rangle+2|2\rangle)$ and $|\phi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3|1\rangle-i|2\rangle)$.
Show solution

$|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\binom{1}{2}$, $|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}\binom{3}{-i}$, so $\langle\phi|=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3\ \ i)$ (starring $-i$ gives $i$).

$$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3\ \ i)\cdot\frac{1}{\sqrt5}\binom12 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{50}}(3\cdot1+i\cdot2) = \frac{1}{5\sqrt2}(3+2i)$$
Problem 3

(a) Find the total vector from the linear combination $|\Xi\rangle\equiv a|\psi\rangle+b|\phi\rangle$, where $|\psi\rangle\equiv\binom{c_1}{c_2}$, $|\phi\rangle\equiv\binom{d_1}{d_2}$, each normalized.

(b) Since $|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle$ aren't generally orthogonal, normalize $|\Xi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt{\langle\tilde\Xi|\tilde\Xi\rangle}}|\tilde\Xi\rangle$ — compute $|\Xi\rangle$ as a vector in terms of $a,b,c_1,c_2,d_1,d_2$.

(c) Expand the normalization factor, collect the cross terms $a^*b$ and $ab^*$ (which are $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle$ and $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$), and find the condition on $a,b$ if $|\psi\rangle\perp|\phi\rangle$ and we want this normalization factor to already equal 1.

Show solution

(a) $|\tilde\Xi\rangle=a\binom{c_1}{c_2}+b\binom{d_1}{d_2}=\binom{ac_1+bd_1}{ac_2+bd_2}\equiv\binom{e_1}{e_2}$.

(b) $\langle\tilde\Xi|\tilde\Xi\rangle=|e_1|^2+|e_2|^2=|ac_1+bd_1|^2+|ac_2+bd_2|^2$, so

$$|\Xi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt{|ac_1+bd_1|^2+|ac_2+bd_2|^2}}\binom{ac_1+bd_1}{ac_2+bd_2}$$

(c) Expanding $\langle\tilde\Xi|\tilde\Xi\rangle$ fully and grouping terms:

$$\langle\tilde\Xi|\tilde\Xi\rangle = |a|^2\big(|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2\big)+|b|^2\big(|d_1|^2+|d_2|^2\big) + a^*b\big(c_1^*d_1+c_2^*d_2\big)+ab^*\big(c_1d_1^*+c_2d_2^*\big)$$

Since $|\psi\rangle,|\phi\rangle$ are each normalized, the first two groups are $|a|^2+|b|^2$; the cross terms are $a^*b\langle\psi|\phi\rangle+ab^*\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=|a|^2+|b|^2+2\operatorname{Re}[a^*b\langle\psi|\phi\rangle]$. If $|\psi\rangle\perp|\phi\rangle$, then $\langle\psi|\phi\rangle=0$, so $\sqrt{\langle\tilde\Xi|\tilde\Xi\rangle}=\sqrt{|a|^2+|b|^2}$. For this to already equal 1 (no normalization needed), $a,b$ must themselves satisfy $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$.

Problem 4
Write out the standard computational basis for a single quantum system with 4 levels, using Descending-Order Convention vectors.
Show solution
$$|1\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |2\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |3\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |4\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$$
Problem 5
Write out the Kronecker product $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ of $|\psi\rangle\equiv\binom{\alpha_1}{\alpha_2}$, $|\phi\rangle\equiv\binom{\beta_1}{\beta_2}$ as a single column vector in terms of $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\beta_1,\beta_2$.
Show solution
$$|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle \equiv \binom{\alpha_1}{\alpha_2}\otimes\binom{\beta_1}{\beta_2} \equiv \binom{\alpha_1\binom{\beta_1}{\beta_2}}{\alpha_2\binom{\beta_1}{\beta_2}} = \begin{pmatrix}\alpha_1\beta_1\\\alpha_1\beta_2\\\alpha_2\beta_1\\\alpha_2\beta_2\end{pmatrix}$$

Note: this is not generally the same as $|\phi\rangle\otimes|\psi\rangle$.

Problem 6

(a) Diagram a two-particle system where one mode is a qubit (2 levels) and the other a qutrit (3 levels), with each mode's levels shown as horizontal lines and computational basis kets labeled beneath.

(b) Write out the ordered bipartite basis using register counting (e.g. $|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle\equiv|a\rangle|b\rangle\equiv|a,b\rangle\equiv|ab\rangle$).

(c) Write out the vector form of each subsystem's computational basis separately, in DOC.

(d) Write out the full bipartite basis in vector form using the Kronecker product, in register counting order, and show each state can be labeled by a two-number (vector) index or a single-number (scalar) index counting 1 to 6.

Show solution

(a) A “$2\times3$” system: mode $A$ (qubit) with 2 levels $\{|1^{(A)}\rangle,|2^{(A)}\rangle\}$ next to mode $B$ (qutrit) with 3 levels $\{|1^{(B)}\rangle,|2^{(B)}\rangle,|3^{(B)}\rangle\}$.

(b) Letting the first mode be $A$ and second be $B$ (so ket position marks which is which):

$$\{|1\rangle|1\rangle,\ |1\rangle|2\rangle,\ |1\rangle|3\rangle,\ |2\rangle|1\rangle,\ |2\rangle|2\rangle,\ |2\rangle|3\rangle\} \equiv \{|11\rangle,|12\rangle,|13\rangle,|21\rangle,|22\rangle,|23\rangle\}$$

(c) Qubit: $|1\rangle\equiv\binom10$, $|2\rangle\equiv\binom01$. Qutrit: $|1\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $|2\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $|3\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$.

(d) Taking Kronecker products:

$$|1\rangle\equiv|1\rangle|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\!,\ |2\rangle\equiv|1\rangle|2\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\!,\ |3\rangle\equiv|1\rangle|3\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\!,\ |4\rangle\equiv|2\rangle|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}\!,\ |5\rangle\equiv|2\rangle|2\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}\!,\ |6\rangle\equiv|2\rangle|3\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

Each state has a two-number vector index (its position within each mode, e.g. $(2,1)$) and a single-number scalar index (its overall position, 1 to 6) — the scalar index follows DOC exactly because register counting with DOC in each mode produces it automatically.

Problem 7

(a) Expand the separable pure state $|\Xi\rangle\equiv|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$, $|\psi\rangle\equiv a|1\rangle+b|2\rangle$, $|\phi\rangle\equiv c|1\rangle+d|2\rangle$, in both ket form and as a vector.

(b) Starting from the final result of (a), show it can be factored back to a Kronecker product of pure states.

(c) Try to factor $|\Phi\rangle\equiv\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle+|2\rangle\otimes|1\rangle)$ into a Kronecker product of two pure states. Explain what prevents this.

Show solution

(a) $|\Xi\rangle=(a|1\rangle+b|2\rangle)\otimes(c|1\rangle+d|2\rangle)=ac|1\rangle\otimes|1\rangle+ad|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle+bc|2\rangle\otimes|1\rangle+bd|2\rangle\otimes|2\rangle = \begin{pmatrix}ac\\ad\\bc\\bd\end{pmatrix}$.

(b) From the ket form: $|\Xi\rangle=a|1\rangle\otimes(c|1\rangle+d|2\rangle)+b|2\rangle\otimes(c|1\rangle+d|2\rangle) = (a|1\rangle+b|2\rangle)\otimes(c|1\rangle+d|2\rangle) = |\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$.

(c) In $|\Phi\rangle$, mode 1 shows a different basis state ($|1\rangle$ or $|2\rangle$) in each term, so no single state can be pulled out of mode 1 — same for mode 2. Therefore $|\Phi\rangle$ cannot be written as a single Kronecker product of pure states in each mode. Note: $|1\rangle\otimes|2\rangle$ is separable on its own, as is $|2\rangle\otimes|1\rangle$ — it's the superposition of the two that prevents factorization, which is exactly what makes $|\Phi\rangle$ entangled (it's the Bell state $|\Psi^+\rangle$).

Problem 8
For each of the following pure states, compute its concurrence to get its entanglement:
  1. $|\psi\rangle = c_1|00\rangle+c_2|01\rangle+c_3|10\rangle+c_4|11\rangle$
  2. $|\psi\rangle = as|00\rangle+ha|01\rangle+sg|10\rangle+gh|11\rangle$
  3. $|\psi\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle-|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)$
  4. $|\psi\rangle = \frac12(|00\rangle+|01\rangle+|10\rangle+|11\rangle)$
  5. $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+e^{i\phi}|11\rangle)$
  6. $|\psi\rangle = \cos(\theta)|01\rangle-\sin(\theta)|10\rangle$, $\theta\in[0,\frac\pi2]$
Show solution

Concurrence for $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$ is $C(|\psi\rangle)\equiv2|ad-bc|$.

(a) $C=2|c_1c_4-c_2c_3|$ — generally entangled, but could be separable for particular coefficient values.

(b) $C=2|as\cdot gh-ha\cdot sg|=2|aghs-aghs|=0$ — separable.

(c) $C=2\left|\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12-\left(-\tfrac12\right)\tfrac12\right|=2\cdot\tfrac14|1+1|=\tfrac12\cdot2=1$ — maximally entangled.

(d) $C=2\left|\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12-\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\right|=0$ — separable.

(e) $C=2\left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{e^{i\phi}}{\sqrt2}-0\cdot0\right|=2\cdot\tfrac12|e^{i\phi}|=1$ — maximally entangled (for any $\phi$).

(f) $C=2|0\cdot0-\cos(\theta)\cdot(-\sin(\theta))|=2|\cos(\theta)\sin(\theta)|=|\sin(2\theta)|=\sin(2\theta)$ for $\theta\in[0,\frac\pi2]$ — entangled unless $\theta=0$ or $\frac\pi2$ (where it reduces to a single basis state).

Homework #04+05

Covers Topics 4–5 (Classical Statistics & Operators). See the Topic 02 study guide page. Quiz 04+05 is based on this assignment — Monday, Sep 28, 2026.

Problem 1

A room's population of 10 people has ages $x_k$: $x_1=5,x_2=24,x_3=18,x_4=7,x_5=35,x_6=42,x_7=89,x_8=5,x_9=13,x_{10}=28$.

(a) What is the population mean age?

(b) A sample of 5 random people has ages $\{18,5,7,42,13\}$ — what is that sample mean? (A sample mean isn't generally equal to the population mean, though it can be a good estimator for it; that typically requires a sample size of at least 30, so it doesn't apply here.)

(c) Make a histogram for the full population using 5 equal-width bins spanning ages 0–100: define the bin intervals, identify the representative bin value $\tilde x_j$ (midpoint) for each bin, quantize each data point to its bin's representative value, count the bin populations, and draw the histogram.

(d) Write the probabilities for each bin in (c); use them to express the average age as a probability-weighted sum of representative bin values, and compare to the population mean (quantization can distort the mean if not enough bins are used).

(e) Find the mean of the squared age $x^2$ two ways: directly from the full population, and using the histogram probabilities from (d) to weight a sum of squared representative bin values.

Show solution

(a) With $n_P=10$: $\langle x\rangle=\frac{1}{10}(5+24+18+7+35+42+89+5+13+28)=\frac{266}{10}=26.6$ years. (It's possible for no individual to actually have the mean age — no one here is “normal.”)

(b) With $n_S=5$: $\bar x=\frac15(18+5+7+42+13)=\frac{85}{5}=17$ years. This happens to be reasonably close to the population mean, but is still not a “good” estimator with only 5 samples — that generally needs $n_S\gtrsim30$.

(c) Bin width $=100/5=20$ years. Bin intervals: $[0,20),[20,40),[40,60),[60,80),[80,100)$, with representative (midpoint) values $\tilde x_1=10,\tilde x_2=30,\tilde x_3=50,\tilde x_4=70,\tilde x_5=90$. Quantized data: $\{10,30,10,10,30,50,90,10,10,30\}$ (for $x_1,\dots,x_{10}$ respectively). Bin populations: $(n_P)_1=5,(n_P)_2=3,(n_P)_3=1,(n_P)_4=0,(n_P)_5=1$ — a bar chart with heights $5,3,1,0,1$ over the five bins.

(d) Bin probabilities $p_b=(n_P)_b/n_P$: $p_1=0.5,p_2=0.3,p_3=0.1,p_4=0.0,p_5=0.1$. Weighted average: $\langle x\rangle\approx\sum_bp_b\tilde x_b=0.5(10)+0.3(30)+0.1(50)+0.0(70)+0.1(90)=5+9+5+0+9=28$ years — reasonably close to, but not exactly, the true population mean of $26.6$ years; the gap is quantization distortion from using only 5 coarse bins.

(e) Direct: $\langle x^2\rangle=\frac{1}{n_P}\sum_kx_k^2=1286.2$ years$^2$. Via histogram: $\langle x^2\rangle\approx\sum_bp_b\tilde x_b^2=1380$ years$^2$. The two don't match exactly for the same reason as (d) — more bins ($n_B=100$ here would give exact agreement for this dataset) shrink the gap. Quantization distortion doesn't apply in a truly discrete system where every outcome gets its own dedicated bin.

Problem 2

(a) Write the outer products $|j\rangle\langle k|$ of all combinations of the computational basis kets (as vectors) for one qubit.

(b) Given the density operator $\rho\equiv\sum_{j,k=1}^{n}\rho_{j,k}|j\rangle\langle k|$, plug in the outer products from (a) to get $\rho$ as a matrix in terms of its elements $\rho_{j,k}$, $j,k\in\{1,2\}$.

(c) Given the single-qubit pure state $|\psi\rangle\equiv\cos(\theta)|1\rangle+\sin(\theta)e^{i\phi}|2\rangle$, $\theta\in[0,\frac\pi2]$, $\phi\in[0,2\pi)$, rewrite it as a vector, find its outer product $|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, and write out what each matrix element $\rho_{j,k}$ is in terms of $\theta,\phi$.

(d) Show the diagonal elements $\rho_{k,k}$ sum to 1 and are each nonnegative, so have a natural interpretation as probabilities.

(e) Show that the magnitude of each off-diagonal element $\rho_{j,k\ne j}$ equals the square root of the product of its corresponding diagonal elements $\rho_{j,j}$ and $\rho_{k,k}$ (the geometric mean of the diagonals) — the “geometric mean rule” for pure states.

(f) Using $\rho$ from (c), take its Hermitian conjugate to show it's Hermitian.

Show solution

(a) $|1\rangle\equiv\binom10$, $|2\rangle\equiv\binom01$, bras $\langle1|=(1\ \ 0)$, $\langle2|=(0\ \ 1)$, so:

$$|1\rangle\langle1|=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |1\rangle\langle2|=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |2\rangle\langle1|=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |2\rangle\langle2|=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$$

(b) $\rho=\rho_{1,1}|1\rangle\langle1|+\rho_{1,2}|1\rangle\langle2|+\rho_{2,1}|2\rangle\langle1|+\rho_{2,2}|2\rangle\langle2| = \begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1,1}&\rho_{1,2}\\\rho_{2,1}&\rho_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}$.

(c) With $c_\theta\equiv\cos(\theta)$, $s_\theta\equiv\sin(\theta)$: $|\psi\rangle=\binom{c_\theta}{s_\theta e^{i\phi}}$, so

$$\rho\equiv|\psi\rangle\langle\psi| = \binom{c_\theta}{s_\theta e^{i\phi}}\big(c_\theta\ \ s_\theta e^{-i\phi}\big) = \begin{pmatrix}c_\theta^2 & \tfrac{s_{2\theta}}{2}e^{-i\phi}\\[4pt] \tfrac{s_{2\theta}}{2}e^{i\phi} & s_\theta^2\end{pmatrix}$$

using $s_\theta c_\theta=\tfrac12\sin(2\theta)\equiv\tfrac{s_{2\theta}}2$. So $\rho_{1,1}=c_\theta^2$, $\rho_{1,2}=s_\theta c_\theta e^{-i\phi}$, $\rho_{2,1}=s_\theta c_\theta e^{i\phi}$, $\rho_{2,2}=s_\theta^2$.

(d) $\rho_{1,1}+\rho_{2,2}=c_\theta^2+s_\theta^2=1$ by the fundamental trig identity. Since $x^2\ge0$ for real $x$, both $c_\theta^2\ge0$ and $s_\theta^2\ge0$, so $\rho_{j,j}\ge0$ for all $j$ — qualifying $\rho_{j,j}$ as probabilities, since $\sum_j\rho_{j,j}=1$ and $\rho_{j,j}\ge0\ \forall j$.

(e) $|\rho_{2,1}|=|s_\theta c_\theta e^{i\phi}|=s_\theta c_\theta$; $|\rho_{1,2}|=s_\theta c_\theta$; and $\sqrt{\rho_{1,1}\rho_{2,2}}=\sqrt{c_\theta^2s_\theta^2}=s_\theta c_\theta$. So $|\rho_{2,1}|=|\rho_{1,2}|=\sqrt{\rho_{1,1}\rho_{2,2}}$ — the geometric mean rule for pure states.

(f) $\rho^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}c_\theta^2 & \frac{s_{2\theta}}2e^{-i\phi}\\ \frac{s_{2\theta}}2e^{i\phi} & s_\theta^2\end{pmatrix}^{T*} =\begin{pmatrix}c_\theta^2 & \frac{s_{2\theta}}2e^{-i\phi}\\ \frac{s_{2\theta}}2e^{i\phi} & s_\theta^2\end{pmatrix}=\rho$ — so $\rho^\dagger=\rho$, confirming $\rho$ is Hermitian.

Problem 3
Write a general operator $A$ in ket-bra form in the computational basis by using $A=IAI$, where each $I$ is expanded using the completeness relation (with a different summation index for each), defining matrix elements $A_{j,k}\equiv\langle j|A|k\rangle$ (but don't write $A$ as a matrix), where $A$ is an $n$-dimensional operator.
Show solution

Completeness: $I=\sum_{j=1}^n|j\rangle\langle j|$. Plugging into $A=IAI$, using $j$ for the left $I$ and $k$ for the right $I$:

$$A = \left(\sum_{j=1}^n|j\rangle\langle j|\right)A\left(\sum_{k=1}^n|k\rangle\langle k|\right) = \sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^n|j\rangle\langle j|A|k\rangle\langle k|$$

Since $\langle j|A|k\rangle$ is just a number, it can slide past the kets/bras freely. Defining $A_{j,k}\equiv\langle j|A|k\rangle$:

$$\boxed{A = \sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^nA_{j,k}|j\rangle\langle k|, \qquad A_{j,k}\equiv\langle j|A|k\rangle}$$
Problem 4
Recalling the trace definition $\operatorname{tr}(A)\equiv\sum_{g=1}^n\langle g|A|g\rangle$, plug in the result of Problem 3 and use orthonormality ($\langle a|b\rangle=\delta_{a,b}$) to express $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ in terms of only matrix elements of $A$.
Show solution

Substituting $A=\sum_{j,k}A_{j,k}|j\rangle\langle k|$ into the trace definition:

$$\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{g=1}^n\langle g|\left(\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^nA_{j,k}|j\rangle\langle k|\right)|g\rangle = \sum_{g=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^nA_{j,k}\langle g|j\rangle\langle k|g\rangle = \sum_{g=1}^n\sum_{j=1}^n\sum_{k=1}^nA_{j,k}\delta_{g,j}\delta_{k,g}$$

$\delta_{g,j}$ picks out $j=g$ from the $j$-sum, and $\delta_{k,g}$ picks out $k=g$ from the $k$-sum, leaving:

$$\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(A) = \sum_{g=1}^nA_{g,g}}$$

— the trace is the sum over all diagonal matrix elements of $A$ (equivalently, one could have just used $\langle g|A|g\rangle=A_{g,g}$ directly for an immediate shortcut).

Problem 5
Using the definition of the trace, show that $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$ can be rewritten as $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$ if $\rho\equiv|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$. (Hint: at some point you'll need to use the completeness relation to simplify something to $I$.)
Show solution

Let $B\equiv\rho A$ in $\operatorname{tr}(B)\equiv\sum_{k=1}^n\langle k|B|k\rangle$, then use $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$:

$$\operatorname{tr}(\rho A) = \sum_{k=1}^n\langle k|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|A|k\rangle$$

Each term is a product of two scalars, so they can be reordered (without flipping the kets and bras themselves) as $\langle\psi|A|k\rangle\langle k|\psi\rangle$, then the sum moved inside to the “$k$” part:

$$\operatorname{tr}(\rho A) = \sum_{k=1}^n\langle\psi|A|k\rangle\langle k|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A\left(\sum_{k=1}^n|k\rangle\langle k|\right)|\psi\rangle$$

Recognizing the completeness relation $\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|=I$:

$$\operatorname{tr}(\rho A) = \langle\psi|AI|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle \qquad (\text{using } AI=A)$$
$$\boxed{\operatorname{tr}(\rho A) = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle}$$
