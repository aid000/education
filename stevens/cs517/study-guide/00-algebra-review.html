Overview

This is Lecture [00] on the syllabus's "Important Dates" schedule — the algebra refresher given in Week 3, immediately before the course dives into Topic 1 (Pure Quantum States and Superposition). CS517 / PEP557 assumes no prior quantum-physics background, but it does assume, in the instructor's own words, that "a firm understanding of algebra is essential." This lecture is that firm foundation: every identity here reappears constantly once the course moves to matrix representations of quantum states, so it is worth over-learning now rather than re-deriving under pressure during a quiz.

📌 A note on scope

The lecture states these identities for real numbers $a,b,c,\dots$ The course itself works over the complex numbers almost immediately (quantum amplitudes are complex), so watch the Look Ahead section at the end of this page for exactly which of these rules need a small patch (a complex conjugate, mostly) once $i=\sqrt{-1}$ enters the picture in Topic 1.

In plain English: before you can do quantum computing, you need to be completely fluent in ordinary algebra — the rules for combining numbers and variables that you can rearrange, expand, or simplify without a second thought. This lecture is a checklist of exactly those rules: how addition and multiplication can be reordered and regrouped, the order operations happen in, how to expand and factor products, how fractions and exponents behave, what absolute value means, how logarithms undo exponents, and a compact shorthand (sigma notation, $\Sigma$) for writing long sums. None of it is new quantum content — it's the toolkit the rest of the course leans on to manipulate vectors and matrices without algebra getting in the way of the physics.

Properties of Real Numbers

Commutative & associative properties

Addition and multiplication can be reordered (commutative) and regrouped (associative) freely:

$$a+b = b+a, \qquad ab = ba$$
$$(a+b)+c = a+(b+c) = b+(a+c), \qquad (ab)c = a(bc) = b(ac)$$

Note what is not on this list: subtraction and division are neither commutative ($a-b\ne b-a$ in general) nor associative. Whenever you "move terms around" a subtraction or division, you're really rewriting it as addition of a negative or multiplication by a reciprocal first, then applying the commutative/associative property to that.

Distributive property

$$a(b+c) = ab+ac, \qquad (a+b)c = ac+bc$$

Running either equation right-to-left is called factoring: $ac+bc=(a+b)c$. Setting $a=-1$ in the first identity gives a rule you'll use constantly to "distribute a minus sign":

$$-(b+c) = -b-c$$

Identity & inverse

$$a+0=a \quad\text{(additive identity)}, \qquad a\cdot 1 = a \quad\text{(multiplicative identity)}$$
$$a+(-a)=0 \quad\text{(additive inverse)}, \qquad a\cdot\frac{1}{a}=1 \ \ (a\ne0) \quad\text{(multiplicative inverse)}$$

These four properties — commutative, associative, distributive, identity/inverse — are the entire axiomatic basis for every algebraic manipulation that follows in this lecture (and, later, for the vector space axioms governing quantum states).

The vector space of quantum states is required to satisfy these exact same axioms (commutative/associative vector addition, distributive scalar multiplication, a zero vector, additive inverses) — just with vectors and complex scalars instead of real numbers. When Topic 1 defines a "pure quantum state," it is implicitly relying on all of the structure introduced here.

Order of Operations

The standard mnemonic "PEMDAS" (Please Excuse My Dear Aunt Sally):

#OperationNote
1Parenthesesinnermost first
2Exponents
3Multiplicationleft to right, same precedence
4Division
5Additionleft to right, same precedence
6Subtraction
⚠️ Misconception: "Multiplication always comes before division"
PEMDAS lists M before D and A before S, but that ordering is alphabetical/mnemonic convenience, not a precedence rule. Multiplication and division share one precedence level and are evaluated strictly left to right; likewise for addition and subtraction. $8 \div 2 \times 4$ is $16$ (left to right: $4\times4$), not $1$ (which would come from wrongly doing all multiplication before any division).

Binomials & the FOIL Method

Product of two binomials

Applying the distributive property twice:

$$(a+b)(c+d) = a(c+d)+b(c+d) = ac+ad+bc+bd$$

This is the origin of the FOIL mnemonic — multiply the Firsts, Outers, Inners, then Lasts, and add the four results: $(a+b)(c+d)=\underbrace{ac}_{F}+\underbrace{ad}_{O}+\underbrace{bc}_{I}+\underbrace{bd}_{L}$. FOIL only works for a product of exactly two binomials — for anything else (a trinomial times a binomial, three binomials at once), it's safest to apply the distributive law directly rather than force-fitting a mnemonic that doesn't apply.

Squared binomials

$$(a+b)^2 = (a+b)(a+b) = a^2+ab+ba+b^2 = a^2+2ab+b^2$$
$$(a-b)^2 = a^2-2ab+b^2$$
⚠️ Misconception: "Exponents distribute over addition"
$(a\pm b)^2 \ne a^2\pm b^2$ — exponents do not distribute over a sum. The correct expansion always carries the cross term $\pm 2ab$: compare $(3+4)^2=49$ against the wrong shortcut $3^2+4^2=25$. This exact error resurfaces later whenever you're tempted to square a sum of quantum amplitudes term-by-term instead of expanding it properly.

Fractions

Definition & distribution over the numerator

Division is defined as multiplication by a reciprocal, $\frac{a}{b}\equiv a\cdot\frac1b$ ($b\ne0$), with $a$ the numerator and $b$ the denominator ("d for downstairs"). A sum in the numerator distributes over a single denominator, in both directions:

$$\frac{a+b}{c} = \frac{a}{c}+\frac{b}{c}$$

The parentheses around a multi-term numerator or denominator are always implicit: $\frac{a+b}{c+d}=\frac{(a+b)}{(c+d)}$.

⚠️ Misconception: distributing over the denominator
$\dfrac{a}{b+c}\ne\dfrac{a}{b}+\dfrac{a}{c}$ — a sum in the denominator does not split the same way a sum in the numerator does. The asymmetry is easy to verify: $\dfrac{a}{b+c}=\dfrac1{b+c}\cdot a$ has one factor of $\frac1{b+c}$, not two separate reciprocals $\frac1b$ and $\frac1c$ added together.

Adding fractions with different denominators

Multiply each fraction by a cleverly-chosen form of 1 to get a common denominator:

$$\frac{a}{b}+\frac{c}{d} = \frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{d}{d}+\frac{c}{d}\cdot\frac{b}{b} = \frac{ad}{bd}+\frac{cb}{db} = \frac{ad+cb}{bd}$$

which also uses the general product rule $\dfrac{a}{b}\cdot\dfrac{c}{d}=\dfrac{ac}{bd}$.

Complex fractions & division by a fraction

"Division by a fraction is multiplication by its reciprocal." Starting from $\frac1b\cdot b=1$ and setting $a\equiv\frac1b$, multiplying both sides by $b$ gives the compact identity for a fraction stacked in a denominator:

$$\frac{1}{\left(\frac1b\right)} = b, \qquad \frac{\frac{a}{b}}{\frac{c}{d}} = \frac{a}{b}\cdot\frac{d}{c} = \frac{ad}{bc}$$

The stacking convention: the shorter fraction bar always belongs to the fraction nested inside the longer bar's denominator (or numerator) — that's how you read a "fraction of fractions" unambiguously.

Normalizing a quantum state — dividing every amplitude by $\sqrt{\sum_i|c_i|^2}$ so the probabilities sum to 1 — is nothing but this fraction algebra applied to complex numbers. Every time you simplify a probability amplitude down to lowest terms, you're using the common-denominator and reciprocal rules from this section.

Exponents & Roots

Integer & rational exponents

For $a>0$ and positive integer $n$: $a^0=1$, $a^1=a$, and $a^n=\underbrace{a\cdot a\cdots a}_{n\text{ factors}}$. Negative exponents invert: $a^{-n}=\frac{1}{a^n}$. Fractional exponents are roots: $a^{1/n}=\sqrt[n]{a}$ (the number which, raised to the $n$th power, gives $a$ back), and more generally $a^{m/n}=\sqrt[n]{a^m}=\left(\sqrt[n]{a}\right)^m$ for any integer $m$.

For $a,b>0$ and any rational $x,y$, the exponent laws:

$$a^xa^y=a^{x+y}, \qquad \frac{a^x}{a^y}=a^{x-y}, \qquad (a^x)^y=a^{xy}, \qquad (ab)^x=a^xb^x, \qquad \left(\frac{a}{b}\right)^x=\frac{a^x}{b^x}\ (b\ne0)$$

Square roots

$$\sqrt{a}\equiv a^{1/2} \ \Rightarrow\ \sqrt{a}\cdot\sqrt{a}=a, \qquad \sqrt{ab}=\sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}, \qquad \sqrt{\frac{a}{b}}=\frac{\sqrt{a}}{\sqrt{b}}$$

(the product/quotient rules generalize to any $n$th root). Two useful, related facts: $|a|\equiv\sqrt{a^2}$, and if $a^2=b$ then $a=\pm\sqrt{b}$ — a square root always has two solutions unless you've restricted the sign of $a$ in advance.

⚠️ Misconception: "roots distribute over addition"
Just like squaring, $\sqrt{a+b}\ne\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$ in general. Roots only distribute over products and quotients, never over sums or differences.
Normalization constants throughout the course are square roots of sums of squared amplitudes (e.g. the Bell state $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle)$) — that $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ is exactly the $a^{-1/2}$ exponent rule applied to $a=2$. Exponent laws with $2^n$ also govern the dimension of an $n$-qubit Hilbert space, which is $2^n$-dimensional.
📏

Absolute Values

$$|a|\ge0, \qquad a = \operatorname{sgn}(a)\,|a|,\quad \operatorname{sgn}(a)\equiv\begin{cases}+1,& a>0\\ 0,& a=0\\ -1,& a<0\end{cases}$$
$$|a|\equiv\sqrt{a^2}, \qquad |ab|=|a||b|, \qquad \left|\frac{a}{b}\right|=\frac{|a|}{|b|}\ (b\ne0), \qquad |a^n|=|a|^n$$

Absolute-value inequalities, for $a>0$:

$$|x|=a \iff x=\pm a, \qquad |x|a \iff x<-a \text{ or } x>a$$
Born's rule — the probability of a measurement outcome equals the squared magnitude of a (generally complex) amplitude, $P=|c|^2$ — is a direct generalization of $|a|\equiv\sqrt{a^2}$ to the complex plane, where $|c|^2=c^*c$ replaces $a^2$. Every probability calculation in this course begins with an absolute-value-squared exactly like this.

Factoring & the Quadratic Formula

Special-case factorizations

$$a^2-b^2=(a+b)(a-b)$$
$$a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2), \qquad a^3+b^3=(a+b)(a^2-ab+b^2)$$

Factoring a general quadratic in $x$

Since $(x+g)(x+h)=x^2+(g+h)x+gh$, factoring $x^2+ax+b$ means finding $g,h$ with $g+h=a$ and $gh=b$. More generally, for any $ax^2+bx+c=0$ with $a\ne0$, the quadratic formula gives the roots directly:

$$x_\pm = \frac{-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}$$

and then $ax^2+bx+c = a(x-x_+)(x-x_-)$, since substituting $x=x_+$ or $x=x_-$ makes one of the two factors vanish.

Finding the eigenvalues of a $2\times2$ matrix — the very first thing you'll do with Pauli operators and single-qubit gates in Topic 2 — means setting $\det(M-\lambda I)=0$, which for a $2\times2$ matrix is exactly a quadratic equation in $\lambda$. The quadratic formula above is how you'll solve it every time.
📉

Logarithms

$p=\log_b(a)$ means "the power that base $b$ must be raised to, to get $a$": $b^p=a$. Core identities:

$$\log_b(x)+\log_b(y)=\log_b(xy), \qquad \log_b(x)-\log_b(y)=\log_b\!\left(\frac{x}{y}\right), \qquad \log_b(a^x)=x\log_b(a)$$
$$\log_b(1)=0, \qquad \log_b(b)=1, \qquad b^{\log_b(x)}=x, \qquad \log_b(b^x)=x$$

Change of base (via natural log $\ln(a)\equiv\log_e(a)$):

$$\log_b(a) = \frac{\log_c(a)}{\log_c(b)} = \frac{\ln(a)}{\ln(b)}, \qquad \log_b(a)=\frac{1}{\log_a(b)}, \qquad \log_{b^n}(a)=\frac1n\log_b(a)$$
Base-2 logarithms are the natural unit of quantum information: an $n$-qubit register has $2^n$ basis states, so $n=\log_2(N)$ counts the qubits needed to index $N$ states — exactly the identity $\log_b(a^x)=x\log_b(a)$ run in reverse. The same $\log_2$ shows up when the course later defines entropy-like quantities for measuring entanglement.

Sigma Notation

Sums as shorthand

$\Sigma$ (Greek "sigma," for "sum") abbreviates a sum over an index $i$ running from a lower bound to an upper bound:

$$\sum_{i=1}^{n} c_i \equiv c_1+c_2+\cdots+c_n$$

The index $i$ starts at the lower value, increments by 1 each step, and stops at the upper value; each version of the summand $c_i$ is added cumulatively. Some standard special cases:

$$\sum_{i=1}^n 1 = \underbrace{1+1+\cdots+1}_{n\text{ terms}} = n, \qquad \sum_{i=1}^n c = c\sum_{i=1}^n 1 = cn$$

The summand can also depend explicitly on the index, e.g. $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n f(k)=f(1)+f(2)+\cdots+f(n)$, or run to infinity, as in the exponential series $\displaystyle e^x=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}\frac{x^k}{k!}$. Informally, "$\Sigma F$" is often shorthand for $\displaystyle\sum_{k=1}^n F_k = F_1+\cdots+F_n$ when the range is clear from context.

Dataset usage: the mean

For a dataset with values $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_N$, summand indices act as labels distinguishing data points that may or may not share the same value. The mean (average) is

$$\bar{x} = \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N x_j$$

For example, with three data points $x_1=3.10$, $x_2=-1.64$, $x_3=3.10$ (m): $\bar x = \frac13(3.10-1.64+3.10)=1.52$ m.

A general $n$-qubit pure state is written as a sum over $2^n$ computational-basis states, $|\psi\rangle=\sum_{i=0}^{2^n-1} c_i|i\rangle$, and normalization is the condition $\sum_i|c_i|^2=1$ — both are sigma notation, applied directly to complex amplitudes indexed exactly the way the dataset sum above indexes $x_j$. Expectation values of measurement outcomes later in the course are computed with this same $\sum_i (\text{value})_i\,(\text{probability})_i$ pattern.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Expand and simplify
Expand $(2x-3)^2$ and $(x+2)(x-5)$.
Show solution

$(2x-3)^2 = (2x)^2-2(2x)(3)+3^2 = 4x^2-12x+9$.

$(x+2)(x-5) = x^2-5x+2x-10 = x^2-3x-10$ (FOIL: F$=x^2$, O$=-5x$, I$=2x$, L$=-10$).

Problem 2 — Fractions
Simplify $\dfrac{1}{2}+\dfrac{1}{3}$, and then simplify $\dfrac{2/3}{4/9}$.
Show solution

$\dfrac12+\dfrac13 = \dfrac{1\cdot3}{2\cdot3}+\dfrac{1\cdot2}{3\cdot2} = \dfrac{3}{6}+\dfrac{2}{6}=\dfrac{5}{6}$.

$\dfrac{2/3}{4/9} = \dfrac23\cdot\dfrac94 = \dfrac{18}{12}=\dfrac32$.

Problem 3 — Normalization-style square root
If $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2=1$ and $c_1=c_2$ with both real and positive, solve for $c_1$.
Show solution

$c_1=c_2$ so $2c_1^2=1 \Rightarrow c_1^2=\frac12 \Rightarrow c_1=\sqrt{\frac12}=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ (taking the positive root, as given). This is exactly the normalization constant of the equal superposition $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+|1\rangle)$ you'll meet in Topic 1.

Problem 4 — Quadratic formula
Solve $\lambda^2-5\lambda+6=0$ for $\lambda$, two ways.
Show solution

Factoring: need $g+h=-5$, $gh=6$: $g=-2,h=-3$, so $\lambda^2-5\lambda+6=(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)=0 \Rightarrow \lambda=2$ or $\lambda=3$.

Quadratic formula: $\lambda_\pm=\dfrac{5\pm\sqrt{25-24}}{2}=\dfrac{5\pm1}{2}$, giving $\lambda=3$ or $\lambda=2$ — matching.

Problem 5 — Sigma notation
Write out $\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^{3} 2^i$ term by term and evaluate it. Then explain why this particular sum is relevant to a 2-qubit system.
Show solution

$\displaystyle\sum_{i=0}^3 2^i = 2^0+2^1+2^2+2^3 = 1+2+4+8=15$.

The four individual terms $2^0,2^1,2^2,2^3$ aren't the relevant quantum-computing quantity here — the upper index range is. A 2-qubit register has basis states indexed $i=0,1,2,3$ (i.e. $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$), exactly $2^2=4$ of them — the same index range $i=0$ to $2^n-1$ that appears in the general state $|\psi\rangle=\sum_i c_i|i\rangle$ from the Look Ahead section below, with $n=2$.

Look Ahead: From Real Algebra to Complex Quantum Amplitudes

Every identity above was stated for real $a,b,c,\dots$ Topic 1 (Pure Quantum States and Superposition) immediately extends this toolkit to complex amplitudes $c_i\in\mathbb{C}$, $i=\sqrt{-1}$. Most of the algebra carries over unchanged — the commutative, associative, and distributive properties, the exponent laws, and sigma notation all hold for complex numbers exactly as written above. The one place you must be careful is anything built from squaring or absolute value:

  • For a real number, $|a|\equiv\sqrt{a^2}$. For a complex number $c=x+iy$, the analogous (and only correct) definition is $|c|^2 \equiv c^*c = x^2+y^2$, where $c^*=x-iy$ is the complex conjugate — not $c^2$, which for a complex number is generally itself complex and not a valid probability.
  • Anywhere this lecture squared a real quantity to get a non-negative number (Problem 3's $|c_1|^2+|c_2|^2=1$ is deliberately written with absolute-value bars, not plain squares, for exactly this reason), the quantum-computing version will use $|c_i|^2=c_i^*c_i$.
  • The quadratic formula, factoring, exponents, logarithms, and sigma notation are otherwise used as-is on complex numbers and matrices throughout the rest of the course — matrix representations (Topic 1) and operators (Topic 2) are really this same algebra, just with numbers replaced by matrices (which are not commutative under multiplication — the first algebra rule from this lecture that will need to be dropped).
