In plain English: this lecture builds the entire language of quantum mechanics from scratch, using nothing but algebra and vectors. A state is just a complete description of a system (position, spin direction, whatever). Quantum states get written as kets $|\psi\rangle$, and the strange part is that a system can be in a weighted combination of several kets at once — a superposition — until it's measured, at which point it randomly collapses to one specific outcome with a probability set by the squared size of that outcome's coefficient. The lecture then shows how to package a state's coefficients into a column vector (a matrix representation), how to combine multiple quantum objects together with the Kronecker product, and finally defines entanglement: a kind of correlation between combined quantum objects that has no classical analogue and can't be undone by separately describing each piece.

🌀

1. Pure Quantum States and Superposition

1a. Quantum States for Curious Cats

A state is a list, arrangement, or distribution of matter and energy, determined by an object's degrees of freedom (DOF) — the ways it can move or store energy. States are global: if you sneeze, the state of the whole universe changes instantaneously (position, energy, temperature, spin direction all count as examples).

Quantum states are named and written as kets:

$$|\psi\rangle \equiv |\text{label for state name}\rangle$$

A label can be a symbol, word, abbreviation, equation, or picture — e.g. $|\uparrow\rangle \equiv |\text{spin axis pointing UP}\rangle$, $|\downarrow\rangle$, or $|x\rangle\equiv|\text{particle with position }x\rangle$. Kets can even be added together to describe a state that is many things simultaneously:

$$|{+}\rangle \equiv \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\uparrow\rangle+|\downarrow\rangle\big) \equiv \big(\text{spin axis pointing both UP {\it and} DOWN!}\big)$$

Bits vs. qubits: a classical bit has 2 possible states, e.g. coin outcomes $|H\rangle$ or $|T\rangle$ (heads or tails). A qubit is the quantum version — a state that can be heads and tails at once:

$$|\psi\rangle = c_H|H\rangle + c_T|T\rangle \quad (\text{heads {\it and} tails};\ |c_H|^2+|c_T|^2=1)$$
$$\boxed{\text{quantum superposition} \equiv \text{existence in different states simultaneously}}$$

Superposition is a wave property — multiple waves can exist in the same place at the same time, the same way multiple ripples overlap on a pond.

Schrödinger's Cat: being in two contradictory states at once is weird enough that Einstein and Schrödinger pointed out it would mean a cat could be both alive and dead:

$$|\psi_{\text{Cat}}\rangle = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\text{alive}\rangle+|\text{dead}\rangle\big)$$

They intended this as a conceptual flaw of quantum mechanics — and questioning your own theories like that is good science, even though they themselves helped discover QM. It turns out this cat-superposition disappears very quickly (so quickly we'd never notice it) because the cat's interactions with its environment effectively measure it, which ruins the superposition.

Measurement collapses superposition

Given a superposition between outcomes 0 and 1, $|\psi\rangle = c_0|0\rangle+c_1|1\rangle$ with $|c_0|^2+|c_1|^2=1$, what happens if we measure it?

$$|\psi\rangle \ \xrightarrow{\ \text{measurement}\ }\ |0\rangle \ \text{or}\ |1\rangle$$

We say measurement collapses $|\psi\rangle$ onto either $|0\rangle$ or $|1\rangle$, and the coefficients tell us the probability of each outcome:

$$p_k = |c_k|^2 \equiv \big(\text{probability of finding the system in state } |k\rangle \text{ if it was in } |\psi\rangle\big) \quad\Rightarrow\quad p_0=|c_0|^2,\ p_1=|c_1|^2$$

d-Level Quantum States: Qudits

A d-level classical system has $d$ possible outcomes — e.g. a 6-sided die has $d=6$ classical states $|1\rangle,|2\rangle,\dots,|6\rangle$ (one per face). The 6-level quantum version can be in full or partial superposition:

$$|\psi\rangle = c_1|1\rangle+\cdots+c_6|6\rangle, \quad \textstyle\sum_{k=1}^6|c_k|^2=1 \qquad(\text{full superposition})$$
$$|\psi\rangle = c_2|2\rangle+c_3|3\rangle+c_6|6\rangle, \quad |c_2|^2+|c_3|^2+|c_6|^2=1 \qquad(\text{partial superposition})$$

Since $|c_k|^2$ are probabilities, what are the coefficients $c_k$ themselves? Naively you might guess $c_k=\sqrt{p_k}$ — but as the next subsection shows, the full picture requires complex numbers.

1b. What Do Quantum State Coefficients Even Mean?

Normalization from a trig identity: the fundamental identity $\cos^2(\theta)+\sin^2(\theta)=1$ (true for any $\theta$) hints at one way to parameterize coefficients so normalization is automatic:

$$c_0=\cos(\theta),\quad c_1=\sin(\theta) \ \Rightarrow\ |c_0|^2+|c_1|^2=\cos^2(\theta)+\sin^2(\theta)=1$$

But quantum states have even more freedom than that — the coefficients can be complex.

Complex numbers refresher

$$z\equiv a+ib \ \ (\text{Cartesian form}),\quad a,b\in\mathbb{R},\quad i\equiv\sqrt{-1}$$
$$|z|\equiv\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\ \ (\text{modulus}), \qquad z=re^{i\phi}\ \ (\text{polar form}),\quad r\equiv|z|,\ \cos(\phi)\equiv\tfrac{a}{|z|},\ \sin(\phi)\equiv\tfrac{b}{|z|}$$

using Euler's theorem $e^{i\phi}=\cos(\phi)+i\sin(\phi)$. Complex conjugation replaces every $i$ with $-i$: $z^*\equiv a-ib$, from which $|z|=\sqrt{z^*z}$, and the real/imaginary parts can be extracted as $\operatorname{Re}[z]=\frac{z+z^*}{2}$, $\operatorname{Im}[z]=\frac{z-z^*}{2i}$.

Coefficients can be complex!

Each coefficient can carry its own unit-complex phase factor:

$$c_0\equiv\cos(\vartheta)e^{i\alpha}, \quad c_1\equiv\sin(\vartheta)e^{i\beta}, \qquad \vartheta\in[0,\tfrac{\pi}{2}],\ \alpha,\beta\in[0,2\pi)$$

Since $|e^{i\phi}|=1$ (unit modulus) for any phase $\phi$, this automatically satisfies $|c_0|^2+|c_1|^2=\cos^2(\vartheta)+\sin^2(\vartheta)=1$ for any choice of $\vartheta,\alpha,\beta$ — no separate normalization check needed. Substituting in, the most general pure qubit is

$$|\psi\rangle = \cos(\vartheta)e^{i\alpha}|0\rangle + \sin(\vartheta)e^{i\beta}|1\rangle$$

It's common to factor out the phase of the first nonzero coefficient — this overall global phase factor $e^{i\alpha}$ is usually discarded since it has no physical effects, leaving only a relative phase $\phi\equiv\beta-\alpha$ that does have measurable effects:

$$\boxed{|\psi\rangle = \cos\!\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)|0\rangle + \sin\!\big(\tfrac{\theta}{2}\big)e^{i\phi}|1\rangle} \qquad (\text{most general pure qubit},\ \theta\in[0,\pi],\ \phi\in[0,2\pi))$$

The Bloch Sphere

The parameters $\theta\in[0,\pi]$, $\phi\in[0,2\pi)$ act like the angles of a point on the surface of a sphere (the Bloch sphere), with $|0\rangle$ at the north pole and $|1\rangle$ at the south pole. The magnitude $|c_0|=|\cos(\theta/2)|$ tells us the amount of superposition for a given outcome, while the relative phase angle $\arg(c_1)=\phi$ tells us the phase of the $|1\rangle$ “wave” relative to $|0\rangle$. Measurement “pops” the Bloch sphere bubble, collapsing the state to one of its poles — but the Bloch sphere itself is just a representation of the degrees of freedom in $c_k$. The actual meaning of a coefficient is simply: the amount of $|k\rangle$ in $|\psi\rangle$.

1c. Why Do We Even Think Quantum Superposition Exists?

The problem: measurement collapses superposition, so we can't directly “see” it. The solution is that the wave-nature of quantum states allows quantum interference, just as two waves can interfere based on relative phase — but first we need some tools.

Overlaps & quantum states as complex vectors

An overlap is the amount of one vector in the direction of another. For real vectors, overlap is the familiar dot product: $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b\equiv|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\cos(\theta_{a,b})$, with $\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\hat{\mathbf x}=1$ and $\hat{\mathbf x}\cdot\hat{\mathbf y}=0$, giving $\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b=a_xb_x+a_yb_y$. For complex vectors, the overlap gets a star on one side, so that a vector's overlap with itself is a sum of squared moduli:

$$\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b \equiv a_x^*b_x+a_y^*b_y \qquad (\text{2D complex overlap}), \qquad \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf a = |a_x|^2+|a_y|^2=|\mathbf a|^2$$

So the overlap of two quantum states $|\phi\rangle\equiv a_0|0\rangle+a_1|1\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle\equiv b_0|0\rangle+b_1|1\rangle$ is defined as:

$$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \equiv a_0^*b_0+a_1^*b_1 \equiv c_{\phi\psi} \equiv \big(\text{a complex number describing the amount of } |\phi\rangle \text{ in } |\psi\rangle\big)$$

The transition probability is $|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2=|c_{\phi\psi}|^2\in[0,1]$ — the probability of finding the system in state $|\phi\rangle$ given that it was actually in $|\psi\rangle$. For a properly normalized state, $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1$.

Bras, orthogonality, and completeness

Overlap is so important that we define a special operation to star one state's coefficients, called a bra — the Hermitian conjugate of a ket:

$$\langle\phi| \equiv a_0^*\langle0|+a_1^*\langle1|, \qquad |\phi\rangle^\dagger \equiv \langle\phi| \equiv \big(\text{bra of ket } |\phi\rangle\big)$$

Two states are orthogonal (perpendicular) iff their overlap is zero:

$$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle \equiv 0 \ \iff\ |\phi\rangle\perp|\psi\rangle$$

A set of mutually orthogonal, normalized states $\{|\psi_1\rangle,\dots,|\psi_n\rangle\}$ has orthonormality $\langle\psi_j|\psi_k\rangle=\delta_{j,k}$, using the Kronecker delta $\delta_{j,k}=1$ if $j=k$, else $0$. E.g. for $|\phi\rangle\equiv|0\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle\equiv|1\rangle$, $\langle0|1\rangle=0$.

Completeness is the property of a set of states $\{|b_1\rangle,\dots,|b_n\rangle\}$ such that every state of the system can be written as a linear combination of them:

$$|\psi\rangle = \sum_{k=1}^n c_k|b_k\rangle \qquad \forall\,|\psi\rangle\in H^{[n]}$$
$$\boxed{\text{basis} \equiv \big(\text{a set of states that is both complete and orthonormal}\big)}$$

Checking overlap and normalization directly from the coefficients confirms this all fits together consistently: expanding $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$ and $\langle\psi|\psi\rangle$ in the $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ basis reproduces $a_0^*b_0+a_1^*b_1$ and $|b_0|^2+|b_1|^2=1$ exactly, term by term, using orthonormality to zero out the cross terms.

How to prove quantum superposition exists (violating Bayes' law)

Given qubit $|\psi\rangle=c_0|0\rangle+c_1|1\rangle$, the probability of finding it in state $|\phi\rangle=c_{\phi|0}|0\rangle+c_{\phi|1}|1\rangle$ is $p_{\phi|\psi}=|\langle\phi|\psi\rangle|^2$. Expanding this out using $|c|^2=c^*c$ gives:

$$p_{\phi|\psi} = \underbrace{p_0p_{\phi|0}+p_1p_{\phi|1}}_{\text{classical Bayesian conditional probability}} + \underbrace{2\operatorname{Re}\big[c_0^*c_1c_{\phi|0}c_{\phi|1}^*\big]}_{\text{additional nonclassical term due to superposition}}$$

Experiment confirms this effect — the extra quantum interference term is actually there (this is part of what Young's double-slit experiment shows). Everyday objects only ever seem to obey the purely classical Bayesian part because their constant interactions with the environment destroy superposition perpetually — but the cat-superposition can, in principle, exist, just too briefly to notice.

1d. Quantization and the Stern-Gerlach Experiment

Why is a measurement outcome always discretely “up or down,” never continuously sideways? In 1922, Stern and Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms (each with one unpaired electron, giving it a magnetic dipole moment) through an inhomogeneous magnetic field. Classically they expected a continuous smear of deflection angles, since the atoms' spin orientations should be random and continuous. Instead they observed exactly two clumps — as if the entering dipoles could only ever be up or down. This is direct experimental evidence that spin is a genuinely quantized, two-level system rather than a continuous classical one.

This up/down quantization is exactly the two-outcome qubit $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ this whole lecture has been building — the Stern-Gerlach experiment is the physical reason a spin-$\frac12$ particle is the canonical real-world qubit.

2. Matrix Representations

2a. Welcome to the Matrix: Compact Representation of Quantum States

The main idea of matrices as representations: relative location on the page of a number tells us which basis state that number is the coefficient of. If we represent the basis $\{|0\rangle,|1\rangle\}$ as $2\times1$ matrices,

$$|0\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |1\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

then $|\psi\rangle=c_0|0\rangle+c_1|1\rangle$ has representation $|\psi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}c_0\\c_1\end{pmatrix}$. For example, $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.30}|0\rangle+\sqrt{0.70}|1\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{0.30}\\\sqrt{0.70}\end{pmatrix}$ — the relative location on the page told us everything about which coefficient belongs to which ket, with no indices or labels needed. (Note: many people call this a “column vector representation,” but that's not quite right — a vector is a vector; a matrix of any dimension is always just one possible representation of it.)

The universal convention is the standard basis representation, following the Descending-Order Convention (DOC): for an orthonormal complete basis $\{|\psi_1\rangle,|\psi_2\rangle,|\psi_3\rangle\}$ with $n=3$, the standard basis is

$$|\psi_1\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad |\psi_2\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\quad |\psi_3\rangle\equiv\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

— an ordered basis where the position of the “1” descends as the ordinal of the basis state increases. Common terminology: “computational basis” $\equiv$ the standard basis.

2b. How Multiple Quantum Objects Play Together

Suppose we have two qubits, labeled qubit $A$ and qubit $B$ — this is called a bipartite system. For a classical system of two bits, there are four pairs of different outcomes: $\{|0^{(A)}\rangle,|0^{(B)}\rangle\}, \{|0^{(A)}\rangle,|1^{(B)}\rangle\}, \{|1^{(A)}\rangle,|0^{(B)}\rangle\}, \{|1^{(A)}\rangle,|1^{(B)}\rangle\}$ (e.g. two magnets: $\uparrow\uparrow,\uparrow\downarrow,\downarrow\uparrow,\downarrow\downarrow$). To get a 4-dimensional standard basis, we use the Kronecker product (tensor product):

$$|a\rangle\otimes|b\rangle \equiv \begin{pmatrix}a_1\\a_2\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{pmatrix} \equiv \begin{pmatrix}a_1\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{pmatrix}\\[4pt] a_2\begin{pmatrix}b_1\\b_2\end{pmatrix}\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}a_1b_1\\a_1b_2\\a_2b_1\\a_2b_2\end{pmatrix}$$

Together, the Kronecker product and the DOC define an ordered standard basis for the joint system in the joint-system Hilbert space $H\equiv H^{(A)}\otimes H^{(B)}$:

$$|0^{(A)}\rangle\otimes|0^{(B)}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |0^{(A)}\rangle\otimes|1^{(B)}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |1^{(A)}\rangle\otimes|0^{(B)}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix},\ \ |1^{(A)}\rangle\otimes|1^{(B)}\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$$

Common abbreviation: $|a^{(A)}\rangle\otimes|b^{(B)}\rangle \equiv |a^{(A)}\rangle|b^{(B)}\rangle \equiv |a\rangle|b\rangle \equiv |a,b\rangle \equiv |ab\rangle$ (the last one is literal writing, not multiplication — bad notation for larger systems, but common shorthand). So the ordered joint-system computational basis for two bits (or qubits) is $|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle$, following register counting (lexicographic order): the rightmost index counts up through its values first, then the index to its immediate left increases by one, and so on.

Multipartite Hilbert space: modes don't have to share the same number of levels. A qubit ($n_1=2$) and a qutrit ($n_2=3$) system has $N=2$ modes and a total of $n=n_1n_2=6$ levels. More generally, an $N$-partite system has $n=n_1n_2\cdots n_N$ total levels (a tripartite $3\times2\times4$ system has $n=24$). Infinitely many levels per mode are also possible, and quantum systems can be discrete (countable levels, as above) or continuous (uncountable).

⚠️ Misconception: “any multi-outcome system is multipartite”
A multipartite system implies coincidence outcomes — every measurement involves a simultaneous outcome from every mode at once (there's never just one mode having an outcome while the others “stay silent”). A classical 4-sided die (a tetrahedron) has 4 possible outcomes $\{|1\rangle,|2\rangle,|3\rangle,|4\rangle\}$, but only one face is down at a time — there's never a coincidence of multiple outcomes. So a 4-level system is not automatically multipartite; only a system genuinely built from multiple interacting modes (like two qubits) is.

3. Entanglement

3a. Separability vs. Entanglement for Pure States

Recall that all possible states of a system of two classical bits are $|\psi_C\rangle=|00\rangle$ OR $|01\rangle$ OR $|10\rangle$ OR $|11\rangle$. But general states of a system of two qubits are superpositions of these computational basis states:

$$|\psi\rangle = c_{00}|00\rangle+c_{01}|01\rangle+c_{10}|10\rangle+c_{11}|11\rangle, \qquad |c_{00}|^2+|c_{01}|^2+|c_{10}|^2+|c_{11}|^2=1$$

Separability of pure states means the two objects are statistically independent (uncorrelated). Mathematically, this means each qubit has its own pure state — e.g. for $|\psi\rangle=wy|00\rangle+wz|01\rangle+xy|10\rangle+xz|11\rangle$ (with $|w|^2+|x|^2=1$, $|y|^2+|z|^2=1$), factoring the sum shows:

$$|\psi\rangle = \big(w|0\rangle+x|1\rangle\big)\otimes\big(y|0\rangle+z|1\rangle\big) \equiv |\psi^{(1)}\rangle\otimes|\psi^{(2)}\rangle$$

Notice each qubit has its own distinct pure state, but each outcome is still a coincidence from the set $\{|00\rangle,|01\rangle,|10\rangle,|11\rangle\}$. So for pure states, achieving this product form is necessary and sufficient for separability:

$$\boxed{\text{entanglement} \equiv \big(\text{if a state is {\it not} separable, it is entangled}\big)} \qquad \Leftrightarrow\qquad |\psi\rangle \ne |\psi^{(1)}\rangle\otimes|\psi^{(2)}\rangle$$

The most extreme examples for two qubits are the four Bell states, which are maximally entangled (also called EPR states, after Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen):

$$|\Phi^+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle+|11\rangle),\quad |\Psi^+\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle+|10\rangle),\quad |\Phi^-\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|00\rangle-|11\rangle),\quad |\Psi^-\rangle=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(|01\rangle-|10\rangle)$$

There is no way to factor out any single-mode basis state from these states.

Superposition is necessary for entanglement, but not sufficient: just as importantly as “can't factor,” entanglement in pure states is really a superposition of nonfactorizable coincidence basis states. If Alice has qubit 1 and Bob has qubit 2 of $|\Phi^+\rangle$, then both qubits are up and both are down — but if Alice measures hers, the whole state randomly collapses to either $|0^{(1)}\rangle\otimes|0^{(2)}\rangle$ or $|1^{(1)}\rangle\otimes|1^{(2)}\rangle$: if Alice gets $|0^{(1)}\rangle$, Bob is guaranteed $|0^{(2)}\rangle$, and vice versa. This holds no matter how far apart Alice and Bob are, even farther than light could travel between them — as if each qubit instantaneously “knows” what the other is doing. This is called nonlocal correlation; Einstein called it “spooky action at a distance.”

A simple measure of entanglement for a general 2-qubit pure state $|\psi\rangle=a|00\rangle+b|01\rangle+c|10\rangle+d|11\rangle$ is the concurrence:

$$C(|\psi\rangle) \equiv 2|ad-bc|; \qquad C=0 \iff |\psi\rangle \text{ separable}, \qquad C=1 \iff |\psi\rangle \text{ maximally entangled}$$

E.g. $C(|\Phi^+\rangle)=2\left|\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\cdot\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}-0\cdot0\right|=1$ (maximally entangled), while for any separable state of the form $wy|00\rangle+wz|01\rangle+xy|10\rangle+xz|11\rangle$, $C=2|wy\cdot xz-wz\cdot xy|=0$ (separable), exactly as expected.

3b. More Thoughts on Entanglement and Quantum States

This is just the tip of the entanglement iceberg — beyond pure states are more complicated mixed states (Topic 2), for which entanglement is very difficult to quantify (only solved for $2\times2$ and $2\times3$ systems); concurrence for mixed states needs a lot more math than this lecture covers.

Entanglement makes special tasks possible that are impossible classically:

  • Quantum teleportation — transport a quantum state from one location to another with unshared qubits
  • Superdense quantum coding — send a message requiring multiple qubits to read (basis for encryption)
  • Quantum encryption — send a message that can never be eavesdropped or decoded by interception
  • Entanglement distillation — obtain highly entangled objects from a larger set of lesser entangled ones
  • Remote synchronization — correlated objects allow remote synchronization without communication
  • Quantum error correction — diagnose & fix errors in a quantum computer without hurting the computation

All of the above require ingredients this lecture hasn't introduced yet: physical operations (unitary operators and more general quantum operations), observable operators (the part of QM theory describing measurable quantities), measurement itself (sensitive and complicated), and quantum mixed states (accounting for interaction with the environment) — exactly the four ingredients Topic 2 builds next.

Think of how you'd get two coins to always land heads up, no matter how far apart they are, without any communication or physical control between them — entanglement (and nonlocal correlation in general) guarantees exactly this kind of coincidence, with zero communication required. Topic 2 formalizes measurement and shows precisely how Bell's Inequality proves this kind of correlation can't come from any classical “hidden variable” explanation.

Problem 1 — Normalization with a relative phase
A qubit is given by $|\psi\rangle=\frac35|0\rangle+c_1|1\rangle$ with $c_1$ real and positive. Find $c_1$, then rewrite $|\psi\rangle$ in the $\theta,\phi$ Bloch-sphere form.
Show solution

Normalization: $\left(\frac35\right)^2+c_1^2=1 \Rightarrow c_1^2=1-\frac{9}{25}=\frac{16}{25} \Rightarrow c_1=\frac45$.

Matching to $\cos(\theta/2)|0\rangle+\sin(\theta/2)e^{i\phi}|1\rangle$: since $c_1$ is real and positive, $\phi=0$, and $\cos(\theta/2)=\frac35,\ \sin(\theta/2)=\frac45 \Rightarrow \theta=2\arccos\!\left(\frac35\right)\approx 106.3^\circ$.

Problem 2 — Overlap and orthogonality
Let $|\phi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle+i|1\rangle)$ and $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(|0\rangle-i|1\rangle)$. Compute $\langle\phi|\psi\rangle$. Are these two states orthogonal?
Show solution

$\langle\phi|=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\langle0|-i\langle1|)$ (star the coefficients: $\left(\frac{i}{\sqrt2}\right)^*=\frac{-i}{\sqrt2}$).

$\langle\phi|\psi\rangle=\frac12\big[(1)(1)+(-i)(-i)\big]=\frac12(1+i^2)=\frac12(1-1)=0$.

Yes — the overlap is exactly zero, so $|\phi\rangle$ and $|\psi\rangle$ are orthogonal (these are, in fact, the $|+i\rangle$ and $|-i\rangle$ eigenstates you'll meet again with the Pauli-$Y$ operator later in the course).

Problem 3 — Kronecker product
Given $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}$ and $|\phi\rangle=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, compute $|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle$ and write the result in ket notation.
Show solution

$|\psi\rangle\otimes|\phi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\otimes\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\cdot1\\1\cdot0\\1\cdot1\\1\cdot0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|00\rangle+|10\rangle\big)$.

This is manifestly separable by construction (it was built as a Kronecker product to begin with) — its concurrence is $C=2|1\cdot0-0\cdot0|=0$, confirming it.

Problem 4 — Concurrence and entanglement
Compute the concurrence of $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}|00\rangle+\sqrt{\frac23}|11\rangle$. Is it separable, partially entangled, or maximally entangled?
Show solution

Here $a=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}$, $b=c=0$, $d=\sqrt{\frac23}$, so $C=2|ad-bc|=2\left|\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\sqrt{\frac23}-0\right|=2\sqrt{\frac{2}{9}}=\frac{2\sqrt2}{3}\approx0.943$.

Since $0partially entangled — entangled, but not maximally so (compare to $|\Phi^+\rangle$'s $C=1$, which uses equal $\frac{1}{\sqrt2}$ weights instead of the unequal $\frac{1}{\sqrt3},\sqrt{\frac23}$ weights here).

Look Ahead: From States to Operators

Topic 2 (Operators & Mixed States) picks up exactly where the “missing ingredients” list at the end of Section 3b left off. It starts with a quick classical-statistics refresher (population vs. sample means, histograms, and probabilities), then defines the identity operator and shows how any operator — not just a state — can be written as a matrix in a chosen basis. From there it builds the density operator $\rho\equiv|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, which represents a quantum state as an operator rather than a vector (essential once mixed states, Topics 6–7, enter the picture), and derives Bell's Inequality — the rigorous version of the “spooky action at a distance” argument sketched in Section 3a, which quantum mechanics is experimentally shown to violate.

⚓ Memory Anchor: what carries forward

Keep everything from this page: kets, bras, overlaps, orthonormality, completeness, the Kronecker product, and the concurrence test for entanglement. The one shift in Topic 2 is a change of representation, not a new set of rules — the same state $|\psi\rangle$ you've been writing as a column vector gets rewritten as the outer product $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$, a square matrix whose diagonal entries are exactly the same $p_k=|c_k|^2$ probabilities you already know how to compute.
