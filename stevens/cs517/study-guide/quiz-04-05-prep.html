Decoder: Density Operator & Trace

These are the symbols Topic 5 adds on top of the ones already covered in the general Notation Decoder Ring. Same format: what it is, and what it's actually for.

Symbol Name What it is What it's for, in plain language
$\rho\equiv|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ density operator (pure state) a matrix, built from a pure state's outer product with itself For the states in HW 04+05, it's just $|\psi\rangle$ multiplied by its own bra — you already know how to compute an outer product. $\rho$ exists because it's more convenient to compute probabilities and averages from a matrix than directly from the state vector every time. (Mixed states, Topic 7, use a more general $\rho$; they are not on this quiz.)
$\rho_{j,k}\equiv\langle j|\rho|k\rangle$ density-matrix element one entry of $\rho$'s matrix Diagonal entries ($j=k$) are probabilities — plain, real, nonnegative numbers that add up to 1. Off-diagonal entries ($j\ne k$) are usually complex and describe superposition between two outcomes — think of them as bookkeeping numbers, not probabilities themselves.
$P_k\equiv|b_k\rangle\langle b_k|$ projection operator a matrix built from one basis state's outer product with itself Same idea as projecting a vector onto an axis in ordinary geometry — $P_k$ is the tool that asks “how much does this state point along direction $k$?”
$\operatorname{tr}(A)\equiv\sum_g\langle g|A|g\rangle$ trace a number: the sum of a matrix's diagonal entries The exact same trace from linear algebra (add up the diagonal). Here it's the one operation that turns a matrix like $\rho$ or $\rho A$ into a single plain number — a probability or an average.
$\langle A\rangle\equiv\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$ mean (expectation) value a number: the average value of observable $A$ The quantum version of a weighted average, $E[X]=\sum_kp_kx_k$, from any intro probability/stats class — just written using matrices instead of a plain sum. For a pure state this also equals $\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$.
$|\rho_{j,k}|=\sqrt{\rho_{j,j}\rho_{k,k}}$ geometric-mean rule a numerical check on the off-diagonals of a pure-state $\rho$ HW 04+05 Problem 2e. For a pure state, the size of each off-diagonal equals the geometric mean of the two matching diagonal probabilities. If this fails, either the arithmetic is wrong or the matrix is not a pure-state $\rho$.

Worked Walkthrough: $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)=\langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$

This result from HW 04+05 Problem 5 is the single most quiz-relevant derivation in Topic 5 — it's the bridge between “$\rho$ as a matrix” and “$\rho$ as a way to compute averages.” Here it is broken into smaller steps than the homework solution uses.

$$\operatorname{tr}(\rho A) \equiv \sum_{k=1}^n\langle k|\rho A|k\rangle$$

Step 1 — this is just the definition of trace: sandwich $\rho A$ between $\langle k|$ and $|k\rangle$ for every basis state $k$, and add up the results.

$$\rho \equiv |\psi\rangle\langle\psi| \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sum_{k=1}^n\langle k|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|A|k\rangle$$

Step 2 — substitute the definition of $\rho$. Read left to right: $\langle k|\psi\rangle$ (a number) times $\langle\psi|A|k\rangle$ (another number), inside a sum over $k$.

$$\sum_{k=1}^n\langle k|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|A|k\rangle = \sum_{k=1}^n\langle\psi|A|k\rangle\langle k|\psi\rangle$$

Step 3 — since both factors are just plain numbers, you're allowed to swap the order they're multiplied in. Note the bras and kets inside each factor don't move — only the two whole numbers swap places. (This step would not be legal if either factor were a matrix instead of a number.)

$$\sum_{k=1}^n\langle\psi|A|k\rangle\langle k|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A\left(\sum_{k=1}^n|k\rangle\langle k|\right)|\psi\rangle$$

Step 4 — $\langle\psi|A$ and $|\psi\rangle$ don't depend on $k$, so pull them outside the sum, leaving $\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|$ by itself in the middle.

$$\langle\psi|A\underbrace{\left(\sum_{k=1}^n|k\rangle\langle k|\right)}_{=\,I}|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|AI|\psi\rangle = \langle\psi|A|\psi\rangle$$

Step 5 — recognize $\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|=I$ (the completeness relation), and use $AI=A$ to drop it. Done.

The whole derivation is really just one trick used twice: (1) numbers can be reordered even when the vectors/matrices around them can't, and (2) $\sum_k|k\rangle\langle k|$ is the identity operator $I$, which acts like multiplying by 1 (so $AI=A$). See the Type-Checking section of the notation primer for more on both of these moves.
⚠️

Common Pitfalls

1. Diagonal and off-diagonal entries of $\rho$ are not the same kind of thing

$\rho_{k,k}$ (matching indices) is a probability — real, between 0 and 1, and the full diagonal always sums to exactly 1. $\rho_{j,k\ne j}$ (mismatched indices) is generally complex and is not a probability at all — it just describes superposition between outcomes $j$ and $k$. Don't try to interpret an off-diagonal entry as a probability.

2. $\rho$ is Hermitian: $\rho_{j,k}^*=\rho_{k,j}$

The entry below the diagonal is always the complex conjugate of the mirror-image entry above it. If you compute two off-diagonal entries and they don't satisfy this, you've made an arithmetic mistake somewhere — it's a built-in way to check your own work.

3. $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$ needs the matrix product $\rho A$ first, not $\rho$ and $A$ separately

You have to multiply the two matrices together before taking the trace — $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)\ne\operatorname{tr}(\rho)\operatorname{tr}(A)$ in general. If $A$ happens to be diagonal, there's a shortcut ($\langle A\rangle=\sum_jp_jA_{j,j}$, using just the diagonals of both), but that shortcut only works because $A$ is diagonal, not as a general rule.

4. A sample mean is not the same thing as a population mean

$\langle x\rangle=\frac1{n_P}\sum_kx_k$ (population, every member) and $\bar x=\frac1{n_S}\sum_kx_k$ (sample, a subset) use the exact same-looking formula, but a small sample ($n_S\lesssim30$) is not a reliable estimate of the population mean — don't treat them as interchangeable just because the arithmetic looks identical.

5. Quantization (histogram binning) distorts the mean unless you use enough bins

Estimating a mean from a histogram, $\langle x\rangle\approx\sum_bp_b\tilde x_b$ (using bin midpoints $\tilde x_b$), generally gives a slightly different number than computing the mean directly from the raw data — that gap is normal and expected with few bins, and only shrinks toward zero as the number of bins grows.

6. The geometric-mean rule is for pure-state $\rho$ only

HW 04+05 Problem 2e: $|\rho_{j,k}|=\sqrt{\rho_{j,j}\rho_{k,k}}$. Use it as a sanity check after you build $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$. If the magnitudes don't match, an arithmetic error (usually a missed conjugate on an off-diagonal) is the first thing to look for. This rule is a pure-state signature; mixed states (Topic 7, after this quiz) can violate it and still be valid density operators.

Extra Problem 1 — classical stats, a different dataset

↩ Covered in Topic 02, Section 4 (Classical Statistics)

A population of 8 quiz scores: $\{62,75,75,88,91,55,70,80\}$.
  1. Find the population mean.
  2. Using 4 equal-width bins spanning $50$–$100$, find each bin's representative (midpoint) value and probability. Use the same left-closed, right-open convention as HW 04+05 ($[50,62.5),[62.5,75),\ldots$), so a value sitting on an edge belongs to the bin that starts there.
  3. Estimate the mean from the histogram probabilities and compare to (a).
Show solution

(a) $\langle x\rangle=\frac18(62+75+75+88+91+55+70+80)=\frac{596}{8}=74.5$.

(b) Bin width $=\frac{100-50}{4}=12.5$. Use the same left-closed, right-open convention as HW 04+05 Problem 1: $[50,62.5),[62.5,75),[75,87.5),[87.5,100]$, with midpoints $56.25, 68.75, 81.25, 93.75$. A value sitting on an edge (here $75$) goes into the bin that starts at that edge, so $75\in[75,87.5)$. Quantized data: $\{56.25,81.25,81.25,93.75,93.75,56.25,68.75,81.25\}$. Bin populations: $2,1,3,2$ (out of 8), so probabilities $p_1=0.25,p_2=0.125,p_3=0.375,p_4=0.25$.

(c) $\langle x\rangle\approx0.25(56.25)+0.125(68.75)+0.375(81.25)+0.25(93.75)=14.0625+8.59375+30.46875+23.4375=76.5625$ — close to but not exactly $74.5$, the usual quantization gap from using only 4 bins.

Extra Problem 2 — build a density matrix from scratch, no formula plugged in

↩ Covered in Topic 02, Section 5c–5e (Density Operator & Its Anatomy)

Let $|\psi\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}|1\rangle+\sqrt{\frac23}\,i|2\rangle$ (a normalized qubit; check that for yourself first).
  1. Write $|\psi\rangle$ as a column vector and find $\langle\psi|$ as a row vector.
  2. Compute the outer product $\rho=|\psi\rangle\langle\psi|$ directly as a $2\times2$ matrix product — don't use the $\rho_{j,k}=c_jc_k^*$ shortcut, multiply it out.
  3. Check $\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=1$ and $\rho^\dagger=\rho$ directly from your matrix.
  4. Check the geometric-mean rule $|\rho_{1,2}|=\sqrt{\rho_{1,1}\rho_{2,2}}$.
Show solution

(a) $|\psi\rangle=\binom{1/\sqrt3}{\ i\sqrt{2/3}}$, so $\langle\psi|=\left(\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\ \ {-i\sqrt{2/3}}\right)$ (conjugating $i\sqrt{2/3}$ flips its sign).

(b) Multiplying the column by the row (outer product):

$$\rho = \binom{1/\sqrt3}{i\sqrt{2/3}}\Big(\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\ \ {-i\sqrt{2/3}}\Big) = \begin{pmatrix}\frac13 & -\frac{i}{\sqrt3}\sqrt{\frac23}\\[4pt] \frac{i}{\sqrt3}\sqrt{\frac23} & \frac23\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\frac13 & -\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}\\[4pt] \frac{i\sqrt2}{3} & \frac23\end{pmatrix}$$

(c) $\operatorname{tr}(\rho)=\frac13+\frac23=1$ ✓. Taking the Hermitian conjugate (transpose, then conjugate every entry): the diagonal entries $\frac13,\frac23$ are real and unaffected; the off-diagonals swap positions and conjugate, sending $-\frac{i\sqrt2}3$ (top-right) to $+\frac{i\sqrt2}3$ (bottom-left) and vice versa — which exactly reproduces the original matrix, so $\rho^\dagger=\rho$ ✓.

(d) $|\rho_{1,2}|=\left|-\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}\right|=\frac{\sqrt2}{3}$, and $\sqrt{\rho_{1,1}\rho_{2,2}}=\sqrt{\frac13\cdot\frac23}=\sqrt{\frac29}=\frac{\sqrt2}{3}$. They match, as they must for a pure-state $\rho$.

Extra Problem 3 — completeness relation, fresh example

↩ Covered in Topic 02, Section 5a–5b (Completeness & Operators as Matrices)

For a qutrit ($n=3$) operator $A$, start from $A=IAI$ and insert the completeness relation for each $I$ (using index $j$ for the left one, $m$ for the right one — deliberately different letters than the homework used, to prove to yourself the letters don't matter). Expand to the double-sum ket-bra form of $A$, then write out what $A_{1,3}$ picks out physically.
Show solution
$$A = \left(\sum_{j=1}^3|j\rangle\langle j|\right)A\left(\sum_{m=1}^3|m\rangle\langle m|\right) = \sum_{j=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3|j\rangle\langle j|A|m\rangle\langle m| = \sum_{j=1}^3\sum_{m=1}^3A_{j,m}|j\rangle\langle m|$$

using $A_{j,m}\equiv\langle j|A|m\rangle$. $A_{1,3}=\langle1|A|3\rangle$ is the coefficient sitting in row 1, column 3 of $A$'s matrix — it's the number you'd read off if you wrote $A$ out as a full $3\times3$ grid and looked at the top-right corner. Swapping which letter labels which sum ($j$ vs. $m$, or any other choice) never changes the final answer — dummy indices are just placeholders.

Extra Problem 4 — mean value of a diagonal observable, then a non-diagonal one

↩ Covered in Topic 02, Section 5f–5g (Probabilities & Mean Values in Terms of Operators) and the Worked Walkthrough above

A qubit is in the pure state $|\psi\rangle=\sqrt{0.7}\,|1\rangle+\sqrt{0.3}\,|2\rangle$, with density matrix $\rho=\begin{pmatrix}0.7 & \sqrt{0.21}\\ \sqrt{0.21} & 0.3\end{pmatrix}$ (Hermitian, trace-1, and it obeys the geometric-mean rule: $|\rho_{1,2}|=\sqrt{0.21}=\sqrt{0.7\cdot0.3}$).
  1. The observable $A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ is the Pauli operator $\sigma_z$ (diagonal). Find $\langle A\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)$.
  2. Now use the non-diagonal observable $X=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$ (Pauli $\sigma_x$). Compute $\rho X$ fully, then $\langle X\rangle=\operatorname{tr}(\rho X)$. The diagonal shortcut from (a) does not apply here.
Show solution

(a) Since $A$ is diagonal, only the diagonal of $\rho$ ends up mattering — but compute $\rho A$ fully first to see it directly:

$$\rho A = \begin{pmatrix}0.7 & \sqrt{0.21}\\ \sqrt{0.21} & 0.3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}0.7 & -\sqrt{0.21}\\ \sqrt{0.21} & -0.3\end{pmatrix}$$

Trace is the sum of the diagonal: $\operatorname{tr}(\rho A)=0.7+(-0.3)=0.4$.

$$\boxed{\langle A\rangle = \langle\sigma_z\rangle = 0.4}$$

Sanity check using $\langle A\rangle=\sum_jp_jA_{j,j}$ for diagonal $A$: $p_1=\rho_{1,1}=0.7$, $p_2=\rho_{2,2}=0.3$, $A_{1,1}=1$, $A_{2,2}=-1$, so $\langle A\rangle=0.7(1)+0.3(-1)=0.7-0.3=0.4$ — matches.

(b) Now $X$ has zeros on the diagonal, so the off-diagonals of $\rho$ are what survive:

$$\rho X = \begin{pmatrix}0.7 & \sqrt{0.21}\\ \sqrt{0.21} & 0.3\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}\sqrt{0.21} & 0.7\\ 0.3 & \sqrt{0.21}\end{pmatrix}$$

$\operatorname{tr}(\rho X)=\sqrt{0.21}+\sqrt{0.21}=2\sqrt{0.21}\approx 0.917$.

$$\boxed{\langle X\rangle = \langle\sigma_x\rangle = 2\sqrt{0.21}\approx 0.917}$$

As a second check from the ket: $\langle\psi|X|\psi\rangle=2\operatorname{Re}(c_1^*c_2)=2\sqrt{0.7\cdot0.3}=2\sqrt{0.21}$, same number. The off-diagonals of $\rho$ are doing real work here; throwing them away would have given $0$.

Extra Problem 5 — elementary outer products, then assemble $\rho$

↩ Covered in Topic 02, Section 5b and HW 04+05 Problem 2a–2b

  1. Write the four outer products $|1\rangle\langle1|$, $|1\rangle\langle2|$, $|2\rangle\langle1|$, $|2\rangle\langle2|$ as $2\times2$ matrices (DOC: $|1\rangle=\binom10$, $|2\rangle=\binom01$).
  2. Using $\rho=\sum_{j,k}\rho_{j,k}|j\rangle\langle k|$ with the numbers $\rho_{1,1}=\frac13$, $\rho_{1,2}=-\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}$, $\rho_{2,1}=\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}$, $\rho_{2,2}=\frac23$ from Extra Problem 2, assemble $\rho$ as a matrix by adding those four scaled outer products. Confirm you recover the same matrix as Extra Problem 2.
Show solution

(a)

$$|1\rangle\langle1|=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad |1\rangle\langle2|=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},\quad |2\rangle\langle1|=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix},\quad |2\rangle\langle2|=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$$

(b) $\rho=\frac13|1\rangle\langle1|-\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}|1\rangle\langle2|+\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}|2\rangle\langle1|+\frac23|2\rangle\langle2|$ puts $\rho_{j,k}$ into row $j$, column $k$:

$$\rho=\begin{pmatrix}\frac13 & -\frac{i\sqrt2}{3}\\ \frac{i\sqrt2}{3} & \frac23\end{pmatrix}$$

Same matrix as Extra Problem 2. This is why $\rho=\sum_{j,k}\rho_{j,k}|j\rangle\langle k|$ and $\rho=\begin{pmatrix}\rho_{1,1}&\rho_{1,2}\\\rho_{2,1}&\rho_{2,2}\end{pmatrix}$ are the same statement.

Extra Problem 6 — translate the symbols into a sentence

↩ Covered in Topic 02, Section 5 (Hello, Operator!)

Without doing any computation, write one plain-English sentence describing what each of the following equations is saying (not how to derive it):
  1. $\sum_k|b_k\rangle\langle b_k|=I$
  2. $p_k=\operatorname{tr}(\rho P_k)$
  3. $\rho_{j,k}^*=\rho_{k,j}$
  4. $|\rho_{j,k}|=\sqrt{\rho_{j,j}\rho_{k,k}}$ for a pure state
Show solution

(a) “Adding up the outer product of every basis state with itself gives you the identity — a legal way to insert ‘do nothing’ into an equation.”

(b) “The probability of outcome $k$ equals the trace of the state's density operator multiplied by the projection operator onto outcome $k$.”

(c) “The density matrix is Hermitian: the entry below the diagonal is always the complex conjugate of the mirror-image entry above the diagonal.”

(d) “For a pure state, the size of each off-diagonal entry equals the geometric mean of the two diagonal probabilities it sits between — a built-in arithmetic check.”

Being able to say the sentence before touching the algebra is a good sign you actually understand what a quiz question is asking, rather than pattern-matching to a memorized derivation.
