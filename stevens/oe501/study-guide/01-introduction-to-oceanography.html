Overview

This is Lecture 1 on the syllabus's Tentative Course Schedule (Sept 2), covering the reading Segar, Chapter 1: "The Ocean Planet." No prior background in oceanography, physics, or chemistry is assumed — OE501 has no prerequisites — so this lecture opens by asking what oceanography even is, why the ocean is worth studying at all, and then equips you with the basic reading and interpretation tools (coordinates, map projections, graphs, contour plots, cross-sections) that every later lecture will lean on to present data about the ocean.

What Is Oceanography?

Definition

Oceanography (also called ocean science or marine science) is a branch of science that studies the physical and biological properties and phenomena of the oceans and seas. It is inherently interdisciplinary — oceanographers draw on biology, chemistry, geography, geology, hydrology, meteorology, and physics to study the world's oceans, covering everything from marine life and ecosystems to ocean waves, sediment transport, and seafloor geology.

The four sub-disciplines

DisciplineStudies
Geological oceanographyComposition of the inner Earth, mobility of the crust, characteristics of seafloor sediments, and the history of Earth's ocean, continents, and climate
Physical oceanographyWave dynamics, astronomical tides, currents, seawater salt content and temperature, and ocean–atmosphere interactions
Biological oceanographyThe nature and distribution of marine organisms, and the impact of oceanic/atmospheric conditions on those organisms
Chemical oceanographyThe ocean's dissolved solids and gases and their relationships to geology and biology

Keep this four-way split in mind as an organizing map for the rest of the semester: Lectures 3 and 10–13 lean chemical/biological (seawater chemistry, sediments, ecology, climate); Lectures 4–9 lean physical (circulation, waves, tides, storm surge); geological oceanography underlies the seafloor maps you'll interpret throughout using the tools introduced later in this lecture.

Basic Ocean Facts

One ocean, five named basins

There is only one global ocean, divided by convention into five interconnected named oceans:

  • Pacific — largest and deepest
  • Atlantic
  • Indian
  • Arctic — smallest and shallowest
  • Southern (Antarctic) Ocean — the extension of the Pacific, Atlantic, and Indian oceans around Antarctica, south of 60°S latitude

Oceans vs. seas

Seas are smaller than oceans, composed of water of varying salinity, and partially or fully enclosed by land. The distinction matters for connectivity: the Yellow Sea is connected to the Pacific Ocean (so it shares the open ocean's water and, largely, its salinity), whereas the Caspian Sea is fully landlocked (so its water chemistry can diverge substantially from the open ocean's).

⚠️ Misconception: "Ocean" and "sea" are interchangeable
They aren't synonyms in the technical sense used in this course. The key test is enclosure and connectivity to the open ocean, not just size — a landlocked "sea" like the Caspian behaves more like a giant saline lake than like a true ocean basin, since it has no open exchange with the rest of the world ocean.

Latitude & Longitude

Locations on Earth are defined using the latitude/longitude coordinate system — the vocabulary you'll need for every chart, station location, and dataset in this course.

Latitude ($\phi$)

  • Determined by the angle between a point, Earth's center, and the equator
  • Expressed in degrees north or south of the equator (0°), with the poles at 90°
  • Latitude lines (parallels) are parallel to the Equator
  • Usually given in degrees, minutes, and seconds: 1 degree = 60 minutes ('), 1 minute = 60 seconds (")

One minute of latitude equals exactly one nautical mile, which equals 1.15 land (statute) miles, or 1.85 km — a conversion factor that resurfaces constantly in navigation and in current-speed/distance problems later in the course.

Longitude ($\lambda$)

  • Measures distance east or west of the prime meridian (0°), defined as the line through Greenwich, England
  • A meridian (or great circle) is a circle of constant longitude; all meridians are the same length, but they are not parallel to each other
  • Meridians converge at the poles

Because meridians converge toward the poles, a minute of longitude does not correspond to a fixed distance the way a minute of latitude does: it shrinks from 69.172 miles (111.321 km) at the equator to zero exactly at the poles.

⚠️ Misconception: "A minute of longitude is always about a nautical mile, just like latitude"
Only true right at the equator. Latitude's 1 minute = 1 nautical mile conversion is constant everywhere on Earth because parallels are (approximately) evenly spaced from equator to pole. Longitude's conversion is not constant — it depends on latitude, shrinking to zero at the poles because meridians physically converge to a point there. Always check which coordinate you're converting before applying the nautical-mile shortcut.

Worked example: the Statue of Liberty

A location is fully specified by a latitude and a longitude in degrees, minutes, and seconds, each signed north/south or east/west. The Statue of Liberty sits at:

$$\text{Latitude: } 40^\circ\,41'\,21''\,\text{N}, \qquad \text{Longitude: } 74^\circ\,02'\,40''\,\text{W}$$

(north of the equator, west of the prime meridian — consistent with its location in New York Harbor).

Every ocean-observation platform in Lecture 7 (buoys, ships, satellites, floats) reports its position in exactly this latitude/longitude system, and current/wave/tide datasets throughout the course are indexed by it. Getting comfortable converting degrees-minutes-seconds now avoids friction on every later dataset you'll read.

Map Projections

Studying oceanography requires working with maps, but accurately representing a three-dimensional spherical object like Earth on a flat, two-dimensional map or chart is fundamentally difficult. A map projection is a scheme for representing the Earth's true area, direction, distance, and shape on a flat map — and every projection sacrifices at least one of those four properties to preserve the others.

Common projections used in oceanography

  • Mercator: latitude and longitude are both straight lines; excellent for navigation because directions (bearings) are preserved exactly, but size and distance become severely distorted at high latitudes (this is why Greenland looks continent-sized on a Mercator world map).
  • Robinson (planisphere): keeps latitude lines horizontal, shows some convergence of longitude toward the poles, with less distortion than Mercator; used mostly for general-purpose data presentation.
  • Mollweide: an equal-area projection (true relative areas), with latitude lines drawn as ellipses; trades this area accuracy for significant shape distortion, especially near the poles and the four "corners" of the map.
  • Equirectangular: the simplest projection (latitude and longitude both plotted as an evenly-spaced grid); easy to compute but distorts area badly at high latitude — equal-area circles drawn on the globe appear as increasingly wide ellipses near the poles on this projection.

⚓ Memory Anchor: no projection wins on everything

A single flat map can preserve at most a subset of {distance, direction, area, shape} — never all four at once, because a sphere cannot be flattened without some distortion (the same obstruction, geometrically, as trying to flatten orange peel without tearing or stretching it). Always ask "what is this particular map optimized to preserve?" before reading distances or areas off of it — a Mercator map is trustworthy for compass bearings but will visually lie to you about how big Antarctica or Greenland really is.

Threats to the Ocean

The lecture closes its "big picture" framing with three interlocking threats, previewing material that Lectures 12–13 (Climate Change & Global Warming, Climate Change Impacts) will return to in depth.

Climate change

Enhancement of the greenhouse effect drives rising global temperatures and sea level. Atmospheric CO2 has risen from a pre-industrial baseline of about 280 ppm to over 420 ppm today — an increase of roughly 3.5 ppm from May 2024 to May 2025 alone, illustrating that the rate of increase is itself still accelerating, not just the total.

Ocean acidification

About 25–30% of anthropogenic CO2 emissions have been absorbed by the oceans. That absorbed CO2 reacts with seawater to form carbonic acid, lowering pH and consuming carbonate ions (which many marine organisms need to build shells and skeletons). Ocean waters remain, on average, weakly alkaline (pH ≈ 8.1 on the 0–14 scale), but that average is trending down — the "acidification" is a directional trend, not (yet) a crossing into acidic territory.

Ocean deoxygenation

Two compounding drivers reduce dissolved oxygen in the deep ocean: anthropogenic CO2 warms the air and, in turn, the oceans, disrupting the circulation and chemistry that normally keep deep water oxygenated; and nitrogen & phosphorus released in large quantities from agriculture and treated sewage discharges fuel excess biological productivity near the surface.

Ocean oxygen structure: the surface layer exchanges oxygen directly with the atmosphere and is where both photosynthesis and respiration occur; below the surface layer (through the pycnocline and into the deep zone), only respiration occurs, consuming oxygen with no local resupply, while sinking detritus and migrating animals carry organic material downward to fuel that respiration.

This sets up two paths to anoxia (zero oxygen):

  1. Circulation slows: a longer residence time gives respiration more time to consume the available oxygen before fresh, oxygenated water arrives.
  2. Respiration increases: a greater supply of detritus (food) fuels more respiration, consuming oxygen faster than it can be replenished.
Coastal and estuarine engineering projects (Lecture 9's subject) routinely have to account for deoxygenation risk directly: nutrient loading from agricultural or wastewater runoff into a slow-circulating estuary is exactly the "respiration increases + circulation slows" combination above, and is a leading cause of coastal "dead zones" such as the seasonal hypoxic zone in the Gulf of Mexico.

Reading Graphs Carefully

Axis truncation can mislead

Graphs visualize relationships between two or more variables, but can be misleading unless read properly — you must carefully examine the axes, not just the shape of the bars or curve. Example: three data values 230, 231.5, and 237 are nearly identical in absolute terms. Plotted on a full axis from 0 to 250, the three bars look almost the same height. Plotted on a truncated axis from 228 to 240, the third bar (237) looks dramatically larger than the other two — same data, very different visual message.

⚠️ Misconception: "If the data is correct, the graph can't be misleading"
Correct data does not guarantee an honest visual impression. Truncating the y-axis (starting it well above zero) is a completely standard trick, sometimes deliberate and sometimes just careless, that exaggerates small differences. Whenever you evaluate a graph in this course — or in the group project's report — check the axis limits first, before drawing any conclusion about how large an effect looks.

Linear vs. logarithmic axes

The same nonlinear relationship, $y=x^2$, plotted three ways looks completely different: linear–linear (a curve, bowing upward), log–linear (still curved, since only one axis is compressed), and log–log (a straight line, because taking $\log$ of both sides of $y=x^2$ gives $\log y = 2\log x$, a linear relationship between $\log y$ and $\log x$). This is not just a plotting curiosity: relationships like light intensity vs. depth, or primary production rate vs. nutrient concentration, are exactly this kind of power-law/exponential shape, and oceanographers routinely re-plot them on log axes specifically to turn a curve into a diagnosable straight line.

Contour Plots & Iso-Lines

What is a contour plot?

A contour plot displays the two-dimensional spatial distribution of a variable (e.g., atmospheric pressure on a weather map). Sometimes that variable is itself a measurement in the third dimension, perpendicular to the map's surface, such as depth or height:

  • Topographic maps depict land-surface height above sea level.
  • Bathymetric maps depict water depth below sea level — the seafloor equivalent of a topographic map, and the primary way this course will present seafloor terrain.

"Iso" lines

Each contour line connects locations that share the same value of the plotted variable ("iso" means "equal"):

TermConnects points of equal…
Isobarspressure
Isothermstemperature
Isobathsdepth

Contour interval changes the picture, not the data

The same underlying dataset drawn with a smaller contour interval (more lines, finer detail) versus a larger interval (fewer lines, coarser detail) can look strikingly different, even though nothing about the physical field has changed. The rule for reading either: closely bunched contours indicate a strong gradient (the variable changes rapidly over a short distance); widely spaced contours indicate a weak gradient (the variable changes slowly).

Reading a seafloor contour map

Applying that gradient rule to interpret seafloor shape from contour spacing and shape alone:

Contour patternSeafloor feature
Widely-spaced, roughly parallel contours over a broad areaA flat plateau
Tightly bunched contours forming a narrow channelA steep-sided valley
Widely-spaced contours over a broad, gently changing areaAn area of gentle slope
Closed contours with values decreasing toward the center ("L")A depression (local low)
Tightly-closed contours with values increasing toward the center ("H")A hill/mountain (very steep if tightly bunched)
Bathymetric contour maps are the primary tool coastal and ocean engineers use to site infrastructure (pipelines, cables, offshore platforms, wind-turbine foundations) and to model wave shoaling and current steering around seafloor terrain — both of which depend directly on gradient (contour spacing), not just absolute depth.

Cross-Sectional Profiles & Vertical Exaggeration

Cross-sectional profiles

A cross-sectional profile shows how a property (e.g., dissolved oxygen concentration) is distributed along a vertical slice through the ocean, typically plotted as depth (increasing downward) against horizontal position along a transect line. These profiles are how oceanographers visualize layered ocean structure — the same "iso" contouring logic from above, just rotated into the vertical plane.

Why the ocean can't be plotted to scale

The ocean's maximum depth (about 11 km, at the Challenger Deep) is small compared to the width of ocean basins (typically thousands of km) — a depth-to-width ratio of roughly 1:100 or more. Plotted truly to scale, seafloor topography would look almost perfectly flat; essentially all interesting vertical structure would be visually invisible. Because of this, cross-sectional profiles almost always require vertical exaggeration: stretching the vertical (depth) axis relative to the horizontal axis so that terrain features become visible.

⚠️ Misconception: "A dramatic-looking seafloor profile means dramatic real-world slopes"
Almost every seafloor cross-section you will see in this course (and in the scientific literature generally) uses vertical exaggeration, often 10× to 100× or more. A profile showing what looks like steep, jagged mountains can correspond to real slopes of only a few degrees once you account for the true horizontal-to-vertical scale. Always check a profile's stated vertical exaggeration factor before judging how "steep" a real seafloor feature actually is.

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Disciplines
A researcher measures how dissolved nitrate concentration in seawater changes with depth and relates it to biological productivity. Which sub-discipline(s) of oceanography does this study span?
Show solution

Primarily chemical oceanography (dissolved nitrate is a dissolved solid/nutrient), with a direct link to biological oceanography (since the goal is relating that chemistry to biological productivity). This is a good example of the four disciplines overlapping in practice rather than being hard, separate boxes.

Problem 2 — Latitude/longitude conversion
Two ships are 5 minutes of latitude apart along the same meridian, near the equator. Approximately how far apart are they, in km? Would your answer change if they were near the North Pole instead?
Show solution

1 minute of latitude = 1 nautical mile = 1.85 km, and this conversion is constant everywhere on Earth (parallels are evenly spaced). So 5 minutes ≈ 5 × 1.85 km = 9.25 km, regardless of whether the ships are near the equator or near the pole — latitude's conversion factor does not depend on location, unlike longitude's.

Problem 3 — Choosing a projection
A ship's navigator needs a chart that preserves compass bearings exactly, for plotting a straight-line course. A climate scientist needs a world map that preserves the true relative area of ocean basins, for comparing their sizes by eye. Which projection should each use, and why?
Show solution

The navigator wants a Mercator projection — it has "excellent" direction preservation, which is exactly what's needed to plot a constant-bearing course, even though its area accuracy is poor. The climate scientist wants an equal-area projection with "excellent" area preservation, such as Miller's cylindrical or, accepting some shape distortion, Mollweide — not Mercator, whose area distortion at high latitudes would make basins like the Arctic Ocean look wildly larger than they really are.

Problem 4 — Reading contour spacing
On a bathymetric map, Region X has isobaths spaced far apart; Region Y (same contour interval) has isobaths packed tightly together. Which region has the steeper seafloor slope, and why?
Show solution

Region Y is steeper. With the same contour interval (the same depth change between adjacent lines), tightly bunched contours mean that depth change happens over a much shorter horizontal distance — a strong gradient, i.e., a steep slope. Widely spaced contours (Region X) mean the same depth change is spread over a longer horizontal distance, a gentle slope.

Problem 5 — Vertical exaggeration sanity check
A cross-sectional profile of an ocean basin, drawn with 100× vertical exaggeration, shows a seamount whose flank appears to rise at roughly 45° on the page. Is the real seafloor slope also about 45°? Explain.
Show solution

No. A 100× vertical exaggeration means the vertical axis is stretched 100 times more than the horizontal axis, so an apparent 45° slope on the page corresponds to a real-world slope whose true rise-over-run is 100 times smaller: $\tan(\theta_{\text{real}}) = \tan(45^\circ)/100 = 1/100$, giving $\theta_{\text{real}} = \arctan(0.01) \approx 0.57^\circ$ — a nearly flat slope in reality. This is exactly the misconception flagged above: always account for the stated exaggeration factor before judging real steepness from a profile.
