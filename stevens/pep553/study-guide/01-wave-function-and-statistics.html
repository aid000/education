Foundations Review (PEP242 Recap)

PEP553 assumes you've already met the experimental case for quantum behavior in introductory modern physics (PEP242). This subsection is a brisk refresher, not new material — if all of it is familiar, skim it and move on to The Wave Function.

The photoelectric effect

Shining light on a metal surface ejects electrons, but classical wave theory predicts the wrong dependence on intensity and frequency: it predicts that any frequency of light, given enough time or intensity, should eventually eject electrons, and that the ejected electrons' kinetic energy should grow with the light's intensity. Experimentally, neither is true. There is a sharp threshold frequency below which no electrons are ejected regardless of intensity, and above threshold the maximum kinetic energy of the ejected electrons grows with frequency, not intensity.

Einstein (1905) explained this by treating light as a stream of quanta ("photons"), each carrying energy $E = hf$, where $h$ is Planck's constant (introduced by Planck in 1900 to fix the blackbody spectrum). A single photon transfers all its energy to a single electron; part of that energy, the work function $\phi$, is spent freeing the electron from the metal, and the rest becomes kinetic energy:

$$K_{\max} = hf - \phi$$

This is a straight line when you plot $K_{\max}$ against $f$, with slope $h$ — exactly what Millikan's precision experiments confirmed. The photoelectric effect is the historical anchor for treating electromagnetic energy as quantized.

De Broglie's matter waves

If light — understood since Young and Maxwell as a wave — also behaves like a stream of particle-like quanta, de Broglie (1924) proposed the converse: matter, understood as particles, should also have a wave character. He assigned every particle of momentum $p$ a wavelength

$$\lambda = \frac{h}{p}$$

For a macroscopic object $\lambda$ is absurdly small and unobservable, but for an electron with a few eV of kinetic energy, $\lambda$ is comparable to atomic spacings — small enough that a crystal lattice can act as a diffraction grating for it.

Davisson & Germer: matter waves confirmed

Davisson and Germer (1927, Phys. Rev. 30, 705) fired low-energy electrons at a nickel crystal and found a diffraction pattern at exactly the angles de Broglie's formula predicted, using the atomic layers of the crystal as the "slits." This was the direct experimental confirmation that electrons — unambiguously particles in every other respect (they have charge, mass, and leave localized tracks) — produce interference and diffraction, the signature phenomena of waves.

Wave–particle duality

The modern statement of duality is sharper than "sometimes a wave, sometimes a particle": a single electron sent through a double slit, one at a time, still builds up the classic two-slit interference pattern, spot by spot, if you wait for enough of them to arrive. Each individual arrival is a localized, particle-like event (a single spot on the screen); the statistical distribution of many such events is wave-like (an interference pattern). Quantum mechanics does not resolve this by picking one description — it replaces both with a new object, the wave function, whose modulus squared predicts exactly this kind of statistical, blip-by-blip pattern. That is where Griffiths Chapter 1, and this course, begins.

The Wave Function & the Statistical Interpretation

The Schrödinger equation

In classical mechanics, the program is to find a particle's trajectory $x(t)$ by solving Newton's second law, $F = m\,d^2x/dt^2$, given initial position and velocity. Quantum mechanics asks a structurally similar question — given an initial condition, predict the future — but the object it solves for is not a trajectory. It is a complex-valued function of space and time, the wave function $\Psi(x,t)$, obtained by solving the Schrödinger equation:

$$i\hbar\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\,\Psi$$

Here $\hbar = h/2\pi = 1.054572\times10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}$, $m$ is the particle's mass, and $V(x)$ is the potential energy function. Given $\Psi(x,0)$, this equation determines $\Psi(x,t)$ for all future time, exactly as Newton's law determines $x(t)$ for all future time given $x(0)$ and $v(0)$. That analogy is useful for orientation, but the object being predicted, and what it means to "know" it, are both completely different from anything in classical mechanics.

Born's statistical interpretation

A position measurement always returns a single, localized outcome $x$ — that much is a fact about measurement. But before a measurement is made, quantum mechanics does not require the particle to already have had some definite position waiting to be revealed; the wave function, spread out over all $x$, is the complete description of the particle's state. How can such an object describe the state of a particle? Born's answer, the statistical interpretation, is that $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$ is a probability density for position: the probability of finding the particle between $x=a$ and $x=b$, if you measure its position at time $t$, is the area under $|\Psi|^2$ over that interval:

$$\int_a^b |\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx \;=\; \text{probability of finding the particle in } [a,b] \text{ at time } t$$

Two things follow immediately, and both are worth stating carefully because they are the seed of the first misconception below. First, $\Psi$ itself is generally complex and has no direct probabilistic meaning; it is $|\Psi|^2 = \Psi^*\Psi$, real and non-negative, that carries the probability. Second, this is a genuinely statistical statement: even with complete knowledge of $\Psi$, quantum mechanics cannot predict the outcome of a single position measurement, only the probabilities of the various possible outcomes. In the idealized limit of an exact projective measurement, if you measure the position and get some value $x_0$, an immediate second measurement will return $x_0$ again — the act of measurement is understood (in the orthodox, "Copenhagen" reading adopted throughout this course) to collapse the wave function to a narrow spike at $x_0$, which then spreads out again under the Schrödinger equation. Real measurements have finite resolution, and an exact position eigenstate (an infinitely narrow spike) is a non-normalizable mathematical idealization, not a state any real apparatus actually prepares.

⚠️ Misconception: "$\Psi$ is the probability"
$\Psi(x,t)$ is a complex number in general — it can be negative or have a nonzero imaginary part, and neither of those is a sensible probability. It is $|\Psi(x,t)|^2 = \Psi^*(x,t)\Psi(x,t)$, not $\Psi$ itself, that is the probability density. And "density" matters too: $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$ by itself is not a probability (it can exceed 1, since it has units of 1/length) — you must integrate it over a range of $x$ to get an actual probability. Watch for both errors: dropping the modulus-squared, and forgetting the $dx$.
The Tonomura group's electron-biprism experiments (1989) are the direct descendant of Davisson & Germer: send electrons through a two-path interferometer one at a time, and the individual, particle-like detection events accumulate, blip by blip, into the same fringe pattern predicted by treating the electron's wave function as a classical wave amplitude. Every modern electron microscope and electron diffractometer is built on the same physics — the instrument's resolution is set by the electron's de Broglie wavelength, not by the wavelength of visible light.

Probability: Discrete and Continuous

Because the statistical interpretation puts probability at the center of quantum mechanics, it is worth fixing the underlying probability-theory vocabulary carefully, first for a discrete variable and then for the continuous case that $x$ actually is.

Discrete distributions: mean, variance, standard deviation

For a discrete variable $j$ with $N(j)$ the number of occurrences of value $j$ out of a total $N$, the probability of getting $j$ is $P(j) = N(j)/N$, normalized so that $\sum_j P(j) = 1$. The average (or expectation value) of $j$, and more generally of any function $f(j)$, is

$$\langle j\rangle = \sum_j j\,P(j), \qquad \langle f(j)\rangle = \sum_j f(j)\,P(j)$$

The spread about the average is measured by the standard deviation $\sigma$, the square root of the variance $\sigma^2 \equiv \langle (\Delta j)^2\rangle$ where $\Delta j \equiv j - \langle j\rangle$. Expanding the square and using linearity of the average gives a much faster working formula, one you will use constantly:

$$\sigma^2 = \langle (\Delta j)^2\rangle = \langle j^2\rangle - 2\langle j\rangle\langle j\rangle + \langle j\rangle^2 = \langle j^2\rangle - \langle j\rangle^2$$

so that $\sigma = \sqrt{\langle j^2\rangle - \langle j\rangle^2}$. Since $\sigma^2 \ge 0$, this also proves $\langle j^2\rangle \ge \langle j\rangle^2$ always — equality only for a distribution with no spread at all. Note also that $\langle j^2\rangle \ne \langle j\rangle^2$ in general: the average of the squares is not the square of the average.

Continuous distributions

For a continuous variable $x$, the probability of getting exactly one real number is zero; the sensible object is a probability density $\rho(x)$, defined so that

$$\{\text{probability that } x \text{ lies between } x \text{ and } x+dx\} = \rho(x)\,dx$$

and the probability of landing in a finite interval is $P_{ab} = \int_a^b \rho(x)\,dx$. The discrete formulas above translate directly:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\rho(x)\,dx = 1, \qquad \langle x\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x\,\rho(x)\,dx, \qquad \langle f(x)\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} f(x)\,\rho(x)\,dx$$
$$\sigma^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2$$

A probability density can be arbitrarily large at a point (it can even diverge, as long as it stays integrable), but a probability itself is always between 0 and 1. For quantum mechanics, the density that matters is $\rho(x) = |\Psi(x,t)|^2$, and everything in this subsection carries over directly — that is the entire content of the next two sections.

Normalization

Why $\Psi$ must be normalized

Since $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$ is a probability density for position, and the particle has to be somewhere, integrating it over all space must give exactly 1:

$$\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} |\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = 1$$

This is called normalizing the wave function. It should bother you a little: the Schrödinger equation is linear, so if $\Psi(x,t)$ solves it, so does $A\Psi(x,t)$ for any constant $A$ — the equation itself doesn't fix the overall scale. Normalization is exactly the extra condition that pins down $|A|$ (its phase is never physically observable). If $\int|\Psi|^2 dx$ diverges, or if $\Psi \equiv 0$, no choice of $A$ can fix it; such non-normalizable solutions are rejected as unphysical. Normalizable physical states are square-integrable ($L^2$) solutions of the Schrödinger equation. Generalized eigenstates such as plane waves and position eigenkets are not normalizable in this sense — they're not physical states on their own, but they are still useful as basis/distributional objects (Topic 4 relies on exactly this kind of delta-normalized plane-wave basis for the free particle).

Normalization is preserved for all time (derivation)

Here is a question that has to be answered before the whole scheme is even consistent: suppose you normalize $\Psi$ at $t=0$. How do you know it stays normalized as $\Psi$ evolves under the Schrödinger equation? You cannot simply renormalize at every instant — the moment the normalization constant depends on $t$, you no longer have a solution of the (linear, first-order-in-time) Schrödinger equation at all. The resolution is that the Schrödinger equation, on its own, guarantees that a wave function normalized at $t=0$ remains normalized for every later time. Since the entire statistical interpretation collapses without this fact, it is worth deriving, not just asserting.

Start from the definition and differentiate under the integral sign (a total derivative on the left, since the integral only depends on $t$; a partial derivative on the right, since the integrand also depends on $x$):

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\Psi|^2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\Psi^*\Psi)\,dx = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t} + \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial t}\Psi\right)dx$$

Now use the Schrödinger equation, $\partial\Psi/\partial t = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi$, and its complex conjugate, $\partial\Psi^*/\partial t = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2} + \frac{i}{\hbar}V\Psi^*$ (valid because $V(x)$ is real). The potential-energy terms are proportional to $\Psi^*\Psi - \Psi^*\Psi = 0$ and cancel exactly, leaving only the kinetic term:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2 = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial^2\Psi}{\partial x^2} - \frac{\partial^2\Psi^*}{\partial x^2}\Psi\right) = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left[\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial \Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)\right]$$

(the last step is just the product rule for $\partial/\partial x$ run in reverse — check it by expanding the right side). This is an exact $x$-derivative, so the $x$-integral evaluates at the boundaries only:

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\Psi|^2\,dx = \left.\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)\right|_{-\infty}^{+\infty}$$

Being square-integrable does not, by itself, force a function to have a pointwise limit at infinity, or any particular pointwise decay rate — $L^2$ is a statement about an integral, not about pointwise behavior. What actually licenses dropping the boundary term is that physically reasonable wave functions (ones in the domain of $\hat H$, with well-behaved boundary/current conditions) have this probability-current-like term vanish at $x\to\pm\infty$; more generally, this whole calculation is just a special case of the fact that a self-adjoint Hamiltonian generates unitary, norm-preserving time evolution. For the well-behaved, localized wave packets used throughout this course, the boundary term vanishing is a safe and standard assumption — just don't mistake "square-integrable" alone for a proof of it. Granting that, the boundary term vanishes and

$$\frac{d}{dt}\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx = 0$$

The integral is therefore constant in time: if it equals 1 at $t=0$, it equals 1 forever. $\blacksquare$

⚠️ Misconception: "Normalization is a fact you check once, at $t=0$, and hope for the best"
It's the reverse: normalization is a theorem about the Schrödinger equation, not a lucky coincidence you have to re-verify at every later time. You only ever need to normalize $\Psi$ once, at whatever instant you're given the initial condition — the derivation above guarantees $\int|\Psi|^2\,dx$ never changes afterward, precisely because $\Psi$ evolves under the Schrödinger equation. If you ever compute $\int|\Psi(x,t)|^2 dx$ at some later $t$ and get something other than 1, that's a sign of an algebra error, not a sign that you need to renormalize.

Worked example: normalizing a triangular wave packet

Take the piecewise-linear ("triangular pulse") wave function at $t=0$:

$$\Psi(x,0) = \begin{cases} A(x/a), & 0 \le x \le a \\ A(b-x)/(b-a), & a \le x \le b \\ 0, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

with $0 < a < b$ and $A$ real and positive. This is a "tent" that rises linearly from 0 at $x=0$ to a peak $A$ at $x=a$, then falls linearly back to 0 at $x=b$.

Step 1 — normalize. Since $\Psi$ is real here, $|\Psi|^2 = \Psi^2$, and

$$1 = \int_0^a A^2\frac{x^2}{a^2}\,dx + \int_a^b A^2\frac{(b-x)^2}{(b-a)^2}\,dx$$

The first integral is $A^2 a/3$ (standard $\int x^2 dx$ evaluated over $[0,a]$, divided by $a^2$). For the second, substitute $u = b-x$, $du=-dx$, limits $u:(b-a)\to 0$, giving $\int_0^{b-a} A^2 u^2/(b-a)^2\,du = A^2(b-a)/3$. So

$$1 = \frac{A^2 a}{3} + \frac{A^2(b-a)}{3} = \frac{A^2 b}{3} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = \sqrt{\frac{3}{b}}$$

Notice the peak height $A$ depends only on $b$, not on where the peak $a$ sits — a useful sanity check, since stretching the base of the tent while keeping its area (probability) fixed at 1 should lower its height, and $A=\sqrt{3/b}$ does exactly that.

Step 2 — most likely position. $|\Psi(x,0)|^2$ is maximized where $\Psi(x,0)$ itself is maximized (since squaring a non-negative real quantity preserves its ordering), which is right at the peak of the tent, $x=a$.

Expectation Values, Operators, and Momentum

$\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle x^2\rangle$

Directly from the statistical interpretation, with $\rho(x,t) = |\Psi(x,t)|^2$:

$$\langle x\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x\,|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx, \qquad \langle x^2\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty} x^2\,|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx$$

It is essential to understand what $\langle x\rangle$ does and does not mean. It is not the average result of repeated position measurements on one particle — the first measurement collapses $\Psi$ to a spike, and any immediate repeat measurement just returns the same value. Rather, $\langle x\rangle$ is the average over measurements performed on an ensemble of identically-prepared particles, each described by the same $\Psi$: prepare many particles in state $\Psi$, measure the position of each one exactly once, and average the results. That average is $\langle x\rangle$.

Deriving the momentum operator

Position measurements are conceptually straightforward: $|\Psi|^2$ already tells you what you need. Momentum is trickier, because nothing so far has told us how to extract a probability distribution for $p$ directly from $\Psi(x,t)$. Griffiths' route (which this course follows) is to postulate that the expectation value of velocity equals the time derivative of the expectation value of position, $\langle v\rangle = d\langle x\rangle/dt$, and then compute the right-hand side explicitly.

Differentiate $\langle x\rangle$ under the integral sign, using the same product-rule trick as the normalization proof above (only now the integrand carries an extra factor of $x$, and after invoking the Schrödinger equation you must integrate by parts to move derivatives off of $x$):

$$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \int x\,\frac{\partial}{\partial t}|\Psi|^2\,dx = \frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int x\,\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)dx$$

Integrating by parts once (peel the $\partial/\partial x$ off the bracket onto $x$, picking up a minus sign and using $\partial x/\partial x = 1$; the boundary term vanishes because $\Psi\to0$ at $\pm\infty$):

$$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = -\frac{i\hbar}{2m}\int\left(\Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial\Psi^*}{\partial x}\Psi\right)dx$$

Integrating by parts a second time, on just the second term (peel the derivative off $\Psi^*$ back onto $\Psi$; another vanishing boundary term), the two terms combine into one:

$$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}\int \Psi^*\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\,dx$$

Multiplying by $m$ (customary since physicists work with momentum $p=mv$ rather than velocity) defines the expectation value of momentum:

$$\langle p\rangle = m\,\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = -i\hbar\int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Psi^*\,\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}\,dx$$

Writing $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle p\rangle$ side by side makes the pattern obvious:

$$\langle x\rangle = \int\Psi^*\,[x]\,\Psi\,dx, \qquad \langle p\rangle = \int\Psi^*\,\left[-i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\right]\Psi\,dx$$

Position is "represented" by the operator $\hat x = x$ (multiply by $x$); momentum is represented by the operator $\hat p = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x$ (differentiate, then multiply by $-i\hbar$). Every classical dynamical quantity $Q(x,p)$ — kinetic energy $T=p^2/2m$, angular momentum, anything built from $x$ and $p$ alone (not mixing the two) — is promoted to an operator by this same replacement, giving the general recipe used throughout this course:

$$\langle Q(x,p)\rangle = \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}\Psi^*\,\Big[Q\big(x,\,-i\hbar\,\partial/\partial x\big)\Big]\,\Psi\,dx$$

This replacement is unambiguous for every $Q$ used in this course, but it's worth flagging the general caveat: because $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ don't commute, a classical expression that mixes $x$ and $p$ in the same term (e.g. a classical $xp$) doesn't have a unique quantum operator — "$\hat x\hat p$" and "$\hat p\hat x$" are different (non-Hermitian) operators, and a common Hermitian choice is the symmetrized $\tfrac12(\hat x\hat p+\hat p\hat x)$. Canonical quantization is not, in general, a unique map from an arbitrary classical function to an operator; it's unambiguous here because every $Q(x,p)$ this course actually uses keeps the $x$-dependent and $p$-dependent factors cleanly separated.

⚓ Memory Anchor: the two basic operators

$\hat x = x$ (position: multiply). $\hat p = \dfrac{\hbar}{i}\dfrac{d}{dx} = -i\hbar\dfrac{d}{dx}$ (momentum: differentiate, then multiply by $-i\hbar$). To get $\langle Q\rangle$ for any quantity built from $x$ and $p$, "sandwich" the corresponding operator between $\Psi^*$ and $\Psi$ and integrate: $\langle Q\rangle = \int \Psi^*\,\hat Q\,\Psi\,dx$.

Worked example: $\langle p\rangle$ for a Gaussian wave packet

Consider a normalized Gaussian modulated by a plane-wave phase (this exact form reappears as the prototypical free-particle wave packet in Topic 4):

$$\Psi(x) = A\,e^{-x^2/(4\sigma^2)}\,e^{ik_0 x}$$

Normalize. $|\Psi(x)|^2 = A^2 e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}$ (the phase factor $e^{ik_0x}$ has modulus 1 and drops out of $|\Psi|^2$). Using the standard Gaussian integral $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}e^{-x^2/(2\sigma^2)}dx = \sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2}$:

$$1 = A^2\sqrt{2\pi\sigma^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad A = (2\pi\sigma^2)^{-1/4}$$

$\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle x^2\rangle$. $|\Psi|^2$ is an even, symmetric Gaussian centered at $x=0$, so $\langle x\rangle = 0$ by symmetry (the integrand $x\,|\Psi|^2$ is odd), and the standard second moment of this Gaussian gives $\langle x^2\rangle = \sigma^2$.

$\langle p\rangle$. First differentiate: $\dfrac{d\Psi}{dx} = \left[-\dfrac{x}{2\sigma^2} + ik_0\right]\Psi$, so

$$\hat p\,\Psi = -i\hbar\frac{d\Psi}{dx} = -i\hbar\left[-\frac{x}{2\sigma^2}+ik_0\right]\Psi = \left[\frac{i\hbar x}{2\sigma^2} + \hbar k_0\right]\Psi$$

Then

$$\langle p\rangle = \int \Psi^*\,\hat p\,\Psi\,dx = \int |\Psi|^2\left[\frac{i\hbar x}{2\sigma^2}+\hbar k_0\right]dx = \frac{i\hbar}{2\sigma^2}\underbrace{\int x\,|\Psi|^2\,dx}_{=\,\langle x\rangle\,=\,0} + \hbar k_0\underbrace{\int|\Psi|^2 dx}_{=\,1}$$
$$\boxed{\langle p\rangle = \hbar k_0}$$

This example is worth studying carefully because it is a compact illustration of exactly the kind of bookkeeping error flagged in the misconception below: the differentiation manufactures an explicitly imaginary term, $i\hbar x/(2\sigma^2)$, inside the integrand, yet the final answer $\hbar k_0$ is perfectly real. The imaginary piece only vanishes because it is odd in $x$ while $|\Psi|^2$ is even — if you'd made a sign error and gotten an even piece multiplying $i$, you'd have (wrongly) concluded $\langle p\rangle$ has a nonzero imaginary part, which is impossible for a real, physically measurable quantity.

⚠️ Misconception: sign and complex-conjugate slips in $\langle p\rangle$
Two errors show up constantly in $\langle p\rangle$ calculations. (1) Forgetting to conjugate the left factor: the integrand is $\Psi^*\,\hat p\,\Psi$, not $\Psi\,\hat p\,\Psi$ — if $\Psi$ has any nontrivial phase (as in the worked example above), using $\Psi$ instead of $\Psi^*$ silently changes the answer. (2) Sign errors in $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$: it is easy to drop the minus sign, or to differentiate $\Psi^*$ instead of $\Psi$. A fast self-check: since $\langle p\rangle$ is the expectation value of something physically measurable, it must always come out real. If your calculation produces a nonzero imaginary part for $\langle p\rangle$ (or for any $\langle Q\rangle$ built from a real dynamical variable $Q$), you have made an algebra error — go back and check the conjugates and signs before doing anything else.
Scanning tunneling and atomic-force microscopy read out exactly this trade-off in practice: the cantilever tip's wave function has to be localized enough to resolve individual atoms (small $\sigma_x$), but the same localization forces a large spread in momentum, which shows up as measurement noise and back-action on the tip. Designing a quantum sensor is, in large part, an exercise in managing where you're willing to pay the $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ price — the subject of the next section.

The Heisenberg Uncertainty Principle

Statement and intuition

Picture shaking one end of a very long rope rhythmically: the resulting wave has a well-defined wavelength (you can point to "about 6 feet between crests") but no well-defined position — it's spread out over the whole rope. Now give the rope a single sharp jerk instead: you get a narrow, well localized bump, but asking "what is its wavelength?" no longer makes sense — it isn't even vaguely periodic. There is an unavoidable trade-off between how sharply a wave's position can be pinned down and how sharply its wavelength can be pinned down; a rigorous version of this statement is a theorem of Fourier analysis, but the qualitative content is exactly this everyday picture.

The wavelength of $\Psi$ is tied to momentum through the de Broglie relation, $p = h/\lambda$ (Foundations Review, above), so a spread in wavelength is a spread in momentum. Position/wavelength trade-off therefore becomes a position/momentum trade-off, quantified by Heisenberg's uncertainty principle:

$$\sigma_x\,\sigma_p \ge \frac{\hbar}{2}$$

where $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_p$ are the standard deviations defined exactly as in the Probability section, computed from the same $\Psi$. This is an inequality, not an equation: there is no lower bound on how large both $\sigma_x$ and $\sigma_p$ can be simultaneously (a "long wiggly line with lots of bumps and potholes and no periodic structure" has both badly spread out), but there is a floor on how small their product can be. Localize $\Psi$ tightly in $x$ (a narrow spike) and $\sigma_p$ is forced to blow up; prepare a state with sharply defined momentum (a long sinusoidal wave) and $\sigma_x$ is forced to blow up.

It is important to be precise about what the "spread" $\sigma_x$ (or $\sigma_p$) means here, because it is easy to smuggle in a classical picture. It does not mean that any single position (or momentum) measurement is imprecise or fuzzy — an individual measurement of either quantity returns a sharp, definite number. $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$ is a statement about the ensemble: measure the position of many identically-prepared particles (all described by the same $\Psi$) and you get a spread of results with standard deviation $\sigma_x$; measure the momentum of an equally-large, identically-prepared ensemble and you get a spread $\sigma_p$. The inequality says these two spreads cannot both be made arbitrarily small for the same $\Psi$.

⚓ Memory Anchor: where the proof is coming from

The qualitative Fourier argument above is enough for this topic and for testing the inequality numerically on the wave functions in Topic 2 (the infinite square well). The full, general proof — via the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality applied to $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ as Hermitian operators, together with their commutator $[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar$ — is deferred to Topic 6: Formal Theory & Matrix Methods, once you have the Hilbert-space machinery (Hermitian operators, inner products) to do it properly.

Gravitational-wave interferometers like LIGO operate right up against a quantum-mechanical floor set by exactly this trade-off, the "standard quantum limit": squeezing the light field's phase uncertainty (position-like) to measure a tiny mirror displacement more precisely necessarily increases the amplitude (momentum-like) uncertainty that feeds back onto the mirror as radiation-pressure noise. LIGO's squeezed-light upgrades are, quite literally, an engineering choice about how to divide up $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ between two conjugate quadratures rather than a way of evading the inequality.

Practice Problems

Practice 1 — Normalize a decaying-exponential wave function, then find $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle x^2\rangle$, and $\sigma_x$

Problem: A particle is described (at some fixed time) by $\Psi(x) = A\,e^{-\lambda|x|}$, with $A$ and $\lambda$ real, positive constants. Find $A$, then $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle x^2\rangle$, and $\sigma_x$.

Solution: Since $|x|$ makes this even, split the normalization integral at 0 and double the positive half:

$$1 = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty}A^2 e^{-2\lambda|x|}dx = 2A^2\int_0^{\infty}e^{-2\lambda x}dx = 2A^2\cdot\frac{1}{2\lambda} = \frac{A^2}{\lambda}$$

so $A = \sqrt{\lambda}$.

$\langle x\rangle$: the integrand $x\,|\Psi|^2 = x\,A^2 e^{-2\lambda|x|}$ is odd (odd function $x$ times an even function of $x$), and it is integrated over a symmetric interval $(-\infty,\infty)$, so $\langle x\rangle = 0$ with no further calculation needed. (This is the fast, reliable way to spot $\langle x\rangle=0$ — always check for odd/even symmetry before grinding through an integral.)

$\langle x^2\rangle$: now the integrand is even, so double the positive half and integrate by parts twice (or look up $\int_0^\infty x^2 e^{-2\lambda x}dx = 2/(2\lambda)^3 = 1/(4\lambda^3)$):

$$\langle x^2\rangle = 2A^2\int_0^{\infty}x^2 e^{-2\lambda x}dx = 2\lambda\cdot\frac{1}{4\lambda^3} = \frac{1}{2\lambda^2}$$

So $\sigma_x = \sqrt{\langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2} = \sqrt{1/(2\lambda^2)} = \dfrac{1}{\lambda\sqrt2}$.

Practice 2 — Why does a real, even wave function always give $\langle p\rangle = 0$?

Problem: Using the same $\Psi(x) = \sqrt{\lambda}\,e^{-\lambda|x|}$ from Practice 1, show that $\langle p\rangle = 0$, and explain in words why this had to be true before you even computed it.

Solution: $\langle p\rangle = -i\hbar\int\Psi^*\,\dfrac{d\Psi}{dx}\,dx$. Because $\Psi$ is real, $\Psi^* = \Psi$, so the integrand is $-i\hbar\,\Psi\,\dfrac{d\Psi}{dx} = -\dfrac{i\hbar}{2}\dfrac{d}{dx}(\Psi^2)$ — an exact derivative. Integrating,

$$\langle p\rangle = -\frac{i\hbar}{2}\Big[\Psi(x)^2\Big]_{-\infty}^{+\infty} = 0$$

since $\Psi\to0$ at $\pm\infty$. More generally: any real wave function has $\langle p\rangle = 0$, because the argument above never used the specific form of $\Psi$, only that it is real and vanishes at infinity. This is a fast diagnostic: if you're asked for $\langle p\rangle$ and notice the given $\Psi$ is purely real, you can write down the answer, 0, immediately — and if your detailed integration produces anything else, that's a sign to recheck your work, not a sign that the shortcut failed.

Practice 3 — Is it normalizable? (boundary behavior at infinity)

Problem: Which of the following can be normalized wave functions on $(-\infty,\infty)$, and why? (a) $\Psi_1(x) = A/(1+x^2)$; (b) $\Psi_2(x) = A\,x\,e^{-x^2}$; (c) $\Psi_3(x) = A/(1+|x|)$; (d) $\Psi_4(x) = A\sin(kx)$ for real constant $k\ne0$.

Solution: A wave function is normalizable exactly when $\int|\Psi|^2 dx$ converges to a finite value, which requires $|\Psi|^2$ to fall off faster than $1/|x|$ at large $|x|$ (so that $\int^{\infty} |\Psi|^2 dx$ converges — recall $\int^\infty dx/x^p$ converges only for $p>1$).

  • (a) Yes. $|\Psi_1|^2 = A^2/(1+x^2)^2 \sim A^2/x^4$ for large $|x|$, which falls off fast enough; $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}dx/(1+x^2)^2$ is a standard finite integral.
  • (b) Yes. The Gaussian factor $e^{-2x^2}$ in $|\Psi_2|^2$ beats any power of $x$ to zero, so the integral converges regardless of the extra factor of $x^2$ out front.
  • (c) No. $|\Psi_3|^2 = A^2/(1+|x|)^2 \sim A^2/x^2$ for large $|x|$, and $\int^{\infty} dx/x^2$ converges — wait, check carefully: $p=2>1$ here, so this integral does converge, and $\Psi_3$ is normalizable. (This item is deliberately here to test the boundary case: it is easy to eyeball $1/(1+|x|)$ as "the same as" $1/(1+x^2)$ and get the wrong exponent — always identify the actual power-law falloff of $|\Psi|^2$, not of $\Psi$, before judging convergence.)
  • (d) No. $|\Psi_4|^2 = A^2\sin^2(kx)$ does not decay at all as $x\to\pm\infty$ — it oscillates between 0 and $A^2$ forever, so $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|\Psi_4|^2 dx$ diverges for any $A\ne0$. A pure sinusoid (a state of perfectly sharp momentum, per the uncertainty principle) is never normalizable on the whole real line; it can only appear as an idealization, or restricted to a finite box (Topic 2).
Practice 4 — Minimum momentum spread from a given position spread

Problem: An electron is confined so that a position measurement is known to have standard deviation $\sigma_x = 1.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{m}$ (roughly one Bohr radius). What is the smallest possible standard deviation in a momentum measurement, and how does the corresponding minimum kinetic energy ($\langle p\rangle=0$ so $\langle p^2\rangle = \sigma_p^2$, giving $\langle T\rangle = \sigma_p^2/2m$) compare to typical atomic binding energies (a few eV)?

Solution: From $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$, the smallest allowed $\sigma_p$ is

$$\sigma_p^{\min} = \frac{\hbar}{2\sigma_x} = \frac{1.055\times10^{-34}\ \text{J·s}}{2(1.0\times10^{-10}\ \text{m})} \approx 5.3\times10^{-25}\ \text{kg·m/s}$$

Taking $\langle p\rangle = 0$ for a bound, symmetric state so that $\sigma_p^2 = \langle p^2\rangle$, the associated kinetic energy is

$$\langle T\rangle_{\min} = \frac{\sigma_p^2}{2m_e} = \frac{(5.3\times10^{-25})^2}{2(9.11\times10^{-31}\ \text{kg})} \approx 1.5\times10^{-19}\ \text{J} \approx 1\ \text{eV}$$

This lands right in the same range as the hydrogen ground-state binding energy (13.6 eV) — not a coincidence. The uncertainty principle alone, with no dynamics beyond "confine an electron to about an atomic radius," already forces a kinetic energy of order an eV or more. This is exactly why atoms don't collapse: squeezing the electron closer to the nucleus (smaller $\sigma_x$) would only drive $\langle T\rangle_{\min}$ up faster than the Coulomb attraction can compensate. You'll make this qualitative argument precise in Topic 8 (hydrogen atom).

Common Pitfalls, Self-Check, and Look Ahead

📝 Common Pitfalls & Self-Check

This is general, instructor-style guidance about where students typically lose points on wave-function/normalization/expectation-value problems — it is not a record of graded feedback on any specific homework (see the note in Overview).

  • Normalizing $\Psi$ instead of $|\Psi|^2$. The normalization integral is $\int|\Psi|^2 dx = \int \Psi^*\Psi\,dx$, not $\int\Psi\,dx$. If $\Psi$ is complex, skipping the complex conjugate is invisible in your algebra until the final numeric answer looks wrong (or worse, looks plausible but is subtly incorrect).
  • Forgetting the piecewise domain when integrating. Wave functions defined in pieces (like the triangular pulse above) require splitting every integral at each boundary point. A shockingly common error is integrating only one piece over the full domain, or using the wrong piece's formula past its boundary.
  • Sign errors from $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$. The minus sign and the factor of $i$ both matter. As emphasized in the Expectation Values section, any physical expectation value must come out real — use that as a running check on every $\langle p\rangle$ or $\langle p^2\rangle$ calculation.
  • Dropping boundary terms carelessly (or keeping them when they shouldn't survive). Every derivation in this topic (normalization is time-independent; the momentum operator) relies on an integration by parts whose boundary term vanishes because $\Psi\to0$ at $\pm\infty$. State that reasoning explicitly rather than silently dropping boundary terms — and remember it is not automatic for wave functions confined to a finite region with hard walls (Topic 2), where the boundary conditions are different and have to be handled directly.
  • Confusing $\langle x^2\rangle$ with $\langle x\rangle^2$. These are different numbers in general, and $\langle x^2\rangle \ge \langle x\rangle^2$ always. If you ever compute $\sigma_x^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 < 0$, you have made an arithmetic error somewhere upstream — a negative variance is not possible.
  • Treating the uncertainty principle as a statement about measurement disturbance. $\sigma_x\sigma_p\ge\hbar/2$ is a property of the state $\Psi$ (specifically, of its Fourier transform), not a claim that measuring position clumsily kicks the momentum. Save the "disturbance" language for informal intuition only; the precise statement is about the spread of an ensemble of identically-prepared measurements, as emphasized above.

Look Ahead

Everything built here — the statistical interpretation of $|\Psi|^2$, normalization (and its preservation in time), the operator recipe $\langle Q\rangle = \int\Psi^*\hat Q\,\Psi\,dx$, and the uncertainty principle — is the toolkit every later topic in this course applies to specific physical systems. The very next topic, the infinite square well, is the first place you'll use this toolkit on a genuine bound-state problem: you'll solve the time-independent Schrödinger equation subject to boundary conditions, normalize the resulting stationary states using exactly the techniques above, and check that the ground state's $\sigma_x\sigma_p$ is consistent with the inequality derived here. From there, the harmonic oscillator, free-particle wave packets, and eventually the full formal (Hilbert-space) theory in Topic 6 all build directly on the definitions of $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, and normalization established in this topic. Nothing here gets thrown away later — it gets reused, over and over, on harder potentials.
