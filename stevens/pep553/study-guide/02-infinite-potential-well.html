Overview: From the Time-Dependent Equation to Quantized Energy Levels

Topic 1 built the statistical machinery of quantum mechanics: the wave function $\Psi(x,t)$, the Born rule $|\Psi(x,t)|^2$ as a probability density, normalization, and the operator recipe $\langle Q(x,p)\rangle = \int \Psi^* \hat{Q}\,\Psi\,dx$ for computing expectation values. What we never did was actually solve for $\Psi(x,t)$. That's the job of this topic.

This page covers Syllabus Lectures 4-1 through 5-2 (course module: "The Time Independent Schrödinger Equation and the Infinite Potential Well"), corresponding to Homework #2. The roadmap:

  • Separation of variables turns the time-dependent Schrödinger equation (TDSE) into two ordinary differential equations — one in $t$, one in $x$. The spatial equation is the time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE).
  • Solutions of the TISE are stationary states — states of definite, sharply-defined energy.
  • We solve the TISE explicitly for the simplest possible potential: the infinite square well (particle in a box), deriving the eigenfunctions $\varphi_n(x)$ and allowed energies $E_n$ from the boundary conditions — not just stating them.
  • We prove normalization and orthogonality of the $\varphi_n$, then use them to compute $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, and $\langle H\rangle$ — both for a single stationary state and for a superposition of two.
  • An advanced example shows how any initial wave function, confined to a well, can be expanded in the energy eigenbasis using Fourier's trick — the seed of the general superposition principle that Topic 4 (Free Particle & Wave Packets) formalizes as $\Psi(x,t) = \sum_n c_n \psi_n(x) e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$.
📌 Midterm Alert

The infinite square well is core midterm material. The midterm review handout explicitly asks students to compute expectation values of position, momentum, and energy for a particle in a box — both in a single stationary state and in a superposition of two. Deriving the Time-Independent Schrödinger Equation

Starting Point: The Full Time-Dependent Schrödinger Equation

Everything in non-relativistic quantum mechanics (in one dimension, for a time-independent potential $V(x)$) starts from

$$ i\hbar \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial x^2} + V(x)\,\Psi. $$

This is a partial differential equation in two variables, $x$ and $t$. The physicist's first line of attack on any PDE is separation of variables: look for solutions that are products of a function of $x$ alone and a function of $t$ alone,

$$ \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,\varphi(t). $$

This looks like a drastic restriction — surely most solutions aren't simple products. But it turns out that the separable solutions we find here can be linearly combined to build every solution to the full time-dependent equation, so nothing is lost.

Substituting and Separating

Because $\Psi$ is a product, the partial derivatives become ordinary derivatives acting on one factor at a time:

$$ \frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = \psi\,\frac{d\varphi}{dt}, \qquad \frac{\partial^2 \Psi}{\partial x^2} = \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}\,\varphi. $$

Substituting into the Schrödinger equation:

$$ i\hbar\,\psi\,\frac{d\varphi}{dt} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2}\,\varphi + V\psi\varphi. $$

Divide both sides by $\psi\varphi$ (legitimate everywhere $\Psi \neq 0$):

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{1}{\varphi}\frac{d\varphi}{dt} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{\psi}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x). $$

Now look carefully at what's on each side. The left side is a function of $t$ alone. The right side is a function of $x$ alone (this step requires $V$ to depend only on $x$, not on $t$ — exactly our assumption). But $x$ and $t$ are independent variables: I can hold $x$ fixed and vary $t$, or vice versa. The only way a function of $t$ alone can equal a function of $x$ alone, for all values of both, is if both sides equal the same constant. Call that constant $E$ (the choice of letter will be justified in the next section — it turns out to be the energy).

Two Ordinary Differential Equations

Setting the left side equal to $E$:

$$ i\hbar\,\frac{1}{\varphi}\frac{d\varphi}{dt} = E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d\varphi}{dt} = -\frac{iE}{\hbar}\varphi. $$

This first-order equation integrates immediately (separate variables, integrate, exponentiate). Absorbing the integration constant into $\psi$ (since only the product $\psi\varphi$ matters), the normalized solution is

$$ \varphi(t) = e^{-iEt/\hbar}. $$

Setting the right side equal to $E$ and rearranging gives the spatial equation:

$$ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{1}{\psi}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x) = E \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} + V(x)\psi = E\psi.} $$

This boxed equation is the time-independent Schrödinger equation (TISE). A single second-order PDE has become a first-order ODE in $t$ (already solved, above) and a second-order ODE in $x$ that we still have to solve — separately, for every potential $V(x)$ we care about. The rest of this course is largely a tour of specific choices of $V(x)$: the infinite well (this page), the harmonic oscillator (Topic 3), the finite well and barriers (Topic 5), and the hydrogen atom (Topic 8).

Stationary States

Why "Stationary"? Probabilities Don't Move

A separable solution has the form

$$ \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,e^{-iEt/\hbar}. $$

Although $\Psi$ itself obviously depends on $t$, the probability density does not:

$$ |\Psi(x,t)|^2 = \Psi^*\Psi = \psi^* e^{+iEt/\hbar}\,\psi\, e^{-iEt/\hbar} = |\psi(x)|^2. $$

The two exponentials — the "wiggle factor" — cancel exactly, because $E$ is real (see Practice Problem territory: this requires proving $E$ can't have an imaginary part, which follows from demanding $\int|\Psi|^2dx$ stay finite and equal to 1 for all $t$). The same cancellation happens inside any expectation-value integral built from an operator with no explicit time dependence, since the recipe $\langle Q(x,p)\rangle = \int \Psi^*[Q(x,-i\hbar\,d/dx)]\Psi\,dx$ always pairs a $\Psi^*$ with a $\Psi$ (an operator that is itself explicitly time-dependent would not have this cancellation — but none of the operators used in this course are). So every such expectation value is constant in time. In particular $\langle x \rangle$ is constant, and hence (recall $\langle p \rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$ from Topic 1) $\langle p \rangle = 0$ identically — for this specific 1D system. Bound states of a real 1D potential can always be chosen real, which forces the probability current to vanish identically, everywhere, not just $\langle p\rangle=0$ on average. That stronger statement doesn't generalize to every "stationary state" in every system, though: Topic 8's hydrogen eigenstates with $m\ne0$ are genuinely complex (there's no way to choose them real, because $L_z$ eigenstates are complex by construction) and carry a steady, nonzero circulating probability current even though $|\Psi|^2$ is still time-independent. "Stationary" always means $|\Psi|^2$ doesn't change — it doesn't always mean the probability current is zero everywhere.

⚓ Memory Anchor: Nothing Ever Happens in a Stationary State

If a problem gives you a single $\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ and asks how $\langle x \rangle$ or $\langle p \rangle$ changes in time, the answer is always "it doesn't" — full stop, no calculation needed. Motion only appears once you combine at least two stationary states with different energies (Section 6 below works this out explicitly).

Definite Energy: the Hamiltonian Eigenvalue Equation

In classical mechanics the total energy (kinetic + potential) is called the Hamiltonian, $H(x,p) = p^2/2m + V(x)$. Applying the canonical substitution $p \to -i\hbar\,d/dx$ gives the Hamiltonian operator,

$$ \hat{H} = -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2}{dx^2} + V(x), $$

and the TISE can be rewritten compactly as an eigenvalue equation:

$$ \hat{H}\psi = E\psi. $$

Because $\hat H \psi = E\psi$, we get $\langle H \rangle = \int \psi^* \hat H \psi\,dx = E\int|\psi|^2dx = E$, and likewise $\hat H^2 \psi = E^2\psi$ so $\langle H^2\rangle = E^2$. The variance of the energy is therefore

$$ \sigma_H^2 = \langle H^2\rangle - \langle H \rangle^2 = E^2 - E^2 = 0. $$

Zero variance means every single measurement of the energy of a particle in a stationary state returns exactly $E$ — not "on average," but with 100% certainty. This is why $E$ deserved to be called the energy from the moment it appeared as the separation constant.

A Preview: Linear Combinations Produce Motion

The TISE is a second-order ODE, so for a given potential it typically yields an infinite family of solutions $\{\psi_n(x)\}$, each with its own allowed energy $\{E_n\}$ — this is exactly what happens for the infinite well below. Because the full TDSE is linear, any combination $\Psi(x,t) = \sum_n c_n \psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$ is also a solution, and it turns out every solution can be written this way for the right choice of constants $c_n$. Such a combination is generally not stationary: the different energies mean the wiggle factors no longer cancel in $|\Psi|^2$, and probabilities genuinely shift with time. Topic 4 develops this superposition principle in full generality; Section 6 below gives you a concrete, fully-worked two-state example for the infinite well right now, because it's exam-relevant material.

The Infinite Square Well: Full Derivation

Setting Up the Potential

The infinite square well (a.k.a. "particle in a box") is the simplest non-trivial potential in quantum mechanics. Using the asymmetric convention (well spans $0$ to $a$ — this is the convention used throughout this page, the homework, and the midterm review sheet; see the Section 8 misconception box for the alternative convention):

$$ V(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & 0 \le x \le a \\ \infty, & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases} $$

Physically: the particle is completely free inside the well, but an infinite force confines it — it can never be found at $x<0$ or $x>a$. Outside the well, $\psi(x) = 0$ identically (the particle simply cannot access that region, no matter how the wave function behaves inside).

Solving Inside the Well

Where $V=0$, the TISE reduces to

$$ -\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = -k^2\psi, \qquad k \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}. $$

(One can show — see Practice Problem 2.2-style arguments — that $E \ge 0$ is required for any normalizable solution, so $k$ is real and this rewriting is legitimate.) This is exactly the classical simple-harmonic-oscillator equation, whose general solution is

$$ \psi(x) = A\sin(kx) + B\cos(kx), $$

with $A$ and $B$ arbitrary constants to be pinned down by boundary conditions.

Applying Boundary Conditions: Quantization Emerges

Ordinarily both $\psi$ and $d\psi/dx$ must be continuous across a boundary. But where the potential itself jumps to infinity, only continuity of $\psi$ survives as a legitimate condition (the general justification, and the exception's origin, belongs to the boundary-condition analysis in Topic 5 — Finite Well & Barriers). Since $\psi = 0$ just outside the well on both sides, continuity requires

$$ \psi(0) = \psi(a) = 0. $$

Left boundary: $\psi(0) = A\sin 0 + B\cos 0 = B$, so continuity forces $B = 0$. The solution simplifies to

$$ \psi(x) = A\sin(kx). $$

Right boundary: $\psi(a) = A\sin(ka) = 0$. Either $A=0$ (the trivial, non-normalizable $\psi \equiv 0$ — rejected), or

$$ \sin(ka) = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad ka = 0, \pm\pi, \pm2\pi, \pm3\pi, \ldots $$

$k=0$ is rejected (again gives $\psi\equiv0$), and negative values of $k$ give nothing new since $\sin(-\theta)=-\sin\theta$ just flips the sign of $A$, which we can absorb. So the distinct allowed wavenumbers are

$$ k_n = \frac{n\pi}{a}, \qquad n = 1,2,3,\ldots $$

This is the heart of quantum mechanics right here: the boundary condition at $x=a$ didn't fix the amplitude $A$ — it fixed which values of $k$, and hence which energies, are even allowed. Quantization is a consequence of confinement, not an extra assumption bolted on.

The Allowed Energies

Since $k = \sqrt{2mE}/\hbar$, solving for $E$ at each allowed $k_n$ gives the quantized energy spectrum:

$$ E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 k_n^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots $$

In radical contrast to a classical particle bouncing in a box (which can have any non-negative energy), a quantum particle confined to this well can only have one of these special discrete values. The $n=1$ state is the ground state (lowest energy, $E_1=\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2 > 0$ — note there is no $p=0$ energy eigenstate in this well; confinement itself forces nonzero kinetic energy, another purely quantum feature); $n=2,3,\ldots$ are excited states, with $E_n \propto n^2$.

⚠️ Misconception: "$\psi$ and $d\psi/dx$ must both be continuous, always."
That rule holds for finite potentials (Topic 5's finite well and barriers). But where $V \to \infty$ — as at the two walls here — only $\psi$ itself needs to be continuous; $d\psi/dx$ is allowed to (and does) jump discontinuously at $x=0$ and $x=a$ for the infinite well. If you try to also impose continuity of the derivative here, you'll incorrectly conclude $A=0$ and lose every solution.
🔢

Normalization and Orthogonality

Fixing the Amplitude: Normalization

The boundary conditions fixed $k_n$ but left $A$ undetermined — that's what normalization is for. Requiring $\int_0^a |\psi_n(x)|^2\,dx = 1$:

$$ \int_0^a |A|^2 \sin^2(k_n x)\,dx = |A|^2\cdot\frac{a}{2} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |A|^2 = \frac{2}{a}. $$

(The integral $\int_0^a \sin^2(k_nx)\,dx = a/2$ follows from $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)$ and the fact that $\cos(2k_nx)$ completes a whole number of cycles over $[0,a]$, so its integral vanishes.) Normalization only fixes the magnitude of $A$; the phase is physically irrelevant, so we conventionally pick the positive real root $A=\sqrt{2/a}$. The complete, normalized energy eigenfunctions are

$$ \boxed{\varphi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\,\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots} $$

These look exactly like the standing-wave modes of a vibrating string of length $a$ pinned at both ends — $\varphi_1$ has no interior nodes, $\varphi_2$ has one, $\varphi_3$ has two, and in general $\varphi_n$ has $n-1$ interior nodes. They alternate even/odd about the well's midpoint $x=a/2$: $\varphi_1$ is even (about $a/2$), $\varphi_2$ is odd, $\varphi_3$ is even, and so on.

Orthogonality

A second essential property: distinct eigenfunctions are orthogonal, $\int_0^a \varphi_m(x)\varphi_n(x)\,dx = 0$ for $m \ne n$. The key step uses the product-to-sum identity $\sin A \sin B = \tfrac12[\cos(A-B)-\cos(A+B)]$:

$$ \int_0^a \varphi_m\varphi_n\,dx = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{(m-n)\pi x}{a}\right) - \cos\!\left(\frac{(m+n)\pi x}{a}\right)\right]dx. $$

Directly: $\int_0^a\cos(N\pi x/a)\,dx = \left[\frac{a}{N\pi}\sin\!\left(\frac{N\pi x}{a}\right)\right]_0^a = \frac{a}{N\pi}\sin(N\pi) = 0$ for any nonzero integer $N$, since $\sin(N\pi)=0$ regardless of whether $N$ is even or odd — this holds for both terms above as long as $m\ne n$ (so $N=m-n$ and $N=m+n$ are both nonzero integers), so both integrals vanish. (It's tempting to say each cosine "completes a whole number of cycles over $[0,a]$," but that's only literally true when $N$ is even; for odd $N$ the domain covers a half-integer number of periods, and the integral still vanishes only because $\sin(N\pi)=0$ at the endpoint regardless of $N$'s parity — the direct antiderivative evaluation above is the safe way to see it.) Combining orthogonality with normalization in one statement, using the Kronecker delta $\delta_{mn}$ (1 if $m=n$, else 0):

$$ \int_0^a \varphi_m(x)\varphi_n(x)\,dx = \delta_{mn}. $$

We say the $\{\varphi_n\}$ are orthonormal. (Note the argument breaks down exactly at $m=n$, where both cosine terms become $\cos(0)$ or a repeated-angle term instead of vanishing — that's precisely the case normalization, not orthogonality, has to handle.) This orthogonality is not a quirk of the infinite well specifically — the general proof that any two eigenfunctions of a Hermitian operator with different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal is Topic 6's job (Formal Theory & Matrix Methods); here we simply establish and use the concrete result.

Completeness: Any Function Can Be Built From These

The $\{\varphi_n\}$ have one more remarkable property: they are complete, meaning any (reasonably well-behaved) function $f(x)$ on $[0,a]$ can be written as a linear combination of them:

$$ f(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n\varphi_n(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} c_n \sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right). $$

This is nothing but the Fourier sine series for $f(x)$ — the $\{\varphi_n\}$ form a complete orthonormal basis of $L^2(0,a)$, a Hilbert-space completeness statement guaranteeing that every square-integrable $f$ on $[0,a]$ can be written this way. (Dirichlet-type conditions are a separate, older story: they're about when a Fourier series converges pointwise, at every individual $x$, to $f(x)$ — a stronger and more delicate question than the $L^2$/Hilbert-space completeness being used here.) Orthonormality lets us extract each coefficient individually via Fourier's trick: multiply both sides by $\varphi_m(x)$ and integrate over $[0,a]$. Every term in the sum vanishes except $n=m$ (courtesy of the Kronecker delta), leaving

$$ c_n = \int_0^a \varphi_n^*(x)\,f(x)\,dx = \int_0^a \varphi_n(x)\,f(x)\,dx \quad (\varphi_n \text{ is real here}). $$

This is the tool Section 7's advanced example uses to expand an arbitrary confined initial wave function in the energy eigenbasis.

Worked Example: Expectation Values $\langle x\rangle$, $\langle p\rangle$, $\langle H\rangle$

📌 This Exact Calculation Is on the Midterm Review Sheet

"Expectation values of position, momentum, and energy for a particle in a box" is explicit on the midterm review handout — cross-check your work below against midterm-exam.html.

Single Stationary State $\varphi_n(x)$

Position. Since $\varphi_n^2(x)$ is symmetric about the midpoint $x=a/2$ (you can check $\sin^2\!\big(\tfrac{n\pi}{a}(a-x)\big) = \sin^2\!\big(\tfrac{n\pi x}{a}\big)$ using $\sin(n\pi - \theta) = \pm\sin\theta$ and squaring), the average position is exactly the midpoint, for every $n$:

$$ \langle x \rangle = \int_0^a x\cdot\frac{2}{a}\sin^2\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)dx = \frac{a}{2}. $$

Carrying out the same integral with an extra factor of $x$ (via $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)$ and integrating $x^2\cos(2k_nx)$ by parts twice) gives the standard result

$$ \langle x^2 \rangle = a^2\left(\frac{1}{3} - \frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\right) \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_x^2 = \langle x^2\rangle - \langle x\rangle^2 = a^2\left(\frac{1}{12} - \frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}\right). $$

Momentum. Because $\varphi_n(x)$ is real, $\langle p \rangle$ vanishes identically for any stationary state — this is worth deriving in general, once and for all:

$$ \langle p \rangle = -i\hbar\int_0^a \varphi_n\,\frac{d\varphi_n}{dx}\,dx = -\frac{i\hbar}{2}\Big[\varphi_n^2(x)\Big]_0^a = 0, $$

since $\varphi_n(0)=\varphi_n(a)=0$ kills the boundary term. (This is consistent with Section 3's general theorem: $\langle p \rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt = 0$ because $\langle x \rangle = a/2$ is constant in a stationary state.)

Energy. $\langle H \rangle = E_n$ and $\sigma_H = 0$ exactly, as proven in Section 3. For the kinetic-energy piece specifically, since $V=0$ inside the well, $\langle H \rangle = \langle T \rangle = \langle p^2\rangle/2m$, so $\langle p^2 \rangle = 2mE_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/a^2$. (You can also get this directly: $-\hbar^2\int_0^a \varphi_n\varphi_n''\,dx = \hbar^2 k_n^2\int_0^a\varphi_n^2\,dx = \hbar^2k_n^2$, matching $2mE_n$ exactly.) Since $\langle p \rangle = 0$, this is also $\sigma_p^2$.

Superposition of Two Eigenstates: Motion Appears

Now let the particle start in an equal-weight combination of the ground state and first excited state, $\Psi(x,0) = A[\varphi_1(x) + \varphi_2(x)]$. Orthonormality makes normalization trivial: $\int|\Psi(x,0)|^2dx = A^2(1+1) = 1$, so $A = 1/\sqrt2$, i.e. $c_1 = c_2 = 1/\sqrt2$.

Tacking on each stationary state's own wiggle factor,

$$ \Psi(x,t) = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\varphi_1(x)e^{-iE_1t/\hbar} + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\varphi_2(x)e^{-iE_2t/\hbar}, $$

and the probability density picks up a genuine cross term (this is Griffiths' Example 2.1 pattern):

$$ |\Psi(x,t)|^2 = \tfrac12\varphi_1^2(x) + \tfrac12\varphi_2^2(x) + \varphi_1(x)\varphi_2(x)\cos(\omega t), \qquad \omega \equiv \frac{E_2-E_1}{\hbar} = \frac{3\pi^2\hbar}{2ma^2}. $$

Since the exponentials no longer cancel, the density genuinely sloshes back and forth at angular frequency $\omega$ — this is what a "superposition state" physically does that a stationary state cannot. Computing $\langle x \rangle(t) = \int_0^a x\,|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx$ requires the cross-term integral $\int_0^a x\,\varphi_1\varphi_2\,dx = -\dfrac{16a}{9\pi^2}$ (a standard integration-by-parts calculation using the product-to-sum identity, same technique as the orthogonality proof above), giving

$$ \langle x \rangle(t) = \frac{a}{2} - \frac{16a}{9\pi^2}\cos(\omega t) \approx \frac{a}{2} - 0.180\,a\,\cos(\omega t). $$

The average position genuinely oscillates about the midpoint, with amplitude $16a/9\pi^2 \approx 0.180a$ — comfortably less than $a/2$, as it must be (the particle can never wander outside the box). Differentiating (or using $\langle p \rangle = m\,d\langle x\rangle/dt$ directly):

$$ \langle p \rangle(t) = \frac{8\hbar}{3a}\sin(\omega t). $$

Finally, the energy expectation value is still constant in time — energy conservation survives superposition, even though position and momentum don't:

$$ \langle H \rangle = |c_1|^2E_1 + |c_2|^2E_2 = \tfrac12 E_1 + \tfrac12 E_2 = \frac{5\pi^2\hbar^2}{4ma^2}. $$

Note $\langle H\rangle$ sits strictly between $E_1$ and $E_2$, and $|c_n|^2$ is literally the probability that a single energy measurement returns $E_n$ — measuring the energy of this particle yields $E_1$ half the time and $E_2$ half the time, never anything else, and never a value in between.

Advanced Example: Expanding an Arbitrary Initial Wave Function

The General Recipe

Given any normalizable initial wave function $\Psi(x,0)$ confined to the well, completeness guarantees it can be written as $\Psi(x,0) = \sum_n c_n\varphi_n(x)$, and Fourier's trick (Section 5) extracts each coefficient: $c_n = \int_0^a \varphi_n(x)\Psi(x,0)\,dx$. Once you know the $c_n$, the full time evolution is immediate — just append each term's wiggle factor:

$$ \Psi(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n\,\varphi_n(x)\,e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}. $$

This is precisely the general superposition formula that Topic 4 develops in full ($\Psi(x,t)=\sum_n c_n\psi_n(x)e^{-iE_nt/\hbar}$) — here we're just seeing its first concrete application.

A Numerical Case Study: An Electron Confined to 1 nm, in the Symmetric Well

This worked case study (adapted from a course computational demonstration) uses the symmetric well convention spanning $-a/2$ to $+a/2$ (see Section 8 for why this differs from the asymmetric convention used above) with $a=1\,\text{nm}$ and an electron mass, giving a ground-state energy of $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2m_ea^2 \approx 0.376\,\text{eV} = 376\,\text{meV}$ — a realistic atomic-scale confinement energy. Because $V(x)$ is an even function of $x$ in this convention, the eigenfunctions split cleanly into two families indexed by $n=1,2,3,\ldots$:

$$ \varphi_{\text{even}}(n,x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\cos\!\left(\frac{(2n-1)\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad \varphi_{\text{odd}}(n,x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\!\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots \text{ (separate counters per family)}. $$
⚠️ Two different labeling conventions for the same states — don't mix them on the exam
This case study numbers the even and odd families with separate counters $n=1,2,3,\ldots$ each, so "$\varphi_{\text{even}}(2,x)$" means the second cosine state. The 2025 final exam's Problem 1 instead uses a single, shared quantum number $n=1,2,3,4,\ldots$ for the combined spectrum, with odd $n$ giving the cosine (even-parity) states and even $n$ giving the sine (odd-parity) states — so "$n=3$" there means the same physical state as "$\varphi_{\text{even}}(2,x)$" here. Both labelings describe identical physics and an identical energy spectrum $E_n=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$; only the bookkeeping differs. Pick one convention per problem and state it explicitly — don't silently swap between "$n$th state of its parity family" and "$n$th state overall" mid-derivation.

Now take an initial wave function confined to a narrow region near the center — physically, an electron whose position is fairly well localized when the well is "switched on." Two natural choices were examined:

  • An even initial function (a symmetric rectangular pulse centered at $x=0$).
  • An odd initial function (an antisymmetric step, positive on one side of the origin and negative on the other, of equal magnitude).

Computing the expansion coefficients numerically via Fourier's trick, $b_{\text{even}}(n) = \int \varphi_{\text{even}}(n,x)\,\Psi_{\text{initial}}(x)\,dx$ and similarly for $b_{\text{odd}}(n)$, produces a clean selection rule: for the even initial pulse, every $b_{\text{odd}}(n)$ comes out numerically zero and only the cosine terms contribute; for the odd initial step, every $b_{\text{even}}(n)$ vanishes and only the sine terms contribute. This is not a coincidence of the specific shapes chosen — it's the general theorem that a symmetric potential's eigenfunctions can always be sorted into even and odd, and an initial state's parity under $x \to -x$ determines which subset of eigenfunctions is needed to build it. Truncating the sum at 50 terms already reproduces the original (sharply-edged) initial function quite well, with small residual "ringing" (Gibbs-phenomenon overshoot) right at the discontinuous edges — exactly the behavior you'd expect from truncating any Fourier series of a discontinuous function.

⚓ Memory Anchor: Symmetry of $\Psi(x,0)$ Tells You Which Coefficients Survive

Before grinding through a Fourier's-trick integral, ask: is the given $\Psi(x,0)$ even, odd, or neither about the center of the well (in a convention where the well is centered at the origin)? If it's even, save yourself the algebra — every odd-eigenfunction coefficient is automatically zero, and vice versa. This is exactly the kind of qualitative check the "most probable energy" and "which coefficients dominate" style of exam question is testing.

Symmetric vs. Asymmetric Well Conventions

⚠️ Misconception: "The infinite well is the infinite well — the convention doesn't matter."

It matters enormously for which boundary conditions you write down and what your eigenfunctions look like. There are two conventions in circulation, and mixing them up is one of the most common sources of sign and boundary-condition errors on this material:

ConventionDomainBoundary ConditionsEigenfunctions
Asymmetric (this page, HW2, midterm review)$0 \le x \le a$$\psi(0)=\psi(a)=0$Pure sines: $\varphi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/a}\,\sin(n\pi x/a)$ for all $n$
Symmetric (Section 7's case study; 2025 Final Problem 1)$-a/2 \le x \le a/2$$\psi(-a/2)=\psi(a/2)=0$Splits by parity: cosines for even states, sines for odd states

Both conventions describe the same physical system (a particle confined to a region of width $a$) and give the same energy spectrum $E_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$ — but the eigenfunctions look completely different, because they're being measured from a different origin. In the symmetric convention, the general solution $\psi(x) = A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$ must satisfy both boundary conditions simultaneously at $\pm a/2$; because the potential is an even function of $x$ in this convention, the solutions sort themselves into a purely-cosine (even) family and a purely-sine (odd) family, rather than the single sine family you get in the asymmetric convention. Applying the asymmetric convention's boundary condition ($\psi(0)=0$, which kills the cosine term) to the symmetric well is the single most common error here — it silently throws away half of the correct solutions.

This page deliberately does not work through the symmetric-well boundary-condition derivation in depth — that exact problem (the symmetric infinite well) is Problem 1 on the 2025 final exam, already administered and graded, and it's reviewed in full depth on final-exam.html. If you want to practice the symmetric case before consulting that page, the key first step is recognizing that Griffiths' general theorem — if $V(x)$ is an even function, the solutions to the TISE can always be chosen even or odd — is what licenses splitting the general solution into the two families above, rather than solving for $A$ and $B$ simultaneously from scratch.

Practice Problems

📝 Common Pitfalls & Self-Check (Homework #2)

No solution PDF was exported for Homework #2, so the notes below are general instructor-style guidance about where students typically lose points on this material — not graded feedback on any specific submission.

  • Sign/convention mixups. Confusing the asymmetric well's $\psi(0)=\psi(a)=0$ boundary conditions with the symmetric well's $\psi(\pm a/2)=0$ conditions (see Section 8) is the single most common error — check which convention a problem statement is using before writing down $A\sin(kx)+B\cos(kx)$.
  • Forgetting that normalization fixes $|A|$, not $A$. The phase of $A$ is never determined by physics; picking $A=\sqrt{2/a}$ real and positive is a convention, not a derived result.
  • Confusing $\langle p \rangle = 0$ with "the particle isn't moving." $\langle p\rangle=0$ in a stationary state reflects zero net average momentum by symmetry — it says nothing about $\langle p^2\rangle$, which is strictly positive ($=2mE_n$) even in the ground state. The particle is never at rest.
  • Skipping the parity check before grinding through a Fourier integral. If the initial state's symmetry already tells you half the coefficients are zero (Section 7), computing them anyway just wastes exam time.
  • Arithmetic slips in the by-parts integrals for $\langle x^2\rangle$ or cross-term integrals like $\int x\varphi_1\varphi_2\,dx$ — these are exactly the multi-step by-parts calculations most prone to sign errors under time pressure. Practice them cold.
Problem 1 — Verify orthogonality directly for $n=1$, $m=3$

Problem: Compute $\int_0^a \varphi_1(x)\varphi_3(x)\,dx$ directly and confirm it equals zero, as the general orthogonality proof (Section 5) guarantees.

Solution: With $\varphi_1 = \sqrt{2/a}\sin(\pi x/a)$ and $\varphi_3 = \sqrt{2/a}\sin(3\pi x/a)$,

$$ \int_0^a \varphi_1\varphi_3\,dx = \frac{2}{a}\int_0^a \sin\!\left(\frac{\pi x}{a}\right)\sin\!\left(\frac{3\pi x}{a}\right)dx = \frac{1}{a}\int_0^a\left[\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi x}{a}\right) - \cos\!\left(\frac{4\pi x}{a}\right)\right]dx. $$

Both $\cos(2\pi x/a)$ and $\cos(4\pi x/a)$ complete a whole number of cycles (one and two, respectively) over $[0,a]$, so each integral is zero over the full period. Hence $\int_0^a\varphi_1\varphi_3\,dx = 0$, confirming orthogonality for this pair explicitly.

Problem 2 — Ground state of an electron in a 0.2 nm box

Problem: An electron is confined to an infinite well of width $a = 0.2\,\text{nm}$ (roughly an atomic diameter). Find $E_1$ in eV, and find $\langle x \rangle$ and $\sigma_x$ for the ground state.

Solution: Using $E_1 = \pi^2\hbar^2/2m_ea^2$ with $\hbar = 6.582\times10^{-16}\,\text{eV·s}$, $m_e = 9.11\times10^{-31}\,\text{kg}$, and converting units carefully (or simply scaling from the Section 7 case study's $a=1\,\text{nm}\Rightarrow E_1=0.376\,\text{eV}$ via $E_1 \propto 1/a^2$):

$$ E_1 = 0.376\,\text{eV} \times \left(\frac{1\,\text{nm}}{0.2\,\text{nm}}\right)^2 = 0.376 \times 25\,\text{eV} \approx 9.4\,\text{eV}. $$

By symmetry (Section 6), $\langle x \rangle = a/2 = 0.1\,\text{nm}$ regardless of confinement scale. For the standard deviation, using $\sigma_x^2 = a^2(1/12 - 1/2\pi^2)$ (the $n=1$ case of the Section 6 formula):

$$ \sigma_x = a\sqrt{\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{2\pi^2}} \approx 0.181\,a \approx 0.036\,\text{nm}. $$

Tighter confinement ($a$ smaller) drives $E_1$ up quadratically — this scaling is exactly why quantum confinement effects (e.g. in quantum dots) become dramatic at the nanometer scale.

Problem 3 — Superposition normalization and energy probabilities

Problem: A particle in the infinite well starts in the state $\Psi(x,0) = A\big[3\varphi_1(x) + 4\varphi_2(x)\big]$. Find $A$, the probability of measuring each allowed energy, and $\langle H \rangle$.

Solution: Orthonormality of $\varphi_1,\varphi_2$ makes normalization a one-line calculation: $\int|\Psi(x,0)|^2dx = A^2(3^2+4^2) = 25A^2 = 1$, so $A = 1/5$. The coefficients are $c_1 = 3A = 3/5$ and $c_2 = 4A = 4/5$, so

$$ P(E_1) = |c_1|^2 = \frac{9}{25} = 0.36, \qquad P(E_2) = |c_2|^2 = \frac{16}{25} = 0.64. $$

(Check: $0.36+0.64=1$, as required.) The expectation value of the energy is

$$ \langle H \rangle = 0.36\,E_1 + 0.64\,E_2 = 0.36\left(\frac{\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\right) + 0.64\left(\frac{4\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}\right) = 2.92\,E_1. $$

Since $4\varphi_2$ carries more weight than $3\varphi_1$, it makes sense that $\langle H\rangle$ sits closer to $E_2=4E_1$ than to $E_1$.

Problem 4 — Check the uncertainty principle for $n=1$ and $n=2$

Problem: Using the Section 6 results $\sigma_x^2 = a^2(1/12 - 1/2n^2\pi^2)$ and $\sigma_p = n\pi\hbar/a$, verify that $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2$ for the $n=1$ and $n=2$ stationary states, and determine which state comes closer to saturating the bound.

Solution: The product is

$$ \sigma_x\sigma_p = a\sqrt{\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}}\cdot\frac{n\pi\hbar}{a} = n\pi\hbar\sqrt{\frac{1}{12}-\frac{1}{2n^2\pi^2}}. $$

For $n=1$: $\sigma_x\sigma_p = \pi\hbar\sqrt{1/12 - 1/2\pi^2} \approx \pi\hbar(0.1808) \approx 0.568\,\hbar$.

For $n=2$: $\sigma_x\sigma_p = 2\pi\hbar\sqrt{1/12 - 1/8\pi^2} \approx 2\pi\hbar(0.2769) \approx 1.740\,\hbar$.

Both comfortably satisfy $\sigma_x\sigma_p \ge \hbar/2 = 0.5\,\hbar$. The ground state ($n=1$) comes noticeably closer to the bound than $n=2$ — and in fact, as $n$ grows the product grows without bound (the well's rectangular eigenfunctions are far from the "minimum uncertainty" shape). No stationary state of the infinite well actually saturates the bound exactly; that distinction belongs to the Gaussian ground state of the harmonic oscillator, which you'll meet in Topic 3.
