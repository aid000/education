Problem 1 — Symmetric Infinite Square Well (30 pts)

Problem 1 (30 points)
Solve the infinite potential well problem for a symmetric well: $V(x) = 0$ for $-a/2 \le x \le a/2$, $V(x) = \infty$ otherwise. Give (a) normalized eigenfunctions, (b) eigenvalues including allowed ranges of quantum numbers. Follow Griffiths §2.2's method, but note the boundary conditions differ: with the well centered at the origin, $V(x) = V(-x)$ is symmetric under inversion, so the eigenfunctions must separate into two families — even and odd under $x \to -x$.

Setup: why parity splits the solutions

Inside the well the time-independent Schrödinger equation is free-particle-like:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\psi}{dx^2} = E\psi \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \psi''(x) + k^2 \psi(x) = 0, \qquad k \equiv \frac{\sqrt{2mE}}{\hbar}$$

The general solution is $\psi(x) = C\sin(kx) + D\cos(kx)$, valid for $-a/2 \le x \le a/2$, with $\psi(x) = 0$ outside (infinite walls). The Hamiltonian here commutes with the parity operator $\hat{P}: x \to -x$, because $V(x) = V(-x)$ exactly (unlike Griffiths' asymmetric convention $0 \le x \le a$, where $V$ is not symmetric about any interior point in a way that makes parity a good quantum number). Because $[\hat H, \hat P] = 0$ and the well is non-degenerate (1D bound states), every energy eigenstate can be chosen to be a simultaneous eigenstate of parity: purely even ($D \ne 0, C = 0$) or purely odd ($C \ne 0, D = 0$). This is the key structural fact the hint is pointing at, and it should be stated explicitly in a written solution, not just asserted.

(a)–(b) Even solutions: eigenfunctions and eigenvalues

Try $\psi_{\text{even}}(x) = D\cos(kx)$. The boundary conditions are continuity of $\psi$ at the infinite walls, i.e. $\psi(\pm a/2) = 0$ (the wavefunction must vanish where $V \to \infty$, exactly as in the asymmetric well — only the wall locations differ here):

$$D\cos\!\left(\frac{ka}{2}\right) = 0$$

Since $D \ne 0$ for a nontrivial solution, we need $\cos(ka/2) = 0$, i.e.

$$\frac{ka}{2} = \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{2}, \ldots = (2j-1)\frac{\pi}{2}, \quad j = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$$

so $k_j = (2j-1)\pi/a$. It is conventional to relabel using the odd integers directly as a single quantum number: let $n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$ (all positive odd integers), so $k_n = n\pi/a$ and the even eigenfunctions are

$$\psi_n^{\text{even}}(x) = D_n \cos\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots$$

Normalize: $\int_{-a/2}^{a/2} |\psi_n^{\text{even}}|^2\,dx = 1$. Using $\cos^2\theta = \tfrac12(1+\cos 2\theta)$,

$$|D_n|^2 \int_{-a/2}^{a/2} \cos^2\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right)dx = |D_n|^2 \left[\frac{x}{2} + \frac{a}{4n\pi}\sin\!\left(\frac{2n\pi x}{a}\right)\right]_{-a/2}^{a/2} = |D_n|^2 \cdot \frac{a}{2} = 1$$

(the sine term vanishes at both endpoints for integer $n$), giving $D_n = \sqrt{2/a}$ — the same normalization constant as the asymmetric well, since it only depends on the well width. So:

$$\boxed{\psi_n^{\text{even}}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\cos\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n = 1, 3, 5, \ldots}$$

(a)–(b) Odd solutions: eigenfunctions and eigenvalues

Try $\psi_{\text{odd}}(x) = C\sin(kx)$. Applying $\psi(\pm a/2) = 0$:

$$C\sin\!\left(\frac{ka}{2}\right) = 0 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad \frac{ka}{2} = \pi, 2\pi, 3\pi, \ldots = n\frac{\pi}{2}, \quad n = 2, 4, 6, \ldots$$

so again $k_n = n\pi/a$ but now restricted to positive even integers $n = 2, 4, 6, \ldots$, and

$$\psi_n^{\text{odd}}(x) = C_n \sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n = 2, 4, 6, \ldots$$

By an identical normalization integral (using $\sin^2\theta = \tfrac12(1-\cos2\theta)$, and the $\cos$ term again vanishing at $x=\pm a/2$ for integer $n$), $C_n = \sqrt{2/a}$. So:

$$\boxed{\psi_n^{\text{odd}}(x) = \sqrt{\frac{2}{a}}\sin\!\left(\frac{n\pi x}{a}\right), \qquad n = 2, 4, 6, \ldots}$$

Combined spectrum and allowed quantum numbers

Both families share the same dispersion relation $k_n = n\pi/a$, so the energy eigenvalues are identical in form to the asymmetric well:

$$E_n = \frac{\hbar^2 k_n^2}{2m} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2ma^2}, \qquad n = 1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots \text{ (all positive integers)}$$

with the parity of the eigenfunction alternating: odd $n$ → even (cosine) eigenfunction; even $n$ → odd (sine) eigenfunction. This is exactly opposite to what you might naively guess from the labels — it's worth stating explicitly in the solution, since it's easy to mix up. The ground state ($n=1$) is the cosine (even) state with no interior nodes, matching the general theorem that a 1D bound-state ground state is nodeless and (for a symmetric potential) even.

Quantum number $n$ParityEigenfunctionEnergy $E_n$
1even$\sqrt{2/a}\cos(\pi x/a)$$\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$
2odd$\sqrt{2/a}\sin(2\pi x/a)$$4\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$
3even$\sqrt{2/a}\cos(3\pi x/a)$$9\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$
4odd$\sqrt{2/a}\sin(4\pi x/a)$$16\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$
⚠️ Contrast with the asymmetric-well convention
The asymmetric well page puts the walls at $x=0$ and $x=a$ and gets a single family $\psi_n(x) = \sqrt{2/a}\sin(n\pi x/a)$ for all $n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots$ — every eigenfunction is a sine, because that origin choice is not a symmetry point of the potential ($V(x) \ne V(-x)$ about $x=0$ or about $x = a/2$ in any useful sense for a single-function parity argument centered there). Shifting the origin to the well's center (this problem's convention) makes $x \to -x$ an exact symmetry of $V$, so parity becomes a good quantum number and the sine family splits: the states that were purely sine in the shifted-origin picture correspond to even $n$ here, and a new cosine family appears for odd $n$. The physical spectrum $E_n = n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/2ma^2$ is identical in both conventions — only the algebraic form and labeling of the eigenfunctions differ, because a rigid translation $x \to x - a/2$ doesn't change energies, only how the boundary conditions are expressed and hence how natural it is to split by parity. This full derivation — including the origin-shift argument — is exactly the kind of "supporting analysis" the grading rubric asks for; do not just quote the asymmetric-well formula and re-center it without deriving the boundary conditions fresh, since the problem explicitly says "the boundary conditions differ."
The even/odd split here is the 1D prototype for why atomic and molecular orbitals are classified by parity (gerade/ungerade) whenever the potential has inversion symmetry — the same logic (₋$[\hat H,\hat P]=0$ in a symmetric potential ⟹ eigenstates can be chosen with definite parity) underlies selection rules in optical spectroscopy: an electric-dipole transition connects states of opposite parity only, because the dipole operator $\hat x$ is itself odd.

Problem 2 — Hydrogen-Atom Superposition State (35 pts)

Problem 2 (35 points, 5 points/part)
A hydrogen atom is in the state $|\Psi(0)\rangle = A\left\{\tfrac12|4,3,-1\rangle + |2,1,0\rangle - i|1,0,0\rangle\right\}$, with $|n,l,m\rangle$ orthonormal energy eigenstates, $E_n = -R/n^2$. Find: (a) $A$. (b) possible energy outcomes and probabilities. (c) $\langle E\rangle$. (d) possible $L^2$ outcomes and probabilities. (e) $\langle L^2\rangle$. (f) possible $L_z$ outcomes and probabilities. (g) $\langle L_z\rangle$.

(a) Normalization constant A

The three kets $|4,3,-1\rangle$, $|2,1,0\rangle$, $|1,0,0\rangle$ are orthonormal (different $n$'s, so automatically orthogonal). Normalization requires $\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle = 1$:

$$|A|^2\left[\left|\frac12\right|^2 + |1|^2 + |{-i}|^2\right] = 1$$

Each coefficient's modulus-squared: $|1/2|^2 = 1/4$, $|1|^2 = 1$, $|{-i}|^2 = (-i)(i)^* $... more carefully, $|-i|^2 = (-i)(\overline{-i}) = (-i)(i) = 1$. Summing:

$$|A|^2\left(\frac14 + 1 + 1\right) = |A|^2 \cdot \frac{9}{4} = 1 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad |A|^2 = \frac{4}{9} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \boxed{A = \frac{2}{3}}$$

(taking $A$ real and positive, the conventional choice — the overall phase of $|\Psi\rangle$ is not physically observable).

(b)–(c) Energy measurement

Each basis ket has a definite $n$, hence a definite energy $E_n = -R/n^2$: the $n=4$ term gives $E_4 = -R/16$, the $n=2$ term gives $E_2 = -R/4$, the $n=1$ term gives $E_1 = -R$. The probability of each outcome is the squared modulus of its (normalized) coefficient:

$$P(E_4) = |A|^2\left|\frac12\right|^2 = \frac49\cdot\frac14 = \frac{1}{9}, \qquad P(E_2) = |A|^2|1|^2 = \frac49\cdot1 = \frac{4}{9}, \qquad P(E_1) = |A|^2|{-i}|^2 = \frac49\cdot1 = \frac{4}{9}$$

Check: $\frac19+\frac49+\frac49 = \frac99 = 1$. ✓ Then

$$\langle E\rangle = \frac19\left(-\frac{R}{16}\right) + \frac49\left(-\frac{R}{4}\right) + \frac49(-R) = -R\left(\frac{1}{144} + \frac{4}{36} + \frac{4}{9}\right)$$

Putting over a common denominator of 144: $\frac{1}{144} + \frac{16}{144} + \frac{64}{144} = \frac{81}{144} = \frac{9}{16}$, so

$$\boxed{\langle E\rangle = -\frac{9}{16}R}$$

(d)–(e) $L^2$ measurement

$L^2$ has eigenvalues $\hbar^2 l(l+1)$ on $|n,l,m\rangle$. Reading off $l$ from each ket: the $n=4$ term has $l=3$ ($L^2 = 12\hbar^2$), the $n=2$ term has $l=1$ ($L^2 = 2\hbar^2$), the $n=1$ term has $l=0$ ($L^2 = 0$).

💡 Shortcut worth stating explicitly

In this particular superposition, each of the three terms has a different $n$, and each $n$ appears exactly once, each paired with a distinct $l$. That means the $L^2$ probabilities are numerically identical to the energy probabilities found in part (b) — not because $L^2$ and $H$ are related in general, but because in this specific state the map from "which term" to "which $n$" and the map from "which term" to "which $l$" happen to be the same one-to-one correspondence. If two terms had shared the same $n$ but different $l$ (impossible here, but worth noting as the general caution), this shortcut would fail and the $L^2$ probabilities would need to be computed by grouping terms by $l$ value directly.

$$P(L^2 = 12\hbar^2) = \frac19, \qquad P(L^2 = 2\hbar^2) = \frac49, \qquad P(L^2 = 0) = \frac49$$
$$\langle L^2\rangle = \frac19(12\hbar^2) + \frac49(2\hbar^2) + \frac49(0) = \frac{12\hbar^2}{9} + \frac{8\hbar^2}{9} = \frac{20\hbar^2}{9}$$
$$\boxed{\langle L^2\rangle = \frac{20}{9}\hbar^2}$$

(f)–(g) $L_z$ measurement

$L_z$ has eigenvalues $m\hbar$. Reading off $m$: the $n=4,l=3$ term has $m=-1$ ($L_z = -\hbar$), the $n=2,l=1$ term has $m=0$ ($L_z = 0$), the $n=1,l=0$ term also has $m=0$ ($L_z = 0$).

⚠️ Two terms share the same $L_z$ eigenvalue — probabilities add
Unlike parts (b)/(d), here two different basis kets ($|2,1,0\rangle$ and $|1,0,0\rangle$) both correspond to the same $m=0$, hence the same $L_z$ eigenvalue $0$. Since $L_z=0$ is a degenerate outcome across these two terms, its total probability is the sum of the two individual probabilities, not just one of them — this is the general rule for degenerate eigenvalues in a superposition (sum $|c_i|^2$ over all $i$ sharing that eigenvalue), and it's exactly the kind of subtlety this problem is designed to test.
$$P(L_z=-\hbar) = |A|^2\left|\frac12\right|^2 = \frac19, \qquad P(L_z=0) = |A|^2|1|^2 + |A|^2|{-i}|^2 = \frac49+\frac49=\frac89$$

Check: $\frac19 + \frac89 = 1$. ✓ Only two distinct $L_z$ outcomes occur: $-\hbar$ (probability $1/9$) and $0$ (probability $8/9$).

$$\langle L_z\rangle = \frac19(-\hbar) + \frac89(0) = -\frac{\hbar}{9}$$
$$\boxed{\langle L_z\rangle = -\frac{\hbar}{9}}$$

Problem 3 — Infinite-Well / Finite-Well Convergence (30 pts)

Problem 3 (30 points)
Compare the Problem 1 infinite-well solution to the finite-well solution. Show how the two converge as $|-V_0| \to \infty$: how the wavefunctions converge in each of the three finite-well regions, and how the energy eigenvalues converge. Note the width mismatch: Problem 1's well is $-a/2$ to $a/2$; the finite well (per lecture-note convention) is $-a$ to $a$ (twice as wide). Make the comparison quantitative.

Setting up matched conventions

Let the finite well be $V(x) = -V_0$ (with $V_0>0$) for $-a \le x \le a$, and $V(x) = 0$ outside, following the lecture-notes convention (well width $2a$, centered at origin). Problem 1's infinite well has width $a$ (i.e. $-a/2$ to $a/2$). To compare apples to apples, rewrite the Problem 1 result for a well of half-width $L$ (so full width $2L$, walls at $\pm L$): repeating the Problem 1 derivation with $a/2 \to L$ everywhere, i.e. $a \to 2L$, the infinite-well spectrum becomes

$$E_n^{(\infty)} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{2m(2L)^2} = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8mL^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots$$

and the eigenfunctions are $\sqrt{1/L}\cos(n\pi x/2L)$ (odd $n$) and $\sqrt{1/L}\sin(n\pi x/2L)$ (even $n$) on $-L\le x\le L$. Setting $L=a$ (matching the finite well's own half-width $a$) makes the two problems directly comparable: the finite well of half-width $a$ should be compared to an infinite well of half-width $a$ (i.e. full width $2a$), not to Problem 1's literal $-a/2$-to-$a/2$ well. This is exactly the width-mismatch bookkeeping the problem statement flags — get this substitution wrong and every subsequent numerical comparison is off by a factor of 4 in energy.

Finite well: three regions and transcendental quantization

For a bound state ($-V_0 < E < 0$), define $\kappa \equiv \sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$ (decay rate outside) and $l \equiv \sqrt{2m(E+V_0)}/\hbar$ (oscillation wavenumber inside). The three regions are:

  • Region I ($x < -a$): $\psi_I(x) = Be^{\kappa x}$ (decaying as $x\to-\infty$).
  • Region II ($-a \le x \le a$): $\psi_{II}(x) = C\cos(lx)$ (even states) or $D\sin(lx)$ (odd states) — parity again splits the problem since $V(x)=V(-x)$.
  • Region III ($x > a$): $\psi_{III}(x) = Fe^{-\kappa x}$ (decaying as $x\to+\infty$).

Matching $\psi$ and $\psi'$ at $x=a$ (and using evenness/oddness to get the $x=-a$ conditions for free) yields the standard transcendental quantization conditions:

$$\text{even states: } \quad l\tan(la) = \kappa \qquad\qquad \text{odd states: } \quad l\cot(la) = -\kappa$$

Quantitative limit $V_0 \to \infty$

The bound-state energy $E \in (-V_0, 0)$ diverges to $-\infty$ as $V_0\to\infty$, so the physically meaningful quantity to track is the energy measured up from the well floor, $\varepsilon \equiv E + V_0 = \hbar^2 l^2/2m$. For a state whose $\varepsilon$ converges to a finite limit as $V_0\to\infty$, the interior wavenumber $l=\sqrt{2m\varepsilon}/\hbar$ stays finite, while the exterior decay rate

$$\kappa = \frac{\sqrt{-2mE}}{\hbar} = \frac{\sqrt{2m(V_0-\varepsilon)}}{\hbar} \;\xrightarrow{V_0\to\infty}\; \frac{\sqrt{2mV_0}}{\hbar} \to \infty$$

diverges. Now examine the even-state condition $l\tan(la)=\kappa$ with $l$ finite and $\kappa\to\infty$: the only way the finite left side can track a divergent right side is if $\tan(la)\to\infty$ as well, which happens precisely as $la$ approaches an odd multiple of $\pi/2$ (where tangent has a pole). So

$$la \to \frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{3\pi}{2}, \frac{5\pi}{2}, \ldots = (2j-1)\frac{\pi}{2}, \qquad j=1,2,3,\ldots$$

By the same reasoning, the odd-state condition $l\cot(la)=-\kappa$ forces $\cot(la)\to-\infty$, which happens as $la$ approaches an integer multiple of $\pi$ from below: $la \to j\pi$, $j=1,2,3,\ldots$. Relabeling both families with a single index $n=1,2,3,\ldots$ (odd $n=2j-1$ for the even family, even $n=2j$ for the odd family — exactly the same odd/even-$n$↔parity pattern found in Problem 1), both conditions combine into $la \to n\pi/2$ for every positive integer $n$, so

$$\varepsilon_n = \frac{\hbar^2 l_n^2}{2m} \;\xrightarrow{V_0\to\infty}\; \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\left(\frac{n\pi}{2a}\right)^2 = \frac{n^2\pi^2\hbar^2}{8ma^2}$$

This is exactly the width-corrected Problem 1 spectrum: substituting the finite well's half-width $L=a$ into Problem 1's general result $E_n^{(\infty)}=n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(2m(2L)^2)$ gives $n^2\pi^2\hbar^2/(8ma^2)$ — identical to $\varepsilon_n$ above. So once the well-floor energy shift $E+V_0$ is used (rather than $E$ itself, which necessarily runs off to $-\infty$) and the width mismatch is corrected ($L=a$, not $L=a/2$, since the finite well spans $-a$ to $a$), the finite well's bound-state spectrum converges term-by-term onto the infinite well's spectrum, with the same even/odd parity assignment per quantum number $n$.

Wavefunction convergence, region by region

  • Region I / III (outside, $|x|>a$): $\psi \sim e^{-\kappa|x|}$ decays over a length scale $1/\kappa$, and since $\kappa\to\infty$ as shown above, this decay length shrinks to zero — the exterior tail collapses onto the wall instantly in the $V_0\to\infty$ limit. This is precisely how the exact boundary condition $\psi(\pm a)=0$ of the infinite well is recovered: for any finite $V_0$ the wavefunction is never exactly zero outside the well, but the region over which it is appreciably nonzero shrinks to nothing as $V_0\to\infty$, so $\psi\to 0$ pointwise for every fixed $x$ with $|x|>a$.
  • Region II (interior, $-a\le x\le a$): the functional form $\cos(lx)$ or $\sin(lx)$ is already exactly the infinite-well form; only the precise value of the quantized wavenumber $l$ differs (satisfying the transcendental condition rather than the exact condition $la=n\pi/2$). As shown above, $la\to n\pi/2$ exactly as $V_0\to\infty$, so the interior wavefunction converges pointwise on $-a\le x\le a$ to the corresponding width-matched infinite-well eigenfunction, and in particular $\psi(\pm a)\to 0$ automatically as this limit is taken — consistent with the vanishing exterior tail described above.

Problem 4 — Larmor Precession (Griffiths 4.35) (30 pts)

Problem 4 (30 points, 10 points/part)
Study Griffiths Example 4.3 (Larmor precession). Then solve Griffiths Problem 4.35 (three parts, 10 points each).

Foundation: Griffiths Example 4.3, Larmor precession

The setup: a spin-1/2 particle with magnetic moment $\vec\mu = \gamma\vec S$ sits in a uniform field $\vec B = B_0\hat z$. The Hamiltonian is $\hat H = -\vec\mu\cdot\vec B = -\gamma B_0 \hat S_z$, so the energy eigenstates are just the $S_z$ eigenstates $|\!\uparrow\rangle, |\!\downarrow\rangle$ with energies $E_\pm = \mp\frac12\gamma B_0\hbar$. Starting from a general spin state at $t=0$ (parametrized by polar/azimuthal angles on the Bloch sphere), the time-dependent state picks up relative phases $e^{-iE_\pm t/\hbar}$, and Griffiths shows that $\langle S_x\rangle$, $\langle S_y\rangle$ trace out a circle in time — the spin precesses about $\hat z$ at the signed angular rate $\omega = \gamma B_0$ (whose sign fixes the precession direction; the observable Larmor frequency is the magnitude $|\gamma|B_0$), while $\langle S_z\rangle$ stays constant. This is the quantum analog of classical magnetic-moment precession in a field.

Rather than re-deriving it here, treat that page as the required background, and use the space in a written exam solution to explicitly re-derive (or at minimum clearly cite and summarize) the key results: the energy eigenvalues $E_\pm$, the precession frequency $\omega=\gamma B_0$, and the time dependence of all three spin expectation values.

Griffiths Problem 4.35, verbatim

The problem statement (3rd edition) is short and refers directly back to the general spin state $|\chi(t)\rangle$ from Example 4.3:

In Example 4.3: (a) If you measured the component of spin angular momentum along the $x$ direction, at time $t$, what is the probability that you would get $+\hbar/2$? (b) Same question, but for the $y$ component. (c) Same, for the $z$ component.

So all three parts ask for the same thing — a measurement probability at time $t$ — just projected onto a different axis's eigenbasis each time. Recall $|\chi(t)\rangle = \big(\cos(\alpha/2)\,e^{i\omega t/2},\ \sin(\alpha/2)\,e^{-i\omega t/2}\big)$ with $\omega \equiv \gamma B_0$, using the $S_z$ basis and Example 4.3's notation.

  • Part (a) — $S_x$: project onto the $S_x$ eigenspinor $\chi_x^+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1)$: $P_{S_x=+\hbar/2}(t) = |\langle\chi_x^+|\chi(t)\rangle|^2 = \dfrac12\big[1 + \sin\alpha\,\cos(\omega t)\big]$. Cross-check: $\langle S_x\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2}\big(2P_{S_x=+\hbar/2}-1\big) = \frac{\hbar}{2}\sin\alpha\cos(\omega t)$, matching Equation 4.164 in Example 4.3 exactly.
  • Part (b) — $S_y$: project onto $\chi_y^+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,i)$: $P_{S_y=+\hbar/2}(t) = \dfrac12\big[1 - \sin\alpha\,\sin(\omega t)\big]$. (The relative sign in front of $\sin(\omega t)$ flips if you instead take $\chi_y^+=\frac1{\sqrt2}(1,-i)$ as the $+\hbar/2$ eigenspinor — both are valid up to the overall phase convention chosen for the $S_y$ eigenbasis; state your convention explicitly and keep it consistent with the $\langle S_y\rangle$ cross-check.)
  • Part (c) — $S_z$: $S_z$ shares eigenstates with $H$, so this one doesn't even need the time dependence: $P_{S_z=+\hbar/2}(t) = |\cos(\alpha/2)|^2 = \dfrac12(1+\cos\alpha)$, constant in time — exactly what "$\langle S_z\rangle$ stays constant" in Example 4.3 already told you.

All three probabilities were derived here the same way: write the $S_x$/$S_y$/$S_z$ eigenspinors in the $S_z$ basis, take the inner product with $|\chi(t)\rangle$, and square the magnitude. Part (c) is the fast one because the eigenbasis you're projecting onto is the same basis $|\chi(t)\rangle$ is already written in.

Larmor precession is the direct quantum-mechanical basis of NMR and MRI: nuclear spins precess about an applied static field at the signed rate $\omega=\gamma B_0$ (observable Larmor frequency $|\gamma|B_0$, tunable via $B_0$ and specific to each nuclear isotope's gyromagnetic ratio $\gamma$), and a resonant oscillating field at that frequency drives spin flips — exactly the physics being probed by the "spin-flip probability" style of follow-up question above.
