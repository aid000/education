Overview

This page covers Syllabus Lectures 6-1 through 7-2 — the module "Quantum Harmonic Oscillator." It maps directly onto Homework #3. The official syllabus table places the Midterm Exam right after this unit — but this study guide's index follows the order things were actually taught and tested, in which Topic 4 (Free Particle & Wave Packets) is covered before the midterm too. Either way, this material is squarely on-deck for exam prep — see the index's note on delivery order for the full picture.

The quantum harmonic oscillator (QHO) is the second exactly-solvable potential in the course, after the infinite square well (Topic 2). It matters far beyond its own problem set: almost any smooth potential looks parabolic near a local minimum, so the QHO is the generic model for small oscillations of any bound system — a diatomic molecule vibrating, an atom in a crystal lattice, a mode of the electromagnetic field. Griffiths & Schroeter (3rd ed., §2.3) present two independent routes to the same energy spectrum:

  • The algebraic (ladder-operator) method — Section 2.3.1, pp. 40–48. This is the method our instructor explicitly emphasizes, and it is the primary subject of this page.
  • The analytic (power-series / Hermite polynomial) method — Section 2.3.2, pp. 48–55. We summarize this for comparison and completeness, without re-deriving it line by line.

Along the way we introduce a first, practical layer of Dirac (bra-ket) notation — just enough to write ladder-operator relations cleanly. The full axiomatic treatment of Hilbert space, operators, and representations is deferred to Topic 6 (Formal Theory and Matrix Methods); here we only need the notation as a bookkeeping tool.

📝 Midterm Emphasis

Setting Up the Problem

Why the harmonic oscillator is universal

Expand any smooth potential $V(x)$ in a Taylor series about a local minimum $x_0$ (where $V'(x_0)=0$):

$$V(x) \approx V(x_0) + \frac{1}{2}V''(x_0)(x-x_0)^2$$

Dropping the constant $V(x_0)$ (an overall energy shift) and identifying an effective spring constant $k \equiv V''(x_0) > 0$ (a genuine, nondegenerate stable minimum — if $V''(x_0)=0$ too, the quadratic term drops out and the leading behavior is governed by whatever higher-order term survives, which is generally not harmonic), this is exactly Hooke's-law potential energy. That is why the QHO shows up everywhere a system sits near a stable equilibrium and oscillates with small amplitude.

The Hamiltonian

Following the instructor's notation (matched throughout this study guide), the Hamiltonian operator for a mass $m$ on a spring of constant $k$, with classical angular frequency $\omega = \sqrt{k/m}$, is:

$$\hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^2}{2m} + \frac{1}{2}k\hat{x}^2 = \frac{1}{2m}\left[\hat{p}^2 + (m\omega\hat{x})^2\right]$$

The time-independent Schrödinger equation is $\hat{H}\phi = E\phi$, or explicitly,

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2\phi}{dx^2} + \frac{1}{2}m\omega^2 x^2 \phi = E\phi$$

We could attack this directly as a differential equation (that's the analytic method, summarized later). But Griffiths' key observation is that the bracketed combination $\hat{p}^2 + (m\omega\hat{x})^2$ has the algebraic form $u^2+v^2$, which factors over the complex numbers as $(iu+v)(-iu+v)$ — if $u$ and $v$ were ordinary numbers. Because $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$ are operators that do not commute, the factorization isn't exact, but it is close enough to be enormously useful. That near-factorization is the entire idea behind the ladder-operator method.

🪜

Ladder Operators & the Commutator

Definition

Define two new operators as linear combinations of $\hat{x}$ and $\hat{p}$:

$$\hat{a} \equiv \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat{x} + \frac{i\hat{p}}{m\omega}\right), \qquad \hat{a}^{\dagger} \equiv \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(\hat{x} - \frac{i\hat{p}}{m\omega}\right)$$

The prefactor $\sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}$ is chosen purely to make later formulas come out clean (it has no independent physical content). Inverting these relations for later use:

$$\hat{x} = \sqrt{\frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}}\left(\hat{a}+\hat{a}^{\dagger}\right), \qquad \hat{p} = i\sqrt{\frac{\hbar m\omega}{2}}\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}-\hat{a}\right)$$

Deriving $[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}]=1$ from $[\hat{x},\hat{p}]=i\hbar$

First, the canonical commutation relation itself. Acting on a test function $f(x)$ with $\hat{p}=-i\hbar\, d/dx$:

$$[\hat{x},\hat{p}]f(x) = x\left(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\right)f - \left(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\right)(xf) = -i\hbar\left(x f' - f - xf'\right) = i\hbar f(x)$$

so, dropping the test function, $[\hat{x},\hat{p}] = i\hbar$. This is the seed from which everything else in this section grows.

Now compute $\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger}$ by direct multiplication, keeping careful track of operator order:

$$\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger} = \frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\left(\hat{x}+\frac{i\hat{p}}{m\omega}\right)\left(\hat{x}-\frac{i\hat{p}}{m\omega}\right) = \frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}\left[\hat{p}^2 + (m\omega\hat{x})^2 - im\omega(\hat{x}\hat{p}-\hat{p}\hat{x})\right]$$

The cross term is exactly the commutator $[\hat{x},\hat{p}] = i\hbar$, so:

$$\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger} = \frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}\left[\hat{p}^2+(m\omega\hat{x})^2\right] - \frac{i}{2\hbar}(i\hbar) = \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\hat{H} + \frac{1}{2}$$

Repeating the multiplication in the opposite order (â† first) instead produces the same quadratic terms but the opposite-sign cross term, since swapping the order of the product reverses the sign of $\hat{x}\hat{p}-\hat{p}\hat{x}$:

$$\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a} = \frac{1}{\hbar\omega}\hat{H} - \frac{1}{2}$$

Subtracting these two boxed results cancels the $\hat H$ term entirely and gives the fundamental ladder-operator commutation relation:

$$[\hat{a},\hat{a}^{\dagger}] = \hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger} - \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a} = 1$$

This single number — 1, with no operators or constants left over — is the algebraic engine of the entire method. Everything about the spectrum of the harmonic oscillator follows from this one relation plus the requirement that physical states be normalizable.

The Hamiltonian in terms of $\hat a$, $\hat a^{\dagger}$

Solving the two boxed relations above for $\hat H$ gives two equivalent expressions:

$$\hat{H} = \hbar\omega\left(\hat{a}\hat{a}^{\dagger} - \frac{1}{2}\right) = \hbar\omega\left(\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a} + \frac{1}{2}\right)$$

The second form — built on $\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}$ — is the one we build on next, because $\hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}$ turns out to have a beautifully simple eigenvalue spectrum: the non-negative integers.

🔢

The Number Operator & Energy Quantization

Definition and non-negativity

Define the number operator $\hat{N} \equiv \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a}$, so that $\hat{H} = \hbar\omega\left(\hat{N}+\tfrac12\right)$. Suppose $\phi_n$ is a normalized eigenstate, $\hat{N}\phi_n = n\phi_n$. Then $n$ must be real and non-negative, because:

$$n = \langle \phi_n | \hat{N} | \phi_n \rangle = \langle \phi_n | \hat{a}^{\dagger}\hat{a} | \phi_n \rangle = \langle \hat{a}\phi_n | \hat{a}\phi_n \rangle = \big\|\hat{a}\phi_n\big\|^2 \geq 0$$

(The middle step moves $\hat a$ from the ket to the bra, which is legal precisely because $\hat a^{\dagger}$ is the operator you get when you do that — that's what "dagger" means.) So every eigenvalue of $\hat N$ is a non-negative real number, and — because $\langle\phi_n|\phi_m\rangle=\delta_{nm}$ for the (Hermitian) $\hat N$'s eigenstates — the states form an orthonormal set indexed by these eigenvalues.

How $\hat a$ and $\hat a^{\dagger}$ shift the ladder

Use the fundamental commutator $[\hat a,\hat a^{\dagger}]=1$ together with the commutator identities (distributive and associative properties covered in the commutator supplement) to find $[\hat N,\hat a]$ and $[\hat N,\hat a^{\dagger}]$:

$$[\hat N,\hat a] = [\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a,\hat a] = \hat a^{\dagger}[\hat a,\hat a] + [\hat a^{\dagger},\hat a]\hat a = 0 + (-1)\hat a = -\hat a$$
$$[\hat N,\hat a^{\dagger}] = [\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a,\hat a^{\dagger}] = \hat a^{\dagger}[\hat a,\hat a^{\dagger}] + [\hat a^{\dagger},\hat a^{\dagger}]\hat a = \hat a^{\dagger}(1) + 0 = \hat a^{\dagger}$$

Now apply these to an eigenstate $\phi_n$ of $\hat N$. From $[\hat N,\hat a] = -\hat a$, i.e. $\hat N\hat a = \hat a\hat N - \hat a = \hat a(\hat N - 1)$:

$$\hat N(\hat a\phi_n) = \hat a(\hat N-1)\phi_n = \hat a(n-1)\phi_n = (n-1)(\hat a\phi_n)$$

So $\hat a\phi_n$ is itself an eigenstate of $\hat N$, with eigenvalue exactly one less than $n$ — hence $\hat a$ is called the lowering (annihilation) operator. The identical argument with $[\hat N,\hat a^{\dagger}]=\hat a^{\dagger}$ gives

$$\hat N(\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_n) = (n+1)(\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_n)$$

so $\hat a^{\dagger}$ is the raising (creation) operator: it produces a new eigenstate one rung higher.

Why $n$ must be a non-negative integer

Here is the crux of the algebraic method. Start from any eigenstate $\phi_n$ with $n\geq 0$ (proven above) and apply $\hat a$ repeatedly: $\hat a\phi_n$, $\hat a^2\phi_n$, $\hat a^3\phi_n,\dots$ Each application lowers the $\hat N$-eigenvalue by exactly 1: $n, n-1, n-2,\dots$. If $n$ were not an integer, this sequence would eventually pass through a negative eigenvalue — but we just showed every eigenvalue of $\hat N$ must satisfy $n\geq 0$. That is a contradiction, unless the lowering sequence terminates cleanly by producing the zero vector exactly when the eigenvalue reaches $0$. The only way the strictly-integer-spaced ladder $n, n-1, n-2,\dots$ can hit the floor $n=0$ exactly (rather than overshooting into forbidden negative values) is if $n$ was a non-negative integer to begin with. Hence:

$$n = 0, 1, 2, 3, \dots \qquad \text{and} \qquad \hat a\,\phi_0 = 0$$

The state $\phi_0$ — annihilated by the lowering operator — is the ground state, the bottom rung of the ladder. Every other stationary state is reached from it by repeated raising.

Energy eigenvalues

Since $\hat H = \hbar\omega(\hat N + \tfrac12)$ and $\hat N\phi_n = n\phi_n$:

$$\hat H \phi_n = \hbar\omega\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\phi_n = E_n\,\phi_n \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \boxed{E_n = \hbar\omega\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)}$$

Note the ground-state energy is $E_0 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega \neq 0$ — the famous zero-point energy. The uncertainty principle explains why it can't be zero: a particle strictly at rest at $x=0$ ($\Delta x=0, \Delta p=0$) is forbidden, so $\langle x^2\rangle$ and $\langle p^2\rangle$ can't both vanish, and since $H=p^2/2m+\tfrac12m\omega^2x^2$ is built entirely from those two terms, $\langle H\rangle$ can't be zero either. But $\Delta x\Delta p\ge\hbar/2$ alone doesn't hand you the exact coefficient $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ — that specific number comes from the oscillator's actual spectrum (the ladder-operator or Hermite-polynomial solution below), or equivalently from minimizing $\langle H\rangle$ subject to $\Delta x\Delta p=\hbar/2$ and checking that the Gaussian ground state saturates the bound exactly.

Normalized ladder action

We know $\hat a\phi_n \propto \phi_{n-1}$ and $\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_n \propto \phi_{n+1}$; we just need the proportionality constants. Using $\langle \hat a\phi_n|\hat a\phi_n\rangle = \langle \phi_n|\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a|\phi_n\rangle = \langle\phi_n|\hat N|\phi_n\rangle = n$, and writing $\hat a\phi_n = c_n\phi_{n-1}$ with $\phi_{n-1}$ normalized, we get $|c_n|^2 = n$. Similarly $\langle \hat a^{\dagger}\phi_n|\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_n\rangle = \langle\phi_n|\hat a\hat a^{\dagger}|\phi_n\rangle = \langle\phi_n|(\hat N+1)|\phi_n\rangle = n+1$. Choosing the conventional (real, positive) phase for both constants gives exactly the instructor's reference formulas:

$$\hat N\phi_n = n\phi_n, \qquad \hat a\,\phi_n = \sqrt{n}\,\phi_{n-1}, \qquad \hat a^{\dagger}\,\phi_n = \sqrt{n+1}\,\phi_{n+1}, \qquad \langle\phi_n|\phi_m\rangle = \delta_{nm}$$

Applying $\hat a^{\dagger}$ repeatedly to the ground state and tracking the accumulated normalization factors gives every excited state without ever solving a differential equation beyond the ground state itself:

$$\phi_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}\left(\hat a^{\dagger}\right)^n \phi_0$$
⚓ Memory Anchor: The Ladder Picture

Picture a literal ladder of evenly spaced energy rungs, spacing $\hbar\omega$, with the ground state $\phi_0$ (energy $\tfrac12\hbar\omega$) on the bottom rung. $\hat a^{\dagger}$ climbs up one rung — it adds exactly one quantum of energy $\hbar\omega$ to the system. $\hat a$ climbs down one rung — it removes one quantum $\hbar\omega$. You cannot climb below the bottom rung: applying $\hat a$ to $\phi_0$ doesn't give $\phi_{-1}$, it gives zero (the null vector, not a physical state). This is exactly the "cat climbing a ladder" picture in Griffiths Figure 2.5 — worth sketching from memory before the exam.

⚠️ Common Misconception

Students often assume $\hat a$ and $\hat a^{\dagger}$ are each Hermitian, or try to treat them as observables you could "measure directly." They are not Hermitian — $\hat a^{\dagger} \neq \hat a$, by construction (check the sign on the $\hat p$ term in the two definitions). Because they're non-Hermitian, they do not correspond to any physically measurable quantity by themselves, and they don't have a probabilistic-measurement interpretation the way $\hat x$, $\hat p$, or $\hat H$ do. What is Hermitian — and therefore is a legitimate observable — is the combination $\hat N=\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a$ (and, of course, $\hat H$ itself, which is built from $\hat N$). Only $\hat N$'s eigenvalues are things you could in principle measure; $\hat a$ and $\hat a^{\dagger}$ are bookkeeping devices for moving between eigenstates, not measurable quantities in their own right.

A Practical First Look at Dirac Notation

You may have noticed the notation above quietly shifted from $\phi_n(x)$ (a function of $x$) to $|\phi_n\rangle$ and $\langle\phi_n|\phi_m\rangle$ (abstract symbols with no explicit $x$-dependence). This is Dirac notation, and at this stage in the course you only need a practical, working level of it — the full formal treatment of Hilbert space, operators, and change-of-representation comes later, in Topic 6: Formal Theory and Matrix Methods.

Kets, bras, and the inner product

Just as an ordinary vector $\vec F$ can be written abstractly or resolved into components along a chosen set of axes, a quantum state can be written abstractly as a ket $|\phi\rangle$, or resolved into components in a chosen representation — most often the coordinate representation, where the ket's component along the "axis" labeled by position $x$ is the wave function itself. Formally, the wave function is the ket's inner product with a position eigenket $|x\rangle$, not the ket itself:

$$\phi(x) = \langle x|\phi\rangle, \qquad \phi^{*}(x) = \langle\phi|x\rangle$$

An abstract ket $|\phi\rangle$ is a single, representation-independent object; $\phi(x)$ is one particular (coordinate-representation) way of writing its components, in the same sense that a vector's Cartesian components are one particular way of writing an abstract $\vec F$.

The dual object $\langle\phi|$ is called a bra; together a bra and a ket combine into a "bra-ket" — the inner product:

$$\langle \psi | \phi \rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \psi^{*}(x)\,\phi(x)\,dx$$

This is exactly the overlap integral you already compute for normalization and orthogonality checks — Dirac notation just gives it a representation-free name. The TISE $\hat H\phi_n = E_n\phi_n$ becomes $\hat H|\phi_n\rangle = E_n|\phi_n\rangle$, with no change in content, only in bookkeeping.

Why it's the natural language for ladder operators

The ladder-operator relations from the previous section read most cleanly in this notation, which is exactly why the instructor introduces it here rather than waiting for Topic 6:

$$\hat N|\phi_n\rangle = n|\phi_n\rangle, \qquad \hat a|\phi_n\rangle = \sqrt{n}\,|\phi_{n-1}\rangle, \qquad \hat a^{\dagger}|\phi_n\rangle = \sqrt{n+1}\,|\phi_{n+1}\rangle$$

There's no coordinate $x$ anywhere in these relations — which makes sense, because the ladder-operator method never had to solve a differential equation to get the spectrum. It's a purely algebraic, representation-free result. You only need to drop back into the coordinate representation ($\phi_n(x)$) when you want to plot a wave function or compute an integral explicitly, as in the worked example below.

Worked Example: Building $\phi_0$ and $\phi_1$ Explicitly

Step 1 — Solve $\hat a\,\phi_0 = 0$ for the ground state

In the coordinate representation, $\hat p = -i\hbar\,d/dx$, so the condition $\hat a\phi_0=0$ becomes:

$$\sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(x + \frac{i}{m\omega}\left(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}\right)\right)\phi_0(x) = 0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; x\,\phi_0 + \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\frac{d\phi_0}{dx} = 0$$

This is a first-order, separable ODE:

$$\frac{d\phi_0}{dx} = -\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\,x\,\phi_0 \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \int\frac{d\phi_0}{\phi_0} = -\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}\int x\,dx \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \ln\phi_0 = -\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2 + \text{const.}$$
$$\phi_0(x) = A\,e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2}$$

Normalize using $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-m\omega x^2/\hbar}\,dx = \sqrt{\pi\hbar/m\omega}$ (a standard Gaussian integral):

$$1 = |A|^2\sqrt{\frac{\pi\hbar}{m\omega}} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; A = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4} \;\;\Longrightarrow\;\; \boxed{\phi_0(x) = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4} e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2}}$$

As a check, plug this directly into $\hat H = \hbar\omega(\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a + \tfrac12)$ and use $\hat a\phi_0=0$: $\hat H\phi_0 = \hbar\omega\cdot\tfrac12\,\phi_0$, confirming $E_0 = \tfrac12\hbar\omega$ without needing to touch the differential equation a second time.

Step 2 — Generate $\phi_1$ by applying $\hat a^{\dagger}$

By the normalized ladder relation, $\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_0 = \sqrt{0+1}\,\phi_1 = \phi_1$ — no extra normalization needed. Compute directly:

$$\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_0 = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(x - \frac{i\hat p}{m\omega}\right)\phi_0 = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\left(x\,\phi_0 - \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\frac{d\phi_0}{dx}\right)$$

Using the ODE result $d\phi_0/dx = -(m\omega/\hbar)\,x\,\phi_0$ from Step 1, the derivative term becomes $-\frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\cdot\left(-\frac{m\omega}{\hbar}x\phi_0\right) = x\phi_0$, so both terms in the parentheses add:

$$\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_0 = \sqrt{\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}}\,\big(2x\,\phi_0\big) = \sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\;x\,\phi_0$$
$$\boxed{\phi_1(x) = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\sqrt{\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}}\;x\;e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2}}$$

Notice $\phi_1$ is odd in $x$ while $\phi_0$ is even — a pattern that continues ($\phi_n$ has parity $(-1)^n$), and one you can use as a fast sanity check on any oscillator wave function you compute.

The Analytic Method (Summary)

Griffiths & Schroeter §2.3.2 solves the same Schrödinger equation directly, as a power series, without ever introducing $\hat a$ or $\hat a^{\dagger}$. It's worth knowing this approach exists and agrees with the algebraic result — but it is not the emphasis of this course, so we summarize only the key waypoints.

The recipe, in outline

  • Introduce the dimensionless variable $\xi \equiv \sqrt{m\omega/\hbar}\,x$ and $K \equiv 2E/\hbar\omega$; the TISE becomes $\dfrac{d^2\psi}{d\xi^2} = (\xi^2 - K)\psi$.
  • Peel off the correct large-$\xi$ asymptotic behavior by writing $\psi(\xi) = h(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$, which turns the equation into the Hermite differential equation: $h'' - 2\xi h' + (K-1)h = 0$.
  • Solve by power series, $h(\xi) = \sum_j a_j \xi^j$. The recursion relation connects $a_{j+2}$ to $a_j$; left unconstrained, the series produces a function that blows up like $e^{\xi^2}$ at large $\xi$ — not normalizable.
  • The series must terminate after a finite number of terms to be physically acceptable. Termination at power $\xi^n$ forces $K = 2n+1$ for a non-negative integer $n$ — reproducing exactly the same quantization condition found algebraically.

Since $K \equiv 2E/\hbar\omega$, the condition $K=2n+1$ gives right back:

$$E_n = \left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega \qquad n = 0,1,2,\dots$$

— identical to the algebraic result, obtained by a completely independent method. The terminating polynomials $h_n(\xi)$ are (up to normalization) the Hermite polynomials $H_n(\xi)$: $H_0=1$, $H_1=2\xi$, $H_2=4\xi^2-2$, $H_3=8\xi^3-12\xi$, etc. The final normalized stationary states are

$$\psi_n(x) = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2^n n!}}\,H_n(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2}$$

which reproduce $\phi_0$ and $\phi_1$ from the worked example above term for term ($H_0=1$ and $H_1=2\xi$ respectively), confirming the two methods are two roads to the same destination.

Quantized harmonic oscillators are the starting point for describing phonons (quantized lattice vibrations in a crystal, central to thermal and electronic transport in semiconductors), molecular vibrational spectra in infrared spectroscopy, and single modes of the quantized electromagnetic field in quantum optics and superconducting-qubit engineering. In every one of these systems, engineers use exactly the $\hat a, \hat a^{\dagger}, \hat N=\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a$ algebra derived here — "creating" and "annihilating" a phonon, a vibrational quantum, or a photon is literally the ladder operator acting on a number state $|n\rangle$. A modern transmon superconducting qubit, for instance, is engineered specifically to be an anharmonic oscillator — deliberately breaking the perfectly even $\hbar\omega$ level spacing you derived above — precisely so that the $|0\rangle \leftrightarrow |1\rangle$ transition can be addressed without also driving $|1\rangle\leftrightarrow|2\rangle$.

Practice Problems

📋 Common Pitfalls & Self-Check (Homework #3)

No solution PDF was exported for Homework #3, so the notes below are general instructor-style guidance on where students most often lose points on this material — not claimed grader comments on any specific submission.

  • Sign errors in $\hat a,\hat a^{\dagger}$. The $+i\hat p$ vs. $-i\hat p$ distinction between $\hat a$ and $\hat a^{\dagger}$ is the single most common source of a wrong-sign commutator. Write out both definitions side by side before multiplying.
  • Forgetting operator order matters. $\hat a\hat a^{\dagger} \neq \hat a^{\dagger}\hat a$ — they differ by exactly 1. If your two computations of $\hat H$ (via $\hat a\hat a^{\dagger}$ and via $\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a$) don't differ by $\hbar\omega$, you swapped an order somewhere.
  • Skipping the normalization check. $\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_n = \sqrt{n+1}\,\phi_{n+1}$, not just "$\propto \phi_{n+1}$." Losing the $\sqrt{n+1}$ (or $\sqrt n$) factor is a very common partial-credit deduction.
  • Treating $\hat a$ as Hermitian when computing expectation values. When you need $\langle x\rangle$ or $\langle x^2\rangle$, substitute $\hat x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(\hat a+\hat a^{\dagger})$ and use the ladder action term by term — do not assume $\langle\phi_n|\hat a|\phi_n\rangle$ is real or nonzero (it's always exactly zero, since $\hat a\phi_n \propto \phi_{n-1} \perp \phi_n$).
  • Not showing the termination argument. Full credit for "derive $E_n$" problems requires the non-negative-norm argument for why $n$ must be a non-negative integer — simply asserting $\hat H = \hbar\omega(n+\tfrac12)$ skips the actual physics content the problem is testing.
Problem 1
Starting only from $[\hat x,\hat p] = i\hbar$ and the definitions of $\hat a$ and $\hat a^{\dagger}$, derive $[\hat a,\hat a^{\dagger}]=1$. Show every step — do not simply cite the result.
Show Solution

$$\hat a\hat a^{\dagger} = \frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}\left(\hat x+\frac{i\hat p}{m\omega}\right)\left(\hat x-\frac{i\hat p}{m\omega}\right) = \frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}\Big[\hat p^2+(m\omega\hat x)^2\Big] - \frac{i}{2\hbar}\big(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x\big)$$

$$\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a = \frac{1}{2\hbar m\omega}\Big[\hat p^2+(m\omega\hat x)^2\Big] + \frac{i}{2\hbar}\big(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x\big)$$

Subtracting: $\hat a\hat a^{\dagger}-\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a = -\dfrac{i}{\hbar}(\hat x\hat p-\hat p\hat x) = -\dfrac{i}{\hbar}[\hat x,\hat p] = -\dfrac{i}{\hbar}(i\hbar) = 1$. Hence $[\hat a,\hat a^{\dagger}]=1$. $\blacksquare$

Problem 2
Using $\hat x = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(\hat a+\hat a^{\dagger})$ and the ladder action on $\phi_n$, show that $\langle\phi_n|\hat x|\phi_n\rangle = 0$ for every $n$, and find $\langle\phi_n|\hat x^2|\phi_n\rangle$.
Show Solution

$\hat x\phi_n = \sqrt{\hbar/2m\omega}\,(\sqrt n\,\phi_{n-1}+\sqrt{n+1}\,\phi_{n+1})$. Both $\phi_{n-1}$ and $\phi_{n+1}$ are orthogonal to $\phi_n$, so $\langle\phi_n|\hat x|\phi_n\rangle = 0$ for every $n$ (the oscillator has no permanent displacement, as symmetry of $V(x)$ about $x=0$ demands).

For $\hat x^2$, expand: $\hat x^2 = \dfrac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\big[\hat a^2 + \hat a\hat a^{\dagger}+\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a+(\hat a^{\dagger})^2\big]$. The $\hat a^2$ and $(\hat a^{\dagger})^2$ terms move $\phi_n$ to $\phi_{n\pm2}$, both orthogonal to $\phi_n$, so they drop out of the expectation value. Only the middle two terms survive:

$$\langle\phi_n|\hat x^2|\phi_n\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\langle\phi_n|(\hat a\hat a^{\dagger}+\hat a^{\dagger}\hat a)|\phi_n\rangle = \frac{\hbar}{2m\omega}\big[(n+1)+n\big] = \frac{\hbar}{m\omega}\left(n+\frac{1}{2}\right)$$

Problem 3
Construct $\phi_2(x)$ by applying $\hat a^{\dagger}$ twice to the normalized ground state $\phi_0$ and dividing by the correct normalization factor. Write the result in closed form.
Show Solution

From $\phi_n = \frac{1}{\sqrt{n!}}(\hat a^{\dagger})^n\phi_0$ with $n=2$: $\phi_2 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}(\hat a^{\dagger})^2\phi_0 = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\hat a^{\dagger}\phi_1$.

Applying $\hat a^{\dagger} = \sqrt{m\omega/2\hbar}\,(x - (\hbar/m\omega)\,d/dx)$ to the $\phi_1$ found in the worked example and simplifying (the algebra parallels Step 2 of the worked example) gives, after normalization,

$$\phi_2(x) = \left(\frac{m\omega}{\pi\hbar}\right)^{1/4}\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\frac{2m\omega}{\hbar}x^2 - 1\right)e^{-\frac{m\omega}{2\hbar}x^2}$$

which matches the analytic-method prediction $\psi_2 \propto H_2(\xi)\,e^{-\xi^2/2} = (4\xi^2-2)e^{-\xi^2/2}$ up to the overall normalization convention, confirming the two methods agree at $n=2$ as well.

Problem 4
A particle in the oscillator potential starts in the superposition $|\psi\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\big(|\phi_0\rangle + |\phi_1\rangle\big)$. If you measure the energy, what values can you get, and with what probabilities? Find $\langle\hat H\rangle$.
Show Solution

Since $|\psi\rangle$ is already written as a linear combination of energy eigenstates with coefficients $c_0=c_1=1/\sqrt2$ (and $|c_0|^2+|c_1|^2 = \tfrac12+\tfrac12=1$, correctly normalized), a measurement of energy can only return $E_0=\tfrac12\hbar\omega$ or $E_1=\tfrac32\hbar\omega$, each with probability $|c_n|^2 = \tfrac12$.

$$\langle\hat H\rangle = \sum_n |c_n|^2 E_n = \frac12\left(\frac12\hbar\omega\right) + \frac12\left(\frac32\hbar\omega\right) = \hbar\omega$$

Note this is a fixed number, independent of time — even though $|\psi(t)\rangle$ itself is not a stationary state and its probability density oscillates in time (at angular frequency $\omega=(E_1-E_0)/\hbar$), $\langle\hat H\rangle$ is conserved, as it must be for any state built from a fixed set of $c_n$'s.
