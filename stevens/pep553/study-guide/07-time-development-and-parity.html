Overview: Why This Topic Gets Its Own Chapter

The syllabus folds this material into "the formal theory of quantum mechanics and matrix methods," but the course organizes it as its own dedicated module: Time Development, Conservation Theorems and Parity. It maps to Homework #6 and to lectures 11-1 through 12-2 (second half). In Griffiths & Schroeter, 3rd edition, this is all of Chapter 6, "Symmetries & Conservation Laws."

⚓ Heads up: this chapter is new

Chapter 6 was added in the 3rd edition specifically to give quantum conservation laws a rigorous symmetry-based treatment. If you go looking online for old worked solutions, solution manuals, Chegg/Course Hero posts, or study guides keyed to the 2nd edition (or earlier), this material simply will not be there — the 2nd edition jumps straight from identical particles to solids. Cross-check problem numbers against your own 3rd-edition copy, and treat this study guide and lecture as your primary sources for this topic.

The big idea threading through the whole chapter: in classical mechanics you learned that momentum, energy, and angular momentum are conserved because of symmetries of the system (translational, temporal, and rotational symmetry, respectively) — that's Noether's theorem. The same statement holds in quantum mechanics, but "symmetry" and "conserved" both need to be defined operator-theoretically. This page builds that machinery in four stages:

  • Ehrenfest's theorem — the general engine that tells you when any expectation value is time-independent, and reduces to Newton's second law for $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle p\rangle$.
  • The parity operator — a concrete, discrete symmetry (spatial inversion) that you've already met informally in the symmetric infinite well, now made rigorous with eigenvalues and selection rules.
  • Noether's theorem — the general pattern (symmetry generator commutes with $H$ ⇒ quantity is conserved) applied to translations (momentum) and time-translations (energy).
  • The Heisenberg picture — a brief look at the "other" way to encode time development, where operators evolve and states sit still, just to contrast with the Schrödinger-picture approach used everywhere else in this course.

Ehrenfest's Theorem: Quantum Newton's Second Law

The general formula

From the course's fundamental-equations reference sheet, the time dependence of any expectation value is governed by:

$$\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,Q]\rangle+\left\langle\frac{\partial Q}{\partial t}\right\rangle$$

Let's derive this from scratch, starting only from the time-dependent Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t = H\Psi$, because the derivation is short and it is exactly the pattern you'll reuse for parity conservation and for Noether's theorem below.

By definition, $\langle Q\rangle(t) = \langle\Psi(t)|\hat Q|\Psi(t)\rangle$. Differentiate using the product rule:

$$\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt} = \left\langle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\Big|\hat Q\Big|\Psi\right\rangle + \left\langle\Psi\Big|\frac{\partial\hat Q}{\partial t}\Big|\Psi\right\rangle + \left\langle\Psi\Big|\hat Q\Big|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\right\rangle$$

The middle term is just $\langle\partial Q/\partial t\rangle$ — it's the piece we want to keep. For the other two, use the Schrödinger equation, $\partial\Psi/\partial t = \frac{1}{i\hbar}H\Psi$, and its conjugate, $\langle\partial\Psi/\partial t| = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\Psi|H$ (valid because $H$ is Hermitian, so $\langle H\Psi| = \langle\Psi|H$):

$$\left\langle\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\Big|\hat Q\Big|\Psi\right\rangle = -\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\Psi|H\hat Q|\Psi\rangle,\qquad \left\langle\Psi\Big|\hat Q\Big|\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial t}\right\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\Psi|\hat Q H|\Psi\rangle$$

Adding these two:

$$\frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle\Psi|\hat QH - H\hat Q|\Psi\rangle = \frac{1}{i\hbar}\langle[\hat Q,H]\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,\hat Q]\rangle$$

(using $1/(i\hbar) = -i/\hbar$ and $[\hat Q,H] = -[H,\hat Q]$). Putting the three pieces back together reproduces the boxed formula above. This derivation is completely general — it works for any operator $\hat Q$, which is exactly why it's the single engine driving everything else in this chapter: plug in $\hat Q = \hat x$, $\hat p$, $\hat\Pi$ (parity), or the generator of any other symmetry, and you get a conservation statement for free whenever that operator commutes with $H$.

Specializing to $\hat x$: recovering $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle p\rangle/m$

Take $\hat Q = \hat x$, which has no explicit time dependence, so $\langle\partial x/\partial t\rangle = 0$. We need $[H,\hat x]$ for $H = \hat p^2/2m + V(\hat x)$. Since $V(\hat x)$ commutes with $\hat x$, only the kinetic term contributes:

$$[H,\hat x] = \frac{1}{2m}[\hat p^2,\hat x]$$

Use the canonical commutator $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ and the identity $[\hat p^2,\hat x] = \hat p[\hat p,\hat x]+[\hat p,\hat x]\hat p = -2i\hbar\hat p$ (since $[\hat p,\hat x]=-i\hbar$):

$$[H,\hat x] = \frac{1}{2m}(-2i\hbar\hat p) = -\frac{i\hbar}{m}\hat p$$

Plugging into the general Ehrenfest formula:

$$\frac{d\langle x\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\left\langle-\frac{i\hbar}{m}\hat p\right\rangle = \frac{\langle p\rangle}{m}$$

This is exactly the classical relation $p = m\,dx/dt$, now holding for expectation values rather than definite trajectories.

Specializing to $\hat p$: recovering $d\langle p\rangle/dt = -\langle\partial V/\partial x\rangle$

Now take $\hat Q = \hat p$. Here the kinetic term commutes trivially with $\hat p$ (it's built from $\hat p$ alone), so only $V(\hat x)$ contributes: $[H,\hat p] = [V(\hat x),\hat p]$. Using the standard operator identity $[\hat p, f(\hat x)] = -i\hbar\, f'(\hat x)$ (which follows directly from $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ applied term-by-term to a Taylor series for $f$), we get $[V,\hat p] = -[\hat p,V] = i\hbar\,V'(\hat x)$. Then:

$$\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,\hat p]\rangle = \frac{i}{\hbar}\big(i\hbar\langle V'(\hat x)\rangle\big) = -\left\langle\frac{\partial V}{\partial x}\right\rangle$$

This is quantum Newton's second law — but read the right-hand side carefully: it's $-\langle\partial V/\partial x\rangle = m\,d^2\langle x\rangle/dt^2$, the average of the force evaluated at each point the particle's wave function has amplitude, not the classical force evaluated at the average position, $-V'(\langle x\rangle)$. Those two are equal only for potentials that are at most quadratic ($V'$ then linear, so averaging commutes with evaluating it), which is exactly why Ehrenfest's theorem gives exact classical motion for the free particle and the harmonic oscillator but only an approximate one elsewhere — good when the wave packet stays narrow enough, and the potential varies slowly enough across that packet, that $\langle V'(\hat x)\rangle\approx V'(\langle x\rangle)$. Keeping that caveat in mind, the pair of equations here and above is the precise statement of Bohr's correspondence principle for a moving wave packet.

The commutator-algebra techniques used here — turning $[\hat p^2,\hat x]$ or $[V(\hat x),\hat p]$ into something computable by repeatedly applying $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$ — are exactly the ones used for the raising/lowering (ladder) operators in Topic 3 and the matrix-mechanics formalism of Topic 6; see PositionMomentumAnnhilationCommutators.pdf in the course files for a worked reference sheet of these identities if the algebra above feels unfamiliar. In semiconductor device physics, the same Ehrenfest-style logic underlies semiclassical transport theory: the standard crystal equations of motion used to model electrons and holes drifting through a transistor channel are $\hbar\,dk/dt = F$ (force from an applied field) and $v = \frac{1}{\hbar}\nabla_k E(k)$ (group velocity from the band structure), with the electron's effective mass coming from the curvature of the energy band, $1/m^*\propto d^2E/dk^2$ — the more precise bridge from the quantum band structure to classical-looking electron dynamics in a crystal, in the same spirit as Ehrenfest's theorem bridging quantum and classical mechanics here.

The Parity Operator

Definition and eigenvalues

The parity operator $\hat\Pi$ implements a spatial inversion. In one dimension:

$$\hat\Pi\,\psi(x) = \psi(-x)$$

and in three dimensions, $\hat\Pi\,\psi(\mathbf{r}) = \psi(-\mathbf{r})$. Two structural facts follow immediately from the definition:

  • $\hat\Pi$ is its own inverse: applying the inversion twice returns the original function, $\hat\Pi^2 = 1$.
  • $\hat\Pi$ is Hermitian (a short integration-by-substitution argument, left as Griffiths Problem 6.8), and combined with $\hat\Pi^2=1$ this makes $\hat\Pi$ unitary as well: $\hat\Pi^{-1}=\hat\Pi=\hat\Pi^\dagger$.

Deriving the eigenvalues. Suppose $\hat\Pi\,\psi = \lambda\,\psi$ for some eigenfunction $\psi$. Apply $\hat\Pi$ again:

$$\hat\Pi^2\psi = \hat\Pi(\lambda\psi) = \lambda(\hat\Pi\psi) = \lambda^2\psi$$

But $\hat\Pi^2 = 1$, so $\hat\Pi^2\psi = \psi$. Comparing, $\lambda^2\psi = \psi$, and since $\psi\neq 0$ this forces $\lambda^2=1$, i.e.

$$\lambda = \pm 1$$

Deriving even/odd eigenfunctions. Writing out the eigenvalue equation explicitly, $\psi(-x) = \lambda\psi(x)$. For $\lambda=+1$ this reads $\psi(-x)=\psi(x)$ — an even function. For $\lambda=-1$ it reads $\psi(-x)=-\psi(x)$ — an odd function. So the eigenvalue $\pm1$ of $\hat\Pi$ literally is the even/odd character of the wave function; there is no additional assumption needed.

Parity conservation

A system has inversion symmetry if the Hamiltonian is unchanged by a parity transformation, $\hat\Pi^\dagger\hat H\hat\Pi=\hat H$, which (using unitarity of $\hat\Pi$) is equivalent to

$$[\hat H,\hat\Pi]=0$$

For a particle of mass $m$ in a 1-D potential, this condition reduces to $V(x)=V(-x)$: the potential must be an even function of position. Two consequences follow immediately:

  1. Because $\hat H$ and $\hat\Pi$ commute, they share a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates — every bound-state stationary state of a symmetric 1-D potential can be chosen purely even or purely odd (or, in the degenerate case, some linear combination arranged to be one or the other).
  2. By Ehrenfest's theorem with $\hat Q=\hat\Pi$ (which has no explicit time dependence), $d\langle\Pi\rangle/dt = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,\Pi]\rangle = 0$. Parity is conserved. And it's conserved in the strong sense discussed in Section 6.3 of the text: because $\hat H$ and $\hat\Pi$ commute, not just the expectation value $\langle\Pi\rangle$ but the full probability of measuring $\Pi=+1$ versus $\Pi=-1$ is frozen in time. If your wave function starts out purely even, it stays purely even forever, for any symmetric potential.
⚓ Anchor back to Topic 2: the symmetric infinite well

This is precisely the phenomenon flagged in the misconception box of Topic 2 — The Infinite Potential Well concerning the symmetric infinite well (walls at $x=\pm a/2$ rather than at $0$ and $L$): the eigenfunctions there alternate between even and odd as $n$ increases, and students often treat this as an accident of the specific well shape. It isn't an accident — it's parity conservation in its simplest possible setting. The symmetric well's potential satisfies $V(-x)=V(x)$ exactly, so $[\hat H,\hat\Pi]=0$, so every one of its non-degenerate 1-D bound states is forced to be a parity eigenstate — purely even or purely odd — by the general argument above. The "shifted" infinite well (walls at $0$ and $L$) that you first meet in Topic 2 does not have this symmetry about the origin, which is exactly why its eigenfunctions ($\sin(n\pi x/L)$) don't sort into even/odd pairs the way the centered well's do.

🔧 Worked Example: a discrete two-level "toy parity" (adapted from the professor's Example 3.8 notes)

It's worth seeing the even/odd mechanism in the simplest possible setting: a two-dimensional Hilbert space, using the same two-level system worked out in Topic 6 (Griffiths Example 3.8 in the guided lecture notes). Let $|1\rangle=\binom{1}{0}$ and $|2\rangle=\binom{0}{1}$ be an orthonormal basis, and take the Hamiltonian

$$H = \begin{pmatrix}h & g\\ g & h\end{pmatrix}$$

Notice $H$ is symmetric under exchanging the two basis labels $1\leftrightarrow2$: the diagonal entries are both $h$ and the off-diagonal entries are both $g$. Define an exchange operator $\hat P$ that swaps the two basis kets, $\hat P|1\rangle=|2\rangle$ and $\hat P|2\rangle=|1\rangle$ — the discrete, two-state analog of spatial inversion. In matrix form, $\hat P=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$.

Just like $\hat\Pi$, this operator satisfies $\hat P^2=1$ (swapping twice returns the original state), so its eigenvalues are $\pm1$ by the identical argument given above. And because $H$'s diagonal entries are equal and its off-diagonal entries are equal, direct matrix multiplication confirms $[\hat H,\hat P]=0$ — the two-level analog of inversion symmetry. Solving the eigenvalue problem (as in Topic 6) gives energies $E_\pm = h\pm g$ with eigenvectors

$$|s_\pm\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\ \pm1\end{pmatrix}$$

Check directly: $\hat P|s_+\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\binom{1}{1} = |s_+\rangle$ (eigenvalue $+1$, the "even," symmetric combination), while $\hat P|s_-\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\binom{1}{-1} = -|s_-\rangle$ (eigenvalue $-1$, the "odd," antisymmetric combination). So the energy eigenstates of a basis-exchange-symmetric Hamiltonian are forced to be symmetric or antisymmetric combinations — exactly the even/odd split that $\hat\Pi$ enforces on the continuous infinite well, now visible in a $2\times2$ matrix you can check by hand. This is the same mechanism that produces bonding/antibonding molecular orbitals from two symmetric atomic sites, and symmetric/antisymmetric normal modes from two identical coupled oscillators.

Parity selection rules

Selection rules tell you when a matrix element $\langle b|\hat Q|a\rangle$ is forced to vanish by symmetry alone, with no integral required. The key operator identity is how a given operator transforms under parity: $\hat\Pi^\dagger\hat Q\hat\Pi = \hat Q(-\hat{\mathbf r},-\hat{\mathbf p})$. Position and momentum are both odd under parity,

$$\hat\Pi^\dagger\hat x\hat\Pi = -\hat x,\qquad \hat\Pi^\dagger\hat p\hat\Pi=-\hat p$$

(this follows directly from the definitions: $\hat\Pi^\dagger\hat x\hat\Pi\,f(x) = \hat\Pi\,\big[x\,f(-x)\big]\big|_{x\to -x}$, tracked carefully through the substitution, gives $-x\,f(x)$). Now sandwich the position operator between two parity eigenstates $\psi_a$ (parity $\alpha=\pm1$) and $\psi_b$ (parity $\beta=\pm1$), and insert $\hat\Pi^\dagger\hat\Pi=1$ next to $\hat x$:

$$\langle b|\hat x|a\rangle = \langle b|\hat\Pi^\dagger(\hat\Pi\hat x\hat\Pi^\dagger)\hat\Pi|a\rangle = \langle b|\hat\Pi^\dagger(-\hat x)\hat\Pi|a\rangle = -\beta\alpha\,\langle b|\hat x|a\rangle$$

using $\hat\Pi|a\rangle=\alpha|a\rangle$ and $\langle b|\hat\Pi^\dagger = (\hat\Pi|b\rangle)^\dagger = (\beta|b\rangle)^\dagger=\beta\langle b|$ (parity eigenvalues are real). This says $\langle b|\hat x|a\rangle = -\alpha\beta\,\langle b|\hat x|a\rangle$, so:

$$\langle b|\hat x|a\rangle\big(1+\alpha\beta\big) = 0$$

If $a$ and $b$ have the same parity ($\alpha\beta=+1$, i.e. $\langle\text{even}|\hat x|\text{even}\rangle$ or $\langle\text{odd}|\hat x|\text{odd}\rangle$), the factor $(1+\alpha\beta)=2\neq0$, forcing the matrix element to vanish. If $a$ and $b$ have opposite parity ($\alpha\beta=-1$), the factor vanishes identically and the matrix element is unconstrained by symmetry (generically nonzero). This is exactly the general form of Laporte's rule derived in the text for the hydrogenic electric-dipole operator $\hat{\mathbf p}_e=q\hat{\mathbf r}$: matrix elements between states of the same parity vanish, $\langle n'\ell'm'|\hat{\mathbf p}_e|n\ell m\rangle=0$ whenever $\ell+\ell'$ is even.

Physically: $\hat x$ is odd under parity, so its matrix elements are forced to vanish by symmetry between states of the same parity, while between states of opposite parity the symmetry argument imposes no constraint (the matrix element is generically nonzero, but symmetry alone doesn't guarantee it). Any operator that is even under parity (such as $\hat L$, orbital angular momentum) has exactly the reverse selection rule — its matrix elements are forced to vanish by symmetry between states of opposite parity, while between states of the same parity the symmetry argument again imposes no constraint (Griffiths Problem 6.11). A selection rule tells you which matrix elements must be zero, never which ones must be nonzero.

Noether's Theorem: Symmetry ⇒ Conservation

The statement, in words and as an operator criterion

Noether's theorem, in its classical-mechanics form, says that every continuous symmetry of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity. The quantum-mechanical version of the theorem is a precise operator statement, and Ehrenfest's theorem is the machine that proves it:

⚓ The general pattern

Let a continuous symmetry transformation be generated by a Hermitian operator $\hat Q$ (with no explicit time dependence), so that the unitary transformation operator is $\hat U(\alpha) = \exp[-i\alpha\hat Q/\hbar]$ for a continuous parameter $\alpha$. If this transformation is a symmetry of the system — i.e. it leaves the Hamiltonian unchanged, $\hat U^\dagger\hat H\hat U=\hat H$ — then (by the same unitarity argument used in Section 6.2.2 of the text) this is equivalent to $[\hat H,\hat Q]=0$. By Ehrenfest's theorem, $d\langle Q\rangle/dt = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,Q]\rangle = 0$. $Q$ is conserved.

This single pattern — symmetry $\Rightarrow$ generator commutes with $H$ $\Rightarrow$ Ehrenfest's theorem $\Rightarrow$ generator is conserved — is exactly what produces every classical conservation law you already know:

Symmetry of $H$Generator of the symmetryConserved quantity
Translation in space, $\hat T(a)=\exp[-ia\hat p/\hbar]$Momentum $\hat p$Momentum
Translation in time, $\hat U(t)=\exp[-it\hat H/\hbar]$The Hamiltonian $\hat H$ itselfEnergy
Rotation about an axis (preview, Topic 8)Angular momentum $\hat L_z$Angular momentum
Spatial inversion (discrete, not continuous)Parity $\hat\Pi$Parity quantum number

Translational symmetry ⇒ momentum conservation

The translation operator is $\hat T(a)=\exp[-ia\hat p/\hbar]$ — momentum is literally defined as "the generator of translations." A system has continuous translational symmetry if $V(x+a)=V(x)$ for every $a$, i.e. the potential is constant (a free particle). Using the infinitesimal form $\hat T(\delta)\approx 1-i\delta\hat p/\hbar$, translational symmetry means $[\hat H,\hat T(\delta)]=0$ for infinitesimal $\delta$, which (expanding) forces $[\hat H,\hat p]=0$. Then Ehrenfest's theorem gives

$$\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,p]\rangle = 0$$

— momentum conservation, derived as a special case of the same Noether pattern (and consistent with the $-\langle\partial V/\partial x\rangle$ result above: a constant potential has zero force). The same argument, applied to a system of interacting particles whose potential depends only on the difference $x_1-x_2$, shows that the total momentum $\hat P=\hat p_1+\hat p_2$ is conserved even though the potential is certainly not translationally symmetric for either particle individually (Griffiths Problem 6.7) — this is the quantum statement of Newton's third law.

Time-translation symmetry ⇒ energy conservation

Symmetrically, the Hamiltonian itself is the generator of translations in time: for a time-independent $H$, the time-evolution operator that propagates a wave function forward by time $t$ is

$$\hat U(t) = \exp\left[-\frac{it}{\hbar}\hat H\right]$$

A system has time-translation invariance if the physics doesn't care when you start the clock — the experiment proceeds identically whether you run it today or next Tuesday. Requiring this for the infinitesimal evolution operator $\hat U(t_0+\delta,t_0)\approx 1-\frac{i}{\hbar}\hat H(t_0)\delta$ forces $\hat H(t_1)=\hat H(t_2)$ for every pair of times, i.e. $\partial\hat H/\partial t = 0$: time-translation invariance is precisely the statement that the Hamiltonian has no explicit time dependence. Then the generalized Ehrenfest theorem, applied to $Q=H$ itself, gives

$$\frac{d\langle H\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,H]\rangle + \left\langle\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}\right\rangle = 0 + 0 = 0$$

since $[H,H]=0$ trivially for any operator. Energy conservation is a consequence of time-translation invariance. Notice the elegant symmetry with the momentum case: there, translation symmetry in $x$ made $\hat p$ (the generator of $x$-translations) commute with $H$; here, translation symmetry in $t$ makes $\hat H$ (the generator of $t$-translations) trivially commute with itself. Between this result, momentum conservation above, and angular momentum conservation (previewed in Topic 8), quantum mechanics recovers all three classical conservation laws, each tied to a specific continuous symmetry of the Hamiltonian — plus, as you saw above, a genuinely new one (parity conservation) tied to a discrete symmetry with no classical-mechanics analog of quite the same flavor.

A Glimpse of the Heisenberg Picture

Everywhere else in this course we've worked in the Schrödinger picture: wave functions evolve in time according to the Schrödinger equation, while operators like $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ carry no time dependence of their own. But the time-evolution operator $\hat U(t)=\exp[-it\hat H/\hbar]$ derived above suggests a second, entirely equivalent bookkeeping scheme. Define the Heisenberg-picture version of any operator by transforming it with $\hat U$, exactly the way parity or translation transformed operators earlier in this chapter:

$$\hat Q_H(t) = \hat U^\dagger(t)\,\hat Q\,\hat U(t)$$

In this picture, the roles flip: the wave function is frozen at its initial value, $\Psi_H(x)=\Psi(x,0)$, and all of the time dependence is carried by the operators. Differentiating Equation above with respect to time produces the Heisenberg equation of motion,

$$i\hbar\frac{d}{dt}\hat Q_H(t) = \big[\hat Q_H(t),\hat H\big]$$

which looks like a mirror image of Ehrenfest's theorem (indeed, taking the expectation value of both sides reproduces it exactly, confirming the two pictures are physically equivalent: $\langle\hat Q\rangle$ computed either way must agree). Working this out for a free particle or harmonic oscillator gives $\hat x_H(t) = \hat x_H(0) + \hat p_H(0)\,t/m$ (free particle) or $\hat x_H(t) = \hat x_H(0)\cos\omega t + \hat p_H(0)\sin(\omega t)/(m\omega)$ (harmonic oscillator) — the Heisenberg-picture operators literally obey the classical equations of motion. This is the cleanest possible illustration of why Ehrenfest's theorem looks so much like Newton's second law: in the Heisenberg picture, it practically is Newton's second law, just promoted to an operator equation.

The two pictures are bookkeeping choices, not different physics — a common analogy is an ordinary clock (hands move, numbers fixed — the Schrödinger picture) versus a clock with the hands fixed and the numbers rotating backward underneath them (the Heisenberg picture). Either way, the clock reads the same time; either picture gives the same $\langle Q\rangle$ at every instant. We won't need the Heisenberg picture again in this course, but recognizing the name and the core idea — operators evolve, not states — will help if you encounter it later (e.g. in the interaction picture used for time-dependent perturbation theory).

Practice Problems

Problem 1 — Verify Ehrenfest's theorem for the harmonic oscillator
A particle of mass $m$ moves in the potential $V(x)=\tfrac12 m\omega^2x^2$. Use the two specialized Ehrenfest results derived above to show that $\langle x\rangle(t)$ obeys the classical equation of motion for a mass on a spring, $\ddot{\langle x\rangle} = -\omega^2\langle x\rangle$.
Show solution

From the general position result, $d\langle x\rangle/dt = \langle p\rangle/m$. From the general momentum result, $d\langle p\rangle/dt = -\langle\partial V/\partial x\rangle = -\langle m\omega^2 x\rangle = -m\omega^2\langle x\rangle$.

Differentiate the first equation once more with respect to time and substitute the second:

$$\frac{d^2\langle x\rangle}{dt^2} = \frac{1}{m}\frac{d\langle p\rangle}{dt} = \frac{1}{m}\big(-m\omega^2\langle x\rangle\big) = -\omega^2\langle x\rangle$$

This is exactly the classical simple-harmonic-oscillator equation, now holding for the expectation value $\langle x\rangle(t)$ regardless of what quantum state the particle is in — a clean illustration of Ehrenfest's theorem (and consistent with the exact Heisenberg-picture result $\hat x_H(t)=\hat x_H(0)\cos\omega t + \hat p_H(0)\sin(\omega t)/(m\omega)$ quoted in the Heisenberg-picture section).

Problem 2 — A symmetry argument for zero dipole moment
Without doing any integral, explain why any energy eigenstate of a symmetric potential $V(x)=V(-x)$ must have $\langle x\rangle = 0$. Then explain why the hydrogen-atom ground state has $\langle\mathbf p_e\rangle=0$ for its electric dipole moment, again with no calculation (Griffiths Problem 6.13(a)).
Show solution

Since $V(x)=V(-x)$, the Hamiltonian commutes with parity, so every non-degenerate bound eigenstate $\psi_n$ can be chosen to have definite parity $\alpha=\pm1$: $\hat\Pi\psi_n = \alpha\psi_n$. The expectation value $\langle x\rangle = \langle n|\hat x|n\rangle$ is exactly the "same-state" case of the selection-rule argument derived above, with $a=b=n$ and $\alpha=\beta$, so $\alpha\beta=\alpha^2=+1$ automatically (any number squared is $+1$ here, since $\alpha=\pm1$). The vanishing condition $(1+\alpha\beta)\langle n|\hat x|n\rangle=0$ then forces $\langle n|\hat x|n\rangle=0$ regardless of which parity $\psi_n$ has — same parity against itself always kills a matrix element of the odd operator $\hat x$.

The hydrogen ground state $\psi_{100}$ is a central-potential eigenstate with $\ell=0$, so by the $(-1)^\ell$ result (Griffiths Problem 6.1(c)) it has definite parity $+1$ (even) — and, being the ground state, it is automatically non-degenerate, so the argument above applies directly: $\langle\mathbf p_e\rangle = q\langle\mathbf r\rangle = 0$, with no radial integral needed.

Problem 3 — When does parity not apply?
Consider the ordinary (asymmetric) infinite square well from Topic 2, with walls at $x=0$ and $x=L$, so $V(x)=0$ for $0<x<L$ and $V(x)=\infty$ otherwise. Is $[\hat H,\hat\Pi]=0$ for this potential? Are its eigenfunctions $\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ eigenstates of parity? Contrast with the symmetric well centered at the origin.
Show solution

Check the symmetry condition directly: is $V(-x)=V(x)$? Take a point just inside the well, say $x=L/4$ (where $V=0$); then $-x=-L/4$ is outside $[0,L]$, where $V=\infty$. So $V(-x)\neq V(x)$ — this potential is not symmetric about the origin, and $[\hat H,\hat\Pi]\neq0$. Parity is simply not a good quantum number for this system: $\hat H$ and $\hat\Pi$ do not share simultaneous eigenstates, so there is no reason to expect $\psi_n(x)=\sqrt{2/L}\sin(n\pi x/L)$ to be even or odd about $x=0$ — and indeed it is not; $\psi_n(-x)$ isn't even defined inside the physical domain of the problem in any useful sense, since the well itself isn't symmetric under $x\to-x$.

Compare this with the symmetric well (walls at $\pm a/2$) referenced in the anchor box above: there, $V(-x)=V(x)$ holds exactly, $[\hat H,\hat\Pi]=0$, and the eigenfunctions genuinely do alternate even/odd. The lesson: before invoking any parity argument (selection rule, "this matrix element must vanish," etc.) you must first verify that the specific potential in the problem is symmetric about the point you're taking inversion around. Symmetric-looking wells are not automatically symmetric about the origin — it depends entirely on where you've put your coordinate origin.

Problem 4 — Two-particle momentum conservation as a Noether statement
Two particles of mass $m_1,m_2$ interact via a potential that depends only on their separation, $V(|x_1-x_2|)$, so $\hat H = \frac{\hat p_1^2}{2m_1}+\frac{\hat p_2^2}{2m_2}+V(|\hat x_1-\hat x_2|)$. Using the general Noether pattern from this page, explain why the total momentum $\hat P=\hat p_1+\hat p_2$ is conserved, even though the individual momenta $\hat p_1,\hat p_2$ are not.
Show solution

The two-particle translation operator that shifts both coordinates by $a$ is $\hat T(a)=\exp[-ia\hat P/\hbar]$ with $\hat P=\hat p_1+\hat p_2$ — this is the generator of the "translate everything together" symmetry. Acting on a wave function, $\hat T(a)\psi(x_1,x_2) = \psi(x_1-a,x_2-a)$. Under this simultaneous shift, the separation $x_1-x_2$ is unchanged ($ (x_1-a)-(x_2-a) = x_1-x_2$), so $V(|x_1-x_2|)$ is invariant — the Hamiltonian commutes with $\hat T(a)$ for every $a$, i.e. the system has continuous translational symmetry under this simultaneous shift. By the Noether pattern (symmetry ⇒ generator commutes with $H$ ⇒ Ehrenfest's theorem ⇒ generator conserved), $[\hat H,\hat P]=0$ and hence $d\langle P\rangle/dt=0$: total momentum is conserved.

By contrast, shifting only $x_1$ (holding $x_2$ fixed) changes the separation $x_1-x_2$ and therefore changes $V(|x_1-x_2|)$ whenever $V$ is not constant — that transformation is generated by $\hat p_1$ alone, is not a symmetry of $H$, so $[\hat H,\hat p_1]\neq0$ in general and $\hat p_1$ individually is not conserved. Physically, this is the quantum-mechanical statement of Newton's third law: the particles can trade momentum back and forth through their mutual interaction, but the total is protected because the interaction itself only ever depends on their relative position.

Common Pitfalls & Self-Check

⚠️ A note on this section

No solution PDF was exported for Homework 6 (the assignment mapped to this topic). The self-check items below are general instructor-style guidance on where students typically go wrong with this material — they are not grader comments transcribed from an actual graded set, and none of the specific numbers below should be read as referring to a real submitted homework.

  • Dropping the explicit-time-dependence term. The full Ehrenfest formula has two pieces, $d\langle Q\rangle/dt = \frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[H,Q]\rangle + \langle\partial Q/\partial t\rangle$. For $\hat x$, $\hat p$, and $\hat\Pi$ the second term is always zero (none of these operators has explicit time dependence), which is why it silently disappears in most of this page's derivations — but it does not disappear in general, and dropping it reflexively for every operator is a common shortcut that fails the moment you meet a genuinely time-dependent operator or Hamiltonian.
  • Sign errors in $[H,\hat x]$ versus $[H,\hat p]$. Remember which piece of $H$ contributes to which commutator: the kinetic term $\hat p^2/2m$ is what survives in $[H,\hat x]$ (it doesn't commute with $\hat x$), while the potential term $V(\hat x)$ is what survives in $[H,\hat p]$ (it doesn't commute with $\hat p$) — the pieces that trivially commute with the operator in question drop out, and it's easy to keep the wrong piece by muscle memory.
  • Assuming every potential has parity eigenstates. Parity conservation requires $V(-x)=V(x)$ about your chosen origin — see Practice Problem 3 above. A well that "looks symmetric" but is shifted away from the origin does not automatically give even/odd eigenfunctions. Always check the symmetry condition explicitly before invoking any parity-based selection rule.
  • Confusing "parity is conserved" with "the wave function must be symmetric." Parity conservation means that if a state starts as a pure parity eigenstate, it stays one. It says nothing about a generic superposition of even and odd states — such a superposition is not an eigenstate of $\hat\Pi$ at all, and asking "is it even or odd?" is simply the wrong question for it.
  • Treating the Heisenberg picture as "different physics." The Schrödinger and Heisenberg pictures are equivalent bookkeeping conventions — every measurable expectation value must come out identical either way. If a calculation done in one picture disagrees with the same calculation in the other, the error is in the algebra, not in quantum mechanics.
  • Reusing 2nd-edition solution manuals. As flagged in the Overview above, Chapter 6 ("Symmetries & Conservation Laws") is new to the 3rd edition. Old solution sets, problem-numbering schemes, and online solution manuals keyed to earlier Griffiths editions will not line up with this material at all — verify against your own 3rd-edition textbook.
