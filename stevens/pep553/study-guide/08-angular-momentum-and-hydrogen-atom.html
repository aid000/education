Overview: The Hydrogen Atom, In Full

This page covers syllabus lectures 13-1 through 14-2 — the module "Quantum Mechanics in 3 Dimensions and the Hydrogen Atom Problem." It maps to Homework #7 and corresponds to Griffiths & Schroeter (3rd ed.) Chapter 4: §4.1 (the Schrödinger equation in spherical coordinates, the angular and radial equations), §4.2 (the hydrogen atom's radial wave function and spectrum), §4.3 (angular momentum eigenvalues and eigenfunctions), and §4.4 (spin, including Example 4.3 on Larmor precession).

Everything up to now has been one-dimensional. This unit does two things at once: it promotes the machinery of quantum mechanics to three spatial dimensions, and it solves — exactly, in closed form — the single most important exactly-solvable problem in all of physics: the nonrelativistic Schrödinger-Coulomb model of the hydrogen atom. "Exactly solved" means exactly that model, not the real atom down to arbitrary precision — fine structure, spin-orbit coupling, hyperfine splitting, and the Lamb shift (a QED effect) are all real, measurable corrections layered on top of what's derived here. Along the way we discover that angular momentum, which you already met in outline form as a conserved quantity in Topic 7, has a rich quantized structure of its own, and that nature supplies a second, wholly non-classical kind of angular momentum — spin — that has nothing to do with motion through space at all.

⭐ This unit is the single most heavily examined topic on the final

  • Problem 2 (35 pts): a hydrogen atom prepared in a three-term superposition of $|n,l,m\rangle$ energy eigenstates, asking for the normalization constant, and the possible outcomes/probabilities/expectation values of $H$, $L^2$, and $L_z$. The final exam page fully solves its specific numbers but explicitly sends you here for "the general method in full generality" — that method, worked through a fresh example of its own, is built from scratch in the Worked Example: Superposition section below.
  • Problem 4 (30 pts): "Study Example 4.3 on Larmor precession in Griffiths. Then solve Griffiths Problem 4.35." The final exam page reproduces the physical picture but sends you here for "the explicit computation of $\langle S_x(t)\rangle$, $\langle S_y(t)\rangle$, $\langle S_z(t)\rangle$ from the time-evolved spinor" — that full derivation is in the Worked Example: Larmor Precession section below.

This page builds the material in the order you need it, each stage a prerequisite for the next:

  • Two-body → one-body reduction — the physical setup: hydrogen is really an electron and a proton, and the first job is showing rigorously why we're allowed to treat it as one particle orbiting a fixed center.
  • Separating the 3D Schrödinger equation in spherical coordinates — the angular equation (spherical harmonics) and the radial equation, leading to the Bohr energy spectrum $E_n = E_1/n^2$.
  • Angular momentum operators — deriving the $L^2$ and $L_z$ eigenvalues by the same ladder-operator method you already used on the quantum harmonic oscillator in Topic 3.
  • Spin-1/2 — a genuinely new kind of angular momentum, the Pauli matrices, and the two flagship worked examples described above.

Setting Up: Reducing the Two-Body Problem to One Body

Why this comes first

Griffiths' treatment of hydrogen in §4.2 quietly writes down a one-body Schrödinger equation for "the electron," with the proton fixed at the origin — but a hydrogen atom is actually two particles (an electron and a proton) interacting through the Coulomb force, each free to move. Before solving anything, it's worth seeing exactly why the one-body treatment is legitimate, because the same reduction reappears constantly in physics (positronium, excitons in semiconductors, diatomic molecules, planetary two-body orbits).

Let $\mathbf r_1,\mathbf p_1$ be the position and momentum of the electron (mass $m_1$) and $\mathbf r_2,\mathbf p_2$ the position and momentum of the proton (mass $m_2$), interacting via a central potential $V(|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|)$ (Coulomb, for hydrogen). The classical Hamiltonian for the pair is

$$H_{1\text{-}2} = \frac{|\mathbf p_1|^2}{2m_1} + \frac{|\mathbf p_2|^2}{2m_2} + V(|\mathbf r_1-\mathbf r_2|)$$

Written in terms of $(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf p_1;\mathbf r_2,\mathbf p_2)$, this Hamiltonian is not separable — the potential couples the two position vectors together. The fix is a coordinate transformation to center-of-mass and relative coordinates:

$$\mathbf R \equiv \frac{m_1\mathbf r_1+m_2\mathbf r_2}{m_1+m_2}, \qquad \mathbf r \equiv \mathbf r_2-\mathbf r_1$$

with conjugate momenta $\mathbf P \equiv \mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2$ (total momentum) and a relative momentum $\mathbf p$ to be determined below.

Deriving the separated Hamiltonian

Inverting the coordinate transformation gives $\mathbf r_1=\mathbf R-\frac{m_2}{M}\mathbf r$ and $\mathbf r_2=\mathbf R+\frac{m_1}{M}\mathbf r$, where $M\equiv m_1+m_2$. Differentiating with respect to time,

$$\dot{\mathbf r}_1 = \dot{\mathbf R} - \frac{m_2}{M}\dot{\mathbf r}, \qquad \dot{\mathbf r}_2 = \dot{\mathbf R} + \frac{m_1}{M}\dot{\mathbf r}$$

Substitute into the classical kinetic energy $T = \tfrac12 m_1\dot{\mathbf r}_1^2 + \tfrac12 m_2\dot{\mathbf r}_2^2$ and expand each square:

$$m_1\dot{\mathbf r}_1^2 = m_1\left[\dot{\mathbf R}^2 - \frac{2m_2}{M}\dot{\mathbf R}\cdot\dot{\mathbf r} + \frac{m_2^2}{M^2}\dot{\mathbf r}^2\right], \qquad m_2\dot{\mathbf r}_2^2 = m_2\left[\dot{\mathbf R}^2 + \frac{2m_1}{M}\dot{\mathbf R}\cdot\dot{\mathbf r} + \frac{m_1^2}{M^2}\dot{\mathbf r}^2\right]$$

Adding these, the cross terms are $-\frac{2m_1m_2}{M}\dot{\mathbf R}\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}$ and $+\frac{2m_1m_2}{M}\dot{\mathbf R}\cdot\dot{\mathbf r}$ — they cancel exactly, which is the whole point of choosing $\mathbf R$ this way rather than, say, the simple average $(\mathbf r_1+\mathbf r_2)/2$. What remains is

$$m_1\dot{\mathbf r}_1^2+m_2\dot{\mathbf r}_2^2 = M\dot{\mathbf R}^2 + \frac{m_1m_2(m_1+m_2)}{M^2}\dot{\mathbf r}^2 = M\dot{\mathbf R}^2 + \frac{m_1m_2}{M}\dot{\mathbf r}^2$$

Defining the reduced mass $\mu \equiv \dfrac{m_1m_2}{m_1+m_2}$, the total kinetic energy separates cleanly: $T = \tfrac12 M\dot{\mathbf R}^2 + \tfrac12\mu\dot{\mathbf r}^2$. Identifying the conjugate momenta $\mathbf P \equiv M\dot{\mathbf R} = \mathbf p_1+\mathbf p_2$ (confirming $\mathbf P$ really is the total momentum) and $\mathbf p \equiv \mu\dot{\mathbf r}$ (a short substitution shows this equals $(m_1\mathbf p_2-m_2\mathbf p_1)/M$), the Hamiltonian becomes

$$\boxed{H_{1\text{-}2} = \frac{P^2}{2M} + \frac{p^2}{2\mu} + V(r) \equiv H_{CM} + H_{rel}}$$

a sum of a free-particle piece (the atom's center of mass drifting through space) and a one-body-like piece (a fictitious particle of mass $\mu$ orbiting a fixed force center) — exactly separable.

Promoting to operators, and why the reduction survives quantization

The canonical commutators for the original two-particle operators are $[\hat r_{1j},\hat p_{1j}]=i\hbar$ and $[\hat r_{2j},\hat p_{2j}]=i\hbar$ ($j=x,y,z$), with every other pair of operators commuting. A direct (if slightly tedious) substitution using the coordinate transformation above shows that the new pairs inherit exactly the same canonical structure:

$$[\hat R_j,\hat P_j] = i\hbar, \qquad [\hat r_j,\hat p_j]=i\hbar, \qquad j=x,y,z$$

with all cross-commutators between $(\hat{\mathbf R},\hat{\mathbf P})$ and $(\hat{\mathbf r},\hat{\mathbf p})$ vanishing. This is the crucial fact: it means $[\hat H_{CM},\hat H_{rel}]=0$, so by the separable-Hamiltonian theorem (the same logic used throughout this course whenever $H=H_1+H_2$ with $[H_1,H_2]=0$), the full two-body wave function factors,

$$\psi(\mathbf r_1,\mathbf r_2) = \psi_{CM}(\mathbf R)\,\psi_{rel}(\mathbf r)$$

and the full time-independent Schrödinger equation splits into two independent one-body equations:

$$\frac{\hat P^2}{2M}\psi_{CM}(\mathbf R) = E_{CM}\,\psi_{CM}(\mathbf R), \qquad \left[\frac{\hat p^2}{2\mu}+V(\hat r)\right]\psi_{rel}(\mathbf r) = E_{rel}\,\psi_{rel}(\mathbf r)$$

The first equation is just the free-particle TISE in three dimensions for the atom's center of mass (relevant to atom trapping and laser cooling, where you manipulate exactly this center-of-mass wave function). The second equation is the one Griffiths solves in §4.2 — a single fictitious particle of mass $\mu$ bound by the central potential $V(r)$. For hydrogen, $V(r) = -e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0 r$ and $\mu = m_e m_p/(m_e+m_p) \approx m_e$ to about one part in 1800 (since $m_p\approx 1836\,m_e$), which is exactly why Griffiths' one-body treatment with "the electron mass" $m_e$ is such an excellent approximation — but the reduction above is what justifies using $m_e$ (or, more precisely, $\mu$) in the first place rather than something else.

This same center-of-mass/relative-coordinate reduction is the standard first step for excitons in semiconductor physics — a bound electron-hole pair behaves exactly like a hydrogen atom, with the reduced mass built from the electron and hole effective masses ($\mu^{-1}=m_e^{*-1}+m_h^{*-1}$) and the Coulomb interaction screened by the host material's dielectric constant. The resulting "exciton Bohr radius" and binding energy — computed by literally recycling the Bohr formula below with $m_e\to\mu$ and $\epsilon_0\to\epsilon_0\epsilon_r$ — set the length and energy scales for optoelectronic devices like LEDs and photodetectors.

The 3D Schrödinger Equation: Separation and the Hydrogen Spectrum

Spherical coordinates and separation of variables

Having reduced hydrogen to a one-body problem, drop the "rel" subscripts and work with the ordinary 3D time-independent Schrödinger equation for a central potential $V(r)$:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\nabla^2\psi + V(r)\psi = E\psi$$

In spherical coordinates $(r,\theta,\phi)$ the Laplacian is $\nabla^2 = \frac{1}{r^2}\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\left(r^2\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{r^2\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2}{\partial\phi^2}$. Seeking product solutions $\psi(r,\theta,\phi) = R(r)Y(\theta,\phi)$ and separating (a function of $r$ alone set equal to a function of $\theta,\phi$ alone, hence both equal to a constant, written suggestively as $l(l+1)$) splits the equation into a radial equation and an angular equation:

$$\frac{1}{R}\frac{d}{dr}\left(r^2\frac{dR}{dr}\right) - \frac{2mr^2}{\hbar^2}\left[V(r)-E\right] = l(l+1)$$
$$\frac{1}{\sin\theta}\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\left(\sin\theta\frac{\partial Y}{\partial\theta}\right) + \frac{1}{\sin^2\theta}\frac{\partial^2 Y}{\partial\phi^2} = -l(l+1)\,Y$$

The remarkable feature of this split: the angular equation is completely independent of the potential $V(r)$. Every central-potential problem — the infinite spherical well, hydrogen, an isotropic 3D harmonic oscillator, anything — shares exactly the same angular solutions. Only the radial equation cares what $V(r)$ actually is.

The angular equation's solutions: spherical harmonics (qualitative)

Separating $Y(\theta,\phi)=\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\phi)$ splits the angular equation once more. The $\phi$-equation is easy: $d^2\Phi/d\phi^2 = -m^2\Phi \Rightarrow \Phi(\phi)=e^{im\phi}$, and requiring $\Phi$ to be single-valued under $\phi\to\phi+2\pi$ forces $m$ to be an integer. The $\theta$-equation is the associated Legendre equation; its physically acceptable solutions (finite at $\theta=0,\pi$) are the associated Legendre functions $P_l^m(\cos\theta)$, which exist only for $l=0,1,2,\dots$ a non-negative integer, with $m$ restricted to $-l,-l+1,\dots,l-1,l$ (so $2l+1$ values of $m$ for each $l$). We will not re-derive the Legendre-polynomial machinery in detail here — the physically important upshot is what matters, and it will be re-derived independently and more transparently by the algebraic ladder-operator method in the next section. The normalized product $\Theta(\theta)\Phi(\phi)$ is called a spherical harmonic,

$$Y_l^m(\theta,\phi) = \sqrt{\frac{(2l+1)}{4\pi}\frac{(l-m)!}{(l+m)!}}\;e^{im\phi}\,P_l^m(\cos\theta)$$

and these form a complete orthonormal set on the sphere: $\int Y_l^{m*}Y_{l'}^{m'}\,d\Omega = \delta_{ll'}\delta_{mm'}$. A few low-order examples: $Y_0^0 = 1/\sqrt{4\pi}$ (spherically symmetric — an "s state"), $Y_1^0 = \sqrt{3/4\pi}\cos\theta$ and $Y_1^{\pm 1} = \mp\sqrt{3/8\pi}\sin\theta\,e^{\pm i\phi}$ (the three "p states"). Griffiths Table 4.3 lists the first several explicitly if you need to plug in numbers.

The radial equation and the centrifugal term

Substituting $u(r)\equiv rR(r)$ turns the radial equation into a form identical to the 1D Schrödinger equation you've used all course:

$$-\frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{d^2u}{dr^2} + \left[V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2}{2m}\frac{l(l+1)}{r^2}\right]u = Eu, \qquad \int_0^\infty|u|^2\,dr=1$$

with an effective potential $V_{\text{eff}} = V(r) + \frac{\hbar^2 l(l+1)}{2mr^2}$ — the extra piece is the centrifugal term, the quantum analog of the classical centrifugal barrier that pushes an orbiting particle away from the origin whenever it carries nonzero angular momentum. This is exactly the equation the guided lecture notes ("TwoBodytoOneBody.pdf") arrive at for the relative-motion problem after the two-body reduction above, and it is identical in form to Griffiths Eq. 4.37.

Solving the radial equation for hydrogen: the Bohr formula

For hydrogen, $V(r) = -\dfrac{e^2}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\dfrac{1}{r}$. Rather than reproduce the full Frobenius (power-series) solution line by line — it is a close cousin of the harmonic-oscillator power-series method from Topic 3 — we summarize the key waypoints, which are exactly the ones the "get from setup to spectrum" logic of this course always follows:

  • Asymptotics first. Defining $\kappa\equiv\sqrt{-2mE}/\hbar$ (real for bound states, $E<0$) and rescaling $\rho\equiv\kappa r$, the equation becomes $\dfrac{d^2u}{d\rho^2} = \left[1-\dfrac{\rho_0}{\rho}+\dfrac{l(l+1)}{\rho^2}\right]u$ with $\rho_0\equiv m e^2/(2\pi\epsilon_0\hbar^2\kappa)$. As $\rho\to\infty$ the constant term dominates, forcing $u\sim e^{-\rho}$ (rejecting the blowing-up solution $e^{+\rho}$); as $\rho\to 0$ the centrifugal term dominates, forcing $u\sim \rho^{l+1}$ (rejecting the singular solution $\rho^{-l}$).
  • Peel off the asymptotics. Writing $u(\rho) = \rho^{l+1}e^{-\rho}v(\rho)$ and substituting turns the equation into one solvable by a power series $v(\rho)=\sum_j c_j\rho^j$, with a two-term recursion relating $c_{j+1}$ to $c_j$.
  • The series must terminate. Left unconstrained, the series behaves like $e^{2\rho}$ at large $\rho$ — not normalizable, since combined with the prefactor $e^{-\rho}$ this would blow up as $e^{+\rho}$, exactly the rejected asymptotic behavior. The only way to avoid this is for the series to terminate after a finite number of terms: there must exist an integer $N$ with $c_{N-1}\ne 0$ but $c_N=0$. Applying this to the recursion relation forces
$$\rho_0 = 2(N+l) \equiv 2n, \qquad n\equiv N+l = 1,2,3,\ldots$$

Since $\rho_0$ was defined directly in terms of $E$ (through $\kappa$), this quantization condition on $\rho_0$ is the energy quantization. Working through the algebra (Griffiths Eqs. 4.68–4.70) gives the celebrated Bohr formula:

$$\boxed{E_n = -\left[\frac{m_ee^4}{2\hbar^2}\left(\frac{1}{4\pi\epsilon_0}\right)^2\right]\frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{E_1}{n^2}, \qquad n=1,2,3,\ldots}$$

Because $n\equiv N+l$ with $N=1,2,3,\ldots$ and $l=0,1,2,\ldots$, the allowed values of $l$ for a given $n$ are $l=0,1,\ldots,n-1$ — and for each $l$ there are $2l+1$ values of $m$. Summing $(2l+1)$ over $l=0$ to $n-1$ gives a total degeneracy of $n^2$ for the energy level $E_n$ — larger than the $(2l+1)$-fold degeneracy you'd expect from spherical symmetry alone (which is all you'd get for a generic central potential, like the infinite spherical well). This "accidental" extra degeneracy — energy depending on $n$ alone, with no dependence on $l$ — is special to the exact $1/r$ Coulomb potential, and is the reason $\psi_{200}$ and $\psi_{210}$ (different $l$, same $n=2$) are exactly degenerate in hydrogen.

⚠️ $n$, $N$, and $l$ are not independent, but they're also not the same thing
It's tempting to think of $n$ as "the" quantum number and $l,m$ as afterthoughts, but the derivation above shows the actual logical structure: $l$ arises first, from the angular equation (and is common to every central potential); the radial quantization then introduces a second integer $N$ (the number of terms in the terminating series $v(\rho)=\sum_{j=0}^{N-1}c_j\rho^j$, i.e. a polynomial of degree $N-1$); and $n\equiv N+l$ is a relabeling introduced purely because it happens to be the combination that the energy depends on. Be careful with the node count specifically: since $u(\rho)=\rho^{l+1}e^{-\rho}v(\rho)$ and neither $\rho^{l+1}$ nor $e^{-\rho}$ vanishes for $\rho>0$, the number of radial nodes equals the number of roots of the degree-$(N-1)$ polynomial $v$, which is $N-1$ — not $N$. Equivalently, in terms of the more familiar labels, the radial node count is $n-l-1$. Check it against the ground state: $n=1,l=0\Rightarrow N=1$, and $N-1=0$ radial nodes, matching the well-known fact that $\psi_{100}$ has none. For a generic central potential (like the infinite spherical well of Griffiths Example 4.1) there is no such simple relabeling — $N$ and $l$ enter the energy independently, $E_{Nl}=\hbar^2\beta_{Nl}^2/2ma^2$, and you cannot get away with a single quantum number. The clean $n=N+l$ split is special to hydrogen's exact $1/r$ potential.

Angular Momentum Operators: Deriving $L^2$ and $L_z$ Eigenvalues

⚓ Memory Anchor: This Is the QHO Ladder Method, Recycled

In Topic 3 you found the harmonic oscillator's energy spectrum without ever solving a differential equation, purely by exploiting the commutator $[\hat a,\hat a^\dagger]=1$: define ladder operators, show they shift an eigenvalue up or down by a fixed unit, then argue that the ladder must have a bottom rung (a normalizable state annihilated by the lowering operator) to avoid negative-norm contradictions. The exact same logical skeleton — define ladder operators from a commutator, show they shift a quantum number by one unit, argue for a top and/or bottom rung — is about to be applied to a completely different operator algebra (angular momentum instead of position/momentum), and it will hand us the eigenvalues of $L^2$ and $L_z$ with no differential equation in sight. If any step below feels unfamiliar, go back and compare it side-by-side with the QHO derivation.

The commutators (given, and where they come from)

Classically $\mathbf L = \mathbf r\times\mathbf p$, i.e. $L_x=yp_z-zp_y$, $L_y=zp_x-xp_z$, $L_z=xp_y-yp_x$. Promoting to operators via the canonical commutator $[\hat x,\hat p_x]=i\hbar$ (with all "unlike" position/momentum pairs commuting), a direct calculation — expanding $[L_x,L_y]$ term by term and using the canonical commutators to collapse most of the cross terms to zero — gives the fundamental angular-momentum algebra:

$$[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z, \qquad [L_y,L_z]=i\hbar L_x, \qquad [L_z,L_x]=i\hbar L_y$$

These three relations are exactly the ones supplied on the course reference sheet, and they are the entire algebraic seed for everything that follows — notice that, unlike $\hat x$ and $\hat p$, the three components of $\mathbf L$ do not all commute with each other: you cannot know $L_x$, $L_y$, and $L_z$ simultaneously with arbitrary precision. But the operator $L^2\equiv L_x^2+L_y^2+L_z^2$ (the square of the total angular momentum) commutes with every individual component,

$$[L^2,L_x]=[L^2,L_y]=[L^2,L_z]=0$$

so $L^2$ and (conventionally) $L_z$ can be assigned simultaneous eigenstates — exactly the pair of "compatible observables" whose eigenvalues we now derive.

Ladder operators and how they shift $L_z$

Notation note: starting here, the ladder-operator argument below uses only the commutation relations $[L_x,L_y]=i\hbar L_z$ etc. — it never again refers back to $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$. That means everything through the boxed result a few steps down applies to any operator triple obeying this algebra, not just orbital angular momentum specifically (this is exactly why it also produces spin's half-integer eigenvalues in the next section). Many textbooks mark this genericness by switching notation to $\mathbf J,j,m$ for the abstract case and reserving $\mathbf L,l,m_l$ for orbital angular momentum specifically; this page keeps the $L,l,m$ labels throughout for continuity with the $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ setup above, but keep the distinction in mind — it's exactly the "which constraint comes from where" question the next subsection addresses.

Define, in exact parallel to $\hat a,\hat a^\dagger$ from Topic 3, the raising and lowering combinations:

$$L_\pm \equiv L_x \pm iL_y$$

Their commutator with $L_z$ follows directly from the fundamental algebra: $[L_z,L_\pm] = [L_z,L_x]\pm i[L_z,L_y] = i\hbar L_y \pm i(-i\hbar L_x) = \pm\hbar(L_x\pm iL_y)$, i.e.

$$[L_z,L_\pm] = \pm\hbar L_\pm, \qquad [L^2,L_\pm]=0$$

Now suppose $f$ is a simultaneous eigenfunction, $L^2f=\lambda f$ and $L_zf=\mu f$. Because $[L^2,L_\pm]=0$, the function $L_\pm f$ is also an eigenfunction of $L^2$ with the same eigenvalue $\lambda$ — raising/lowering never changes the total angular momentum. And because $L_z(L_\pm f) = (L_zL_\pm - L_\pm L_z)f + L_\pm L_zf = \pm\hbar L_\pm f + \mu L_\pm f = (\mu\pm\hbar)(L_\pm f)$, applying $L_\pm$ shifts the $L_z$-eigenvalue by exactly $\pm\hbar$ — precisely the ladder-rung structure from the QHO, now with $\hbar$ playing the role of the QHO's unit energy quantum $\hbar\omega$.

The ladder must terminate: deriving $\lambda = \hbar^2 l(l+1)$

Exactly as with the QHO's lowering operator hitting a floor at $\phi_0$, this ladder cannot climb or descend forever: the $z$-component can never exceed the total (a short argument using $\langle L_x^2\rangle,\langle L_y^2\rangle\ge 0$ shows $\lambda\ge\mu^2$, so $|\mu|$ is bounded). There must be a top rung $f_t$ with $L_+f_t=0$ and a bottom rung $f_b$ with $L_-f_b=0$. Write the top-rung eigenvalue as $\hbar l$ (anticipating the standard letter) and the bottom-rung eigenvalue as $\hbar\bar l$: $L_zf_t=\hbar l f_t$, $L_zf_b=\hbar\bar l f_b$.

The algebraic identity $L^2 = L_\mp L_\pm + L_z^2 \pm \hbar L_z$ (obtained by direct multiplication of $L_\pm L_\mp = L_x^2+L_y^2 \mp i[L_x,L_y] = L^2-L_z^2\mp i(i\hbar L_z)$ and rearranging) lets us evaluate $\lambda$ two different ways. At the top rung, using $L_-L_+f_t = L_-(0) = 0$:

$$L^2f_t = (L_-L_+ + L_z^2 + \hbar L_z)f_t = (0+\hbar^2l^2+\hbar^2l)f_t = \hbar^2l(l+1)\,f_t \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda=\hbar^2l(l+1)$$

At the bottom rung, using $L_+L_-f_b=0$:

$$L^2f_b = (L_+L_- + L_z^2 - \hbar L_z)f_b = (0+\hbar^2\bar l^2-\hbar^2\bar l)f_b = \hbar^2\bar l(\bar l-1)\,f_b \quad\Rightarrow\quad \lambda=\hbar^2\bar l(\bar l-1)$$

Equating the two expressions for $\lambda$: $l(l+1)=\bar l(\bar l-1)$, whose only physically sensible solution (the alternative $\bar l=l+1$ would put the bottom rung above the top rung) is

$$\bar l = -l$$

So the ladder of $L_z$-eigenvalues runs symmetrically from $-\hbar l$ to $+\hbar l$ in unit steps of $\hbar$ — meaning the top-to-bottom span $2l$ must itself be a non-negative integer, so $l$ is either a non-negative integer or a non-negative half-integer. Relabeling the eigenvalue as $m$ (an integer count of rungs above the bottom, i.e. $m=-l,-l+1,\ldots,l-1,l$), the final result is:

$$\boxed{L^2f_l^m = \hbar^2l(l+1)f_l^m, \qquad L_zf_l^m = \hbar m\,f_l^m, \qquad l=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\ldots;\quad m=-l,\ldots,l}$$

Every step above used only the commutator algebra — no differential equation, no spherical harmonics, no Legendre polynomials. This is a genuinely more powerful and more general result than the separation-of-variables solution in the previous section, and — critically — it allows half-integer $l$, which the coordinate-space (spherical-harmonic) derivation never could (single-valuedness under $\phi\to\phi+2\pi$ forces $m$, and hence $l$, to be an integer for orbital angular momentum specifically). The half-integer solutions aren't spurious — they are exactly what makes spin possible, as the next section shows.

Why orbital $l$ is restricted to integers

The purely algebraic derivation above permits $l=0,\tfrac12,1,\tfrac32,\ldots$ — but when $L$ is specifically orbital angular momentum (i.e. $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$, realized as a differential operator acting on functions of position), an extra physical constraint applies. In the coordinate representation $L_z = -i\hbar\,\partial/\partial\phi$, and demanding the eigenfunction be single-valued as $\phi$ advances by $2\pi$ (a point in space is the same point in space, and $\phi\to\phi+2\pi$ must return the same wave function) forces $e^{im\cdot 2\pi}=1$, i.e. $m$ — and hence $l$ — must be an integer. Half-integer $l$ is algebraically consistent but geometrically forbidden for angular momentum built from $\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$: a half-integer-$l$ "wave function" would change sign after a full $2\pi$ rotation, and no ordinary function of position can do that. This single-valuedness argument is precisely what's missing from generic angular momentum $\mathbf J$, and precisely why spin — which is not built from $\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ at all, and so faces no such constraint — is free to take the half-integer value $s=\tfrac12$.

🌀

Spin: A Genuinely New Kind of Angular Momentum

Orbital vs. intrinsic angular momentum

Classically, a rigid body carries orbital angular momentum ($\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$, from the motion of its center of mass) and spin angular momentum ($\mathbf S=I\boldsymbol\omega$, from rotation about its own center of mass) — but classically these are really the same thing at different scales: a spinning top's "spin" is just the sum of the orbital angular momenta of all its constituent atoms circling the rotation axis. Quantum mechanically, this distinction becomes fundamental. An electron's spatial wave function can carry orbital angular momentum $\mathbf L=\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ (described by the spherical harmonics above) — this is a property of the probability distribution's shape and phase, not a classical trajectory; hydrogen stationary states are not tiny planetary orbits. The electron also carries an entirely separate intrinsic angular momentum — spin — that has nothing to do with motion through space at all. It is not a function of $r,\theta,\phi$, it cannot be decomposed into orbital angular momenta of constituent parts (the electron is, as far as we know, a structureless point), and yet it obeys the identical commutation algebra as orbital angular momentum:

$$[S_x,S_y]=i\hbar S_z, \qquad [S_y,S_z]=i\hbar S_x, \qquad [S_z,S_x]=i\hbar S_y$$

Because this is postulated as the defining algebraic property of spin (rather than derived from an $\mathbf r\times\mathbf p$ construction), spin faces no single-valuedness restriction, and the half-integer solutions from the general ladder-operator derivation above are fully physical: $s=0$ ($\pi$ mesons), $s=\tfrac12$ (electrons, protons, neutrons, quarks, all leptons), $s=1$ (photons — though as massless particles, photons don't actually have a full $2s+1=3$-fold multiplet of $m_s$ states at rest the way a massive spin-1 particle would; a physical photon's spin state is restricted to helicity $\pm1$, a detail beyond what this course needs but worth knowing before it causes confusion later), $s=\tfrac32$ ($\Delta$ baryons), and so on. Unlike orbital $l$ (which changes for a given particle depending on its state of motion), $s$ is fixed and immutable for a given species of particle — the electron is always spin-$\tfrac12$, no matter what it's doing.

Spin-1/2: spinors and the Pauli matrices

For $s=\tfrac12$ there are only two eigenstates of $S_z$: $|\tfrac12,\tfrac12\rangle\equiv\chi_+$ ("spin up," $\uparrow$) and $|\tfrac12,-\tfrac12\rangle\equiv\chi_-$ ("spin down," $\downarrow$). The general spin state is a two-component column vector (a spinor),

$$\chi = \binom{a}{b} = a\chi_+ + b\chi_-, \qquad \chi_+=\binom10,\quad \chi_-=\binom01, \qquad |a|^2+|b|^2=1$$

Because the eigenstates aren't functions of position, the spin operators become $2\times 2$ matrices rather than differential operators. Working out their action on $\chi_\pm$ directly from the general ladder formulas $S_\pm|s,m\rangle = \hbar\sqrt{s(s+1)-m(m\pm1)}\,|s,m\pm1\rangle$ (specialized to $s=\tfrac12$: $S_+\chi_-=\hbar\chi_+$, $S_-\chi_+=\hbar\chi_-$, and $S_+\chi_+=S_-\chi_-=0$, since raising/lowering past the top/bottom rung must annihilate the state) and matching matrix elements gives:

$$S_z = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad S_x = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad S_y = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad S^2 = \frac{3\hbar^2}{4}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}$$

Since every component carries a common factor of $\hbar/2$, it's conventional to write $\mathbf S = \frac{\hbar}{2}\boldsymbol\sigma$, where $\boldsymbol\sigma$ are the famous, dimensionless Pauli spin matrices supplied on the course reference sheet:

$$\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$$

All three Pauli matrices (and $S_x,S_y,S_z,S^2$) are Hermitian, as required for observables. The raising/lowering matrices $S_\pm=S_x\pm iS_y$ are not Hermitian — a useful reminder that $S_+,S_-$ are bookkeeping devices for moving between eigenstates, not measurable quantities in their own right, exactly parallel to $\hat a,\hat a^\dagger$ in Topic 3.

Eigenspinors of $S_x$ (needed for Larmor precession below)

Solving the characteristic equation $\det(S_x-\lambda I)=0$ gives eigenvalues $\lambda=\pm\hbar/2$ — the same possible outcomes as for $S_z$, as must be true since $S_x$ and $S_z$ are related by nothing more than a choice of axis label. The (normalized) eigenspinors are

$$\chi_+^{(x)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\binom11 \ \left(\text{eigenvalue}+\frac\hbar2\right), \qquad \chi_-^{(x)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\binom{1}{-1} \ \left(\text{eigenvalue}-\frac\hbar2\right)$$

If a particle is prepared in the definite $S_z$ eigenstate $\chi_+$ and you then measure $S_x$, expanding $\chi_+ = \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\chi_+^{(x)} + \frac{1}{\sqrt2}\chi_-^{(x)}$ shows the outcome is a coin flip: $+\hbar/2$ or $-\hbar/2$, each with probability $1/2$. This is not ignorance of some hidden "true" $x$-component — the particle in state $\chi_+$ simply does not have a well-defined $S_x$, for exactly the same uncertainty-principle reason $\hat x$ and $\hat p$ cannot be simultaneously sharp.

⚠️ Spin is not a tiny classical top
Griffiths (Problem 4.28) has you check this quantitatively: if you model the electron as a classical solid sphere of the "classical electron radius" $r_c=e^2/4\pi\epsilon_0m_ec^2$ and ask how fast its equator would need to move to produce angular momentum $\hbar/2$, the answer comes out faster than the speed of light — and the electron's actual radius is known experimentally to be far smaller than $r_c$, which only makes the required equatorial speed worse. Spin is a genuinely quantum-mechanical, non-classical property; the word "spin" and the picture of a tiny spinning ball are useful mnemonics, not literal descriptions.

Worked Example: Measuring $H$, $L^2$, $L_z$ on a Superposition of Hydrogen States

📋 Why this example exists

final-exam.html Problem 2 asks you to fully analyze a specific three-term hydrogen superposition and explicitly flags one subtlety (two terms sharing the same $L_z$ eigenvalue) as the crux of the problem, then sends you here for "the general method in full generality." The example below uses a different superposition — built the same way, with the same subtlety deliberately built in — so you can see every step of the method once, then apply it yourself to the real exam numbers.

The general method, stated once

Every energy eigenstate $|n,l,m\rangle$ of hydrogen is a simultaneous eigenstate of three mutually compatible operators — $\hat H$ (eigenvalue $E_n=-R/n^2$), $L^2$ (eigenvalue $\hbar^2l(l+1)$), and $L_z$ (eigenvalue $\hbar m$) — because, as shown above, $[\hat H,L^2]=[\hat H,L_z]=[L^2,L_z]=0$ for any central potential. Given a normalized superposition $|\Psi\rangle = \sum_i c_i|n_i,l_i,m_i\rangle$ of such states, the general recipe for any of these three observables $\hat Q\in\{\hat H,L^2,L_z\}$ is always the same four-step process:

  1. Normalize by requiring $\sum_i|c_i|^2=1$ (using orthonormality of the basis kets, which is automatic here since kets with different $n$ are automatically orthogonal, being eigenstates of $\hat H$ with different eigenvalues).
  2. Read off each term's eigenvalue of $\hat Q$ directly from its $|n,l,m\rangle$ label — no integral required, because each basis ket is already an eigenstate of all three operators at once.
  3. Group terms by eigenvalue. If two or more different basis kets happen to share the same eigenvalue of the observable in question, that outcome is degenerate: its total probability is the sum of $|c_i|^2$ over every $i$ sharing that eigenvalue — not just one term's contribution. This is the single most commonly mishandled step, and it is exactly the subtlety both this example and the real exam problem are designed to test.
  4. Compute the expectation value as the probability-weighted sum $\langle Q\rangle = \sum_{\text{outcomes}} P(\text{outcome})\times(\text{eigenvalue})$, using the grouped probabilities from step 3 (equivalently, $\langle Q\rangle=\sum_i|c_i|^2 q_i$ computed term-by-term always gives the same answer — grouping is only needed when you want the individual outcome probabilities, e.g. for part (b)-style "what values might you get" questions).

A concrete example, worked from scratch

Consider a hydrogen atom prepared at $t=0$ in the state

$$|\Psi(0)\rangle = A\Big\{\,i\,|3,2,1\rangle + 2\,|2,1,1\rangle - |1,0,0\rangle\,\Big\}$$

where $|n,l,m\rangle$ are the usual energy eigenstates, $E_n=-R/n^2$. Find: (a) the normalization constant $A$; (b) the possible energy outcomes and their probabilities, and $\langle H\rangle$; (c) the possible $L^2$ outcomes and probabilities, and $\langle L^2\rangle$; (d) the possible $L_z$ outcomes and probabilities, and $\langle L_z\rangle$.

(a) Normalization

The three kets have different $n$ (3, 2, 1), so they are automatically mutually orthogonal (being $\hat H$-eigenstates with distinct eigenvalues) — no cross terms survive in $\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle$:

$$|A|^2\big[|i|^2+|2|^2+|{-1}|^2\big] = |A|^2(1+4+1) = 6|A|^2 = 1 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \boxed{A=\frac{1}{\sqrt6}}$$

(taking $A$ real and positive by convention — the overall phase of $|\Psi\rangle$ carries no physical content).

(b) Energy: outcomes, probabilities, $\langle H\rangle$

Reading $n$ off each ket: the first term has $n=3$ ($E_3=-R/9$), the second $n=2$ ($E_2=-R/4$), the third $n=1$ ($E_1=-R$). All three $n$'s are distinct, so there is no degeneracy to worry about here — each term contributes its own outcome:

$$P(E_3)=|A|^2|i|^2=\frac16, \qquad P(E_2)=|A|^2|2|^2=\frac{4}{6}=\frac23, \qquad P(E_1)=|A|^2|{-1}|^2=\frac16$$

Check: $\frac16+\frac23+\frac16=\frac{1+4+1}{6}=1$. ✓ Then

$$\langle H\rangle = \frac16\left(-\frac R9\right)+\frac23\left(-\frac R4\right)+\frac16(-R) = -R\left(\frac{1}{54}+\frac16+\frac16\right)$$

Common denominator 54: $\frac1{54}+\frac9{54}+\frac9{54}=\frac{19}{54}$, so

$$\boxed{\langle H\rangle = -\frac{19}{54}R}$$

(c) $L^2$: outcomes, probabilities, $\langle L^2\rangle$

Reading $l$ off each ket: term 1 has $l=2$ ($L^2=\hbar^2\cdot2\cdot3=6\hbar^2$), term 2 has $l=1$ ($L^2=2\hbar^2$), term 3 has $l=0$ ($L^2=0$). Again all three $l$ values are distinct, so no grouping is needed:

$$P(L^2=6\hbar^2)=\frac16, \qquad P(L^2=2\hbar^2)=\frac23, \qquad P(L^2=0)=\frac16$$
$$\langle L^2\rangle = \frac16(6\hbar^2)+\frac23(2\hbar^2)+\frac16(0) = \hbar^2+\frac43\hbar^2 = \boxed{\frac{7}{3}\hbar^2}$$
💡 Why the $L^2$ and $H$ probabilities came out numerically identical here

Notice $P(L^2=6\hbar^2)=P(E_3)$, $P(L^2=2\hbar^2)=P(E_2)$, and $P(L^2=0)=P(E_1)$ — the exact same three numbers, $\tfrac16,\tfrac23,\tfrac16$. This is not a general fact about $H$ and $L^2$; it happens here purely because this particular superposition pairs each distinct $n$ with a distinct $l$ in a one-to-one way (term 1: $n{=}3,l{=}2$; term 2: $n{=}2,l{=}1$; term 3: $n{=}1,l{=}0$). If any two terms shared an $n$ but had different $l$'s, the energy probabilities would need grouping by $n$ while the $L^2$ probabilities would still need grouping by $l$ separately, and the two probability distributions would generally differ. Always regroup from scratch for each observable — never assume one observable's probability table transfers to another.

(d) $L_z$: outcomes, probabilities, $\langle L_z\rangle$ — the degenerate case

Reading $m$ off each ket: term 1 ($n{=}3,l{=}2$) has $m=1$; term 2 ($n{=}2,l{=}1$) has $m=1$; term 3 ($n{=}1,l{=}0$) has $m=0$ (forced, since $l=0\Rightarrow m=0$).

⚠️ Two different basis kets share the same $m$ — probabilities add
Terms 1 and 2 are different states — different $n$, different $l$ — but they happen to share $m=1$, and hence the same $L_z$ eigenvalue $\hbar$. Since a measurement of $L_z$ cannot distinguish which basis ket "produced" the outcome $\hbar$, the correct probability is the sum of both terms' individual probabilities, not either one alone: $P(L_z=\hbar) = |A|^2|i|^2 + |A|^2|2|^2 = \tfrac16+\tfrac23=\tfrac56$. Reporting only $\tfrac16$ (from term 1) or only $\tfrac23$ (from term 2) — or, just as wrong, listing $\hbar$ as two separate outcomes with two separate probabilities that don't get combined — is the single most common error on this type of problem, and it is exactly the subtlety final-exam.html Problem 2(f) is testing with its own pair of degenerate $m=0$ terms.
$$P(L_z=\hbar) = |A|^2|i|^2+|A|^2|2|^2 = \frac16+\frac46 = \frac56, \qquad P(L_z=0) = |A|^2|{-1}|^2=\frac16$$

Check: $\frac56+\frac16=1$. ✓ Only two distinct $L_z$ outcomes occur (not three, even though there are three terms):

$$\langle L_z\rangle = \frac56(\hbar) + \frac16(0) = \boxed{\frac{5}{6}\hbar}$$
⚓ Applying this to the real exam problem

final-exam.html Problem 2's state $A\{\tfrac12|4,3,-1\rangle + |2,1,0\rangle - i|1,0,0\rangle\}$ has its own degenerate pair — $|2,1,0\rangle$ and $|1,0,0\rangle$ both carry $m=0$ — which is handled by literally the same step 3 above: identify which terms share an eigenvalue, then sum their probabilities. Every other part of that problem (normalization, reading off $E_n$/$L^2$/$L_z$ eigenvalues, computing expectation values) follows the identical four-step recipe worked in full above.

Worked Example: Larmor Precession (Griffiths Example 4.3)

📋 Why this example exists

final-exam.html Problem 4 assigns Griffiths' Example 4.3 as required reading, then asks you to solve Griffiths Problem 4.35 (a three-part follow-up) — but sends you here for "the explicit computation of $\langle S_x(t)\rangle$, $\langle S_y(t)\rangle$, $\langle S_z(t)\rangle$ from the time-evolved spinor." Below is that full derivation, reproducing Griffiths' Example 4.3 faithfully (this is the assigned reading, so full reproduction is appropriate here) with added annotation at each step.

Setup: a magnetic dipole in a magnetic field

A spinning charged particle is a magnetic dipole, with magnetic dipole moment proportional to its spin, $\boldsymbol\mu=\gamma\mathbf S$, where $\gamma$ (the gyromagnetic ratio) is a constant characteristic of the particle. Classically, a magnetic dipole in a field $\mathbf B$ has energy $H=-\boldsymbol\mu\cdot\mathbf B$ — lowest when the dipole is parallel to the field, just like a compass needle. Promoting this to an operator (with $\mathbf S$ the appropriate spin matrix) gives the Hamiltonian for a spinning charged particle at rest in a field $\mathbf B$:

$$\hat H = -\gamma\mathbf B\cdot\mathbf S$$

Now specialize to a spin-$\tfrac12$ particle at rest in a uniform field along $\hat z$, $\mathbf B=B_0\hat k$:

$$\hat H = -\gamma B_0 S_z = -\frac{\gamma B_0\hbar}{2}\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$$

Because $\hat H$ is just a multiple of $S_z$, its eigenstates are the same as $S_z$'s — $\chi_+$ and $\chi_-$ — with energies

$$E_+ = -\frac{\gamma B_0\hbar}{2}\ (\text{state }\chi_+), \qquad E_- = +\frac{\gamma B_0\hbar}{2}\ (\text{state }\chi_-)$$

Which of these is actually the lower energy depends on the sign of $\gamma$, so don't memorize "$E_+$ is always the ground state." If $\gamma>0$, $E_+ \lt E_-$ (spin-up along $B_0$ is lower energy); if $\gamma<0$ — the physically relevant case for the electron — the inequality flips and $E_-$ is the lower energy. Classical intuition ("lowest energy when the dipole moment is parallel to the field") is still exactly right, but it's the magnetic moment $\boldsymbol\mu=\gamma\mathbf S$ that has to align with $\mathbf B$ for lowest energy, not necessarily the spin vector itself — for the electron's negative $\gamma$, spin-up (along $B_0$) means the magnetic moment points opposite to $B_0$, which is why it's the electron's spin-down state that ends up lower in energy.

Time evolution of a general initial spin state

Since $\hat H$ is time-independent, the general solution to $i\hbar\,\partial\chi/\partial t = \hat H\chi$ is a superposition of the two stationary states with their usual $e^{-iEt/\hbar}$ phases:

$$\chi(t) = a\,\chi_+e^{-iE_+t/\hbar} + b\,\chi_-e^{-iE_-t/\hbar} = \begin{pmatrix}ae^{i\gamma B_0t/2}\\ be^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}\end{pmatrix}$$

with $a,b$ fixed by the initial condition $\chi(0)=\binom ab$ (and $|a|^2+|b|^2=1$). Without loss of generality (this is purely a choice of overall phase and an assumption that $a,b$ are real, which only shifts the origin of time) write $a=\cos(\alpha/2)$, $b=\sin(\alpha/2)$ for a fixed angle $\alpha$:

$$\chi(t) = \begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha/2)\,e^{i\gamma B_0t/2}\\ \sin(\alpha/2)\,e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}\end{pmatrix}$$

Explicit computation of $\langle S_x(t)\rangle$, $\langle S_y(t)\rangle$, $\langle S_z(t)\rangle$

This is the calculation both this page and the final exam page point to — carried out here in full. Start with $\langle S_x\rangle = \chi(t)^\dagger S_x\chi(t)$:

$$\langle S_x\rangle = \Big(\cos(\alpha/2)e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}\ \ \sin(\alpha/2)e^{i\gamma B_0t/2}\Big)\,\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\cos(\alpha/2)e^{i\gamma B_0t/2}\\ \sin(\alpha/2)e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}\end{pmatrix}$$

Multiplying the matrix into the ket first: $S_x\chi(t) = \frac\hbar2\binom{\sin(\alpha/2)e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}}{\cos(\alpha/2)e^{i\gamma B_0t/2}}$. Taking the inner product with the bra:

$$\langle S_x\rangle = \frac\hbar2\Big[\cos(\alpha/2)\sin(\alpha/2)\,e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2}e^{-i\gamma B_0t/2} + \sin(\alpha/2)\cos(\alpha/2)\,e^{i\gamma B_0t/2}e^{i\gamma B_0t/2}\Big]$$
$$= \frac\hbar2\sin(\alpha/2)\cos(\alpha/2)\Big[e^{-i\gamma B_0t}+e^{i\gamma B_0t}\Big] = \frac\hbar2\sin(\alpha/2)\cos(\alpha/2)\cdot2\cos(\gamma B_0t)$$

Using the double-angle identity $2\sin(\alpha/2)\cos(\alpha/2)=\sin\alpha$:

$$\boxed{\langle S_x(t)\rangle = \frac\hbar2\sin\alpha\cos(\gamma B_0t)}$$

An identical calculation with $S_y=\frac\hbar2\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}$ (the only change is an overall factor of $i$ inserted asymmetrically, which turns the sum of exponentials into a difference, i.e. a sine instead of a cosine) gives

$$\boxed{\langle S_y(t)\rangle = -\frac\hbar2\sin\alpha\sin(\gamma B_0t)}$$

And for $S_z$, since $\chi(t)^\dagger S_z\chi(t)$ only ever multiplies each spinor component by itself (no cross terms, because $S_z$ is diagonal), all the time-dependent phases cancel against their own complex conjugates immediately:

$$\langle S_z(t)\rangle = \frac\hbar2\Big[\cos^2(\alpha/2) - \sin^2(\alpha/2)\Big] = \boxed{\frac\hbar2\cos\alpha}$$

$\langle S_z\rangle$ is exactly constant in time — it never picks up a phase to cancel against, precisely because $\chi_\pm$ are the stationary states of this particular $\hat H$.

Interpreting the result: Larmor precession

Collect the three results: $\langle S_x\rangle = \frac\hbar2\sin\alpha\cos(\omega t)$, $\langle S_y\rangle = -\frac\hbar2\sin\alpha\sin(\omega t)$, $\langle S_z\rangle=\frac\hbar2\cos\alpha$, where

$$\omega \equiv \gamma B_0 \quad (\text{the signed angular precession rate appearing in the formulas above})$$

Note that $\omega=\gamma B_0$ here is a signed rate — its sign, via $\gamma$, fixes whether $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ precesses clockwise or counterclockwise about $\hat z$, and it can be negative (e.g. for the electron's negative $\gamma$). The observable Larmor frequency — the rate you'd actually read off a clock or a spectrum — is the magnitude $|\omega|=|\gamma|B_0$. Loosely calling $\omega=\gamma B_0$ itself "the frequency" is fine as informal shorthand as long as you remember it can carry a sign; don't drop that sign when a problem specifically asks for the (necessarily non-negative) observable frequency.

The vector $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ is tilted at a fixed angle $\alpha$ from the $z$-axis (set entirely by the initial condition, and never changing), while its $x$- and $y$-components trace out a circle of fixed radius $\frac\hbar2\sin\alpha$ at angular frequency $\omega$. This is exactly the picture of a classical magnetic dipole precessing about an applied field, like a tilted spinning top precessing about the vertical under gravity — and that agreement is no coincidence: Ehrenfest's theorem (Topic 7) guarantees $\langle\mathbf S\rangle$ must obey the classical equations of motion for angular momentum in a torque, since $\mathbf S$ has no explicit time dependence and the underlying commutator algebra is identical to $\mathbf L$'s.

Notice, too, that the ordering of the two flagship examples on this page is not incidental: the eigenspinors $\chi_\pm^{(x)}$ derived in the Spin section above are exactly the states you'd need to analyze a measurement of $S_x$ at any fixed time $t$ during this precession — e.g. the probability of finding $S_x=+\hbar/2$ is $|\langle\chi_+^{(x)}|\chi(t)\rangle|^2$, computed by the identical projection method used throughout the superposition example above.

Larmor precession, worked out explicitly above, is the direct quantum-mechanical foundation of nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) and MRI. Nuclear spins in a static field $B_0$ precess at $\omega=\gamma B_0$ — a frequency in the radio range for typical clinical field strengths, and specific to each nuclear isotope's gyromagnetic ratio (this is literally why MRI can selectively image hydrogen nuclei in water/fat while ignoring other nuclei: tune the RF pulse to hydrogen's particular $\gamma$). A resonant oscillating field applied transverse to $B_0$, at exactly this Larmor frequency, drives controlled spin flips — the same physics Griffiths Problem 4.36 (an oscillating-field variant of this setup) explores directly.

Problem 1 — Degeneracy counting
Without listing every individual state, find the total number of independent hydrogen wave functions $\psi_{nlm}$ with $n=4$, and confirm this matches the general formula $d(n)=n^2$.
Show Solution

For $n=4$, the allowed values of $l$ are $l=0,1,2,3$ (i.e. $l=0,\ldots,n-1$). For each $l$ there are $2l+1$ values of $m$:

$$d(4) = \sum_{l=0}^{3}(2l+1) = 1+3+5+7 = 16$$

which indeed equals $n^2=4^2=16$. (The general proof that $\sum_{l=0}^{n-1}(2l+1)=n^2$ is a standard sum-of-odd-integers identity: the first $n$ odd positive integers always sum to $n^2$.) This is the "extra" hydrogen degeneracy discussed above — for a generic central potential you would only expect the $(2l+1)$-fold degeneracy from spherical symmetry alone for whichever specific $l$ happens to have that energy, not a full $n^2$.

Problem 2 — Ladder operators on a specific $Y_l^m$
Using $L_+Y_1^0 = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)-m(m+1)}\,Y_1^1$ with the appropriate $l,m$, and given $Y_1^0=\sqrt{3/4\pi}\cos\theta$, find the numerical coefficient $A_1^0$ relating $L_+Y_1^0$ to $Y_1^1$ (i.e. find $A_1^0$ in $L_+Y_1^0 = A_1^0\,Y_1^1$). You do not need to compute $Y_1^1$ itself — just the ladder coefficient.
Show Solution

Here $l=1$, $m=0$. Using the general formula $A_l^m = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)-m(m+1)} = \hbar\sqrt{(l-m)(l+m+1)}$ derived from the normalized ladder action (Griffiths Eq. 4.121, the spherical-harmonic analog of the $\sqrt n,\sqrt{n+1}$ factors in the QHO ladder relations from Topic 3):

$$A_1^0 = \hbar\sqrt{1(1+1)-0(0+1)} = \hbar\sqrt{2-0} = \hbar\sqrt2$$

So $L_+Y_1^0 = \hbar\sqrt2\,Y_1^1$. (As a check, this is exactly the same style of calculation as $\hat a^\dagger\phi_n=\sqrt{n+1}\,\phi_{n+1}$ from Topic 3 — a normalized ladder step, computed algebraically with no integral required.)

Problem 3 — A spin-1/2 probability calculation
An electron is in the spin state $\chi = A\binom{3i}{4}$. (a) Find the normalization constant $A$ (take it real and positive). (b) If you measure $S_z$, what values might you get, and with what probabilities?
Show Solution

(a) Normalization requires $\chi^\dagger\chi=1$: $|A|^2\big(|3i|^2+|4|^2\big) = |A|^2(9+16)=25|A|^2=1$, so $A=1/5$.

(b) In the $\chi=\binom{a}{b}$ notation, $a=3i/5$ and $b=4/5$. The possible $S_z$ outcomes are $\pm\hbar/2$ with probabilities $|a|^2$ and $|b|^2$:

$$P\left(S_z=+\frac\hbar2\right) = |a|^2 = \left|\frac{3i}{5}\right|^2 = \frac{9}{25}, \qquad P\left(S_z=-\frac\hbar2\right) = |b|^2 = \left(\frac45\right)^2=\frac{16}{25}$$

Check: $\frac{9}{25}+\frac{16}{25}=1$. ✓

Problem 4 — Consistency check on the superposition example
For the worked-example state $|\Psi(0)\rangle = \frac{1}{\sqrt6}\big\{i|3,2,1\rangle + 2|2,1,1\rangle - |1,0,0\rangle\big\}$ from the Worked Example section above, verify directly (by recomputing $\langle L_z\rangle$ a second, independent way) that $\langle L_z\rangle = \frac56\hbar$, without first grouping the two $m=1$ terms into a single "$P(L_z=\hbar)=5/6$" probability.
Show Solution

The shortcut $\langle Q\rangle=\sum_i|c_i|^2q_i$ works here specifically because the basis being expanded in ($|n,l,m\rangle$) is a basis of eigenstates of $L_z$ itself, with eigenvalue $q_i=m_i\hbar$ for each $|n,l,m\rangle$ — that diagonal structure is exactly what lets the expectation value collapse to a simple weighted sum with no off-diagonal terms. It's not a property of expansions in general: for a generic operator $\hat Q$ that ISN'T diagonal in your chosen basis, the correct formula has cross terms, $\langle Q\rangle = \sum_{i,j} c_i^*c_j\langle i|\hat Q|j\rangle$, and those off-diagonal matrix elements $\langle i|\hat Q|j\rangle$ ($i\ne j$) generally don't vanish. Because $L_z$ happens to be diagonal here, all those cross terms are automatically zero and only the $i=j$ pieces survive — which is why term-by-term and grouped-then-multiplied give the same answer for this calculation, not evidence that the simple sum works unconditionally. Term by term, with $|A|^2=1/6$:

$$\langle L_z\rangle = |A|^2|i|^2(\hbar\cdot1) + |A|^2|2|^2(\hbar\cdot1) + |A|^2|{-1}|^2(\hbar\cdot0)$$
$$= \frac16(\hbar) + \frac46(\hbar) + \frac16(0) = \frac{1+4}{6}\hbar = \frac56\hbar$$

which matches the grouped calculation exactly (as it must — both are the same sum, just associated differently: $\frac16\hbar+\frac46\hbar$ term-by-term versus $\left(\frac16+\frac46\right)\hbar$ grouped-then-multiplied). This is a useful sanity check to run on any degenerate-eigenvalue problem: the term-by-term sum and the grouped-probability calculation must always agree, since they're the same arithmetic expressed two ways.

Common Pitfalls & Self-Check (Homework #7)

📋 No solution PDF was exported for Homework #7

As noted in the Overview, this section is general instructor-style guidance on where students most commonly lose points on this material — not claimed comments on any specific graded submission (contrast with Topic 6, the only page in this study-guide set with a real exported grader-feedback PDF).

  • Confusing the two-body reduced mass $\mu$ with the electron mass $m_e$. The Bohr formula and Bohr radius are only exactly correct with $\mu$ in place of $m_e$; using $m_e$ outright is an excellent approximation for hydrogen (since $\mu/m_e\approx 0.99946$) but is not exactly correct, and the difference matters for high-precision spectroscopy problems or for other hydrogenic systems (positronium, muonic hydrogen, excitons) where the two masses are comparable. State which one you're using.
  • Mixing up $l$, $m$, and $n$'s allowed ranges. Remember the nesting: for a given $n$, $l$ runs $0,\ldots,n-1$; for a given $l$, $m$ runs $-l,\ldots,l$. A state like $\psi_{2,2,0}$ is not a typo you can just "fix" — it is forbidden, since $l$ must be strictly less than $n$. Always check this constraint before writing down or trusting a labeled hydrogen state.
  • Treating $L_+$ or $L_-$ (or $S_+,S_-$) as observables. They are not Hermitian, so they don't have a probabilistic-measurement interpretation, and expectation values like $\langle L_+\rangle$ or $\langle S_+\rangle$ are not directly physically meaningful the way $\langle L_z\rangle$ is. They are bookkeeping tools for moving between eigenstates — build $L_x,L_y$ (or $S_x,S_y$) from them first if you need an actual observable.
  • Forgetting to check degeneracy before assigning probabilities. As both worked examples above stress: before writing down "the outcome $q$ has probability $|c_i|^2$," check whether any other term in the superposition shares that same eigenvalue of the observable you're measuring. If so, the probabilities must be summed. This single check is worth explicitly writing out on any exam involving a superposition of $|n,l,m\rangle$ states.
  • Not showing why the radial series must terminate. "The energy is quantized" is not, by itself, a derivation — full credit for deriving $E_n=E_1/n^2$ requires the actual termination argument (an unterminated series blows up like $e^{2\rho}$, which combined with the $e^{-\rho}$ prefactor is not normalizable), exactly parallel to the non-negative-integer argument required for the QHO spectrum in Topic 3.
  • Sign and factor-of-$\hbar$ slips in the Larmor-precession algebra. Double-check which sign convention you're using for $\gamma$ (Griffiths notes the electron's gyromagnetic ratio is negative, $\gamma=-e/m$, which flips the direction — but not the frequency — of precession relative to a positive-$\gamma$ particle), and keep track of the factor of $2$ that appears and disappears between $E_\pm=\mp\gamma B_0\hbar/2$ and $\omega=\gamma B_0$ — it's easy to accidentally carry an extra factor of 2 (or drop one) into the final precession frequency.
