Overview: Why Quantum Mechanics Needs a Formal Theory

Topics 1–5 built quantum mechanics one solved potential at a time: infinite well, harmonic oscillator, free particle, finite well. Each time you solved the Schrödinger equation for a specific $V(x)$, found eigenfunctions, and computed probabilities. This topic (syllabus lectures 11-1 through 12-2, Canvas module “The Formal Theory of Quantum Mechanics,” corresponding to Homework #5) steps back and asks: what is actually going on, independent of any particular potential? The answer is Griffiths & Schroeter Chapter 3, Formalism, together with the linear-algebra Appendix (A.3–A.6).

🔗 Paying Off Topic 1's Promise

Back in Topic 1 you were handed the Heisenberg uncertainty principle $\sigma_x\sigma_p\geq \hbar/2$ as a fact to be checked on specific wave functions, with a note that a genuine proof was coming later. This is that page. Section 5 below proves the fully general statement $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2\geq\left(\frac{1}{2i}\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\right)^2$ for any two observables $A$ and $B$, and then specializes it to recover $\sigma_x\sigma_p\geq\hbar/2$ as a two-line corollary.

Three ideas drive everything in this topic, and they are really one idea seen from three angles:

  • Observables are Hermitian operators. The reason measured values always come out real is a theorem about a class of linear operators, not a coincidence.
  • States are vectors in an abstract Hilbert space, and $\Psi(x,t)$ is just one particular way of writing that vector down (in the “position basis”). Dirac's bra–ket notation lets you talk about the vector without committing to a basis.
  • Operators, in any given basis, are matrices, and the linear algebra you may have half-forgotten from a sophomore course — eigenvalues, diagonalization, unitary transformations — turns out to be quantum mechanics once you're working in a finite-dimensional (or countable) Hilbert space.
📋 Where This Fits

Hilbert Space, Observables, and Hermitian Operators

Hilbert Space

A Hilbert space is, in general, any complete inner-product vector space. For a spinless particle's spatial wave function in one dimension, the relevant Hilbert space is $L^2$, the set of square-integrable functions on the line — all $f(x)$ with $\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}|f(x)|^2\,dx<\infty$. That's the realization used throughout Topics 1–5, but it's not the only one this course uses: Topic 8's spin-1/2 states live in the finite-dimensional Hilbert space $\mathbb{C}^2$, with no wave function or integral in sight, and the same postulates below apply there unchanged. Either way, a Hilbert space is a vector space: you can add two members and multiply by scalars and stay inside the space. It comes equipped with an inner product,

$$\langle f|g\rangle \equiv \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x)^* g(x)\,dx,$$

which is linear in the second argument, antilinear in the first ($\langle cf|g\rangle = c^*\langle f|g\rangle$, $\langle f|cg\rangle=c\langle f|g\rangle$), and satisfies $\langle f|g\rangle=\langle g|f\rangle^*$. Every physically allowed wave function $\Psi(x,t)$ is a vector in this space, with $\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=1$ enforcing normalization. The inner product also obeys the Schwarz inequality,

$$\langle f|f\rangle\langle g|g\rangle \geq |\langle f|g\rangle|^2,$$

which is the single most important inequality in this topic — it is the engine that drives the uncertainty-principle proof in Section 5.

Observables Are Hermitian Operators

A physical observable (position, momentum, energy, angular momentum…) is represented in quantum mechanics by a linear operator $\hat Q$ acting on Hilbert space. Not just any operator will do: because the outcome of a measurement is a real number, and because the average of many measurements, $\langle Q\rangle=\langle \Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle$, must also be real for every state $\Psi$, the operator representing an observable must satisfy

$$\langle f|\hat Q f\rangle = \langle \hat Q f|f\rangle \quad \text{for all } f,$$

or, more generally (and this is the definition you'll use constantly from here on),

$$\langle \alpha|\hat Q\beta\rangle = \langle \hat Q\alpha|\beta\rangle \quad \text{for all } \alpha,\beta.$$

An operator with this property is called Hermitian. In a discrete/matrix representation this becomes $\hat Q^\dagger=\hat Q$, i.e. the matrix equals its own transpose-conjugate. This is Postulate I of quantum mechanics (as enumerated in Liboff): to every observable $A$ corresponds a Hermitian operator $\hat A$, and the possible measured values are the eigenvalues of $\hat A$.

⚠️ Misconception: “Hermitian” just means “symmetric”
A real symmetric matrix ($T_{ij}=T_{ji}$) is Hermitian, but that's a special case. In general Hermitian means transpose AND complex-conjugate equal the original: $T_{ij}=T_{ji}^*$. Diagonal entries of a Hermitian matrix must therefore be real (since $T_{ii}=T_{ii}^*$), but off-diagonal entries can be complex, as long as mirror-image entries are complex conjugates of each other. This is exactly the check performed in Problem A.28 below: $T=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1-i\\ 1+i & 0\end{pmatrix}$ is Hermitian even though it has complex entries, because $T_{12}=1-i=T_{21}^*$.

Determinate States

A measurement of observable $A$ on a general state $\Psi$ gives a spread of possible results with standard deviation $\sigma_A$. A state is called determinate for $A$ (measurement is guaranteed to return one specific value with zero spread, $\sigma_A=0$) exactly when $\Psi$ is an eigenfunction of $\hat A$: $\hat A\Psi = a\Psi$. This is Postulate II: after a measurement returning value $a$, the system collapses into the corresponding eigenstate $\phi_a$. Stationary states $\psi_n$ of the Hamiltonian are determinate states for energy — that's why $\langle H\rangle=E_n$ exactly, with zero spread, for a particle prepared in a single stationary state.

📝 The Four Postulates (Liboff enumeration, useful exam scaffolding)

I. Observables & Operators — every observable $A$ has a Hermitian operator $\hat A$; measured values are eigenvalues of $\hat A$. II. Measurement — measuring $A$ and getting result $a$ collapses the system into the eigenstate $\phi_a$. III. The State Function — $\Psi(\vec r,t)$ contains all information about the system; $\langle C\rangle = \int\Psi^*\hat C\Psi\,d\vec r$. IV. Time Development — $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t=\hat H\Psi$ (the Schrödinger equation). Everything else in Chapter 3 — the uncertainty principle, Dirac notation, matrix mechanics — is machinery for making these four postulates precise and computable.

Eigenfunctions of a Hermitian Operator

Discrete Spectra

Suppose $\hat Q\, f_n(x) = q_n\, f_n(x)$, with $f_n$ normalizable and the $q_n$ forming a discrete list. Two theorems make discrete-spectrum eigenfunctions extremely convenient (full proofs, in the Hermitian-transformation language, appear in Section 8):

  1. The eigenvalues are real.
  2. Eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal: $\langle f_m|f_n\rangle=0$ for $m\neq n$.

Combined with normalization, this gives orthonormality, $\langle f_m|f_n\rangle=\delta_{mn}$, and (for a physically sensible observable) the $f_n$ are also complete: any function in Hilbert space can be written as a linear combination $\sum_n c_n f_n(x)$. Completeness is what makes the generalized statistical interpretation (Section 4) possible.

Continuous Spectra

Position and momentum are the classic examples of observables whose eigenvalues form a continuum, and whose eigenfunctions are not themselves normalizable. The position operator $\hat x$ has eigenfunction $g_y(x)=\delta(x-y)$ for every real number $y$: $\hat x\, g_y(x) = x\,\delta(x-y) = y\,\delta(x-y)$. The momentum operator $\hat p=-i\hbar\, d/dx$ has eigenfunctions $f_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar}$ for every real $p$. Neither $\delta(x-y)$ nor $e^{ipx/\hbar}$ is square-integrable, so strictly they are not members of Hilbert space — but they satisfy a continuum analog of orthonormality, Dirac orthonormality:

$$\langle g_y|g_{y'}\rangle = \delta(y-y'), \qquad \langle f_p|f_{p'}\rangle = \delta(p-p').$$

This is why a free particle is always represented by a wave packet (a superposition over a range of momenta/positions), never by a single momentum or position eigenfunction — the pure eigenfunctions aren't normalizable, hence aren't physical states, even though they're indispensable as a basis for expanding physical states.

The Generalized Statistical Interpretation

Chapter 1 gave you the statistical interpretation for position only: $|\Psi(x,t)|^2\,dx$ is the probability of finding the particle in $[x,x+dx]$. The generalized version extends this to any observable. Because the eigenfunctions $f_n$ of an observable $\hat Q$ are complete, any state can be expanded

$$\Psi(x,t) = \sum_n c_n(t)\, f_n(x), \qquad c_n(t) = \langle f_n|\Psi\rangle = \int f_n(x)^*\Psi(x,t)\,dx \quad \text{(Fourier's trick)}.$$

The generalized statistical interpretation says: a measurement of $Q$ will yield one of the eigenvalues $q_n$, with probability $|c_n|^2$. Normalization of $\Psi$ guarantees $\sum_n|c_n|^2=1$, and the expectation value comes out as $\langle Q\rangle = \sum_n q_n|c_n|^2$, exactly what you'd expect from a probability distribution over outcomes $q_n$. Applying this machinery to $\hat x$ (whose Dirac-orthonormalized eigenfunction is $g_y(x)=\delta(x-y)$) reproduces the original position statistical interpretation exactly, as a special case; applying it to $\hat p$ produces the momentum-space wave function $\Phi(p,t)=\langle f_p|\Psi\rangle$, the Fourier transform of $\Psi(x,t)$, with $|\Phi(p,t)|^2\,dp$ the probability of measuring momentum in $[p,p+dp]$.

⚠️ Misconception: “$|c_n|^2$ is the probability the particle IS in state $f_n$”
This is subtly wrong and a favorite trap on exams. The particle is in state $\Psi$, period — not partly in $f_1$ and partly in $f_2$. $|c_n|^2$ is the probability that a measurement of $Q$ returns the value $q_n$. It is correct to say $|c_n|^2$ is the probability that a particle currently in state $\Psi$ will be found in state $f_n$ after such a measurement (because measurement collapses the state) — but that's a claim about the measurement process, not a claim about what $\Psi$ secretly “is” beforehand.

The Generalized Uncertainty Principle (Full Proof)

3.5.1 — Proof of $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2\geq\left(\frac{1}{2i}\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\right)^2$

For any observable $A$ in state $\Psi$, the variance is $\sigma_A^2 = \langle(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)\Psi\,|\,(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)\Psi\rangle = \langle f|f\rangle$, where we define $f\equiv(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)\Psi$. Likewise, for any other observable $B$, $\sigma_B^2=\langle g|g\rangle$ with $g\equiv(\hat B-\langle B\rangle)\Psi$. The Schwarz inequality from Section 2 immediately gives

$$\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 = \langle f|f\rangle\langle g|g\rangle \geq |\langle f|g\rangle|^2. \tag{i}$$

Now use a fact about complex numbers: for any $z$, $|z|^2 = [\text{Re}(z)]^2+[\text{Im}(z)]^2 \geq [\text{Im}(z)]^2 = \left[\frac{1}{2i}(z-z^*)\right]^2$. Setting $z=\langle f|g\rangle$ (so $z^*=\langle g|f\rangle$) turns (i) into

$$\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \geq \left(\frac{1}{2i}\left[\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle\right]\right)^2. \tag{ii}$$

Now evaluate $\langle f|g\rangle$ explicitly, using the fact that $(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)$ is Hermitian to move it across the inner product:

$$\langle f|g\rangle = \big\langle (\hat A-\langle A\rangle)\Psi \,\big|\, (\hat B-\langle B\rangle)\Psi\big\rangle = \big\langle\Psi\big|(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)(\hat B-\langle B\rangle)\Psi\big\rangle = \langle\hat A\hat B\rangle - \langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle,$$

(expand $\langle f|g\rangle = \langle(\hat A-\langle A\rangle)\Psi|(\hat B-\langle B\rangle)\Psi\rangle$ termwise: the two cross terms, $-\langle B\rangle\langle\hat A\rangle$ and $-\langle A\rangle\langle\hat B\rangle$, combine with the leftover $+\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=+\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$ term to cancel exactly, since $\langle A\rangle,\langle B\rangle$ are just ordinary numbers that pull straight out of the inner product and $\langle\Psi|\Psi\rangle=1$ — leaving only $\langle\hat A\hat B\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$ as shown). Similarly $\langle g|f\rangle=\langle\hat B\hat A\rangle-\langle A\rangle\langle B\rangle$. Subtracting,

$$\langle f|g\rangle - \langle g|f\rangle = \langle\hat A\hat B\rangle - \langle \hat B\hat A\rangle = \big\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\big\rangle, \qquad [\hat A,\hat B]\equiv \hat A\hat B-\hat B\hat A.$$

Substituting into (ii) gives the boxed result:

$$\boxed{\ \sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2 \geq \left(\frac{1}{2i}\big\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\big\rangle\right)^2\ }$$

The factor of $i$ is not a mistake: the commutator of two Hermitian operators is itself anti-Hermitian, so $\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle$ is purely imaginary, and dividing by $2i$ makes the whole right-hand side real and non-negative, as a squared quantity must be.

Recovering Heisenberg's $\sigma_x\sigma_p\geq\hbar/2$

Take $\hat A=\hat x$, $\hat B=\hat p=-i\hbar\, d/dx$. Their commutator is the canonical one, $[\hat x,\hat p]=i\hbar$. Plugging straight into the boxed formula:

$$\sigma_x^2\sigma_p^2 \geq \left(\frac{1}{2i}\cdot i\hbar\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^2 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \sigma_x\sigma_p \geq \frac{\hbar}{2}.$$

This is the payoff promised back in Topic 1: the familiar Heisenberg relation is not a separate axiom, it's the $x$-$p$ special case of one universal theorem about non-commuting Hermitian operators. Any two observables with a nonzero commutator are called incompatible — they cannot share a complete set of eigenfunctions (Griffiths Problem 3.16), meaning there's no basis of simultaneous eigenstates you could use for both. That's weaker than saying no state can ever be determinate for both at once: the uncertainty bound $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2\geq\left(\frac{1}{2i}\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\right)^2$ is state-dependent through $\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle$, and a particular state can make that expectation value zero (e.g. a state where $\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle=0$ even though $[\hat A,\hat B]\ne0$ as an operator), so isolated common eigenvectors of incompatible observables can still exist — you just can't build a complete shared basis out of them the way you can for compatible observables. Observables that do commute are compatible and admit simultaneous eigenfunctions (a fact this page proves formally in Section 7, and which is exactly what makes the hydrogen atom's $\hat H$, $\hat L^2$, $\hat L_z$ triad work in Topic 8).

3.5.2 — The Minimum-Uncertainty Wave Packet

The proof above used two inequalities: the Schwarz inequality (i), and dropping $\text{Re}(z)$ in (ii). Both become equalities under specific conditions, and together those conditions tell you exactly which wave functions saturate the uncertainty bound. Schwarz is saturated when $g=cf$ for some constant $c$; dropping $\text{Re}(z)$ costs nothing when $\text{Re}\langle f|g\rangle=0$, which combined with $g=cf$ forces $c$ to be purely imaginary, $c=ia$ with $a$ real. For the position–momentum pair this condition, $\left(-i\hbar\frac{d}{dx}-\langle p\rangle\right)\Psi = ia(x-\langle x\rangle)\Psi$, is a first-order ODE whose solution is a Gaussian:

$$\Psi(x) = A\, e^{-a(x-\langle x\rangle)^2/2\hbar}\, e^{i\langle p\rangle x/\hbar}.$$

This is exactly why the two minimum-uncertainty states you've already met — the harmonic-oscillator ground state (Topic 3) and the Gaussian free-particle wave packet (Topic 4) — are both Gaussians. It's not a coincidence; it's the unique functional form that saturates $\sigma_x\sigma_p=\hbar/2$.

3.5.3 — The Energy–Time Uncertainty Principle

$\Delta t\Delta E\geq \hbar/2$ is often paired with $\Delta x\Delta p\geq\hbar/2$, but it is a genuinely different kind of statement, because time is not an observable in nonrelativistic quantum mechanics — there's no Hermitian operator $\hat t$, and you never measure “the time” of a particle the way you measure its position. To derive the energy–time relation honestly, start from the rate of change of any expectation value. Differentiating $\langle Q\rangle=\langle\Psi|\hat Q\Psi\rangle$ and using the Schrödinger equation $i\hbar\,\partial\Psi/\partial t=\hat H\Psi$ together with the hermiticity of $\hat H$ gives the generalized Ehrenfest theorem:

$$\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt} = \frac{i}{\hbar}\big\langle[\hat H,\hat Q]\big\rangle + \left\langle\frac{\partial\hat Q}{\partial t}\right\rangle.$$

Now specialize the boxed generalized uncertainty relation to $\hat A=\hat H$, $\hat B=\hat Q$ (with $\hat Q$ having no explicit time-dependence, the ordinary case):

$$\sigma_H^2\sigma_Q^2 \geq \left(\frac{1}{2i}\big\langle[\hat H,\hat Q]\big\rangle\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2i}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{i}\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^2\left(\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\right)^2 \;\Longrightarrow\; \sigma_H\sigma_Q\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}\left|\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\right|.$$

Defining $\Delta E\equiv\sigma_H$ and $\Delta t \equiv \sigma_Q\big/\left|\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}\right|$ — the time it takes $\langle Q\rangle$ to shift by one standard deviation — gives $\Delta E\,\Delta t\geq\hbar/2$. Crucially, $\Delta t$'s meaning depends on which observable $Q$ you pick to track the system's evolution: it might be an oscillation period, a transit time, or a decay lifetime, but it is never a spread in measured “time values,” because there is no such measurement.

Immediate corollary: if $\hat Q$ commutes with $\hat H$ and has no explicit time dependence, $d\langle Q\rangle/dt=0$ — $Q$ is a conserved quantity. This single line is the formal statement of conservation laws in quantum mechanics and is exactly the tool Topic 7 uses for parity conservation.

⚠️ Misconception: “You can borrow energy $\Delta E$ as long as you pay it back within $\Delta t$”
This popular phrase is not licensed by the energy–time uncertainty principle. Nowhere in the derivation above does energy conservation get relaxed — $\hat H$ is still Hermitian with real, sharply-defined eigenvalues on energy eigenstates. What is true: a state with a short characteristic lifetime $\Delta t$ (e.g., an unstable particle) necessarily has energy spread $\Delta E\gtrsim\hbar/(2\Delta t)$ in repeated measurements of its rest mass — the $\Delta$ particle (lifetime $\sim10^{-23}\,\text{s}$) has a measured mass spread of about $120\,\text{MeV}/c^2$, consistent with $\Delta E\Delta t\approx\hbar/2$. That's a statement about the width of an ensemble of identical unstable systems, not about violating conservation in any single trial.
Every spectral linewidth you'll ever see on an oscilloscope or in an atomic-clock spec sheet is an energy–time uncertainty measurement in disguise: an excited atomic state with lifetime $\tau$ has a natural linewidth $\Delta E \sim \hbar/2\tau$, which directly sets the frequency stability limit of laser-cooled atomic clocks and the spectral purity of laser cavities. In quantum-dot engineering, a shorter exciton lifetime (faster radiative recombination) is a direct trade against narrower emission linewidth — a design constraint straight out of this equation.

Vectors, Operators, and Dirac Notation

3.6.1 — Bases in Hilbert Space

An ordinary vector $\mathbf A$ in the plane is one geometric object, but you describe it with different components depending on which coordinate axes you choose. The state of a quantum system works the same way: it is a vector $|S(t)\rangle$ living abstractly in Hilbert space, and the wave function you're used to writing, $\Psi(x,t)$, is really just the set of components of $|S(t)\rangle$ with respect to one particular basis — the position basis $\{|x\rangle\}$:

$$\Psi(x,t) = \langle x|S(t)\rangle.$$

Equally valid: expand $|S(t)\rangle$ in the momentum basis $\{|p\rangle\}$ to get $\Phi(p,t)=\langle p|S(t)\rangle$, or in the energy eigenbasis $\{|n\rangle\}$ to get the coefficients $c_n(t)=\langle n|S(t)\rangle$ from Section 4. All three — $\Psi$, $\Phi$, and $\{c_n\}$ — contain exactly the same information, just expressed with respect to three different bases of the same underlying vector.

🔗 This Is What Topic 3's $|n\rangle$ Notation Really Meant

In the harmonic-oscillator topic, you used $\hat a_+|n\rangle=\sqrt{n+1}|n+1\rangle$ informally, treating $|n\rangle$ as a shorthand label for the $n$-th stationary state $\psi_n(x)$. Now that shorthand is formalized: $|n\rangle$ is literally the abstract Hilbert-space vector, and $\psi_n(x)=\langle x|n\rangle$ is its position-basis representation. The ladder operators $\hat a_\pm$ are basis-independent operators acting directly on the kets $|n\rangle$; you only recover the familiar differential-operator form $\hat a_\pm = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\hbar m\omega}}(\mp i\hat p+m\omega\hat x)$ when you choose to represent everything in the position basis.

3.6.2 — Dirac Notation: Bras, Kets, and the Resolution of the Identity

Dirac split the inner product $\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$ into a ket $|\beta\rangle$ (an ordinary vector) and a bra $\langle\alpha|$ (a linear functional: something that eats a ket and returns a complex number). In a finite basis, kets are column vectors and bras are their conjugate-transpose row vectors, so $\langle\beta|\alpha\rangle = \mathsf{b}^\dagger\mathsf{a}$ is ordinary matrix multiplication. A particularly useful object built from a bra and a ket is the projection operator $\hat P\equiv|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ (for normalized $|\alpha\rangle$), which picks out the component of any vector lying along $|\alpha\rangle$: $\hat P|\beta\rangle = (\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle)\,|\alpha\rangle$.

Summing projections over a complete orthonormal basis $\{|e_n\rangle\}$ gives the single most useful identity in this whole formalism, the resolution of the identity:

$$\sum_n |e_n\rangle\langle e_n| = \hat 1 \qquad \text{(discrete)}, \qquad\qquad \int |e_z\rangle\langle e_z|\,dz = \hat 1 \qquad \text{(continuous)}.$$

Acting this operator on any state $|\alpha\rangle$ just recovers its expansion $\sum_n(\langle e_n|\alpha\rangle)|e_n\rangle = |\alpha\rangle$ — the identity resolves into a sum over “how much of each basis vector is present.” Functions of operators (needed for time evolution $e^{-i\hat Ht/\hbar}$ in Topic 7) are defined the same way you'd define them for matrices, via the power series $e^{\hat Q}\equiv \hat 1+\hat Q+\frac{1}{2}\hat Q^2+\cdots$

3.6.3 — Changing Bases in Dirac Notation

Because $\langle p|\hat 1|S(t)\rangle=\langle p|S(t)\rangle$, you can insert a resolution of the identity to translate between representations effortlessly. For example, to get from the position-space wave function to the momentum-space wave function, insert $\hat 1=\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|$:

$$\Phi(p,t) = \langle p|S(t)\rangle = \left\langle p\left|\left(\int dx\,|x\rangle\langle x|\right)\right|S(t)\right\rangle = \int \langle p|x\rangle\,\langle x|S(t)\rangle\,dx = \int \langle p|x\rangle\,\Psi(x,t)\,dx.$$

Since $\langle x|p\rangle = f_p(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{ipx/\hbar}$ is just the momentum eigenfunction in the position basis, $\langle p|x\rangle=\langle x|p\rangle^*=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}e^{-ipx/\hbar}$, and the formula above reproduces exactly the Fourier-transform relation $\Phi(p,t)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi\hbar}}\int e^{-ipx/\hbar}\Psi(x,t)\,dx$ you already knew — but now derived from pure basis-change bookkeeping, with no Fourier-analysis machinery required. This is the general technique: insert a resolution of the identity in the basis you have, and read off the transformation coefficients as inner products between the old and new basis vectors.

⚠️ Misconception: A bra is “half of” a ket
A ket $|\alpha\rangle$ is a vector; the corresponding bra $\langle\alpha|$ is not a vector at all — it's a name for the operation “take the inner product of whatever ket comes next, with $\alpha$.” The set of all bras forms a separate vector space (the dual space). This distinction rarely bites you in practice, but it explains why an expression like $\hat Q|\alpha\rangle$ makes sense (operator acting on a vector, producing another vector) while $\langle\alpha|\hat Q$ is a different object entirely (a new bra, dual to $\hat Q^\dagger|\alpha\rangle$) — and why an expression like “$\hat Q\alpha$” without the ket bars is, strictly, nonsense: operators act on vectors, not on the label $\alpha$ itself.

Matrix Mechanics: Operators as Matrices

Representing Operators in a Basis

Just as a vector $|\alpha\rangle=\sum_n a_n|e_n\rangle$ is represented, with respect to an orthonormal basis $\{|e_n\rangle\}$, by its column of components $a_n=\langle e_n|\alpha\rangle$, an operator $\hat T$ is represented by its matrix elements:

$$T_{ij} \equiv \langle e_i|\hat T|e_j\rangle.$$

The abstract relation $|\beta\rangle=\hat T|\alpha\rangle$ becomes ordinary matrix multiplication of components, $b_i = \sum_j T_{ij}a_j$, i.e. $\mathsf b=\mathsf{T}\mathsf a$. Composing two operators corresponds to multiplying their matrices in the same order, $\widehat{ST}\leftrightarrow \mathsf{ST}$, with $(\mathsf{ST})_{ik}=\sum_j S_{ij}T_{jk}$ — the ordinary row-times-column matrix product rule.

Changing Bases: Similarity Transformations

If you switch from basis $\{|e_i\rangle\}$ to a new orthonormal basis $\{|f_i\rangle\}$ via a matrix $\mathsf S$ (with $\mathsf S$ unitary, precisely because both bases are orthonormal), the same operator $\hat T$ is represented in the new basis by

$$\mathsf T^f = \mathsf S\,\mathsf T^e\,\mathsf S^{-1}.$$

Two matrices related this way are called similar. Although the individual matrix elements change completely under a similarity transformation, two numbers built from them are basis-independent invariants:

$$\det(\mathsf T^f) = \det(\mathsf S)\det(\mathsf T^e)\det(\mathsf S^{-1}) = \det(\mathsf T^e), \qquad \text{Tr}(\mathsf T^f) = \text{Tr}(\mathsf S\,\mathsf T^e\mathsf S^{-1}) = \text{Tr}(\mathsf T^e\mathsf S^{-1}\mathsf S)=\text{Tr}(\mathsf T^e),$$

using $\det(\mathsf{AB})=\det(\mathsf A)\det(\mathsf B)$ and the cyclic property $\text{Tr}(\mathsf{T}_1\mathsf{T}_2)=\text{Tr}(\mathsf T_2\mathsf T_1)$. This invariance of trace and determinant under a change of basis is exactly the sanity check performed in Problem A.28 in the HW Feedback section below — if your diagonalized matrix doesn't have the same trace and determinant as the original, you've made an arithmetic error somewhere.

Eigenvalues, Diagonalization, and Normal Matrices

The eigenvalue equation $\mathsf T\mathsf a=\lambda\mathsf a$ (nonzero $\mathsf a$) is equivalent to $(\mathsf T-\lambda\mathsf I)\mathsf a=0$, which has a nontrivial solution only when $\mathsf T-\lambda\mathsf I$ is singular:

$$\det(\mathsf T-\lambda\mathsf I) = 0 \quad\text{(the characteristic equation)}.$$

For an $n\times n$ matrix this is an $n$-th order polynomial in $\lambda$, so there are exactly $n$ roots (counted with multiplicity) — the spectrum. If the eigenvectors of $\mathsf T$ span the whole space, using them as a basis reduces $\mathsf T$ to diagonal form with the eigenvalues strung along the diagonal — this is diagonalization, and it is the central computational technique of this entire topic (and of Homework #5). A sufficient (not necessary) condition guaranteeing diagonalizability is normality: $[\mathsf N^\dagger,\mathsf N]=0$. Every Hermitian matrix is normal (since $\mathsf N^\dagger=\mathsf N$ trivially commutes with itself), and so is every unitary matrix — both classes are always diagonalizable, which is precisely why it's safe to build quantum mechanics on them.

Why Commuting Hermitian Operators Share an Eigenbasis

This is the single most important structural fact this page has to offer, and it is exactly what Homework Problem A.25 (below) is built to demonstrate numerically. The claim: two diagonalizable matrices $\mathsf T$ and $\mathsf V$ can be simultaneously diagonalized by the same similarity matrix $\mathsf S$ — i.e., there's a single basis in which both are diagonal — if and only if they commute, $[\mathsf T,\mathsf V]=0$.

Commute $\Rightarrow$ share an eigenbasis (the direction that matters for A.25): Suppose $\mathsf T\mathsf a^{(i)}=\lambda_i\mathsf a^{(i)}$ with $\mathsf T$'s spectrum nondegenerate (all $\lambda_i$ distinct), and $[\mathsf T,\mathsf V]=0$. Then

$$\mathsf T\left(\mathsf V\mathsf a^{(i)}\right) = \mathsf V\mathsf T\mathsf a^{(i)} = \lambda_i\left(\mathsf V\mathsf a^{(i)}\right),$$

which says the vector $\mathsf V\mathsf a^{(i)}$ is itself an eigenvector of $\mathsf T$ with the same eigenvalue $\lambda_i$. But the spectrum is nondegenerate, so the eigenspace for $\lambda_i$ is one-dimensional — forcing $\mathsf V\mathsf a^{(i)}$ to be a multiple of $\mathsf a^{(i)}$ itself: $\mathsf V\mathsf a^{(i)}=\nu_i\mathsf a^{(i)}$. In other words, $\mathsf a^{(i)}$ is automatically an eigenvector of $\mathsf V$ too. (If $\mathsf T$'s spectrum is degenerate the argument needs one more step — you can always find some linear combination within the degenerate eigenspace that diagonalizes $\mathsf V$ as well, by solving a smaller eigenvalue problem restricted to that subspace; see Practice Problem 2 below for a worked example of this subtlety.)

Share an eigenbasis $\Rightarrow$ commute is the easy direction: in the shared eigenbasis, both matrices are diagonal, and any two diagonal matrices trivially commute (diagonal $\times$ diagonal just multiplies entrywise); since the commutator vanishes in one basis, it vanishes in every basis (similarity transformations preserve commutation relations).

The physical translation: compatible observables commute, and commuting observables share a complete set of simultaneous eigenstates — states that are simultaneously determinate for both. This is why the hydrogen atom (Topic 8) is solved by finding simultaneous eigenfunctions of $\hat H$, $\hat L^2$, and $\hat L_z$ (which pairwise commute): the theorem proved above guarantees such a common eigenbasis must exist before you even start looking for it.

Hermitian & Unitary Transformations: The Three Guarantees

The fundamental, basis-independent definition of the Hermitian conjugate $\hat T^\dagger$ of an operator is: the operator satisfying $\langle\hat T^\dagger\alpha|\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha|\hat T\beta\rangle$ for all vectors $\alpha,\beta$. A Hermitian transformation is one with $\hat T^\dagger=\hat T$, i.e. $\langle\alpha|\hat T\beta\rangle=\langle\hat T\alpha|\beta\rangle$. Three theorems about Hermitian operators are the entire reason quantum mechanics can use them to represent observables:

Theorem 1: Eigenvalues of a Hermitian Operator Are Real

Proof. Let $\hat T|\alpha\rangle=\lambda|\alpha\rangle$ with $|\alpha\rangle\neq 0$. Then $\langle\alpha|\hat T\alpha\rangle=\langle\alpha|\lambda\alpha\rangle=\lambda\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle$. But hermiticity also gives $\langle\alpha|\hat T\alpha\rangle=\langle\hat T\alpha|\alpha\rangle=\langle\lambda\alpha|\alpha\rangle=\lambda^*\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle$. Since $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle\neq 0$, we get $\lambda=\lambda^*$, i.e. $\lambda$ is real. $\blacksquare$

This is why every measured value of a physical observable comes out real: it's forced by the observable being represented by a Hermitian operator, nothing more mysterious than that.

Theorem 2: Eigenvectors With Distinct Eigenvalues Are Orthogonal

Proof. Let $\hat T|\alpha\rangle=\lambda|\alpha\rangle$, $\hat T|\beta\rangle=\mu|\beta\rangle$, $\lambda\neq\mu$. Then $\langle\alpha|\hat T\beta\rangle=\mu\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$, while hermiticity gives $\langle\alpha|\hat T\beta\rangle=\langle\hat T\alpha|\beta\rangle=\lambda^*\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=\lambda\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$ (using Theorem 1, $\lambda=\lambda^*$). So $\mu\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=\lambda\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$, and since $\lambda\neq\mu$, we must have $\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle=0$. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 3: Eigenvectors of a Hermitian Operator Span the Space

This one is stated rather than proved here (the finite-dimensional proof is a standard linear-algebra result; it is genuinely subtle in infinite dimensions, which is part of why Chapter 3 treats discrete and continuous spectra as separate cases). Equivalently: every Hermitian matrix is diagonalizable. This completeness is the technical fact underwriting the generalized statistical interpretation of Section 4 — without it, the expansion $\Psi=\sum_n c_n f_n$ wouldn't be guaranteed to exist for an arbitrary observable.

Unitary Transformations

A unitary operator satisfies $\hat U^\dagger\hat U=\hat 1$, equivalently $\hat U^\dagger=\hat U^{-1}$. Unitary transformations preserve inner products, $\langle\hat U\alpha|\hat U\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha|\hat U^\dagger\hat U\beta\rangle=\langle\alpha|\beta\rangle$ — they're the quantum analog of rotations: they move vectors around without changing lengths or angles between them. Their eigenvalues have modulus 1 (write $\hat U|\alpha\rangle=\lambda|\alpha\rangle$; preserving $\langle\alpha|\alpha\rangle$ forces $|\lambda|^2=1$), and their eigenvectors belonging to distinct eigenvalues are orthogonal (same style of proof as Theorem 2). Unitary matrices are exactly the matrices $\mathsf S$ that carry one orthonormal basis into another — which is why the similarity transformations of the previous section, and the diagonalizing matrix $\mathsf S$ you construct in Problem A.28 below, are always unitary.

This is not just formalism for its own sake: a quantum computer's gates are, by definition, unitary matrices acting on qubit state vectors, and the entire discipline of quantum error correction and gate design is linear algebra on Hermitian and unitary operators exactly as developed on this page. NMR spectroscopy, similarly, manipulates spin states with sequences of unitary "pulse" operators built from Hermitian spin Hamiltonians.

Griffiths A.25 — Commuting Matrices Share an Eigenbasis

📝 Problem Statement

Given $\mathsf A=\begin{pmatrix}1&4&1\\4&-2&4\\1&4&1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathsf B=\begin{pmatrix}1&-2&-1\\-2&2&-2\\-1&-2&1\end{pmatrix}$: (a) verify both are diagonalizable and that they commute; (b) find the eigenvalues/eigenvectors of $\mathsf A$ and verify its spectrum is nondegenerate; (c) show the eigenvectors of $\mathsf A$ are eigenvectors of $\mathsf B$ too.

(a) Diagonalizability and commutation. Both $\mathsf A$ and $\mathsf B$ are visibly symmetric with real entries, so $\mathsf A^\dagger=\mathsf A$ and $\mathsf B^\dagger=\mathsf B$: they're Hermitian, hence normal ($[\mathsf A^\dagger,\mathsf A]=0$ trivially), hence diagonalizable by Theorem 3 above — no extra work is needed to verify diagonalizability once you've spotted hermiticity. The commutator is computed directly by multiplying out both orderings:

$$[\mathsf A,\mathsf B] = \mathsf{AB}-\mathsf{BA} = \begin{pmatrix}-8&4&-8\\4&-20&4\\-8&4&-8\end{pmatrix} - \begin{pmatrix}-8&4&-8\\4&-20&4\\-8&4&-8\end{pmatrix} = 0. \checkmark$$

(b) Eigenvalues via the characteristic equation. Expanding $\det(\mathsf A-\lambda\mathsf I)=0$:

$$\begin{vmatrix}1-\lambda & 4 & 1\\ 4 & -2-\lambda & 4\\ 1&4&1-\lambda\end{vmatrix} = -(1-\lambda)^2(2+\lambda)+16+16+(2+\lambda)-16(1-\lambda)-16(1-\lambda) = -\lambda^3+36\lambda = 0,$$

giving $\lambda(\lambda-6)(\lambda+6)=0$, so the eigenvalues are $\boxed{0,\ 6,\ -6}$ — three distinct values for a $3\times 3$ matrix, so the spectrum is nondegenerate, confirming part (b)'s requirement without any further argument. Plugging each $\lambda$ back into $\mathsf A\mathsf v=\lambda\mathsf v$ gives three scalar equations per eigenvalue (one is always redundant for a nondegenerate root); solving and normalizing:

$$\lambda_1=0:\ \mathsf v_1=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_2=6:\ \mathsf v_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda_3=-6:\ \mathsf v_3=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\1\end{pmatrix}.$$

(c) The eigenvectors of $\mathsf A$ diagonalize $\mathsf B$ too — and this is not a coincidence. Direct computation confirms it:

$$\mathsf B\mathsf v_1 = 2\mathsf v_1, \qquad \mathsf B\mathsf v_2 = -2\mathsf v_2, \qquad \mathsf B\mathsf v_3 = 4\mathsf v_3.$$

Every one of $\mathsf A$'s eigenvectors is simultaneously an eigenvector of $\mathsf B$ (with its own, different, eigenvalues $2,-2,4$). This is exactly the theorem proved in the Matrix Mechanics section above: because $\mathsf A$'s spectrum is nondegenerate and $[\mathsf A,\mathsf B]=0$, the argument $\mathsf A(\mathsf B\mathsf v_i)=\mathsf B(\mathsf A\mathsf v_i)=\lambda_i(\mathsf B\mathsf v_i)$ forces $\mathsf B\mathsf v_i$ to lie in $\mathsf A$'s (one-dimensional) $\lambda_i$-eigenspace, i.e. to be a multiple of $\mathsf v_i$. A student who only checks part (c) numerically without connecting it to the commutator from part (a) has missed the actual point of the problem — the numerical coincidence in (c) is guaranteed in advance by the vanishing commutator verified in (a).

Full credit: States the general theorem (commuting + nondegenerate $\Rightarrow$ shared eigenbasis) and cites it as the reason (c) works, not just as a lucky check.
Partial credit: Correct arithmetic throughout but treats (c) as an independent verification rather than a consequence of (a).
Common error: Forgetting to normalize eigenvectors, or sign errors in the $3\times3$ determinant expansion (double-check by re-expanding along a different row).

Griffiths A.28 — Diagonalizing a Hermitian Matrix, and the Trace/Det Sanity Check

📝 Problem Statement

Given $\mathsf T=\begin{pmatrix}1 & 1-i\\ 1+i & 0\end{pmatrix}$: (a) verify $\mathsf T$ is Hermitian; (b) find its (real) eigenvalues; (c) find and normalize its (orthogonal) eigenvectors; (d) construct the unitary diagonalizing matrix $\mathsf S$ and check explicitly that it diagonalizes $\mathsf T$; (e) check that $\det(\mathsf T)$ and $\text{Tr}(\mathsf T)$ agree before and after diagonalization.

(a) Hermiticity. $\mathsf T^\dagger=\tilde{\mathsf T}^* = \begin{pmatrix}1 & 1-i\\1+i & 0\end{pmatrix} = \mathsf T$. $\checkmark$ — note $T_{12}=1-i$ and $T_{21}=1+i$ are complex conjugates of each other, exactly the pattern flagged in the Hermitian misconception box in Section 2.

(b) Eigenvalues. $\det(\mathsf T-\lambda\mathsf I)=(1-\lambda)(-\lambda)-(1-i)(1+i) = -(1-\lambda)\lambda-2=\lambda^2-\lambda-2=0$, giving $\lambda=\frac{1\pm3}{2}$: $\lambda_1=2$, $\lambda_2=-1$ — both real, as guaranteed by Theorem 1 above.

(c) Eigenvectors, normalized and orthogonal. Solving $\mathsf T\mathsf a=\lambda\mathsf a$ for each root and normalizing ($|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$):

$$\mathsf a^{(1)} = \tfrac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}1-i\\1\end{pmatrix} \ (\lambda=2), \qquad \mathsf a^{(2)}=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt6}\begin{pmatrix}i-1\\2\end{pmatrix}\ (\lambda=-1).$$

Direct check: $\mathsf a^{(1)\dagger}\mathsf a^{(2)} = \frac{1}{3\sqrt2}\big[(1+i)(i-1)+1\cdot2\big] = \frac{1}{3\sqrt2}(i-1-1-i+2)=0$. $\checkmark$ — confirming Theorem 2 (distinct eigenvalues $\Rightarrow$ orthogonal eigenvectors) numerically.

(d) The unitary diagonalizing matrix. Following Equation A.81, the columns of $\mathsf S^{-1}$ are the eigenvectors $\mathsf a^{(j)}$; here $\mathsf S=\mathsf (S^{-1})^\dagger$ because $\mathsf S$ is unitary, giving

$$\mathsf S = \frac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}1+i & 1\\ -\tfrac{1+i}{\sqrt2} & \sqrt2\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \mathsf S\,\mathsf T\,\mathsf S^{-1} = \begin{pmatrix}2&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}. \checkmark$$

(e) The invariant check — teach yourself to do this on every diagonalization problem. Directly: $\text{Tr}(\mathsf T)=1+0=1$ and $\det(\mathsf T)=0-(1+i)(1-i)=0-2=-2$. From the diagonalized form: $\text{Tr}=2+(-1)=1$ $\checkmark$ and $\det=2\times(-1)=-2$ $\checkmark$. This is the single fastest error-catching habit you can build for this entire topic: since similarity transformations preserve trace and determinant (proved in the Matrix Mechanics section above), if your eigenvalues don't multiply to $\det(\mathsf T)$ and add to $\text{Tr}(\mathsf T)$, you have an arithmetic error in the characteristic equation — full stop, go back and recheck before doing anything else.

Full credit: Performs the Tr/det check explicitly as verification, not as an afterthought; keeps track of which eigenvector normalization uses $|\alpha|^2+|\beta|^2=1$ correctly with complex components.
Common error: Building $\mathsf S$ from unnormalized eigenvectors (breaks unitarity) or transposing rows/columns when assembling $\mathsf S$ from Equation A.81.

Liboff 4.4 — Translating Integrals Into Dirac Notation

📝 Problem Statement & Pattern-Matching Rules

Rewrite five ordinary integral/functional expressions in bra–ket notation. The real skill being tested is recognizing which Dirac-notation idiom each integral pattern corresponds to:

Integral formDirac notationPattern rule
$f(x)=g(x)$$|f\rangle=|g\rangle$Ordinary function equality $\to$ ket equality (same vector, same name convention).
$c=\int g^*(x')h(x')\,dx'$$c=\langle g|h\rangle$“Conjugate of one function times another, integrated” $\to$ inner product, textbook definition.
$f(x)=\sum_n\phi_n(x)\int\phi_n^*(x')f(x')\,dx'$$|f\rangle=\sum_n|\phi_n\rangle\langle\phi_n|f\rangle$Resolution of the identity: recognize $\sum_n|\phi_n\rangle\langle\phi_n|=\hat 1$ hiding inside the sum.
$\hat O\equiv\psi(x)\int dx'\,\phi^*(x')[\cdots]$$\hat O\equiv|\psi\rangle\langle\phi|$“A function, times an instruction to integrate against the conjugate of another function” $\to$ outer product (this builds an operator, not a number).
$\dfrac{\partial f(x)}{\partial x}=h(x)\displaystyle\int h^*(x')g(x')\,dx'$$\dfrac{\partial}{\partial x}\langle x|f\rangle=h(x)\,\langle h|g\rangle$The left side is already an ordinary coordinate-space derivative of the function $f(x)$ — i.e. of $\langle x|f\rangle$, the ket's component along $x$ — not a derivative of the abstract ket $|f\rangle$ itself. An abstract ket carries no $x$-dependence to differentiate; only its coordinate-representation component does. The right side is still the familiar outer-product pattern, $\langle h|g\rangle$ scaled by the ordinary function $h(x)$.

The key insight to internalize (and the one graders specifically look for): whenever you see “(function A) $\times \int$(conjugate of function B)(something)$\,dx'$,” that is the outer product $|A\rangle\langle B|$ acting as an operator — feed it a ket and it returns function A scaled by the overlap of B with whatever you fed it. Whenever you see “$\int$(conjugate of function A)(function B)$\,dx'$” standing alone as a number, that's the inner product $\langle A|B\rangle$, a scalar. Confusing these two patterns is the single most common notational error on this problem — and don't compound it by promoting an ordinary coordinate derivative $\partial f(x)/\partial x$ into a derivative "$\partial|f\rangle/\partial x$" of the abstract ket; $x$ is not a parameter the ket itself depends on.

Liboff 4.5 — Operator or Number? Reading $|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$ Correctly

📝 Problem Statement

Given the outer-product operator $\hat O\equiv|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$ and an arbitrary state $f$, classify each of the following as an operator, a vector, or a scalar.

The entire problem hinges on one rule: reading left to right, every complete $\langle\,\cdot\,|\,\cdot\,\rangle$ pair collapses into a single complex number, and whatever bra or ket is left over (unpaired) survives as a bra or ket.

$$\langle f|\hat O = \langle f|\phi\rangle\langle\psi| = C\langle\psi|, \quad C=\langle f|\phi\rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad \textbf{a bra (dual vector)}, \text{ scaled by scalar } C.$$
$$\hat O|f\rangle = |\phi\rangle\langle\psi|f\rangle = C|\phi\rangle, \quad C=\langle\psi|f\rangle \quad\Rightarrow\quad \textbf{a ket (vector)}, \text{ scaled by scalar } C.$$
$$\langle f|\hat O|f\rangle = \langle f|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|f\rangle = DC \quad\Rightarrow\quad \textbf{a scalar}: \text{ the product of two complex numbers, } D=\langle f|\phi\rangle,\ C=\langle\psi|f\rangle.$$
$$\langle f|\hat O|\psi\rangle = \langle f|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|\psi\rangle = D \quad\Rightarrow\quad \textbf{a scalar}: \langle\psi|\psi\rangle=1 \text{ (normalized) collapses the second pair to } 1.$$

Why this matters beyond bookkeeping: $\hat O=|\phi\rangle\langle\psi|$ is already an operator the moment it's written down — that doesn't change depending on what you multiply it by afterward. What changes is what you get when you evaluate a specific expression built from it: acting on a ket, $\hat O|f\rangle$, produces a ket (case (b) above); multiplying from the left by a bra, $\langle f|\hat O$, produces a bra (case (a)); and sandwiching it between a full bra and a full ket, $\langle f|\hat O|g\rangle$, consumes both open slots and leaves a single scalar matrix element (cases (c) and (d)). Count the open bra/ket slots in whatever expression you're evaluating to know whether the result is a vector, a bra, or a scalar — but $\hat O$ itself is an operator throughout, not just in the cases where it happens to output a vector. This exact bookkeeping is what makes the projection operator $\hat P=|\alpha\rangle\langle\alpha|$ from Section 6 well-defined as an operator.

Full credit: Explicitly identifies, for each sub-part, which bra/ket pairs collapse to scalars and which single bra or ket survives.
Common error: Treating $\langle f|\hat O|\psi\rangle$ as if it must reduce further using $\langle f|\phi\rangle$ — it doesn't, because $\phi$ and $f$ are unrelated arbitrary states; $D=\langle f|\phi\rangle$ is simply left as a general overlap, not simplified to a number like 1.

Problem 1 — Diagonalize a Hermitian Matrix (parallel to A.28)
Let $\mathsf T=\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1+i\\ 1-i & 3\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Verify $\mathsf T$ is Hermitian. (b) Find its eigenvalues. (c) Find and normalize its eigenvectors, and confirm they are orthogonal. (d) Check that $\text{Tr}(\mathsf T)$ and $\det(\mathsf T)$ match the sum and product of your eigenvalues.
Show Solution

(a) $\mathsf T^\dagger=\begin{pmatrix}2 & 1+i\\1-i&3\end{pmatrix}=\mathsf T$. $\checkmark$

(b) $\det(\mathsf T-\lambda\mathsf I)=(2-\lambda)(3-\lambda)-(1+i)(1-i) = \lambda^2-5\lambda+6-2=\lambda^2-5\lambda+4=(\lambda-1)(\lambda-4)=0$, so $\lambda_1=1,\ \lambda_2=4$.

(c) For $\lambda=1$: $(2-1)a+(1+i)b=0\Rightarrow a=-(1+i)b$; normalizing ($|a|^2+|b|^2=2+1=3$ for $b=1$) gives $\mathsf a^{(1)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}-(1+i)\\1\end{pmatrix}$. For $\lambda=4$: $(2-4)a+(1+i)b=0\Rightarrow b=a(1-i)$; normalizing ($1+2=3$ for $a=1$) gives $\mathsf a^{(2)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1-i\end{pmatrix}$. Orthogonality: $\mathsf a^{(1)\dagger}\mathsf a^{(2)}=\frac{1}{3}\left[-(1-i)(1)+1\cdot(1-i)\right]=0$. $\checkmark$

(d) $\text{Tr}(\mathsf T)=2+3=5=1+4$. $\checkmark$   $\det(\mathsf T)=6-(1+i)(1-i)=6-2=4=1\times4$. $\checkmark$

Problem 2 — Non-Commuting Operators Do NOT Share an Eigenbasis
Consider the two Pauli-type matrices $\hat\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\hat\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$. (a) Compute $[\hat\sigma_z,\hat\sigma_x]$ and show it is nonzero. (b) Find the eigenvectors of $\hat\sigma_z$. (c) Verify directly that these are not eigenvectors of $\hat\sigma_x$. Why doesn't this contradict anything proved in the Matrix Mechanics section?
Show Solution

(a) $\hat\sigma_z\hat\sigma_x=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix}$, $\hat\sigma_x\hat\sigma_z=\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $[\hat\sigma_z,\hat\sigma_x]=\begin{pmatrix}0&2\\-2&0\end{pmatrix}=2i\begin{pmatrix}0&-i\\i&0\end{pmatrix}=2i\hat\sigma_y\neq0$.

(b) $\hat\sigma_z$'s eigenvectors are simply $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalue $+1$) and $\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ (eigenvalue $-1$), since $\hat\sigma_z$ is already diagonal.

(c) $\hat\sigma_x\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$, which is not a multiple of $\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ — not an eigenvector of $\hat\sigma_x$.

No contradiction: the “commuting $\Rightarrow$ shared eigenbasis” theorem is an if, not an unconditional guarantee — here $[\hat\sigma_z,\hat\sigma_x]\neq0$, so the theorem's hypothesis simply isn't satisfied, and there is no reason to expect (and every reason not to expect) a shared eigenbasis. This is the algebraic seed of Topic 8: the angular momentum operators satisfy $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=i\hbar\hat L_z$ (structurally identical to this problem), which is exactly why no state can be a simultaneous eigenstate of two different components of angular momentum.

Problem 3 — Dirac-Notation Fluency
(a) A state $|\psi\rangle=\sum_n c_n|n\rangle$ is expanded in an orthonormal discrete basis $\{|n\rangle\}$. Write $\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle$ purely in terms of the coefficients $c_n$ and the matrix elements $A_{mn}=\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle$. (b) Using the position-basis resolution of the identity, show that $\int f^*(x)\left[\int\delta(x-x')g(x')\,dx'\right]dx = \langle f|g\rangle$.
Show Solution

(a) Insert two resolutions of the identity (or just expand both bra and ket): $$\langle\psi|\hat A|\psi\rangle = \left(\sum_m c_m^*\langle m|\right)\hat A\left(\sum_n c_n|n\rangle\right) = \sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n\langle m|\hat A|n\rangle = \sum_{m,n}c_m^*c_n A_{mn}.$$ This is the general formula for computing an expectation value directly from an operator's matrix representation, without ever returning to position space — the matrix-mechanics analogue of $\langle A\rangle=\int\Psi^*\hat A\Psi\,dx$.

(b) The inner bracket is just $g(x)$ (sifting property of the delta function), so the whole expression is $\int f^*(x)g(x)\,dx=\langle f|g\rangle$ by definition. In Dirac language: $\int\delta(x-x')g(x')\,dx' = \langle x|\hat 1|g\rangle=\langle x|g\rangle$ using $\hat 1=\int|x'\rangle\langle x'|\,dx'$, so the whole left side is $\int f^*(x)\langle x|g\rangle\,dx = \langle f|\left(\int|x\rangle\langle x|\,dx\right)|g\rangle=\langle f|\hat 1|g\rangle=\langle f|g\rangle$ — the resolution of the identity in the position basis, inserted and immediately removed.

Problem 4 — A Generalized Uncertainty Relation (Preview of Angular Momentum)
Two Hermitian operators satisfy $[\hat A,\hat B]=i\hbar\hat C$, where $\hat C$ is also Hermitian. In some state, $\langle\hat C\rangle = \hbar/2$. What is the minimum possible value of $\sigma_A\sigma_B$ in that state? (This is exactly the algebraic shape of $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=i\hbar\hat L_z$, which you'll meet in Topic 8.)
Show Solution

From the boxed generalized uncertainty principle, $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2\geq\left(\frac{1}{2i}\langle[\hat A,\hat B]\rangle\right)^2 = \left(\frac{1}{2i}\cdot i\hbar\langle\hat C\rangle\right)^2 = \left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\langle\hat C\rangle\right)^2$. With $\langle\hat C\rangle=\hbar/2$: $\sigma_A^2\sigma_B^2\geq\left(\frac{\hbar}{2}\cdot\frac{\hbar}{2}\right)^2=\frac{\hbar^4}{16}$, so $\sigma_A\sigma_B\geq\frac{\hbar^2}{4}$. Notice the structural point: because $\hat C$ itself has a nonzero expectation value, the uncertainty bound is state-dependent, not a fixed number the way $\sigma_x\sigma_p\geq\hbar/2$ is — exactly the situation you'll encounter with $\sigma_{L_x}\sigma_{L_y}\geq\frac{\hbar}{2}|\langle\hat L_z\rangle|$ in Topic 8.

Topic 7 — Time Development & Parity

The generalized Ehrenfest theorem derived in Section 5, $\frac{d\langle Q\rangle}{dt}=\frac{i}{\hbar}\langle[\hat H,\hat Q]\rangle+\langle\partial\hat Q/\partial t\rangle$, is the central tool of Topic 7: it's how you show $\langle x\rangle$ obeys Newton's second law on average (Ehrenfest's theorem proper, the special case $\hat Q=\hat x$), and how you show that any observable commuting with $\hat H$ is conserved. Topic 8 — Angular Momentum & the Hydrogen Atom

The angular momentum operators $\hat L_x,\hat L_y,\hat L_z$ are each individually Hermitian (real eigenvalues, as guaranteed by Theorem 1) but satisfy $[\hat L_x,\hat L_y]=i\hbar\hat L_z$ and cyclic permutations — they do not commute with each other. By the uncertainty principle proved in Section 5 (worked concretely in Practice Problem 4 above), no state can simultaneously be a determinate eigenstate of two different components of angular momentum. This is precisely why Topic 8 builds the hydrogen-atom eigenbasis around the mutually commuting triad $\hat H$, $\hat L^2$, $\hat L_z$ instead — a direct application of the “commuting operators share an eigenbasis” theorem from Section 7, which you've now seen proved in the abstract and demonstrated concretely in Griffiths A.25.

🔗 The One-Sentence Summary of This Entire Topic

Physical observables are Hermitian operators because that's what guarantees real eigenvalues; two observables can be simultaneously sharp exactly when their operators commute, because that's what guarantees a shared eigenbasis; and everything you'll do with angular momentum, time evolution, and spin for the rest of this course is an application of these two facts.
