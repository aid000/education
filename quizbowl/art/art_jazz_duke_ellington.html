Quick Overview

Duke Ellington, c. 1946
Instrument / rolePiano; composer, arranger & bandleader (the orchestra was his "instrument")
Born / diedWashington, D.C. 1899 – New York 1974
Era / styleSwing / big band; "beyond category" — also extended/sacred works
Home cityNew York / Harlem; resident at the Cotton Club 1927–31
Key ensembleThe Duke Ellington Orchestra; longtime collaborator Billy Strayhorn
Nicknames"Duke" (boyhood); the orchestra's theme was "Take the 'A' Train"

Defining traits

  • Composer first: treated his orchestra as a single instrument, writing parts for the specific tone of individual players (the "Ellington effect").
  • "Jungle" style at the Cotton Club — growling, plunger-muted brass from Bubber Miley and Cootie Williams.
  • Elevated jazz toward concert music with extended works and suites (e.g. Black, Brown and Beige) and later Sacred Concerts.
  • Lifelong writing partnership with Billy Strayhorn, who wrote the band's theme "Take the 'A' Train."
  • Famously said his music was "beyond category."

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "This bandleader's theme song was 'Take the A Train.'"
  • "Mood Indigo," "It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing)," "Sophisticated Lady."
  • "House band at the Cotton Club."
  • "Wrote with Billy Strayhorn."

Signature Compositions (Very Common)

The compositions most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

"Take the 'A' Train"
1939 (recorded 1941) · composed by Billy Strayhorn · RCA Victor
recogThe Ellington Orchestra's theme song — its title refers to the A subway line up to Harlem.
recogTrumpet solo by Ray Nance; became the band's signature opener.
powerActually composed by Billy Strayhorn, not Ellington; the title came from Strayhorn's directions to Ellington's apartment. It replaced "Sepia Panorama" as the theme after a 1941 ASCAP radio ban pushed Ellington to commission new material from Strayhorn and his son Mercer.

"It Don't Mean a Thing (If It Ain't Got That Swing)"
1931 · lyrics by Irving Mills · vocal by Ivie Anderson
recogIts title coined the word "swing" years before the Swing Era; the "doo-wah" vocal is iconic.
recogEarly vocal by Ivie Anderson, the band's featured singer; lyric credited to manager Irving Mills.
powerEllington said the phrase came from his former trumpeter Bubber Miley, who was dying when the song was written. Recorded for Brunswick in 1932; one of the earliest pieces to use "swing" in a title.

"Mood Indigo"
1930 · co-credited to Barney Bigard · originally "Dreamy Blues"
recogFamous for its inverted, "upside-down" voicing — muted trumpet, trombone, and low clarinet in an unusual blend.
recogOriginally titled "Dreamy Blues"; the clarinet melody is often credited to Barney Bigard.
powerThe signature "microphone tone" / blended timbre is a textbook example of the "Ellington effect." First broadcast/recorded in 1930.
wiki ↗
Black, Brown and Beige
1943 · premiered at Carnegie Hall · extended "tone parallel"
recogEllington's most ambitious extended work — a "tone parallel to the history of the American Negro," premiered at Carnegie Hall in January 1943.
recogIts spiritual "Come Sunday" later became a feature for Mahalia Jackson and Johnny Hodges.
powerCritics initially panned it for blurring jazz and concert music; it anchored a series of annual Carnegie Hall concerts. Part of Ellington's drive to prove jazz a serious American art form.

"Diminuendo and Crescendo in Blue" (Newport 1956)
1956 · Newport Jazz Festival · Ellington at Newport (Columbia)
recogSaxophonist Paul Gonsalves played a famous 27-chorus solo bridging the two halves, igniting the crowd.
recogThe performance revived Ellington's career and put him on the cover of Time magazine.
powerCaptured on the live album Ellington at Newport (1956), one of the best-selling records of his career. The two-part piece dates to 1937.

"Sophisticated Lady"
1932 · lyrics later by Mitchell Parish & Irving Mills
recogA lush ballad standard, a staple of the band's book alongside "Prelude to a Kiss" and "In a Sentimental Mood."
recogFeatures the warm alto sound of Johnny Hodges, Ellington's star soloist.
powerEllington said the melody was inspired by schoolteachers he knew. Lyrics added by Mitchell Parish; the chromatic bridge is famously tricky to harmonize.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Sidemen / collaborators

  • Billy Strayhorn — composer-arranger; wrote "Take the 'A' Train," "Lush Life"
  • Johnny Hodges — star alto saxophonist; lush ballad tone
  • Bubber Miley & Cootie Williams — plunger-mute "jungle" trumpets
  • Juan Tizol — valve trombonist; co-wrote "Caravan"
  • Paul Gonsalves — tenor sax; the 27-chorus Newport solo
  • Irving Mills — early manager/lyricist; Ivie Anderson — vocalist

Innovations / "first to…"

  • The "Ellington effect" — orchestrating for the individual player's tone
  • Brought jazz to the concert hall with extended suites (Carnegie Hall, 1943)
  • Pioneered "jungle" sound with plunger-muted growl brass
  • Among the first to write sacred jazz (the Sacred Concerts)
  • Treated the orchestra itself as his instrument

Key works for buzzing

  • "Take the 'A' Train" — Strayhorn; band theme
  • "It Don't Mean a Thing" — coined "swing"
  • "Mood Indigo" — inverted voicing ("Dreamy Blues")
  • Black, Brown and Beige — Carnegie Hall tone parallel
  • Newport 1956 — Gonsalves's 27 choruses
  • "Sophisticated Lady," "Caravan," "Satin Doll"

Biographical / "also did" trivia

  • Born Edward Kennedy Ellington, Washington, D.C., 1899
  • House band at the Cotton Club, Harlem, 1927–31 (radio broadcasts made him famous)
  • Called his music "beyond category"
  • Awarded the Presidential Medal of Freedom (Nixon, 1969)
  • Scored films (Anatomy of a Murder, 1959) and toured for the State Department
  • Died 1974; son Mercer Ellington took over the band

Buzz Traps (Answer-Line Confusions)

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Count BasieBoth lead swing big bands, but Basie's is Kansas City, riff-based, spare piano ("One O'Clock Jump," "April in Paris"). Ellington is the composer with extended suites and the Cotton Club / Strayhorn axis.
Billy StrayhornStrayhorn actually wrote "Take the 'A' Train" and "Lush Life," but he is Ellington's collaborator, not the bandleader. If the answer is the orchestra's leader, it's Ellington; clues stressing the unsung writer point to Strayhorn.
Louis ArmstrongArmstrong is a trumpet/vocal soloist from New Orleans; Ellington is a pianist/composer/bandleader from D.C. and Harlem. Cotton Club + suites + Strayhorn = Ellington.
Fletcher HendersonHenderson was an earlier big-band arranger (and arranged for Benny Goodman). Ellington's distinctive "jungle" sound and individual-tone scoring set him apart.
Cab CallowayCalloway also fronted a Cotton Club band ("Minnie the Moocher," hi-de-ho), but he was a vocalist/showman, not the composer of suites like Black, Brown and Beige.
Benny GoodmanGoodman is the white "King of Swing" clarinetist; his 1938 Carnegie Hall concert predates Ellington's. The composer behind extended jazz suites and "beyond category" is Ellington.

Cross-discipline big picture Ellington reframed jazz as American concert music: his extended suites (Black, Brown and Beige) carried jazz into Carnegie Hall and link to African-American history as a "tone parallel." He scored Hollywood films (Anatomy of a Murder), toured as a State Department cultural ambassador (yielding The Far East Suite), set Shakespeare to music (Such Sweet Thunder), and wrote sacred music late in life. In quizbowl terms he spans music, American history, film, and even literature.
