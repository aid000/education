Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Stravinsky died in 1971; his scores and photos remain under copyright. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection. If an image fails to load, the filename may differ slightly from the Wikipedia article title.

NationalityRussian-born; French citizen 1934; American citizen 1945; died New York, 1971
DatesBorn Oranienbaum (near St. Petersburg), 17 June 1882
TeacherPrivate student of Rimsky-Korsakov (1902–1908)
ImpresarioBreakthrough works commissioned by Sergei Diaghilev / Ballets Russes
Three periodsRussian (folk-infused) → Neoclassical → Serial / 12-tone (late)
OutputBallets, operas, symphonies, concertos, choral works, chamber music spanning ~60 years

Defining traits

  • Radical rhythmic innovation: irregular meters, polyrhythm, sudden metrical shifts — especially in The Rite of Spring.
  • Russian period hallmarks: folk-melody borrowings, pungent orchestral color, bitonal / polytonal harmony.
  • Neoclassical aesthetic: transparent textures, clear forms (suite, concerto grosso, fugue) recast with modern dissonance and irony.
  • Late serial turn: after Schoenberg's death (1951), adopted twelve-tone technique — a late conversion that surprised the musical world.
  • Collaborator of Pablo Picasso, Jean Cocteau, Coco Chanel, T. S. Eliot, and choreographers Nijinsky and Balanchine.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The Rite of Spring premiere riot (29 May 1913, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris) — the single most asked-about Stravinsky clue.
  • The Firebird / Petrushka / Rite of Spring — the "three Diaghilev ballets" trio; naming any two in a clue almost always points to Stravinsky.
  • The octatonic scale / "Stravinsky scale" as a harmonic fingerprint.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Rite of Spring (Le Sacre du printemps)
1913 · ballet · premiered Paris, 29 May 1913 · Ballets Russes / choreography: Nijinsky
recogPremiere caused a riot in the audience — booing, catcalling, and near-fistfights at the Théâtre des Champs-Élysées, Paris.
recogSubject: pagan Slavic ritual sacrifice — an adolescent girl dances herself to death in the "Sacrificial Dance" finale.
powerOpens with a solo bassoon in its highest register — a Lithuanian folk tune (Tu, tu, mano seserele). The score uses constantly changing time signatures (e.g., alternating 5/8, 3/16, 2/8 in the Sacrificial Dance). Featured in Disney's Fantasia (1940) with dinosaurs.
wiki ↗
The Firebird (L'Oiseau de feu)
1910 · ballet · premiered Paris, 25 June 1910 · Ballets Russes / choreography: Fokine
recogBased on Russian folk tales: the Firebird helps Prince Ivan defeat the immortal sorcerer Kashchei the Deathless by locating his soul hidden in an egg.
recogThe "Infernal Dance of King Kashchei" and the closing "Finale" are the most performed excerpts; three orchestral suites extracted (1911, 1919, 1945).
powerStravinsky's first major commission from Diaghilev (originally offered to Anatoly Lyadov, who failed to produce it). The score uses octatonic harmonies for Kashchei's evil world vs. diatonic for the human world — a classic Stravinsky fingerprint.
wiki ↗
Petrushka
1911 · ballet · premiered Paris, 13 June 1911 · Ballets Russes / choreography: Fokine
recogThree puppets at a Russian Shrovetide Fair: Petrushka (the clown), the Ballerina, and the Moor. Petrushka loves the Ballerina; the Moor kills him.
recogThe "Petrushka chord" — simultaneous C major and F-sharp major triads (a bitonal polytonal superimposition) — is named after and from this ballet.
powerStravinsky originally conceived it as a Concert piece for piano and orchestra (hence the prominent piano part). The opening "Russian Dance" and "Danse russe" use borrowed street-song and barrel-organ material, evoking the fairground. Revised 1947 for smaller orchestra.
wiki ↗
The Soldier's Tale (L'Histoire du soldat)
1918 · theatre piece (narrator, dancers, 7 instruments) · premiered Lausanne, 28 Sep 1918
recogA soldier sells his violin to the Devil (based on a Russian folk tale); scored for a tiny ensemble of 7 players (violin, double bass, clarinet, bassoon, cornet, trombone, percussion) and narrator.
recogWritten for a touring chamber ensemble during WWI austerity; Stravinsky called it a work for the stage but not an opera or ballet.
powerLibretto by C. F. Ramuz; conductor/musical director Ernest Ansermet at premiere. The score blends ragtime, tango, waltz, and Lutheran chorale — an early example of genre collage. The "Royal March" and "Tango" are frequently performed separately.
wiki ↗
Symphony of Psalms
1930 · choral symphony · premiered Brussels, 13 Dec 1930 · commissioned by Koussevitzky / Boston Symphony
recogSets Latin texts from Psalms 38, 39, and 150 ("Laudate Dominum") for chorus and orchestra, with no violins or violas — an austere, archaic sound.
recogA cornerstone of his Neoclassical period; dedicated "to the glory of God and to the Boston Symphony Orchestra."
powerThe opening E-minor oboe/bassoon figure is referred to as the "Psalms motif"; the third movement's fugue (double fugue: voices first, then orchestra) is widely analyzed as a model of Stravinsky's Neoclassical counterpoint. Commissioned by Serge Koussevitzky for the BSO's 50th anniversary.
wiki ↗
The Rake's Progress
1951 · opera in three acts · premiered Venice, 11 Sep 1951 · libretto: W. H. Auden & Chester Kallman
recogLibretto by W. H. Auden and Chester Kallman; inspired by Hogarth's 1734 engraving series of the same name — a rake who sells his soul to the Devil.
recogThe work ends with a moralizing epilogue in which the characters address the audience directly, echoing Don Giovanni.
powerThe rake Tom Rakewell is led astray by the devil-figure Nick Shadow; love interest is Anne Trulove. Uses harpsichord for recitatives — a deliberately Baroque reference that marks the peak of Stravinsky's Neoclassicism. Premiered at the Teatro La Fenice, Venice. After this opera Stravinsky began his serial period.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Biographical hard clues

  • Born Oranienbaum (now Lomonosov), near St. Petersburg, 1882
  • Father Fyodor Stravinsky — principal bass at the Imperial Opera
  • Private composition student of Rimsky-Korsakov (1902–1908); Rimsky's death (1908) freed his style
  • Met Diaghilev in 1909; The Firebird commission launched his career
  • Lived in Switzerland during WWI, then Paris 1920–1939, then Hollywood / New York from 1939
  • Close collaborator and friend of Robert Craft in later life — conductor, amanuensis, co-author of conversation books
  • Died New York, 6 April 1971; buried in the San Michele cemetery, Venice — same island as Diaghilev

Key collaborators

  • Sergei Diaghilev — Ballets Russes impresario; commissioned Firebird, Petrushka, Rite
  • Vaslav Nijinsky — choreographer of the Rite of Spring premiere
  • George Balanchine — lifelong choreographic partner; Apollo, Orpheus, Agon
  • Pablo Picasso — set/costume designer for Pulcinella (1920)
  • Jean Cocteau — librettist for Oedipus Rex (Latin version via Jean Daniélou)
  • W. H. Auden & Chester Kallman — librettists, The Rake's Progress
  • C. F. Ramuz — librettist, The Soldier's Tale

Style markers & terms

  • Octatonic scale (alternating whole and half steps) — pervasive harmonic device across all three periods
  • "Petrushka chord" — C major + F-sharp major bitonality
  • Polyrhythm / polymeter — simultaneous different time signatures or rapid metrical change
  • Ostinato — short repeated rhythmic/melodic cell driving forward motion
  • Neo-classicism — deliberate reference to Baroque and Classical forms (Pergolesi, Bach, Handel)
  • Serial method adopted after Schoenberg's death (1951) — often noted as ironic given their rivalry

Famous quotes & numbers

  • "Music is, by its very nature, essentially powerless to express anything at all." — from Chronicle of My Life (1936)
  • "Good composers do not imitate; they steal." (often attributed, possibly apocryphal)
  • Three Russian ballets: Firebird (1910), Petrushka (1911), Rite of Spring (1913)
  • Rite of Spring premiere: 29 May 1913, Théâtre des Champs-Élysées
  • Three periods: Russian → Neoclassical (1920–1951) → Serial (1952–1966)
  • Requiem Canticles played at Robert Kennedy's funeral (1968) and at Stravinsky's own

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Sergei ProkofievBoth Russian modernists born within a decade of each other. Prokofiev = neoclassical humor, fairytale subjects (Peter and the Wolf, Romeo and Juliet, Cinderella), worked in the Soviet Union (returned 1936), died same day as Stalin (5 March 1953). Stravinsky = radical rhythmic violence, lived in the West. Clue "wolf theme for oboe" or "Romeo and Juliet ballet" → Prokofiev.
Sergei RachmaninoffRachmaninoff is a late Romantic pianist-composer — lush, chromatic, dense piano writing (Piano Concerto No. 2, Rhapsody on a Theme of Paganini, Symphonic Dances). Never associated with riot premieres or ballets. Both fled Russia and lived in the West.
Nikolai Rimsky-KorsakovRimsky-Korsakov is Stravinsky's teacher and a member of The Five (Mighty Handful). His orchestral showpieces (Scheherazade, Capriccio Espagnol, Flight of the Bumblebee) are lush, colorful, Late Romantic — not rhythmically radical. Clue "thousand and one nights suite" → Rimsky, not Stravinsky.
Arnold SchoenbergSchoenberg invented twelve-tone technique (1921); Stravinsky adopted it only after Schoenberg died (1951). Schoenberg = Pierrot lunaire (Sprechstimme), Verklärte Nacht, Five Orchestral Pieces. Their rivalry (Stravinsky's neoclassicism vs. Schoenberg's serialism) is called the "War of the Romantics" by historians.
Béla BartókBartók uses ethnomusicological folk research (Hungarian, Romanian peasant music) and percussive piano writing (Allegro Barbaro, Music for Strings, Percussion, and Celesta, Concerto for Orchestra). Both modernists use rhythmic complexity and folk sources, but Bartók never worked with Diaghilev or wrote a riot-premiering ballet.
Claude DebussyDebussy is French Impressionism — whole-tone scale, modal harmony, shimmering textures (Prelude to the Afternoon of a Faun, La Mer, Pelléas et Mélisande). Stravinsky admired and was influenced by him; they collaborated briefly. Clue "Faun's afternoon" or "Pelléas" → Debussy.

Cross-discipline big picture Stravinsky is the central figure bridging Russian nationalism (Rimsky-Korsakov lineage, folk-tune borrowing) through modernist rupture (Rite of Spring as the musical 20th century's starting gun) and Neoclassical irony (Pulcinella, Rake's Progress) to serialism. His Ballets Russes years connect directly to visual arts tossups (Picasso's Pulcinella designs, Nijinsky's choreography) and literature (Cocteau, Auden). The Rite of Spring premiere riot belongs to the same category of category-crossing answers as Guernica's unveiling or the opening night of Ibsen's Ghosts — questions about art scandals. His Requiem Canticles ties to American history (Robert F. Kennedy's funeral, 1968). The octatonic scale bridges to music theory tossups.
