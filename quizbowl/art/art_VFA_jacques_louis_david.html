Quick Overview

📷 Image note: David died in 1825, so all works are firmly public domain and available on Wikimedia Commons at high resolution. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection.

NationalityFrench (born Paris, 1748–1825)
PeriodLate 18th – early 19th century; active 1770s–1820s
MovementNeoclassicism — the defining figure
PoliticsJacobin; voted to execute Louis XVI; Napoleon's premier peintre
TrainingStudent of Joseph-Marie Vien; studied in Rome on the Prix de Rome
LegacyTeacher of Ingres, Gros, Gérard, Isabey — shaped the entire 19th-c. French tradition

Defining traits

  • Heroic, austere classicism — figures from Greco-Roman antiquity deployed as political allegory; flat, even lighting; sculpted forms.
  • Revolutionary and Napoleonic propaganda: many canvases were direct state commissions celebrating the Republic or Bonaparte.
  • Moral grandeur: subjects choose civic duty over personal survival — oath-taking, self-sacrifice, stoic death.
  • Large-format history painting elevated to political weapon; intimate, psychologically charged portraiture in parallel.
  • Exiled to Brussels after Napoleon's fall (1816); died there 1825.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Three right arms thrust toward three swords — the single most given-away clue (Oath of the Horatii).
  • Marat dead in his bathtub, pen still in hand, letter visible — the second easiest clue.
  • "Neoclassical French painter" or "painter who served as Napoleon's premier peintre" — common category-level giveaways.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Oath of the Horatii
1784 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerCommissioned for the French crown by d'Angiviller; David added the father-figure and the famous outstretched-arm gesture from Corneille's play Horace (not found in Livy). Exhibited in Rome before Paris, 1785.
recogThree brothers each extend a right arm toward three swords held by their father; women weep at right.
recogStark tripartite arch background; figures lit as if on a stage; the weeping woman at right is Sabina (married to a Curiatius enemy).

The Death of Marat
1793 · oil on canvas · Royal Museums of Fine Arts, Brussels
powerDavid was Marat's close friend and a deputy; he visited Marat the day before his assassination by Charlotte Corday. The letter Marat holds is forged by David to improve his image; the wooden crate used as his desk bears David's signature.
recogJean-Paul Marat slumped dead in his medicinal bathtub, quill in hand, a letter from Corday visible; a knife on the floor.
recogStark, dark background (no decorative detail); stark Pietà-like pose. One of the most reproduced paintings of the French Revolution.
wiki ↗
Napoleon Crossing the Alps
1801–1802 · oil on canvas · five versions; Château de Malmaison (main)
powerNapoleon specifically requested a calm horse and "peaceful on a fiery horse" pose; David made five versions for different courts. The rocks in the foreground are inscribed with Hannibal and Karolus Magnus (Charlemagne) alongside Bonaparte.
recogNapoleon on a rearing white horse, pointing upward with his right hand; billowing red cape; snowy Alpine pass.
recogHeroic propaganda portrait — in reality Napoleon crossed on a mule. Compare to Delaroche's 1850 Napoleon Crossing the Alps (mundane mule version).
wiki ↗
The Coronation of Napoleon (Le Sacre de Napoléon)
1807 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerDavid included himself in the painting (upper left gallery, sketch in hand); also included Napoleon's mother Letizia seated in the front row — though she did not actually attend. Pope Pius VII raises his hand in blessing but does not crown.
recogNapoleon crowns Joséphine (not himself) in Notre-Dame; Pope Pius VII seated behind, hands raised.
recogEnormous canvas (~10 m wide), crammed with ~200 identifiable figures; gold and ermine everywhere.
wiki ↗
The Death of Socrates
1787 · oil on canvas · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
powerPainted for Charles-Michel Trudaine de la Sablière; Denis Diderot's friend d'Angiviller championed it; David consulted the antiquarian Quatremère de Quincy for architectural accuracy. Winckelmann's aesthetics visibly applied.
recogSocrates reaches for the hemlock cup while still gesturing upward, disciples weep around him; Plato shown as an old man at foot of bed (anachronistically).
recogTheatrical lighting, frieze-like arrangement; exemplar of Neoclassical virtue-through-stoic-death.
wiki ↗
Intervention of the Sabine Women
1799 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerPainted during David's imprisonment after Thermidor; widely read as a plea for national reconciliation after the Terror. David charged admission to view the canvas — an unprecedented commercial move for a history painter.
recogHersilia (Sabine wife of Romulus) throws herself between armed Romans and Sabines, arms outstretched; babies on the ground.
recogFigures almost nude in Greek style (David argued Romans and Sabines would have fought thus); shields and helmets in archaic Greek form.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Outstretched arms taking oaths or imploring — the David "gesture"
  • Frieze-like arrangement of figures parallel to the picture plane
  • Stark, bare backgrounds — architectural arches or plain dark voids
  • Theatrical, raking light from one side; deep shadows
  • Draped vs. undraped contrast — weeping women clothed, heroic men near-nude
  • Virtuous death scenes: hemlock cup, bathtub, bier

Historical links

  • French Revolution: Jacobin, voted for Louis XVI's execution
  • Committee of General Security — organized Revolutionary festivals
  • Designed the Festival of the Supreme Being (1794) for Robespierre
  • Napoleon's premier peintre from 1804
  • Exiled to Brussels 1816 as regicide; died there 1825
  • Friend of Marat; the Death of Marat is personal witness

Techniques & terms

  • Neoclassicism — reaction against Rococo frivolity
  • Prix de Rome — won on fourth attempt, 1774
  • Influenced by Poussin, Raphael, and Roman relief sculpture
  • Winckelmann's idea of "noble simplicity, quiet grandeur"
  • Portraits known for psychological directness and clean backgrounds
  • History painting (peinture d'histoire) as the highest genre

Students & circle

  • Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres — most famous student
  • Antoine-Jean Gros — carried Napoleonic painting further
  • François Gérard and Anne-Louis Girodet — major pupils
  • Teacher Joseph-Marie Vien (early Neoclassicist)
  • Rival/contrast: Jean-Baptiste Greuze (sentimental moralism)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Jean-Auguste-Dominique IngresDavid's student; smoother, more sensuous line; known for Grande Odalisque, La Source, Turkish-bath harem scenes. More Romantic-adjacent. David = harder, more austere, politically charged.
Nicolas Poussin17th-century Frenchman who inspired David; also classicizing, also Roman subjects. Poussin = smaller, cooler, more meditative. David = large-format, politically urgent, 18th–19th century.
Antoine-Jean GrosDavid's pupil who painted Napoleonic battle scenes (Napoleon at Eylau, Battle of Abukir). Gros = battlefield carnage, looser brushwork — transitional to Romanticism. David = controlled, stoic, indoor or formal-outdoor.
Eugène DelacroixRomantic, not Neoclassical: loose brushwork, writhing diagonal compositions, vivid color (Liberty Leading the People, Death of Sardanapalus). The stylistic opposite of David's cool, linear clarity.
Benjamin WestAnglo-American Neoclassicist, contemporary. Best known for The Death of General Wolfe (1770). West used modern dress; David used classical or appropriate costume. Both painted stoic deaths.
Élisabeth Vigée Le BrunFemale court painter of the same era; associated with Marie Antoinette (fled France at Revolution). Softer, Rococo-influenced portraits — contrast to David's austere republican canvases.

Cross-discipline big picture David's career is a direct mirror of French political history: Oath of the Horatii (1784) prefigures Revolutionary republicanism; Death of Marat (1793) is Jacobin martyrology; Napoleon Crossing the Alps (1801) and Coronation (1807) are Imperial propaganda; Intervention of the Sabine Women (1799) pleads for post-Terror reconciliation. Linking David's canvases to their political context — ancien régime → Revolution → Directory → Consulate → Empire → Bourbon Restoration — is the cleanest bridge from art tossups to French history ones. His student Ingres and pupil Gros anchor follow-up questions, and the Death of Socrates links to philosophy (Plato, hemlock) and Classical antiquity.
