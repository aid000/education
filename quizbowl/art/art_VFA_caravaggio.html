Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Caravaggio's works are well over 400 years old and in the public domain; images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect. Requires an internet connection. If an image fails, the Wikimedia file title likely differs — the file title is a best guess.

Full nameMichelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio
NationalityItalian; born Milan (or Caravaggio), 1571–1610
Period / MovementLate Renaissance → Baroque; founder of Tenebrist naturalism
Signature techniqueChiaroscuro / tenebrism — extreme light-dark contrast
Key patronsCardinal Francesco Maria Del Monte; Marchese Vincenzo Giustiniani
DeathDied age 38, Porto Ercole, 1610; on the run after killing Ranuccio Tomassoni

Defining traits

  • Tenebrism: figures emerge from near-total darkness — a single strong raking light source, no ambient fill.
  • Radical naturalism: peasants, street people, and tavern models used for sacred figures; dirty feet visible on saints.
  • Drama and violence: executions, beheadings, and martyrdoms rendered at maximum physical intensity.
  • No preparatory drawings: worked directly on the canvas; incised lines in the ground layer sometimes still visible.
  • Self-portraits hidden as severed heads: his own face appears as Goliath (David with the Head of Goliath) and possibly as Holofernes.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Chiaroscuro" or "tenebrism" in the question almost always points to Caravaggio.
  • Light slanting into dark rooms, dirty feet on sacred figures, or extreme foreshortening of a dead body.
  • Killed a man (Ranuccio Tomassoni, 1606) and fled Rome — his violent biography is a common opening clue.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Calling of Saint Matthew
1599–1600 · oil on canvas · Contarelli Chapel, San Luigi dei Francesi, Rome
recogChrist and Peter (faces obscured) enter a tavern; a beam of light falls across a group of tax collectors counting coins.
recogMatthew (disputed figure — old bearded man or young man pointing at himself?) sits at a money-changing table.
powerCommissioned for the Contarelli Chapel alongside The Inspiration of Saint Matthew and The Martyrdom of Saint Matthew; Christ's pointing gesture echoes Michelangelo's Creation of Adam. First public commission, career-making.

Judith Beheading Holofernes
c. 1598–1599 · oil on canvas · Galleria Nazionale d'Arte Antica, Rome
recogA young, elegantly dressed Judith saws through Holofernes's neck with a sword; an old servant (Abra) watches behind her.
recogHolofernes's face is contorted, mouth open; stark white bedsheet against black background.
powerJudith's face is identified as Caravaggio's mistress Fillide Melandroni; the work is often compared to Artemisia Gentileschi's later, more violent version of the same scene.

David with the Head of Goliath
c. 1610 · oil on canvas · Galleria Borghese, Rome
recogA melancholy young David holds the severed head of Goliath at arm's length; the head's face is Caravaggio's own self-portrait.
recogPainted near the end of his life while in exile; sword inscribed with "H-AS OS" (Humilitas occidit superbiam — humility kills pride).
powerThought to have been sent to Cardinal Scipione Borghese as a plea for a papal pardon; the painting encodes Caravaggio's guilt — he is simultaneously hero and villain, executioner and executed.
wiki ↗
The Supper at Emmaus
1601 · oil on canvas · National Gallery, London
recogThe resurrected Christ blesses bread at a tavern table; two apostles recoil in shock, arms flung wide in extreme foreshortening.
recogA basket of fruit teeters at the edge of the table; an innkeeper stands obliviously to one side.
powerChrist is depicted as a beardless young man (atypical); the basket of fruit at the picture-plane edge is a signature trompe-l'oeil device. Patron: Ciriaco Mattei. A second, darker version (1606) hangs in Milan's Pinacoteca di Brera.
wiki ↗
The Entombment of Christ
1603–1604 · oil on canvas · Pinacoteca Vaticana, Vatican
recogChrist's pale, extremely foreshortened body is lowered into a tomb slab by Nicodemus and John; the Virgin, Mary Magdalene, and another Mary mourn behind.
recogNicodemus stares directly out at the viewer; the stone slab extends into viewer space.
powerCommissioned for the Chiesa Nuova (Santa Maria in Vallicella), Rome; Rubens made an oil-sketch copy. Considered one of Caravaggio's most classically composed works — unusual diagonal descends from raised arm to stone edge.
wiki ↗
The Death of the Virgin
1601–1606 · oil on canvas · Musée du Louvre, Paris
recogThe Virgin Mary lies dead on a cot, feet bare and bloated, surrounded by grieving apostles and a weeping Mary Magdalene.
recogRejected by the Carmelite friars who commissioned it; large red curtain draped across the top of the canvas.
powerRejected because the Virgin looked like a drowned prostitute — reportedly modeled on a sex worker pulled from the Tiber. Bought by the Gonzaga (then Louis XIV) after Peter Paul Rubens urged its purchase.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual / technique motifs

  • Tenebrism — figures lit from near-total black background
  • Chiaroscuro — extreme light/shadow without mid-tones
  • Dirty / bare feet on sacred figures (naturalism)
  • Strong diagonal light raking across the scene
  • Foreshortened bodies thrusting into viewer's space
  • Fruit baskets at picture-plane edge (trompe-l'oeil)

Biographical hard clues

  • Born Milan (or Caravaggio), 1571
  • Trained under Simone Peterzano in Milan
  • Early patron: Cardinal Francesco Maria Del Monte
  • Killed Ranuccio Tomassoni in a brawl (1606); fled Rome
  • Received the Order of Malta (Knight of Malta, 1608)
  • Died en route back to Rome, Porto Ercole, 1610, age 38

Followers & influence

  • Caravaggisti — broad movement of followers across Europe
  • Artemisia Gentileschi — most celebrated female Caravaggist
  • Gerrit van Honthorst — Dutch candlelight specialist
  • Jusepe de Ribera — Spanish Caravaggist in Naples
  • Georges de La Tour — French candlelit nocturnes
  • Influenced Rembrandt and Baroque art across Europe

Key locations & commissions

  • Contarelli Chapel (San Luigi dei Francesi) — Matthew triptych
  • Cerasi Chapel (Santa Maria del Popolo) — Paul + Peter pair
  • Galleria Borghese — David with Goliath, Boy with Fruit, Bacchus
  • Valletta, Malta — Beheading of St. John, his largest work
  • Naples — Seven Works of Mercy; late exile works

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Artemisia GentileschiFemale Caravaggist; also painted Judith Beheading Holofernes (her 1614–1620 version is more brutal, Judith's arms strain). Gentileschi's Judith has sleeves rolled up; Caravaggio's Judith is dainty, dressed. Museum ID: Caravaggio's is in Rome (GNAA), Gentileschi's in Uffizi.
Peter Paul RubensAlso Baroque, also dramatic, but lush color, fleshy nudes, mythological grandeur. Rubens uses full ambient light; Caravaggio uses brutal darkness. Rubens copies Caravaggio (Death of the Virgin) but doesn't imitate his blackness.
RembrandtDutch, later (1606–1669); also chiaroscuro but warmer amber tones, psychological interiority, portraits and etchings. Caravaggio is all oil-paint violence; Rembrandt is psychological portraiture.
Georges de La TourFrench Caravaggist known for candlelit nocturnes (Penitent Magdalene, St. Joseph the Carpenter). La Tour's light source is visible (a candle); Caravaggio's light source is off-canvas.
Michelangelo (sculptor)Both named Michelangelo; the painter's full name is Michelangelo Merisi da Caravaggio. Questions will say "this Milanese-born Baroque painter" — not "this Renaissance sculptor."
Annibale CarracciContemporary Roman Baroque rival; founded the Bolognese school; idealized bodies, classical compositions, ceiling frescoes (Farnese Gallery). Carracci = idealism; Caravaggio = brutal naturalism.

Cross-discipline big picture Caravaggio's tenebrism is the art answer when questions describe chiaroscuro, dirty feet on saints, or extreme darkness. His Caravaggisti movement spreads the technique to Artemisia Gentileschi (art — women's history angle), Rembrandt (Dutch Golden Age), and Georges de La Tour (French art). The Calling of Saint Matthew and Entombment are also frequent museum-building clues (San Luigi dei Francesi / Pinacoteca Vaticana). His violent biography — manslaughter, exile, Malta, death on the road — opens biographical tossups about Baroque Italy and connects to Renaissance-to-Baroque transition questions.
