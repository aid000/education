Quick Overview

Full nameJohann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750)
NationalityGerman; born Eisenach, Thuringia
PeriodBaroque (late Baroque; active ~1700–1750)
PositionsOrganist at Weimar; Kapellmeister at Köthen; Cantor at St. Thomas, Leipzig (1723–1750)
Family~20 children; sons C.P.E. Bach and J.C. Bach both became composers
RevivalMendelssohn revived the St. Matthew Passion in 1829, sparking the "Bach Revival"

Defining traits

  • Contrapuntal mastery — fugue, canon, invertible counterpoint; the supreme practitioner of Baroque polyphony.
  • Encyclopedic output: sacred cantatas, passions, oratorios, keyboard works, orchestral suites, chamber music — wrote in virtually every genre except opera.
  • Lutheran faith permeates his sacred works; manuscripts often signed S.D.G. (Soli Deo Gloria — "to God alone be glory").
  • Numbers/numerology: scholars find symbolic number sequences embedded in his scores (B-A-C-H = 14 in German letter-numbering).

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "This Leipzig cantor" / "this Baroque composer" are near-universal lead-ins.
  • The Art of Fugue, Well-Tempered Clavier, and St. Matthew Passion are the three highest-yield tossup answers.
  • Mendelssohn's 1829 revival of the St. Matthew Passion is itself a frequent power clue.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be a tossup answer. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

The Well-Tempered Clavier (Das Wohltemperierte Klavier)
Book I: 1722; Book II: 1742 · Keyboard (harpsichord/clavichord)
recog48 preludes and fugues — one in every major and minor key (24 + 24), in two books.
recogIntended to demonstrate the viability of equal (well) temperament — all 24 keys playable without retuning.
powerThe title page of Book I is dated 1722 at Köthen. WTC = standard abbreviation; a foundational text for every keyboard player since its publication — "the Old Testament of piano music."
wiki ↗
Mass in B Minor (Messe h-Moll)
Compiled 1748–49 (movements composed over decades) · Vocal/orchestral
recogA complete Catholic mass setting by a Lutheran composer — the full Ordinary (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei).
recogConsidered his supreme achievement in vocal music; never performed in its entirety during his lifetime.
powerBach assembled it partly by revising and adapting earlier cantata movements (parody technique). The Sanctus section dates to 1724. Added to UNESCO Memory of the World in 2015.
wiki ↗
St. Matthew Passion (Matthäus-Passion)
BWV 244 · Premiered Good Friday 1727, Leipzig · Vocal/orchestral
recogSetting of the Passion narrative from the Gospel of Matthew, chapters 26–27, with libretti by Picander (Christian Friedrich Henrici).
recogWritten for double chorus and double orchestra — two separate choirs and orchestras in dialogue.
powerRevived by Felix Mendelssohn on 11 March 1829 — the first performance since Bach's death, credited with launching the Bach Revival. Opening chorus: "Kommt, ihr Töchter, helft mir klagen."
wiki ↗
The Art of Fugue (Die Kunst der Fuge)
BWV 1080 · c. 1740s; published 1751 (posthumously) · Keyboard/ensemble
recogA collection of 14 contrapuncti (fugues) and 4 canons, all derived from a single subject in D minor.
recogThe final Contrapunctus XIV breaks off mid-phrase — traditionally believed Bach stopped when he introduced the B-A-C-H motif.
powerPublished the year after Bach's death; instrumentation is unspecified. The incomplete final fugue has fascinated theorists — Douglas Hofstadter devoted much of Gödel, Escher, Bach to its recursive structure.
wiki ↗
Brandenburg Concertos (Brandenburgische Konzerte)
BWV 1046–1051 · Dedicated 1721 (composed earlier) · Orchestral
recogSix concertos dedicated to Margrave Christian Ludwig of Brandenburg — Bach's most famous orchestral works.
recogEach uses a different combination of solo instruments; No. 2 features the high trumpet; No. 5 gives a long solo to the harpsichord (an early harpsichord concerto).
powerWritten at Köthen, where Bach had no church duties and could focus on secular instrumental music. The Margrave apparently never had them performed — the set was largely ignored until the 19th-century revival.
wiki ↗
Goldberg Variations (Goldberg-Variationen)
BWV 988 · Published 1741 · Keyboard (harpsichord)
recogAria followed by 30 variations, then the aria repeated — the complete work called Clavier-Übung IV.
recogNamed for harpsichordist Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, reputedly commissioned by Count Kaiserling to help him sleep.
powerEvery third variation is a canon (at intervals of a unison through a ninth); Var. 25 in G minor is the single most emotionally intense moment. Glenn Gould's 1955 debut recording made this piece a 20th-century icon.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Positions & places

  • Eisenach — birthplace (1685)
  • Lübeck — walked ~400 km to hear Buxtehude on the organ (c. 1705)
  • Weimar — court organist & concertmaster (1708–17); imprisoned briefly for quitting
  • Köthen — Kapellmeister (1717–23); wrote most instrumental music here
  • Leipzig / St. Thomas Church — Cantor from 1723 until death; wrote ~300 cantatas here

Compositional techniques

  • Fugue — subject, answer, countersubject, stretto, augmentation, inversion
  • Canon — strict imitation at a fixed interval/time delay
  • Counterpoint / invertible counterpoint
  • Chorale prelude — organ setting of a Lutheran hymn melody
  • Parody technique — reusing & reworking earlier movements in new contexts
  • Basso continuo — Baroque figured-bass foundation

Key terms & symbols

  • S.D.G. — Soli Deo Gloria (Bach's manuscript signature)
  • BWV — Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis (catalogue number system by Schmieder, 1950)
  • B-A-C-H motif — B♭–A–C–B♮ (German notation)
  • Clavier-Übung — four-volume keyboard publication (1731–42)
  • Picander — librettist for St. Matthew Passion and many cantatas

Legacy & connections

  • Mendelssohn's 1829 revival — St. Matthew Passion; launched "Bach Revival"
  • Glenn Gould — 1955 Goldberg Variations recording made Bach a recording-era icon
  • Gödel, Escher, Bach — Hofstadter's 1979 Pulitzer-winning book on Bach's recursive structures
  • Nikolaus Harnoncourt / John Eliot Gardiner — led complete cantata recordings
  • Voyager Golden Record — included Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 2

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
George Frideric HandelAlso German-Baroque, but wrote opera (Rinaldo, Giulio Cesare) and English oratorios (Messiah, Israel in Egypt). Bach = no operas, Lutheran, contrapuntal; Handel = operatic, English court, more harmonic. Both born 1685 — "these twin titans of Baroque."
Dietrich BuxtehudeDanish-German organist Bach admired and visited; associated with Lübeck and the Abendmusik concerts. Earlier generation, not the Leipzig cantor.
C.P.E. BachSon of J.S. Bach; key figure of the Empfindsamer Stil / pre-Classical style. "Carl Philipp Emanuel" = the "Berlin Bach" or "Hamburg Bach," not the Leipzig cantor.
Heinrich SchützEarlier German Baroque (1585–1672); wrote German sacred music before Bach. Tossup clue: "Saul, Saul, was verfolgst du mich" or Symphoniae Sacrae.
Johann PachelbelGerman Baroque organist; answer is almost always his Canon in D. Not a cantor; not the fugue master.
Antonio VivaldiItalian Baroque; concerto grosso, The Four Seasons. Bach transcribed Vivaldi's concertos for keyboard — Vivaldi was an influence on Bach's orchestral style, but the two are distinct answers.

Math / cross-discipline link Bach's counterpoint is inherently mathematical: fugues encode transformations (inversion, retrograde, augmentation, stretto) that parallel group-theory operations. The Art of Fugue's unfinished Contrapunctus XIV inspired Douglas Hofstadter's Gödel, Escher, Bach (1979 Pulitzer Prize — General Non-Fiction), connecting self-referential loops in music, visual art, and mathematical logic. Bach's music also appeared on the Voyager Golden Record (1977) as humanity's mathematical/aesthetic representative to the cosmos.
