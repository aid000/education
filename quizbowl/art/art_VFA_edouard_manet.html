Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Manet died in 1883, so his works are in the public domain. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection. If an image fails, the filename likely differs slightly — swap ?width=560 versions on Wikimedia Commons.

NationalityFrench (born Paris, 1832–1883)
PeriodMid-to-late 19th century; active c. 1858–1882
MovementBridge from Realism to Impressionism; often called the "father of modern art"
Key techniqueFlat, broad brushwork; minimal tonal modeling; strong contour lines; "unfinished" surfaces
Salon contextScandalized Paris — two works rejected or mocked at the Salon des Refusés (1863) and the official Salon
Muse / modelVictorine Meurent — his primary female model (Olympia, Luncheon)

Defining traits

  • Confrontational gaze: nude or déclassé figures stare directly at the viewer — breaking academic convention.
  • Flat light and compressed space: eliminates dramatic shadow and depth typical of Academic painting; influenced by Japanese woodblock prints (japonisme).
  • Old-master quotations: re-stages Raphael, Titian, Velázquez, and Goya in modern Paris — a recurring clue frame.
  • Urban Realism: café scenes, train stations, bars — Parisian modernity as subject.
  • Influenced the Impressionists (especially Monet, Berthe Morisot) but himself rarely painted en plein air.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • A nude woman staring boldly out at the viewer in a contemporary setting — instantly Manet (Olympia or Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe).
  • Scandal at the Salon / Salon des Refusés in 1863.
  • Black cat at the foot of the bed (Olympia).
  • Friend and champion: the poet/critic Charles Baudelaire; defended by Émile Zola.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe (Luncheon on the Grass)
1863 · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogA nude woman sits on the grass beside two fully-clothed men in modern dress; a second, lightly-clad woman bathes in the background stream.
recogRejected from the Salon of 1863; shown at the Salon des Refusés — shocked Paris with its mixture of nudity and bourgeois contemporary clothing.
powerComposition quotes Raphael's Judgment of Paris (via a Marcantonio Raimondi engraving) and Giorgione/Titian's Fête champêtre. The model for the nude woman is Victorine Meurent; the men include Manet's brother Gustave and future brother-in-law Ferdinand Leenhoff.

Olympia
1863 (exhibited 1865) · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogA nude white woman reclines on a bed staring boldly at the viewer; a Black maid presents a bouquet of flowers; a black cat at the foot of the bed arches its back.
recogDeliberately quotes Titian's Venus of Urbino — same pose, but the context is clearly a Paris courtesan, not a goddess.
powerThe name "Olympia" was a common pseudonym for Parisian prostitutes. The maid is thought to be modeled on Laure. Defended in a famous 1866 essay by Émile Zola. Now considered the foundational painting of modernism's confrontational female nude.
wiki ↗
A Bar at the Folies-Bergère (Un bar aux Folies-Bergère)
1882 · oil on canvas · Courtauld Gallery, London
recogA barmaid faces the viewer behind a marble counter laden with bottles; behind her is a large mirror reflecting the crowded café — her reflection appears to speak to a top-hatted man at an impossible angle.
recogThe reflection is spatially inconsistent — the barmaid's mirror-image is shifted to the right, a deliberate perspectival paradox.
powerThe model for the barmaid is Suzon, an actual Folies-Bergère employee. The impossible mirror logic has generated decades of debate — some read it as two different moments. Manet's last major exhibited work; his health was failing (ataxia/locomotor ataxy).
wiki ↗
The Execution of Emperor Maximilian
1868–69 · oil on canvas · Kunsthalle Mannheim (main version)
recogA firing squad of Mexican Republican soldiers shoots Emperor Maximilian of Mexico and two of his generals at close range.
recogThe Mexican soldiers wear uniforms that closely resemble French army uniforms — a political indictment of Napoleon III for abandoning Maximilian.
powerThere are four versions (three paintings, one lithograph). Directly quotes Goya's The Third of May 1808 in composition. Censored by the French government — the lithograph was banned. The most complete version is in Mannheim; fragments of another version are in the National Gallery, London.
wiki ↗
The Fifer (Le Fifre)
1866 · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogA young French Imperial Guard soldier plays a fife against a near-featureless flat grey background, with virtually no shadow or ground plane.
recogRejected by the Salon of 1866. The flatness was mocked — critic Théophile Gautier called it "a playing card."
powerManet's debt to Velázquez (especially Pablo de Valladolid) is most explicit here — isolated figure against empty ground. Also shows japonisme influence: outlined forms, no depth cues, flat color fields.
wiki ↗
The Balcony (Le Balcon)
1868–69 · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogThree figures on a green-railed balcony: two women in white dresses (one seated, one standing) and a man behind them; they do not interact with one another.
recogThe seated woman is Berthe Morisot (in her first major appearance in Manet's work); the standing woman is the painter Fanny Claus.
powerQuotes Goya's Majas on a Balcony. The bright green iron railing is a compositional signature. The emotional disconnection among the three figures is deliberately modern — no narrative, no eye contact. René Magritte later painted Perspective II: Manet's Balcony replacing the figures with coffins.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual signatures

  • Flat brushwork — no tonal gradation, broad strokes
  • Bold contour lines separating figure from ground
  • Near-featureless backgrounds — subject floats
  • Confrontational, direct gaze of female subjects
  • Strong blacks as a positive color (not just shadow)
  • Green iron railings (The Balcony, Bar background)

Subjects & people

  • Victorine Meurent — primary model (Olympia, Déjeuner, Fifer)
  • Berthe Morisot — Impressionist painter; modeled for Manet; later married his brother Eugène
  • Émile Zola — defended Manet in the press (1866 essay)
  • Charles Baudelaire — poet friend; championed Manet as painter of "modern life"
  • Stéphane Mallarmé — Symbolist poet; close friend; Manet illustrated his works

Artistic sources & influences

  • Velázquez — flat backgrounds, isolated figures (The Fifer)
  • Goya — Balcony pose, Execution composition
  • Titian — Venus of Urbino → Olympia
  • Raphael (via engraving) → Déjeuner composition
  • Japonisme — flat color, outline, diagonal cropping
  • Trained under Thomas Couture (Academic), then rebelled

Context & trivia

  • Salon des Refusés, 1863 — key date
  • Born into upper-class Parisian family; father a judge
  • Refused the Legion of Honor for years; accepted it in 1882
  • Friend of the Impressionists but rejected pure plein air; worked largely in studio
  • Died of locomotor ataxia (syphilis complications), 1883, age 51
  • Do NOT confuse with Claude Monet — they were contemporaries and the names are often confused

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Claude MonetThe most common name-confusion in art history. Monet = Impressionist landscapes, haystacks, water lilies, Rouen Cathedral series — never figure subjects, never scandal. Manet = Paris figures, nudes, urban realism, studio-based. Monet's brushwork is dissolved and atmospheric; Manet's is flat and outlined.
Gustave CourbetBoth Realists, both scandal-makers: Courbet's Origin of the World and Stone Breakers. Courbet predates Manet, uses darker tonality (palette knife), shows rural/working-class subjects. Manet's subjects are bourgeois and urban; his palette is brighter and flatter.
Edgar DegasFellow Impressionist-era Parisian. Degas = ballet dancers, racehorses, café singers, asymmetrical cropping from photography. Degas rarely depicts outdoor scenes; his figures are caught in motion. Manet is more confrontational (figures look at viewer); Degas is more voyeuristic.
Berthe MorisotMorisot modeled for Manet and was deeply influenced by him, but her own work features domestic, intimate scenes (women and children in gardens, nurseries) painted with a looser, more atmospheric stroke. She is the answer when the clue says "woman Impressionist" or mentions the Berthe Morisot painting.
Francisco GoyaManet quotes Goya's compositions (Majas on a Balcony → The Balcony; Third of May → Execution of Maximilian). Goya is Spanish, 18th–19th century, and the source. If the clue describes the original, the answer is Goya; if it describes a modern Parisian reinterpretation, likely Manet.
James WhistlerBoth were radically "flat" painters who broke with Academic tradition simultaneously. Whistler = tonal harmonies ("Arrangements," "Nocturnes"), muted color, his mother. Manet = strong blacks and whites, Paris/Spanish references, scandalous nudes.

Big Picture: Manet and the Birth of Modernism Manet is the canonical bridge figure in the narrative of Western art: too modern for Academic approval, yet too studio-bound to be a pure Impressionist. His strategic quotation of Old Masters (Titian, Raphael, Goya, Velázquez) in explicitly contemporary Parisian contexts broke the unwritten rule that high painting required historical or mythological subjects. His flat brushwork and confrontational nudes forced a rupture that made Impressionism, Post-Impressionism, and eventually abstraction legible as art — which is why tossups on him often close with "this French painter who is considered the father of modern art."
