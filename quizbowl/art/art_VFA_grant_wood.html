Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Grant Wood died in 1942, so most works are in the public domain or widely reproduced. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection. If any image fails, the filename likely differs slightly — swap it as needed.

NationalityAmerican (born Anamosa, Iowa, 1891–1942)
PeriodEarly 20th century; active 1920s–1940s
MovementAmerican Regionalism (a.k.a. the "American Scene")
Home baseCedar Rapids, Iowa; championed Midwest rural life
Defining workAmerican Gothic (1930) — the most parodied American painting

Defining traits

  • Regionalism: elevated ordinary Iowa farm life, small-town Midwest, rolling hills — a conscious rejection of European modernism and urban abstraction.
  • Smoothly rounded, stylized forms: hills like green velvet corduroy, hair like corrugated iron, trees like perfect spheres — a decorative near-Art-Deco simplification.
  • Flemish influence: three study trips to Munich and Paris (1920–28) led him to admire 15th-century Flemish panel painters — the hard-edged clarity and minute detail of van Eyck and Memling.
  • Satirical undercurrent: beneath the apparent celebration of rural virtue, works like Daughters of Revolution and Parson Weems' Fable mock American myths and Puritan self-righteousness.
  • Recurring motifs: Iowa farmhouses, pitchforks, overalls, rounded hills, neat rows of crops, Gothic-windowed barns.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The pitchfork and the Gothic window in the red barn behind the couple in American Gothic.
  • "This Regionalist" or "this Iowa artist" are common lead-in clue frames.
  • The trio of American Regionalists: Wood + Thomas Hart Benton + John Steuart Curry.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 5 works most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

American Gothic
1930 · oil on beaver board · Art Institute of Chicago
recogA stern farmer holding a pitchfork stands beside a woman in front of a white house with a Gothic-arched window.
recogModels were Wood's sister Nan Wood Graham (the woman) and his dentist Dr. Byron McKeeby — not husband and wife as commonly assumed.
powerThe house that prompted the painting is the Dibble House in Eldon, Iowa; Wood was struck by the incongruity of the Carpenter Gothic window on a modest farmhouse. Submitted to and won bronze medal at the Art Institute of Chicago's 43rd Annual Exhibition (1930).
wiki ↗
Daughters of Revolution
1932 · oil on masonite · Cincinnati Art Museum
recogThree prim, self-satisfied elderly women holding teacups in front of Emanuel Leutze's Washington Crossing the Delaware.
recogA satirical attack on the Daughters of the American Revolution (DAR), who had blocked Wood's commission for a stained glass window because it was made in Germany.
powerThe teacup held by the central figure was identified as a Washington Commemorative teacup. The painting skewers nativist, jingoistic patriotism; it prompted angry responses from DAR chapters nationwide.
wiki ↗
Stone City, Iowa
1930 · oil on wood panel · Joslyn Art Museum, Omaha
recogRolling, rounded Iowa hills with neat rows of crops; small farms and wagons visible in a deep green valley.
recogNamed for the actual Stone City, Iowa; shows the distinctive corduroy-textured hills that define Wood's landscape style.
powerPainted the same year as American Gothic; launched Wood's signature landscape idiom. He later ran a Stone City Art Colony (summer 1932–33) in those same hills, bringing art to Depression-era Iowa.
wiki ↗
Parson Weems' Fable
1939 · oil on canvas · Amon Carter Museum, Fort Worth
recogParson Mason Weems pulls back a curtain to reveal young George Washington (depicted with the adult Gilbert Stuart face) holding a hatchet beside a felled cherry tree.
recogWashington's face is the famous Gilbert Stuart portrait face transplanted onto a child's body — a deliberate anachronism exposing the myth.
powerThe work mocks Mason Locke ("Parson") Weems, the hagiographic biographer who invented the cherry-tree story. Wood's title card within the painting quotes Weems's 1800 biography. Satirizes the manufacture of American national mythology.
wiki ↗
Young Corn
1931 · oil on masonite · Cedar Rapids Museum of Art
recogPerfectly rounded, billowing hills with alternating strips of young cornfields and green meadow, a lone tree, and a red farmhouse.
recogNo figures — a pure landscape showing Wood's idealized, almost toy-like Iowa countryside.
powerThe contoured, quilted-field pattern of the hills (viewed from above) is Wood's most imitated stylistic signature; the painting is a touchstone of American Regionalist landscape painting. Held at the Cedar Rapids Museum of Art — Wood's hometown institution.
wiki ↗
Midnight Ride of Paul Revere
1931 · oil on masonite · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
recogA bird's-eye aerial view of a toy-like New England village at night, with a tiny Paul Revere on horseback charging through.
recogWhite churches and houses glow under a full moon; the scale makes Revere comically small against the village — a deflating of the heroic myth.
powerUses the same mythological-deflation strategy as Parson Weems' Fable; the crisp, aerial perspective and nighttime lighting are distinctive. Wood said the bird's-eye view was inspired by illustrations in 19th-century children's books.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Rounded, corduroy-textured hills — Wood's unmistakable landscape signature
  • Pitchfork in portraiture (American Gothic)
  • Gothic-arched windows on farmhouses
  • Aerial / bird's-eye viewpoint of rolling countryside
  • Neat crop rows as patterned, decorative strips
  • Hard-edged, near-Art-Deco stylization of organic forms

Subjects & settings

  • Iowa farm life — fields, barns, farmhouses, harvesters
  • American historical myths (Washington, Revere, Revolution)
  • Satire of provincial smugness (DAR, Puritanism)
  • His sister Nan Wood Graham as model (American Gothic)
  • His mother Hattie Wood as model (Woman with Plants)
  • Home: Cedar Rapids, Iowa; taught at University of Iowa

Techniques & influences

  • Flemish panel painting clarity (van Eyck, Memling) — learned in Munich trips
  • Painstaking smooth, enamel-like surface
  • Painted on beaver board / masonite, not canvas
  • Regionalism as ideological counterweight to European modernism
  • Friend and ally of Thomas Hart Benton and John Steuart Curry

"Also did" trivia

  • Wrote the manifesto essay "Revolt Against the City" (1935) — advocating Regionalism over urban modernism
  • Ran the Stone City Art Colony (1932–33), Iowa's Depression-era art school
  • Designed stained glass windows for the Cedar Rapids Veterans Memorial Building
  • Created book illustrations for a 1939 edition of Sinclair Lewis's Main Street
  • Taught at the University of Iowa in Iowa City (died of pancreatic cancer, 1942)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Thomas Hart BentonBenton = muscular, writhing, sinuous figures in murals; his Regionalism depicts labor and industry with expressionist distortion. Wood = smooth, static, decorative stillness. Both Regionalists, but entirely different surfaces.
John Steuart CurryCurry's signature is dramatic weather and animals: a tornado, John Brown, baptism in a Kansas creek. Less satirical than Wood, more heroic-naturalist. Curry = Kansas; Wood = Iowa; Benton = Missouri.
Edward HopperHopper also painted American rural/small-town scenes, but his hallmark is urban loneliness, harsh light, deep shadow (Nighthawks, gas stations). No rolling hills; no satire of mythology. Hopper = alienation; Wood = Midwest nostalgia/satire.
Andrew WyethBoth paint rural America with extreme realism. Wyeth = muted, melancholic, tempera or dry-brush watercolor (Christina's World — barren Pennsylvania field). Wood = bright, rounded, patterned oil surfaces. Wyeth never used Wood's Flemish stylization.
Charles BurchfieldBurchfield painted American Gothic houses and small towns, but in a visionary, expressionist watercolor style — buzzing, agitated brushwork, haunted mood. Nothing like Wood's smooth enamel finish.
Norman RockwellBoth made iconic, widely-reproduced American rural imagery. Rockwell = narrative illustration, Saturday Evening Post, sentimental warmth. Wood = fine art painting, deadpan or satirical tone, Flemish technique, no storytelling sentimentality.

Cross-discipline big picture Grant Wood's Regionalism sits at the intersection of art history and American cultural history: his satires of the DAR and of Washington-myth link to U.S. history tossups (DAR, Mason Weems, Paul Revere, the Carpenter Gothic style). Parson Weems' Fable connects to American literature (Weems's 1800 biography of Washington as the source of fabricated mythology). Wood's Revolt Against the City maps onto the broader 1930s Regionalist vs. Modernist culture war, a recurring context clue in both art and American studies questions.
