Quick Overview

📷 Image note: O'Keeffe died in 1986; most works remain under copyright and load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect. An internet connection is required. If an image fails to load, the file title may differ slightly.

NationalityAmerican (born Sun Prairie, Wisconsin, 1887–1986)
PeriodEarly 20th century; active 1910s–1970s
MovementAmerican Modernism; associated with Precisionism and abstraction
Home baseNew Mexico (Ghost Ranch / Abiquiú) from 1929 on
Key relationshipWife of photographer/dealer Alfred Stieglitz; promoted at his 291 gallery
Epithet"Mother of American modernism"

Defining traits

  • Extreme close-up magnification of flowers — fills the canvas so detail becomes near-abstract.
  • New Mexico desert iconography: animal skulls (often with flowers), red/ochre rock formations, Pedernal mesa, adobe churches.
  • Precisionist lines and smooth gradients — no visible brushwork; hard edges defining organic forms.
  • Chromatic abstraction of the 1910s–20s: watercolor charcoal "Special" series and large charcoal abstract drawings exhibited by Stieglitz.
  • Consistently rejected sexual interpretations of her flower paintings.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Giant close-up flower filling the canvas — single most given-away clue for her work.
  • A bleached animal skull floating over the New Mexico desert.
  • "Mother of American modernism" or "longtime resident of Ghost Ranch."
  • Connection to Alfred Stieglitz and Gallery 291 (now spelled "291").

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Black Iris III
1926 · oil on canvas · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
recogA deep-purple/black iris in extreme close-up, petals curving inward — nearly abstract, nearly figurative.
recogHeld at the Metropolitan Museum of Art; one of her most-reproduced flower works.
powerO'Keeffe deliberately chose black (an unusual iris color) to avoid easy floral sentiment; she argued the close-up scale forces viewers to see what they otherwise overlook. One of her first large-format flower canvases.

Cow's Skull: Red, White, and Blue
1931 · oil on canvas · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
recogA bleached cow skull centered on a vertically striped background of red, white, and blue — American flag colors.
recogOne of O'Keeffe's deliberate responses to calls for a "great American painting" — fusing the Southwest desert with national symbolism.
powerO'Keeffe said she painted it partly as a joke on the men who told her to paint American subject matter; the skull series began after her first summer at Ghost Ranch, New Mexico (1929).

Jimson Weed / White Flower No. 1
1932 · oil on canvas · Crystal Bridges Museum, Bentonville, Arkansas
recogAn enormous white jimson weed bloom fills the entire canvas — luminous petals, dark green center.
recogSold in November 2014 for $44.4 million — the highest auction price ever for a work by a female artist at that time.
powerHoused at Crystal Bridges Museum (Bentonville, AR); the jimson weed is a poisonous Southwestern desert plant — O'Keeffe returned to it repeatedly as a New Mexico motif.

Radiator Building — Night, New York
1927 · oil on canvas · Fisk University, Nashville
recogThe American Radiator Building in Manhattan at night, illuminated orange against a blue-black sky — Precisionist vertical lines.
recogPart of her New York skyscraper series from the 1920s; the glowing building resembles a circuit board or Art Deco jewel.
powerO'Keeffe included a tiny glowing "Alfred Stieglitz" sign in red on the building facade — a personal tribute to her husband. Held at Fisk University (one of its Alfred Stieglitz collection gifts).

Black Cross, New Mexico
1929 · oil on canvas · Art Institute of Chicago
recogA large black wooden Penitente cross fills the foreground, framing a glowing red-and-purple New Mexico landscape at dusk.
recogHeld at the Art Institute of Chicago; the cross references the Penitente Brotherhood (Hispanic Catholic tradition of northern New Mexico).
powerPainted during her very first summer alone in New Mexico; the monumental cross nearly touches the canvas edges, creating a cropped, close-up effect unusual for landscape. Marks the beginning of her Southwest phase.

Red Canna
c. 1924 · oil on canvas · multiple versions; University of Arizona Museum of Art
recogA canna lily in blazing reds and oranges, petals curling outward — one of her most sensuous and most-recognized flower paintings.
recogMultiple versions exist (1919–1924); the deep red fills the canvas with no visible background — pure flower-form abstraction.
powerThe canna series predates O'Keeffe's New Mexico period; she painted canna lilies in Lake George, New York where Stieglitz's family had a summer home. One version is at the University of Arizona Museum of Art.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Giant close-up flowers (iris, canna, jimson weed, poppy, jack-in-the-pulpit)
  • Bleached animal skulls floating over desert — cow, horse, deer, ram, pelvis
  • Pedernal mesa — flat-topped blue mountain near Ghost Ranch
  • New Mexico red/ochre cliffs and hills (Red Hills series)
  • Smooth, hard-edged Precisionist forms — no visible brushstroke
  • Sky / clouds seen from aerial perspective (late career)

Places & biography

  • Born Sun Prairie, Wisconsin, 1887
  • Early career: taught art in Canyon, Texas (West Texas State Normal)
  • Lake George, New York — Stieglitz summer home; early landscape paintings
  • Ghost Ranch and Abiquiú, New Mexico — permanent home after Stieglitz died 1946
  • Died Santa Fe, 1986 — age 98
  • Georgia O'Keeffe Museum — Santa Fe (opened 1997)

Relationships & institutions

  • Alfred Stieglitz — photographer, dealer, husband (married 1924)
  • Gallery 291 (later "An American Place") — first exhibited her work 1916
  • Arthur Wesley Dow — influential teacher at Columbia; taught her design principles
  • Fisk University — received part of Stieglitz collection (incl. Radiator Building)
  • Associated with Alfred Stieglitz circle: Paul Strand, Edward Steichen, Marsden Hartley

Techniques & terms

  • Precisionism — clean lines, industrial/geometric forms, American scenes
  • Scale inversion — tiny subject (flower, skull) blown up to monumental size
  • "Special" charcoal series (1915–16) — pure abstract forms; exhibited by Stieglitz without her permission initially
  • Influenced by Arthur Dow's Japonist design principles (flat space, negative space)
  • Late period: aerial-view landscapes — rivers, roads, clouds seen from planes

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Imogen CunninghamPhotographer, not a painter — also made close-up botanical images but in black-and-white photography. O'Keeffe = oil paint, large canvases.
Frida KahloAnother female modernist, but Mexican, Surrealist-adjacent, self-portraits — highly personal, symbolic, figurative. O'Keeffe = American, Precisionist/abstract, rarely figurative or narrative.
Charles DemuthFellow Precisionist (grain elevators, factories, "I Saw the Figure 5 in Gold") — also in the Stieglitz circle. Demuth = industrial architecture; O'Keeffe = flowers, skulls, New Mexico landscapes.
Marsden HartleyStieglitz circle colleague; expressionist, used German military symbols, later Maine landscapes. Darker palette, rougher texture than O'Keeffe's smooth surfaces.
Arthur DoveAlso an American abstract modernist in the Stieglitz circle — made biomorphic abstractions. Dove's shapes are more ambiguously organic; O'Keeffe's are identifiably floral or skeletal even when magnified.
Edward HopperSame era, American, but Realist — gas stations, diners, isolated figures. No flowers, no skulls, no abstraction. Hopper = urban/suburban isolation; O'Keeffe = Southwest desert and extreme close-ups.
