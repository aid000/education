Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Degas died in 1917; most works are in the public domain. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection. If any image fails, the file title may differ slightly on Wikimedia Commons.

NationalityFrench; born Paris, 1834–1917
Period / MovementImpressionism (though he preferred "Realist" and avoided plain-air)
Primary subjectsBallet dancers, horse races, café scenes, bathers, laundresses
Signature methodCropped, off-center compositions; pastel as a primary medium
InfluencesIngres (line), Japanese woodblock prints (flat planes, cropping), photography
Key friend / rivalÉdouard Manet — close colleague; both rejected "Impressionist" label

Defining traits

  • Obsessive study of movement — trained at the École des Beaux-Arts, drew from Old Masters; applied that to modern subjects.
  • Unusual vantage points: views from the wings, from above, from below the stage; dancers cut off by the frame edge.
  • Pastel over monotype — his signature late technique for luminous color in dancer and bather series.
  • Wax/bronze sculpture — made ~74 sculptures discovered after his death; the only one cast in his lifetime was Little Dancer Aged Fourteen.
  • Never painted outdoors — opposed to the plain-air method; used artificial light and studio reconstruction.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Ballet dancers (especially tutus and the Paris Opéra stage) — the near-universal giveaway for Degas.
  • Jockeys and horse races at Longchamp or Epsom — second-most-common subject cluster.
  • Little Dancer — the bronze/wax ballerina in a real tutu and slippers.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Dance Class (La classe de danse)
1874 · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogDancers rehearse in a large studio while ballet master Jules Perrot watches with a cane; a dog and a watering can are visible.
recogGirls in white tutus crowd the room; some scratch their backs or adjust ribbons — unglamorous backstage reality.
powerDegas painted two versions: the 1874 Orsay canvas shows Perrot; a slightly earlier 1873–76 version is at the Metropolitan Museum. The figure of Perrot was reportedly added from a separate study after the old master's death.

Little Dancer Aged Fourteen (La Petite Danseuse de Quatorze Ans)
1881 (wax original) · bronze casts · many museums worldwide
recogThe only sculpture Degas exhibited in his lifetime; the wax original wore a real muslin tutu, linen bodice, silk ribbon, and horsehair wig.
recogModel was Marie van Goethem, a student at the Paris Opéra Ballet school; she stands in fourth position, chin raised.
powerCritics found it shocking at the 1881 Impressionist exhibition — called it "bestial," "degenerate," more sculpture than painting. Degas kept the wax in his studio until death; the 28+ bronze posthumous casts (1921) are at major museums.

L'Absinthe (In a Café)
1875–76 · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogA woman in a white négligée and a man in a top hat sit side by side at a café table — both staring vacantly, no interaction.
recogA glass of pale green absinthe sits before the woman; the diagonal of marble tables recedes sharply into the background.
powerModels were actress Ellen Andrée and artist Marcellin Desboutin. When exhibited in London (1893) it caused a scandal for depicting degraded Parisian bohemian life. The sharp perspective recession is influenced by Japanese print composition.

The Ballet Class (L'École de danse)
1871–74 · oil on canvas · Philadelphia Museum of Art
recogDancers warm up and stretch in a high-ceilinged studio; a large mirror reflects the room and multiplies the figures.
recogSeveral girls practice unsupervised — slouching, adjusting earrings — capturing the boredom between rehearsals.
powerCommissioned by Louisine Havemeyer (later Degas's key American patron); the Philadelphia version is one of at least four related "classroom" compositions. Note distinction from the Orsay Dance Class featuring Perrot.
wiki ↗
Blue Dancers (Danseuses bleues)
c. 1897 · pastel · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogFour dancers in vivid blue tutus, cropped at the waist; heads bent as they adjust their straps or hair — no stage, no audience.
recogBold, flat blocks of color; late pastel technique — hatching and layering over monotype.
powerA key example of his failing-eyesight era: as his vision deteriorated (retinal disease), Degas shifted to larger, bolder pastels with looser, more abstract mark-making — the figures barely individualized, color over form.
wiki ↗
The Bellelli Family (La Famille Bellelli)
c. 1858–67 · oil on canvas · Musée d'Orsay, Paris
recogA large formal family portrait: Degas's aunt Laura Bellelli stands upright in black; her husband Gennaro sits with his back to the viewer; two daughters between them.
recogThe composition is split — wife and daughters form one group, husband another — conveying marital tension and emotional distance.
powerPainted during a stay in Florence after his grandfather's death; Degas worked on it for nearly a decade before sending it to the Salon of 1867. The portrait of his late grandfather Hilaire De Gas hangs on the wall behind Laura, a mourning reference.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Ballet tutus and barre — overwhelming primary signature
  • Cropped, off-center framing — figures cut by edges, Japanese-style
  • Overhead / oblique vantage — stage boxes, backstage wings
  • Jockeys on horses before race; Longchamp / Epsom backgrounds
  • Women bathing / drying — seen from above, "keyhole" intimacy
  • Café interiors — isolation, modern Parisian alienation

Technique markers

  • Pastel over monotype — signature late medium
  • No plain-air painting — always studio or artificial light
  • Study of Japanese woodblock prints (Hiroshige, Utamaro) for flat planes and cropping
  • Photography influence — Eadweard Muybridge's motion studies
  • Trained under Ingres disciple; admired precise contour line

Key people & places

  • Paris Opéra (Palais Garnier) — primary setting for ballet works
  • Louisine Havemeyer — American patron; championed him in the US
  • Mary Cassatt — close friend; introduced Havemeyer to Degas
  • Édouard Manet — close friend, fellow realist, occasional rival
  • Marie van Goethem — model for Little Dancer
  • New Orleans, 1872–73 — visited American relatives; painted the Cotton Office

Periods & biographical clues

  • Early: history/portraiture (classical training, Salon ambition)
  • 1860s–70s: modern life subjects — races, cafés, dancers begin
  • 1880s–90s: bather series + pastel dominates
  • Late: failing eyesight → bold, abstract color; wax sculpture
  • Never married; reclusive in later life; died Paris 1917
  • Hated the "Impressionist" label; called himself a Realist

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Mary CassattClose friend, fellow Impressionist — but Cassatt's subjects are mothers and children, domestic interiors, never ballet or horses. Cassatt was American; Degas French.
Auguste RenoirBoth paint dancers and social scenes, but Renoir's style is warm, dappled, openly pleasurable (Moulin de la Galette). Degas's palette is cooler, compositions more angular, mood more detached.
Toulouse-LautrecAlso paints Montmartre cabaret, dancers, and cafés, but Toulouse-Lautrec = flat poster-like color, Moulin Rouge specifically, and was a generation younger. Degas's ballet vs. Lautrec's can-can.
Édouard ManetVery close friends — both reject "Impressionist." But Manet's signature works are large, provocative figure paintings (Olympia, Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe). Manet rarely painted dancers.
Georges SeuratSeurat also paints leisure scenes and dancers (La Grande Jatte, The Circus), but his method is pointillism / divisionism — tiny dots, no pastel, very different texture. Seurat = systematic; Degas = gestural.
Berthe MorisotFemale Impressionist — specializes in domestic interiors, gardens, women with children. No ballet, no café alienation, no sculpture.

🔗 Cross-discipline big picture Degas's obsessive study of movement put him at the intersection of art, science, and modernity: his ballet and horse-race works directly parallel Eadweard Muybridge's chronophotography (both were capturing motion circa 1878–80); his use of unusual camera-like cropping anticipates 20th-century photography. The Little Dancer scandal connects to broader debates about class, criminology, and the "dangerous" bodies of working-class girls in Third Republic France (Lombroso's physiognomy was being cited by critics). His New Orleans period links Impressionism to American Gilded Age commerce — the cotton office was one of the first major Impressionist paintings of a modern business interior.
