Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Gaudí died in 1926; his buildings are public landmarks and photographs are served via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect. Images require an internet connection. If any image fails to load, the file title may differ slightly.

BornAntoni Gaudí i Cornet; Reus (or Riudoms), Catalonia, 1852–1926
NationalityCatalan (Spanish); worked almost entirely in Barcelona
MovementCatalan Modernisme (the Catalan Art Nouveau); deeply personal organic style
DiedStruck by a tram in Barcelona; died June 10, 1926; buried in the Sagrada Família crypt
Chief patronIndustrialist Eusebi Güell (Park Güell, Palau Güell, Colònia Güell)
UNESCOSeven "Works of Antoni Gaudí" are World Heritage Sites

Signature structural & decorative innovations

  • Catenary arches — pointed parabolic arches found by hanging weighted chains (his upside-down hanging chain models computed pure-compression form, eliminating flying buttresses)
  • Trencadís — mosaic of broken ceramic/tile shards (e.g., the serpentine Park Güell bench)
  • Hyperboloids, helicoids & ruled surfaces — geometry drawn from nature; warped columns and vaults
  • Biomorphic / organic forms — bone-like columns, scale-like roofs, undulating "wave" facades
  • Polychrome ceramics, wrought iron, and tilted "tree" columns

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • An unfinished basilica in Barcelona still under construction → Sagrada Família.
  • A facade of bone-like balconies and a scaly, dragon-spine roof → Casa Batlló.
  • A serpentine trencadís bench and a mosaic lizard/salamander ("el drac") → Park Güell.
  • A wavy stone apartment block nicknamed "La Pedrera" (the quarry) → Casa Milà.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Basílica de la Sagrada Família
1882–present · Barcelona · (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
recogVast unfinished basilica bristling with tapering openwork spires; planned 18 towers (12 apostles, 4 evangelists, Mary, and the tallest for Jesus Christ).
recogThree grand facades: the lush Nativity facade (built in Gaudí's lifetime), the austere Passion facade, and the Glory facade. Interior columns branch like a stone forest.
powerGaudí took over from Francisco de Paula del Villar in 1883 and spent his last years (living on-site) on it; only the crypt, apse, and Nativity facade were done at his death. Funded as an "expiatory temple" by alms. Consecrated by Pope Benedict XVI in 2010; long targeted for completion around 2026, the centenary of Gaudí's death.

Casa Batlló
1904–1906 · Passeig de Gràcia, Barcelona · (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
recogFacade of bone-like columns and skull-shaped balconies (nicknamed "Casa dels ossos," the House of Bones); shimmering trencadís of glass and ceramic; a scaly, arched dragon-spine roof.
recogRead as the legend of Saint George (Sant Jordi) slaying the dragon — the cross-topped turret is the lance piercing the dragon's back; the balconies are skulls of victims.
powerA remodel (not new build) of an existing house for textile magnate Josep Batlló. Sits on the "Illa de la Discòrdia" ("Block of Discord") on Passeig de Gràcia, alongside buildings by rival Modernistes Domènech i Montaner (Casa Lleó Morera) and Puig i Cadafalch (Casa Amatller).
wiki ↗
Casa Milà ("La Pedrera")
1906–1912 · Passeig de Gràcia, Barcelona · (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
recogUndulating self-supporting limestone facade like a cliff or wave; nicknamed "La Pedrera" ("the quarry") for its rough stone; twisting wrought-iron balconies by Josep Maria Jujol.
recogThe rooftop is famous for its surreal, helmeted warrior-like chimneys and ventilation towers (the "espanta-bruixes," witch-scarers), some clad in trencadís.
powerGaudí's last secular/civil work before he devoted himself to the Sagrada Família. Built for Pere Milà and Roser Segimon; has no load-bearing walls (a self-supporting stone facade hung on the frame) and a free, open floor plan. The attic's catenary-arch ribs suggest a ribcage.
wiki ↗
Park Güell
1900–1914 · Carmel Hill, Barcelona · (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
recogA mosaic salamander/dragon ("el drac") guards the entrance stairway; above, the Hypostyle Hall of tilted Doric columns supports a plaza ringed by a serpentine trencadís bench.
recogGingerbread-like gatehouse pavilions; leaning stone colonnaded viaducts built of unworked local stone that seem to grow from the hillside.
powerCommissioned by Eusebi Güell as an English-style garden-city housing estate (hence the English spelling "Park"); it failed commercially — only two houses sold — and became a municipal park in 1926. The undulating bench was largely executed by collaborator Josep Maria Jujol.
wiki ↗
Casa Vicens
1883–1885 · Gràcia, Barcelona · (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
recogGaudí's first major commission (a private house); riot of green-and-white checkerboard ceramic tiles with marigold flower motifs and exposed brick.
recogStrong Orientalist / Moorish (Mudéjar) flavor — turret-like corners and tiled patterning — built for tile/brick manufacturer Manuel Vicens.
powerAn early work (pre-dating his mature organic style) showing Neo-Mudéjar / Moorish Revival influence; named a UNESCO World Heritage Site and opened to the public as a museum in 2017.
wiki ↗
Palau Güell
1886–1888 · Carrer Nou de la Rambla, Barcelona · (UNESCO World Heritage Site)
recogA dark, opulent town mansion with twin parabolic-arch entrance gates and an iron gate bearing the Catalan coat of arms; a soaring central parabolic-domed hall pierced for starlight.
recogThe rooftop sprouts 20 fantastical trencadís-clad chimneys and a central spire — an early run-up to Casa Milà's roof.
powerBuilt as the city residence of his patron Eusebi Güell, just off La Rambla; one of Gaudí's earliest works and among the first Gaudí sites named a UNESCO World Heritage Site (1984).

Recognition / Clue Bank

Structural & decorative motifs

  • Catenary / parabolic arches (hanging-chain models)
  • Trencadís — broken-tile/ceramic mosaic
  • Hyperboloids, helicoids, ruled surfaces
  • Tilted/branching "tree" or bone columns
  • Undulating "wave" facades & wrought iron
  • Sculptural rooftop chimneys & vent towers

Buildings → quick ID

  • Sagrada Família — unfinished spired basilica
  • Casa Batlló — bones + dragon-spine roof
  • Casa Milà / La Pedrera — wavy stone "quarry"
  • Park Güell — mosaic lizard + serpentine bench
  • Casa Vicens — first house; Moorish tilework
  • Palau Güell — parabolic-domed mansion, roof chimneys

People & context

  • Eusebi Güell — chief patron (Park, Palau, Colònia Güell)
  • Josep Maria Jujol — collaborator (Park Güell bench, Milà ironwork)
  • Domènech i Montaner & Puig i Cadafalch — rival Modernista architects
  • Catalan Modernisme — the Catalan branch of Art Nouveau / Jugendstil
  • Devout Catholic; nicknamed "God's Architect"; cause for beatification opened

Biography trivia

  • Born 1852 near Reus, son of a coppersmith; rheumatism as a child sharpened his eye for nature
  • Trained at the Barcelona school of architecture (director reputedly said he'd given a degree "to a genius or a madman")
  • Inspired by nature, Gothic, and Moorish forms; admired theorist Viollet-le-Duc
  • In his final years lived ascetically on the Sagrada Família site
  • Killed by a tram in 1926; mistaken for a beggar; buried in the basilica's crypt
  • Seven works inscribed as UNESCO World Heritage Sites

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Lluís Domènech i MontanerFellow Catalan Modernista; designed the Palau de la Música Catalana and the Hospital de Sant Pau, plus Casa Lleó Morera on the Block of Discord. More rationalist and richly decorative brick-and-mosaic; not Gaudí's organic warped geometry. "Palau de la Música" → Domènech, not Gaudí.
Josep Puig i CadafalchThe third great Modernista; designed Casa Amatller (next to Casa Batlló) and Casa de les Punxes ("House of Spikes"). More Northern-European/Gothic stepped-gable style. Pointed conical-tower mansion → Puig, not Gaudí.
Victor Horta / Hector GuimardThe Belgian & French Art Nouveau masters (Horta's Brussels town houses; Guimard's Paris Métro entrances). Whiplash iron curves indoors, but no trencadís, no catenary stone vaulting, no Barcelona. "Paris Métro entrance" → Guimard.
El Greco / Salvador DalíSpanish artists, but painters, not architects. Dalí (also Catalan, Figueres) is sometimes a co-clue for Catalan art — but melting clocks and surrealism are Dalí; warped basilicas and broken-tile mosaic are Gaudí.
Josep Maria JujolGaudí's gifted collaborator, not the lead designer. He executed the Park Güell trencadís bench and Casa Milà ironwork, and has his own buildings, but the famous commissions are credited to Gaudí. For "assistant who made the mosaic bench" → Jujol.
Casa Batlló vs. Casa MilàBoth on Passeig de Gràcia. Batlló = colorful trencadís facade, skull balconies, dragon-spine roof (a remodel). Milà / La Pedrera = raw undulating grey stone, no color, warrior chimneys (a new build, his last civil work). Trap: same street, same era, very different look.

🌐 Cross-discipline big picture Gaudí sits at the intersection of architecture, engineering, geometry, and Catalan history. His hanging-chain (catenary) models tie him to physics and structural engineering — funicular form-finding that anticipated computer-aided shell design. His ruled surfaces, hyperboloids, and helicoids bridge architecture and mathematics. As the icon of Catalan Modernisme, he connects to the late-19th-century Catalan cultural revival (the Renaixença) and to art history's Art Nouveau movement (Horta, Guimard, Mucha). In quizbowl, a "this Catalan architect" clue about an unfinished Barcelona basilica, broken-tile mosaic, a serpentine bench, or warped organic columns almost always leads to Gaudí.
