Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Géricault died in 1824; all works are in the public domain and load from Wikimedia Commons via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect. An internet connection is required. If any image fails, the filename likely differs — swap it manually.

NationalityFrench (born Rouen, 1791–1824)
Active periodEarly 19th century; died at 32
MovementFrench Romanticism — a founding figure
Signature subjectHorses, military figures, shipwrecks, the insane
Key teacherStudied under Carle Vernet, then Pierre-Narcisse Guérin
Key influence onEugène Delacroix (posed for Raft of the Medusa)

Defining traits

  • Raw, dramatic realism — depicted corpses, the insane, and suffering with unflinching accuracy; used actual cadaver parts as studies.
  • Powerful horse anatomy — studied and painted horses obsessively; equestrian subjects dominate his early career.
  • Moral and political edge — The Raft of the Medusa is a direct indictment of French governmental negligence.
  • Monumental scale for genre scenes — painted the Medusa shipwreck survivors at heroic Salon scale (16 × 23 ft).
  • Influenced by Michelangelo, Rubens, Caravaggio, and a stay in Rome (1816–1817).

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The survivors of the wrecked frigate Méduse on a makeshift raft — the single most given-away Géricault clue.
  • Series of portraits of psychiatric patients — the "Monomaniacs" painted at the Salpêtrière asylum.
  • Short life: died age 32 from riding injuries and tuberculosis.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 5 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Raft of the Medusa (Le Radeau de la Méduse)
1818–1819 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerBased on the 1816 wreck of the French frigate Méduse off Mauritania; 147 passengers stranded 13 days on a raft — only 15 survived, partly through cannibalism. Géricault interviewed survivors and studied severed limbs at the morgue for anatomical accuracy.
recogPyramid of desperate figures on a raft — some dead at lower left, men at top frantically waving a cloth toward a distant ship (Argus) on the horizon.
recogThe figure at the apex waves a red-and-white cloth; painted at enormous Salon scale (about 5 × 7 meters); caused a scandal at the 1819 Salon.

Charging Chasseur (Officer of the Imperial Guard)
1812 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerGéricault's debut Salon painting (1812), submitted when he was just 21. The officer twists dramatically in the saddle, saber raised, on a rearing horse amid battle smoke and flame.
recogVivid diagonal composition; the officer wears the green uniform of the Chasseurs à cheval of Napoleon's Imperial Guard.
recogPendant to Wounded Cuirassier — together they bookend Napoleonic glory vs. defeat.
wiki ↗
Wounded Cuirassier Leaving the Field of Battle
1814 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerSubmitted at the 1814 Salon — the year Napoleon abdicated. A heavily armored cuirassier struggles downhill with a wounded horse, reading as a metaphor for the Empire's collapse.
recogCompositional mirror to the Charging Chasseur: where that officer surges forward, the cuirassier retreats, heavily armored, his horse stumbling.
wiki ↗
Portrait of a Woman Suffering from Obsessive Envy (Monomanie de l'envie)
c. 1819–1822 · oil on canvas · Musée des Beaux-Arts, Lyon
powerOne of a series of ten portraits of psychiatric patients commissioned by Dr. Étienne-Jean Georget at the Salpêtrière asylum in Paris — five survive. Each depicts a single "monomania" (obsession).
recogThe sitter fixes the viewer with an intense, asymmetric stare; plain background, no attributes of madness — radical for its dignified, empathetic treatment of the insane.
recogThe series is a landmark in the history of psychiatry and art; the other surviving subjects include a kidnapper, a gambler, a military commander, and a thief.
wiki ↗
The Epsom Derby (The Derby at Epsom)
1821 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
powerPainted during Géricault's 1820–1821 stay in England; shows a horse race using the "flying gallop" pose — all four legs extended, none touching the ground — which Muybridge later proved was anatomically impossible.
recogFour racehorses stretch horizontally across a low, cloudy English sky; jockeys crouch in vivid primary-colored silks.
recogHugely influential on English and French sporting art; the flying-gallop convention lasted decades until photography corrected it.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Rearing and galloping horses — almost always present
  • Muscular male bodies in extremity (dying, struggling, rearing)
  • Cadaverous pallor — green-gray dead flesh from morgue studies
  • Diagonal / twisting compositions with dramatic foreshortening
  • Low, stormy skies — especially in English works

Key works & museums

  • Raft of the Medusa — Louvre (Room 77)
  • Charging Chasseur & Wounded Cuirassier — Louvre
  • Epsom Derby — Louvre
  • Monomanie de l'envie — Musée des Beaux-Arts, Lyon
  • Monomaniacs series spread across Ghent, Springfield (MA), Winterthur

Biographical clues

  • Born Rouen, 1791; died Paris, 1824 (age 32)
  • Died from complications after multiple equestrian falls
  • Studied at the École des Beaux-Arts and under Guérin
  • Spent 1816–1817 in Rome studying antique and Renaissance art
  • Spent 1820–1821 in England — influenced English sporting art and Constable/Turner
  • Eugène Delacroix posed for a figure in the Raft

Techniques & context

  • French Romanticism's founding work = Raft of the Medusa
  • Used actual severed limbs and heads as studio props
  • "Flying gallop" — anatomically impossible horse pose he popularized
  • Pioneered lithography in France (English social scenes, 1820–21)
  • Influenced by Rubens (flesh), Michelangelo (anatomy), Caravaggio (chiaroscuro)
  • Political subtext: Raft targeted the Bourbon Restoration government

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Eugène DelacroixBoth French Romantics — but Delacroix used richer jewel-toned color, loose brushwork, and more exotic Orientalist subjects (Liberty Leading the People, Death of Sardanapalus). Géricault's palette is darker and earthier; his subjects are more contemporarily realistic (real shipwrecks, real asylum patients). Delacroix was younger and survived to 1863.
J.M.W. TurnerTurner also painted shipwrecks and tempests, but with atmospheric dissolving light — nearly abstract. Géricault keeps solid, tangible bodies even in chaos. Turner is British; Géricault is French.
Jacques-Louis DavidNeoclassicism: David's figures are cool, idealized, static in frontal tableaux (Death of Marat, Oath of the Horatii). Géricault's figures are dynamic, tortured, emotionally raw — the generational reaction against David.
Antoine-Jean GrosGros was Géricault's immediate predecessor for Napoleonic military painting (Battle of Abukir, Pesthouse at Jaffa). Both painted large battle/disaster scenes; Géricault pushed further into realism and avoided idealization of Napoleon.
George StubbsBoth painted horse-attacked-by-lion scenes; Stubbs was English and a generation earlier (scientific, naturalistic). Géricault knew Stubbs's work from his London stay.
Francisco GoyaBoth depicted war atrocities and suffering with unflinching realism; Goya was Spanish, older, and worked in a wider range (portraits, tapestry cartoons, "Black Paintings"). Goya's horror is more nightmarish/hallucinatory; Géricault's is documentary-realistic.

🌊 Big Picture: Romanticism, medicine, and political art Géricault sits at the hinge between Neoclassicism and Romanticism and between art and political journalism. The Raft of the Medusa deploys the heroic scale of history painting to condemn a living government scandal — an early model of art as protest. His Monomaniacs series connects directly to the birth of clinical psychiatry (Pinel, Esquirol, Georget), making him a bridge answer between art history and the history of medicine. His flying-gallop horses link to 19th-century debates about animal locomotion later resolved by Muybridge's stop-motion photography — a cross-discipline thread into science history.
