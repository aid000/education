Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Goya died in 1828, so his works are in the public domain. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and need an internet connection. If any image fails to load, the file title likely differs slightly on Wikimedia Commons.

NationalitySpanish (born Fuendetodos, Aragon, 1746–1828)
PeriodLate 18th – early 19th c.; Romanticism / transitional
Court roleFirst Court Painter to Charles IV of Spain from 1799
Key turning pointIllness (~1793) left him completely deaf → darker work
Late phase"Black Paintings" (c.1820–23) — murals in the Quinta del Sordo
LegacyBridge between Old Masters and Romanticism / Expressionism

Defining traits

  • Dual output: flattering court portraits in Rococo style alongside brutally honest, subversive works.
  • Printmaking series: Los Caprichos, The Disasters of War, Los Disparates — biting social / political satire in aquatint and etching.
  • Witness to violence: Peninsular War (1808–14) produced the most harrowing war imagery before photography.
  • Black Paintings: painted directly on plaster walls of his house, featuring extreme, nightmarish scenes — never meant for public viewing.
  • Loose, gestural brushwork in mature works, anticipating Impressionism and Expressionism.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Saturn devouring his son — single most-given clue for Goya.
  • The Third of May 1808 — lantern illuminating a white-shirted man before a French firing squad.
  • "Black Paintings" / Quinta del Sordo (House of the Deaf Man).
  • Los Caprichos plate 43: "The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters."

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Saturn Devouring His Son
c. 1820–23 · oil on plaster, transferred to canvas · Museo del Prado, Madrid
recogA wild-eyed giant gripping a small, headless human body mid-bite — the single most recognized Goya image.
recogOriginally painted directly on the dining room wall of his house, the Quinta del Sordo.
powerPart of the 14 "Black Paintings" — never titled by Goya; transferred to canvas after his death. The giant's knuckles are white; the figure's left arm has already been eaten. Goya never exhibited or explained these works.

The Third of May 1808
1814 · oil on canvas · Museo del Prado, Madrid
recogA man in a white shirt throws his arms wide before a faceless French firing squad lit by a square lantern.
recogPainted six years after the event; companion piece is The Second of May 1808 (the Charge of the Mamelukes).
powerThe white-shirted figure's outstretched arms echo a crucifixion pose; he has a stigma-like mark on his right palm. Commissioned by the liberal government of Ferdinand VII to commemorate resistance to Napoleon — a foundational image of modern war painting, influencing Manet's Execution of Maximilian and Picasso's Guernica.
wiki ↗
The Sleep of Reason Produces Monsters
1799 · aquatint/etching (Los Caprichos, plate 43) · various collections
recogA figure slumped at a writing desk, surrounded by owls, bats, and a lynx emerging from darkness behind.
recogPlate 43 of Los Caprichos — the most famous single plate of the 80-print series.
powerThe Spanish caption reads "El sueño de la razón produce monstruos" — a double meaning: sueño means both "sleep" and "dream." The series satirizes Spanish superstition and Inquisition-era ignorance; Goya initially planned this print as the series frontispiece.
wiki ↗
The Naked Maja (La maja desnuda)
c. 1797–1800 · oil on canvas · Museo del Prado, Madrid
recogReclining nude woman on a green chaise, gazing directly at the viewer — unusually frank and confrontational for the period.
recogAlways paired with the Clothed Maja (La maja vestida) — same pose, same model, dressed.
powerCommissioned by Manuel de Godoy, Prime Minister of Spain. One of the first Western nudes to show pubic hair. The Inquisition summoned Goya in 1814 to explain the paintings. Identity of the model is disputed; the most common claim is the Duchess of Alba.
wiki ↗
The Family of Charles IV
1800–01 · oil on canvas · Museo del Prado, Madrid
recogThirteen members of the Spanish royal family in a stiff group portrait; Goya himself visible at the far left at a large canvas — a nod to Velázquez's Las Meninas.
recogThe dominant, bejeweled Queen Maria Luisa stands at center, not the king — widely read as her political dominance.
powerThe art critic Théophile Gautier famously described it as "the corner baker and his wife after they won the lottery." The unflatteringly honest faces are seen as covert mockery of royalty — or simply Goya's ruthless realism.
wiki ↗
Witches' Sabbath (The Great He-Goat)
c. 1820–23 · oil on plaster, transferred to canvas · Museo del Prado, Madrid
recogA huge black goat (the Devil) presides over a crowd of haggard witches in a night scene.
recogOne of the 14 Black Paintings; painted in the same room as Saturn.
powerAlso called Aquelarre (Basque for "witches' field"). The lone seated young woman in a mantilla at far right is sometimes identified as the only sane observer — possibly Goya's companion Leocadia Zorrilla.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Black, murky backgrounds in later works; thick impasto
  • Grotesque faces / leering crowds (Black Paintings, Caprichos)
  • Owls and bats (symbols of superstition and ignorance)
  • White shirt / illuminated victim (Third of May)
  • Long diagonal brush strokes; gestural, almost Expressionist
  • Tapestry cartoons: bright Rococo palette, peasant leisure scenes

Print series (key facts)

  • Los Caprichos (1799) — 80 plates, social satire, Inquisition criticism
  • The Disasters of War (c.1810–20) — 82 plates; Peninsular War atrocities; published posthumously (1863)
  • Los Disparates / Proverbs (c.1815–23) — 22 surreal, allegorical etchings
  • La Tauromaquia (1816) — 33 prints on bullfighting history

Key people & places

  • Fuendetodos, Aragon — birthplace
  • Quinta del Sordo ("House of the Deaf Man") — his villa outside Madrid
  • Duchess of Alba — patron and possible lover; model disputes
  • Leocadia Zorrilla — companion in his last years; possibly depicted in Woman Reading and Black Paintings
  • Bordeaux — died in exile, 1828

Historical context clues

  • Peninsular War (1808–14) — French occupation of Spain under Napoleon's brother Joseph
  • Spanish Inquisition — targeted by Los Caprichos; summoned Goya over the Majas
  • Charles IV / Ferdinand VII — monarchs Goya served as court painter
  • Manuel de Godoy — PM who commissioned the Majas
  • Deafness onset c. 1793 after severe illness — marks the pivot to dark themes

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
VelázquezVelázquez is the 17th-century Spanish court painter; Goya's Family of Charles IV deliberately echoes Las Meninas. Velázquez = cool, precise space; Goya = confrontational, psychologically charged. Goya admired and copied Velázquez.
Eugène DelacroixBoth Romantic-era painters of violence and passion; but Delacroix is French, uses swirling compositional energy and exotic Orientalist subjects (Liberty Leading the People, Death of Sardanapalus). Goya = Spanish atrocity; Delacroix = French Romanticism.
Jacques-Louis DavidDavid is French Neoclassical — clean, idealized, Greco-Roman clarity (Oath of the Horatii, Death of Marat). Goya's war images are the exact anti-Davidian response: chaos, suffering, anti-heroic.
Henry FuseliBoth painted nightmare / supernatural scenes; but Fuseli is Swiss-British, best known for The Nightmare (incubus on a sleeping woman). Goya's monsters are more politically grounded.
Hieronymus BoschBoth depicted grotesque, demonic crowds; but Bosch is 15th-century Dutch / highly detailed medieval symbolism. Goya's figures are looser, more viscerally psychological. Goya explicitly owned and studied Bosch.
Théodore GéricaultNear-contemporary Romantic painter of dramatic suffering (Raft of the Medusa); both depict mass tragedy. Géricault is French, single masterpiece focus. Goya has far wider output and the prints.

Cross-discipline connections Goya bridges multiple quizbowl categories. His Third of May 1808 anchors tossups on the Peninsular War (Napoleon, Spain, Wellington) and directly influenced Manet's Execution of Maximilian and Picasso's Guernica — connecting art, French history, and 20th-century politics. Los Caprichos and Disasters of War appear in print-media and journalism history questions. His deafness tossups sometimes cross into disability studies. The Black Paintings are frequently cited as proto-Expressionist, bridging into discussions of Munch, Ensor, and German Expressionism.
