Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Delacroix died in 1863, so his works are in the public domain and available on Wikimedia Commons. Images are loaded via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and need an internet connection. If any image fails to load, the file title likely differs slightly.

NationalityFrench (born Charenton-Saint-Maurice, 1798–1863)
Period19th century; active c. 1822–1863
MovementFrench Romanticism — its leading figure
Signature qualityDramatic color, energetic brushwork, turbulent movement
Key rivalIngres — the Ingres/Delacroix "line vs. color" debate defined the era
Key museumLouvre, Paris; Musée National Eugène-Delacroix, Paris

Defining traits

  • Color over line: loose, expressive brushwork (the opposite of Neoclassical precision); Ingres called him "a colorist who's killed painting."
  • Literary and historical sources: Byron, Dante, Shakespeare, ancient history, the Orient — every major canvas draws on a named text or event.
  • Diagonal compositions and swirling figures — bodies twisted in violent or ecstatic motion; dramatic chiaroscuro contrasts.
  • 1832 North Africa trip (Morocco and Algeria) — transformed his palette; produced hundreds of sketches, sparked his Orientalist works.
  • Influence on Impressionists and Post-Impressionists: Cézanne, Renoir, and especially Van Gogh and Baudelaire championed him.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Liberty Leading the People" — allegorical female figure with the French tricolor; most-given Delacroix clue.
  • "Leader of French Romanticism" and "rival of Ingres" — common category descriptors.
  • The Massacre at Chios — shocked the 1824 Salon; Gros called it "the massacre of painting."

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Liberty Leading the People
1830 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
recogA bare-breasted allegorical woman (Marianne) holds the French tricolor aloft while striding over corpses.
recogCommemorates the July Revolution of 1830; armed citizens — a boy with pistols, a top-hatted bourgeois — crowd behind her.
powerThe figure is not a portrait of a specific woman but an allegory; the top-hatted man at right is often said to resemble Delacroix himself. Originally controversial for mixing allegory with gritty realism (dead bodies, proletarian fighters). Inspired Victor Hugo's Les Misérables barricade scenes.

The Death of Sardanapalus
1827 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
recogAn Assyrian king reclines on a vast crimson bed as slaves and concubines are massacred around him before he burns everything.
recogBased on Byron's 1821 play Sardanapalus; extreme luxury and violence fill every inch of the canvas.
powerCaused a scandal at the 1828 Salon — critics attacked its compositional "chaos" and moral excess. The diagonal cascade of nude bodies, horses, and treasure is deliberately anti-Neoclassical. Sometimes called "the Romantic manifesto in paint."
wiki ↗
The Massacre at Chios
1824 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
recogGreek civilians — exhausted, wounded, grieving families — slumped in the foreground; Ottoman horsemen loom behind.
recogDepicts the 1822 Ottoman massacre on the island of Chios during the Greek War of Independence.
powerGros famously called it "the massacre of painting." While preparing it, Delacroix saw Constable's The Hay Wain and repainted the sky using Constable's vibrant broken-color technique — a pivotal moment in Romantic painting history.
wiki ↗
Women of Algiers in Their Apartment
1834 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
recogThree Algerian women lounging in a harem interior, richly dressed, with a servant at right — a warm, sensual palette.
recogPainted directly after his 1832 trip to Morocco and Algeria; among the first European paintings of a real harem interior (he reportedly gained access briefly).
powerProfoundly influenced Renoir (who made his own 1872 version) and Picasso (who made 15 variations in 1954–55 as Les Femmes d'Alger, one of which sold for $179M in 2015). A cornerstone of European Orientalism.
wiki ↗
Dante and Virgil (The Barque of Dante)
1822 · oil on canvas · Louvre, Paris
recogDante and Virgil in a storm-tossed boat on the river Styx, with damned souls clinging to its hull.
recogDelacroix's Salon debut at age 24 (1822); based on Inferno, Canto VIII.
powerThe writhing nude bodies clinging to the boat echo Michelangelo's Last Judgment and Géricault's Raft of the Medusa (his direct model). Gros praised it as "Rubens cooled down" — a signal that the Romantic generation had arrived.
wiki ↗
Greece on the Ruins of Missolonghi
1826 · oil on canvas · Musée des Beaux-Arts, Bordeaux
recogA woman in white and blue (allegorical Greece) kneels atop rubble; an arm protrudes from beneath the stones.
recogMourns the 1826 fall of Missolonghi — the siege where Byron died in 1824 fighting for Greek independence.
powerDelacroix was deeply influenced by Byron — this is his second major philhellenic work after Massacre at Chios. The fallen arm echoes Massacre at Chios's foregrounded victims; the composition presages Liberty Leading the People's allegorical female.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual / stylistic markers

  • Loose, energetic brushwork — "broken color," visible strokes
  • Diagonal compositions — figures cascade from upper-left to lower-right
  • Rich, saturated color — Moroccan warmth, dramatic shadows
  • Turbulent animal scenes — lion, tiger, horse hunts (post-1832)
  • Allegorical women — draped in national colors, striding or mourning

Literary sources (key)

  • Byron — Sardanapalus, Giaour, Missolonghi; Delacroix = the "Byron of painting"
  • Dante — Inferno (Barque of Dante)
  • Shakespeare — Hamlet, Desdemona, Ophelia
  • Walter Scott — Ivanhoe (Rebecca), Quentin Durward
  • Goethe — Faust illustrations (lithograph series, 1828)
  • Ariosto — Orlando Furioso subjects

Biography & context

  • Born Charenton-Saint-Maurice, 1798; died Paris 1863
  • 1832 Morocco/Algeria trip with diplomat Mornay — transformed palette
  • Rivalry with Ingres — "color vs. line" debate
  • Friend of Chopin and George Sand; close to Baudelaire
  • Major mural commissions: Palais Bourbon, Palais du Luxembourg, Saint-Sulpice chapel
  • Admired Rubens and Veronese; also influenced by Constable

Critical / historical tags

  • "Leader/chief of the French Romantic school"
  • "The Byron of painting" (Baudelaire's phrase)
  • Gros: Massacre at Chios = "massacre of painting"
  • Orientalism — major figure in 19th-c. European Orientalist painting
  • Key precursor to Impressionism — color theory, broken brushwork
  • Van Gogh and Cézanne copied Delacroix paintings as exercises

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Théodore GéricaultBoth French Romantics, but Géricault died 1824 (age 32) after the Raft of the Medusa (1819). Delacroix was his younger contemporary and disciple. Raft = monochromatic; Delacroix = rich color. If the clue mentions the Raft, it's Géricault.
Jean-Auguste-Dominique IngresIngres = Neoclassicism — smooth, precise line, polished surface (e.g., Grande Odalisque, The Turkish Bath). Delacroix = Romanticism, visible brushwork. The "line vs. color" debate: Ingres = line; Delacroix = color.
Francisco GoyaGoya is Spanish, slightly earlier (1746–1828). Both painted war atrocities and turbulent subjects, but Goya's The Third of May and Saturn Devouring His Son are his. Delacroix's massacre scenes are Greek/Levantine subjects.
Gustave CourbetCourbet = Realism (1840s–1870s) — gritty, unsentimental contemporary subjects (The Stone Breakers, Burial at Ornans). Delacroix = historical/literary grandeur. Courbet explicitly attacked Delacroix's academic-Romantic style.
Honoré DaumierDaumier = caricature and social satire, political cartoons, smaller scale. Not a history painter.
William TurnerBoth famous for loose, luminous color — but Turner is British, primarily landscape/marine. If the subject is English coast or atmosphere, it's Turner, not Delacroix.
