Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Hokusai died in 1849; his works are in the public domain and available on Wikimedia Commons. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect — an internet connection is required. If any image fails, the filename likely differs slightly from the guess used here.

NationalityJapanese (Edo, now Tokyo; 1760–1849)
Period / EraLate Edo period; active c. 1779–1849
MovementUkiyo-e ("pictures of the floating world")
MediumWoodblock print (nishiki-e), ink painting, illustrated books (manga)
Signature subjectMount Fuji, waves, landscapes, birds, flowers
Name changesUsed 30+ pen names over his life; born Tokitaro; best known as Hokusai ("North Studio")

Defining traits

  • Ukiyo-e woodblock prints — flat areas of bold color, strong outlines, asymmetric compositions.
  • Mastery of Prussian blue (bero-ai) — newly imported pigment used to striking effect from the 1820s onward.
  • Radical compositions: foreground objects (waves, branches, people) frame distant Mount Fuji.
  • Prolific output: estimated 30,000+ works across prints, paintings, illustrated books.
  • Hugely influential in the West — Japonisme; influenced Monet, Van Gogh, Klimt, Art Nouveau.
  • Self-described as "the old man mad about painting" (Gakyo Rojin Manji, his final pen name).

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The towering curling wave about to engulf boats, with Fuji in the distance — gives away The Great Wave.
  • Thirty-six (or Forty-six) Views of Mount Fuji series title.
  • "Old Man Mad About Drawing/Painting" — his self-adopted final pen name.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Great Wave off Kanagawa
c. 1831 · woodblock print · from Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji · multiple collections incl. Metropolitan Museum, MoMA, British Museum
recogA giant curling wave with claw-like foam crests towers over three fishing boats; Mount Fuji sits small in the distance.
recogThe wave's hollow interior frames Fuji; the boats are oshiokuri-bune (fast cargo boats from Izu).
powerOne of the most reproduced images in history; printed first in Prussian blue (bero-ai), replacing traditional sumi ink. The series was titled Fugaku Sanjurokkei (富嶽三十六景). Links to: Thirty-six Views series, Japonisme, Debussy's La Mer (he kept a print on his desk).

Fine Wind, Clear Morning (Red Fuji)
c. 1831 · woodblock print · from Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji · Tokyo National Museum
recogMount Fuji seen up close, painted vivid red-orange in early morning sunlight against a deep blue sky; trees dot the lower slopes.
recogNo foreground framing object — Fuji occupies almost the entire picture plane; sky grades from blue to green.
powerKnown as "Red Fuji" (Aka Fuji); together with The Great Wave, these two prints are the most iconic from Thirty-six Views. Often paired as a contrast in quizbowl: wave = action/sea; Red Fuji = stillness/land.

Ejiri in Suruga Province (Travellers Caught in the Wind)
c. 1831 · woodblock print · from Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji · British Museum
recogTravelers on a road are buffeted by sudden wind; papers and a hat fly into the air; bare trees bend; Fuji is distantly visible.
recogOne traveler holds their hat down; the scattered papers form a diagonal energy cutting across the scene.
powerA favorite for tossups on dynamic composition and implied movement in Japanese prints; the flying papers are sometimes described as "letters" — they are papers/books from travelers' bundles.

The Dream of the Fisherman's Wife
1814 · woodblock print · from Kinoe no Komatsu (an erotic book) · various collections
recogA woman embraced by a large octopus and a smaller one in an erotic scene; her ecstatic expression is explicit.
recogFrom shunga (erotic woodblock) tradition; the large octopus whispers to the woman in text within the image.
powerThis print influenced Western erotic and surreal art (Dalí, etc.) and is the most famous example of Japanese shunga. The title references the ama (female pearl divers). Look for: "octopus," "fisherman's wife," "shunga" clues.
wiki ↗
Hokusai Manga
1814–1878 · 15 volumes of illustrated sketchbooks · Nagoya publisher Eirakuya Toshiro
recogFifteen volumes of sketches (manga = "whimsical pictures") depicting thousands of subjects: people, animals, plants, ghosts, landscapes, wrestling moves.
recogUsed as instruction manuals for artists; smuggled into France where they inspired Impressionists and Art Nouveau designers.
powerThe word manga as used in modern Japanese comics derives from Hokusai's coinage. French artists including Monet collected volumes; Ernest Fenollosa and others distributed them in the West. Key cross-discipline link: Hokusai → Japonisme → Impressionism/Post-Impressionism.
wiki ↗
One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji (Fugaku Hyakkei)
1834–1849 · 3 volumes of illustrated books · various collections
recogThree volumes of ink brushwork (not color woodblock) showing Fuji from every conceivable angle — through caves, behind nets, over rooftops, reflected in pools.
recogConsidered the peak of Hokusai's mature style; the third volume was published posthumously.
powerDistinguished from Thirty-six Views (color prints) by its monochrome ink and book format. Hokusai began this series in his mid-70s — his late-career masterpiece alongside the Waterfalls and Bridges series.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Mount Fuji — in virtually every view, often tiny against sky
  • Curling wave with claw-like foam — The Great Wave giveaway
  • Prussian blue (bero-ai) — the dominant color from c. 1829
  • Foreground framing device — giant wave, branch, arch, barrel framing Fuji
  • Bold black outlines, flat color fills, no Western perspective
  • Octopus — iconic in Dream of the Fisherman's Wife

Key series

  • Thirty-six Views of Mount Fuji (Fugaku Sanjurokkei) — c. 1831; actually 46 prints
  • One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji (Fugaku Hyakkei) — 3 books, 1834–1849
  • Hokusai Manga — 15 volumes, 1814–1878
  • A Tour of Waterfalls in the Provinces — c. 1832, 8 prints
  • Remarkable Views of Bridges — c. 1834, 11 prints
  • Large Flowers (Daisho no Hana) — c. 1833

Pen names / biographical

  • Born Kawamura Tokitaro, 1760, Edo (Tokyo)
  • Apprenticed to woodblock engraver at 14; entered Katsukawa school at 18
  • Used 30+ pen names; key ones: Shunro, Sori, Hokusai, Taito, Iitsu, Manji
  • Final name: Gakyo Rojin Manji = "old man mad about painting"
  • Died at 89, reportedly saying "if only I had another 10 years"
  • Moved house 93 times over his life

Western influence / cross-links

  • Japonisme — the Western craze for Japanese art in the 1860s–1890s
  • Monet collected 250+ Japanese prints; garden at Giverny inspired by ukiyo-e
  • Van Gogh copied Hiroshige prints; admired Hokusai's line
  • Debussy kept The Great Wave on his desk; La Mer is linked to it
  • Klimt, Art Nouveau designers — sinuous line from ukiyo-e
  • Word "manga" coined by Hokusai for his sketchbooks

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Utagawa HiroshigeAlso ukiyo-e, also landscapes, also famous Fuji views (53 Stations of the Tokaido, 100 Famous Views of Edo). Key split: Hokusai = grand, dynamic, powerful waves, bold geometry. Hiroshige = atmospheric, lyrical, rain/snow effects, softer mood. Hiroshige's Tokaido is stations of a road; Hokusai's series orbit Fuji.
Kitagawa UtamaroUkiyo-e master but specialized in bijin-ga (beautiful women portraits), not landscapes. Close-up faces, elaborate coiffures; no Mount Fuji, no waves.
Toshusai SharakuUkiyo-e but actor portraits (yakusha-e) only — bold, caricature-like kabuki actor faces. Active for only ~10 months in 1794–95.
Katsushika OiHokusai's daughter and collaborator; some works formerly attributed to Hokusai were hers. Her style is similar but typically signed differently.
Kuniyoshi / KunisadaUtagawa school, later Edo period; known for warriors (musha-e) and kabuki — not landscape series centered on Fuji.
Sesshu ToyoEarlier ink-wash (sumi-e) painter, 15th–16th c.; monochrome, no woodblock prints; Zen-influenced. Don't confuse for Hokusai on monochrome landscape questions.

🌊 Cross-discipline big picture Hokusai sits at the center of a wide quizbowl answer web. His prints drove Japonisme, directly connecting him to Monet (collected 250+ prints; Water Lilies series), Van Gogh (copied Hiroshige; admired Hokusai), and Klimt (sinuous line). Debussy linked him to music (La Mer). The word manga — now a global cultural category — is his coinage from the Manga sketchbooks. On science tossups, The Great Wave is among the most reproduced images in human history and appears in discussions of rogue wave physics and fluid dynamics. In literature, his influence on French Symbolism (Mallarmé, etc.) surfaces in poetry tossups.
