Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Botticelli died in 1510, so his works are in the public domain. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and need an internet connection. If any thumbnail fails, the Wikimedia Commons filename may differ slightly.

Full nameAlessandro di Mariano di Vanni Filipepi ("Botticelli" = nickname, "little barrel")
Datesc. 1445–1510, Florence
Period / movementEarly Italian Renaissance; Florentine school
PatronMedici family (Lorenzo de' Medici / Lorenzo il Magnifico); also Pope Sixtus IV
TeacherFra Filippo Lippi; also influenced by Verrocchio
Key traitGraceful, linear figures; mythological subjects treated with lyrical elegance; faces modeled after Simonetta Vespucci

Defining traits

  • Sinuous, flowing outlines — figures defined by line more than shadow (not Leonardesque chiaroscuro).
  • Mythological allegory commissioned by the Medici — humanist program fusing Neoplatonism with classical mythology.
  • Female ideal modeled on Simonetta Vespucci, mistress of Giuliano de' Medici.
  • Distinctive tempera-on-panel technique (not fresco) for the major mythological works.
  • Later life marked by religious crisis under Savonarola's influence — allegedly burned some works; last paintings severe and austere.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The shell-born Venus rising from the sea — "The Birth of Venus" is the single most tested Botticelli answer.
  • Zephyr blowing wind at left, hora holding a flowered cloak at right.
  • The Primavera / Spring oranges-grove allegory — Three Graces dancing.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Birth of Venus
c. 1484–1486 · tempera on canvas · Uffizi Gallery, Florence
recogVenus stands nude on a giant clamshell emerging from the sea; her long golden hair covers her body.
recogZephyr (and Chloris/Aura) blow wind from the left; a hora rushes to cloak her with a floral garment at right.
powerCommissioned by the Medici (possibly Lorenzo di Pierfrancesco); the Venus face is modeled on Simonetta Vespucci. Humanist program: Neoplatonic "Heavenly Venus" born from sea foam after Uranus's castration. Pair answer with Primavera — same patron, same program, same room at Castello.

Primavera (Spring)
c. 1477–1482 · tempera on panel · Uffizi Gallery, Florence
recogNine figures in an orange grove: Mercury, Three Graces, Venus, Flora, Chloris, Zephyr; Venus presides at center.
recogThe Three Graces dance in a diaphanous circle at center-right; Cupid aims a flaming arrow above Venus.
powerHumanist program debated for centuries — most read it as a Neoplatonic allegory of Spring's triumph; the orange grove is the Medici emblem. Chloris at right is shown mid-transformation into Flora, illustrating Ovid's Fasti. Possibly hung in the Villa di Castello alongside Birth of Venus.
wiki ↗
Adoration of the Magi
1475 · tempera on panel · Uffizi Gallery, Florence
recogMagi and crowd fill a ruined classical structure; the Medici family members appear as characters — Cosimo, Piero, Giovanni, Lorenzo, Giuliano.
recogBotticelli himself appears at the far right, looking directly at the viewer (self-portrait in a yellow cloak).
powerCommissioned by Gaspare di Zanobi del Lama for his chapel in Santa Maria Novella. The Medici portrait practice was standard for dynastic propaganda — this work established Botticelli as the Medici court painter. A second Adoration (c. 1475–76, National Gallery, Washington) also asked.
wiki ↗
Pallas and the Centaur
c. 1482 · tempera on canvas · Uffizi Gallery, Florence
recogPallas Athena grasps a centaur by the hair while he grimaces; she holds a halberd.
recogPallas's dress bears interlinked rings — the Medici symbol (diamond rings / olive branches); bay of the sea behind.
powerRead as an allegory of Reason subduing Passion; or political allegory of Lorenzo de' Medici's peace mission to Naples (1479–80) — Wisdom taming war. Pair with Primavera as Medici allegorical commissions.
wiki ↗
The Mystical Nativity
1500–1501 · oil on canvas · National Gallery, London
recogThe only signed and dated Botticelli painting — Greek inscription at the top (his only signed work).
recogAngels dancing in a circle above the stable; twelve angels carry olive branches; devils flee into crevices at the bottom.
powerInscribed Greek text references the Apocalypse and the year 1500, reflecting Botticelli's Savonarolan crisis — the only work he inscribed with his name. Unique two-zone composition (heaven above, earth below) with non-naturalistic scale.
wiki ↗
Mars and Venus
c. 1483–1485 · tempera and oil on panel · National Gallery, London
recogMars sleeps deeply while Venus watches; playful satyrs wear his helmet and blow a conch shell in his ear.
recogWasp nest in upper right — possibly a Vespucci family emblem (vespa = wasp), suggesting a wedding gift.
powerAllegory of Love taming War; thought to be a spalliera (furniture panel) for a marriage chest. Wasps in the corner have led scholars to identify the patrons as the Vespucci — and the Venus model again as Simonetta Vespucci.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Shell (Venus's birth vessel — clamshell)
  • Zephyr blowing wind (Birth of Venus, Primavera)
  • Three Graces in diaphanous robes, dancing in a circle
  • Orange / olive grove background (Medici emblem)
  • Long, flowing hair on female figures (Simonetta ideal)
  • Wasp motif — Vespucci family connection (vespa)

Key figures & patrons

  • Lorenzo de' Medici ("il Magnifico") — primary patron
  • Simonetta Vespucci — idealized female model
  • Pope Sixtus IV — commissioned Sistine Chapel frescoes
  • Savonarola — Dominican friar; Botticelli's late religious crisis
  • Fra Filippo Lippi — teacher
  • Dante Alighieri — Botticelli illustrated Divine Comedy

Technique & context

  • Tempera on panel — major mythological works
  • Sinuous, linear style — outline-dominant, not chiaroscuro
  • Neoplatonism — humanist Medici program (Ficino, Poliziano)
  • Sistine Chapel (1481–82) — three Old Testament / New Testament frescoes
  • "Bonfire of the Vanities" (1497) — may have burned own works
  • Rediscovered in the 19th century by the Pre-Raphaelites

Mythology / textual sources

  • Hesiod / Homeric Hymns — source for Venus birth myth
  • Ovid's Fasti — Zephyr-Chloris-Flora transformation in Primavera
  • Angelo Poliziano's Stanze — humanist text inspiring Birth of Venus
  • Lucian's Description of Calumny — source for Calumny of Apelles
  • Boccaccio's Decameron — Nastagio degli Onesti panels (V.8)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Fra Filippo LippiBotticelli's teacher — similar sweet Madonnas but Lippi's figures are rounder and more earthbound; no mythological allegory at Lippi's scale. Lippi predates the Medici mythological program.
RaphaelRaphael also paints the Three Graces (Chantilly) and Madonnas, but his style is High Renaissance — fuller forms, atmospheric depth, sfumato. Botticelli = flat, linear, Gothic-adjacent elongation.
GhirlandaioFellow Florentine Sistine Chapel painter and exact contemporary. Ghirlandaio's style is more documentary / portraiture-based (faces of Florentine citizens); less mythological. Michelangelo trained under Ghirlandaio.
Filippino LippiSon of Fra Filippo; worked in Botticelli's workshop and finished some of his altarpieces. Style is similar but more restless, nervous line. Often a trap for unfinished Botticelli works.
Giulio Romano / MantegnaBoth paint mythological allegories with classical settings, but these are Mantuan / northern Italian court painters with harder, more sculptural forms than Botticelli's Florentine grace.
Titian's Venus AnadyomeneAlso a Venus rising from water, but High Renaissance / Venetian — oil paint, warm flesh tones, no shell, no Zephyr. If the clue says "shell" or "Zephyr," it's Botticelli.

🌐 Big-picture cross-discipline connections Botticelli's mythological program was scripted by humanist scholars (Marsilio Ficino, Angelo Poliziano) in Lorenzo de' Medici's circle — tying visual art directly to philosophy (Neoplatonism), poetry, and classical literature. The Primavera's Zephyr-Chloris-Flora sequence comes from Ovid's Fasti; the Birth of Venus echoes Hesiod and Poliziano's Stanze. In quizbowl, Botticelli questions crossover into: Dante tossups (he illustrated the Divine Comedy), Boccaccio (Nastagio panels), Renaissance history (Medici, Savonarola, Sistine Chapel), and mythology (Venus, the Graces, Mars, Pallas). The Pre-Raphaelite rediscovery in the 1870s–80s also connects to 19th-century British art tossups.
