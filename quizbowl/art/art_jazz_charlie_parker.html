Quick Overview

Charlie "Bird" Parker, c. 1947 (Gottlieb photo)
InstrumentAlto saxophone (the defining alto voice of modern jazz)
Era / StyleFounder of bebop (mid-1940s); active 1940s–1955
Born / DiedKansas City, KS 1920 — died New York City 1955, age 34
Nickname"Bird" (or "Yardbird") — the source of the club name Birdland
Key partnerTrumpeter Dizzy Gillespie; quintet with a young Miles Davis
Key city / sceneNew York's 52nd Street & Harlem; jam sessions at Minton's Playhouse

Defining traits

  • Bebop innovation: fast tempos, virtuosic melodic lines, and extended chord substitutions built on the upper chord extensions (9ths, 11ths, 13ths).
  • Contrafacts: wrote new bebop melodies over the chord changes of pop standards — e.g. "Ornithology" over "How High the Moon," "Ko-Ko" over "Cherokee."
  • Blues-rooted: for all the harmonic complexity, his playing stayed deeply tied to the blues ("Now's the Time," "Parker's Mood").
  • Legendary improviser — recorded "alternate takes" prized by collectors; the Dial and Savoy sessions are canonical.
  • Heroin and alcohol addiction; the coroner estimated his age at death as 50–60 though he was 34.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "This alto saxophonist nicknamed Bird" — the instant giveaway.
  • Co-founder of bebop with Dizzy Gillespie.
  • The club Birdland was named for him; the standard "Yardbird Suite."
  • Clint Eastwood's 1988 biopic Bird (Forest Whitaker as Parker).

Signature Recordings & Compositions

"Ko-Ko"
recorded Nov 26, 1945 · Savoy Records · with Dizzy Gillespie & Miles Davis
recogA blistering bebop showpiece built as a contrafact on the changes of "Cherokee" (Ray Noble).
recogCut at the famous Nov. 1945 Savoy session, often cited as the recorded birth of mature bebop.
powerBecause trumpeter Dizzy Gillespie also played piano on the date, Gillespie takes the trumpet intro/coda while a teenage Miles Davis sat out the breakneck head; Max Roach is on drums. Not to be confused with Duke Ellington's "Ko-Ko."

recogThe title puns on Parker's nickname "Bird" — the "study of birds."

"Now's the Time"
recorded Nov 26, 1945 · Savoy Records
recogA straightforward 12-bar blues in F — proof that bebop kept the blues at its core.
recogIts riff was later lifted for the 1949 R&B hit "The Hucklebuck."
powerFrom the same landmark 1945 Savoy session as "Ko-Ko" and "Billie's Bounce"; a staple head every jazz student learns.

Charlie Parker with Strings
1949–50 · Mercury / Clef · album with string orchestra
recogParker fulfilled a lifelong dream to record with a string section; the lead-off track is "Just Friends," which he called his favorite of his own records.
recogProduced by Norman Granz, who also ran Jazz at the Philharmonic.
powerCriticized by some bebop purists as "commercial," it was Parker's best-selling work and includes "April in Paris" and "Summertime."

"Parker's Mood"
recorded 1948 · Savoy Records
recogA slow, deeply expressive blues opening with a famous unaccompanied alto fanfare.
recogVocalist King Pleasure later set lyrics to Parker's solo ("vocalese").
powerPianist on the date is John Lewis (later of the Modern Jazz Quartet); the lyric eerily references Parker being "buried in Kansas City."

"Confirmation"
composed mid-1940s · definitive studio take 1953 (Verve)
recogOne of Parker's most admired original melodies, with an intricate descending bebop line over fast-moving changes.
recogUnlike most Parker heads, it is an original chord progression, not a contrafact.
powerA required tune for any bebop player; its bridge cycles through ii-V-I sequences that became a textbook study in jazz harmony.

Recognition / Clue Bank

People / collaborators

  • Dizzy Gillespie — trumpeter, co-founder of bebop; the essential Bird partner
  • Miles Davis — young trumpeter in Parker's quintet (1945–48)
  • Max Roach — bebop drummer on the Savoy sides
  • Bud Powell — bebop pianist; played Massey Hall 1953
  • Charles Mingus — bassist at Massey Hall; recorded it on Debut
  • Norman Granz — producer; Jazz at the Philharmonic, the Strings album

Innovations / hallmarks

  • Bebop — fast tempos, complex harmony, virtuosic small-group improvisation
  • Contrafact — new melody over a borrowed chord progression
  • Upper chord extensions — improvising on 9ths/11ths/13ths and substitutions
  • Rhythm changes — "I Got Rhythm" chords ("Anthropology," "Moose the Mooche")
  • Blues core ("Now's the Time," "Parker's Mood," "Au Privave")
  • Hard-driving alto saxophone tone — the model for generations

Key tunes for buzzing

  • "Ko-Ko" — on "Cherokee" changes (NOT Ellington's "Ko-Ko")
  • "Ornithology" — on "How High the Moon"; pun on "Bird"
  • "Confirmation" — rare original (non-contrafact) head
  • "Yardbird Suite" — named for his nickname
  • "Anthropology" / "Donna Lee" — rhythm/Indiana changes
  • Charlie Parker with Strings — "Just Friends"

Biographical / "also" clues

  • Born Kansas City, KS 1920; came up in the K.C. swing scene
  • Nicknames "Bird" / "Yardbird"; the club Birdland is named for him
  • 1946 L.A. breakdown → committed to Camarillo State Hospital
  • Used the pseudonym "Charlie Chan" at Massey Hall (1953) for contract reasons
  • Died NYC 1955, age 34; "Bird Lives" graffiti appeared after
  • Clint Eastwood's 1988 film Bird; labels Savoy, Dial, Verve/Clef

Answer-Line / Buzz Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Dizzy GillespieParker's bebop co-founder — but Gillespie plays trumpet, Parker alto sax. Gillespie pioneered Afro-Cuban / Latin jazz ("Manteca," "A Night in Tunisia"), led big bands, and lived to 1993. If the clue says "trumpet," "bent horn," or "puffed cheeks," it's Dizzy.
"Ko-Ko" (Duke Ellington)Two famous jazz pieces titled "Ko-Ko." Ellington's (1940) is a minor-key Blanton-era big-band feature; Parker's (1945) is a bebop contrafact on "Cherokee." Listen for big band vs. quintet.
John ColtraneBoth saxophone giants, but Coltrane plays tenor/soprano and belongs to the modal / free generation (1950s–60s). Parker = alto, bebop, dies 1955 before Coltrane's fame.
Lester YoungYoung (nickname "Prez") is the tenor stylist of the swing era who influenced Parker; cool, light tone. Don't swap "Bird" (alto, bebop) for "Prez" (tenor, swing).
Cannonball AdderleyAnother major alto saxophonist deeply influenced by Parker — but Adderley is hard-bop/soul-jazz of the late 1950s–60s ("Mercy, Mercy, Mercy," Kind of Blue). Parker predates him.
Ornette ColemanAlso alto sax, also bebop-rooted Texas/L.A. — but Coleman is the free jazz radical (The Shape of Jazz to Come, 1959). Parker is bebop's founder, not its avant-garde successor.

Cross-discipline big picture Parker is the hinge between swing and modern jazz: his harmonic vocabulary (upper extensions, substitutions, contrafacts) became the grammar that Miles Davis, John Coltrane, and the entire post-war scene built on. In quizbowl he bridges music history (bebop's birth on 52nd Street and at Minton's), American culture (the Beats idolized him; "Bird Lives" graffiti), and film (Eastwood's Bird). The "Bird/Yardbird → Birdland" naming chain and the contrafact concept ("Ko-Ko"/"Cherokee," "Ornithology"/"How High the Moon") are the most reliable buzz triggers.
