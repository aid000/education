Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Duchamp died in 1968; most works are still under copyright and appear as fair-use files on English Wikipedia. Images load via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection. If an image fails to load, the filename may differ slightly — swap it with the verified title from the image list.

NationalityFrench-American (born Blainville-Crevon, Normandy, 1887; died 1968)
PeriodEarly 20th century; active 1902–1968
MovementsDadaism, Conceptual art; early Cubism / Futurism
Signature concept"Readymade" — manufactured objects declared art
Alter egoRrose Sélavy (female alter ego; photos by Man Ray)
Late lifeLargely abandoned painting for chess after ~1923

Defining traits

  • Readymades: ordinary manufactured objects (a urinal, a bicycle wheel, a bottle rack) selected and designated as art — the idea, not craft, is the work.
  • Anti-retinal art: Duchamp explicitly rejected "purely visual / retinal" painting in favor of art that engages the intellect.
  • Wordplay & punning: titles and alter-ego names are elaborate puns — "Rrose Sélavy" = "Eros, c'est la vie" (French: "Eros, that's life").
  • The Large Glass is his magnum opus — a two-year-planned, never-"finished" mechanical allegory of desire.
  • Hugely influential on Dada, Fluxus, Pop Art, Conceptual art; often called the father of conceptual art.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • A urinal titled Fountain submitted under the alias "R. Mutt" — the single most-given-away Duchamp clue.
  • Bicycle wheel mounted on a stool — first readymade.
  • "Nude Descending a Staircase" — caused a sensation at the 1913 Armory Show.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 6 works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Fountain
1917 · readymade (porcelain urinal) · original lost; replicas at Tate Modern, Philadelphia Museum of Art, and others
recogAn inverted porcelain urinal signed "R. Mutt 1917" and submitted to the Society of Independent Artists exhibition (which rejected it).
recogThe original was photographed by Alfred Stieglitz at Gallery 291; the object itself was lost — all known versions are 1964 authorized replicas.
powerThe R. Mutt pseudonym riffs on the Mott Works plumbing manufacturer; Duchamp and Beatrice Wood defended the work in the journal The Blind Man. Consistently voted the most influential artwork of the 20th century in critical polls.

Nude Descending a Staircase, No. 2
1912 · oil on canvas · Philadelphia Museum of Art
recogA figure decomposed into overlapping, sequential amber-brown forms cascading down a staircase — motion depicted in a single static image.
recogCaused a scandal at the 1913 Armory Show in New York; a journalist dubbed it "an explosion in a shingle factory."
powerInitially rejected by the Cubist Puteaux Group (including his own brothers Villon and Duchamp-Villon) for its Futurist movement; Duchamp withdrew it from their 1912 Salon des Indépendants. The "No. 2" distinguishes it from the 1911 sketch version.
wiki ↗
The Bride Stripped Bare by Her Bachelors, Even (The Large Glass)
1915–1923 · oil, lead wire, dust on glass (two panels) · Philadelphia Museum of Art
recogTwo large panes of glass: upper panel = the "Bride" (a mechanical form); lower = the "Bachelor Apparatus" (nine malic molds, a chocolate grinder, glider).
recogLeft deliberately "unfinished" in 1923; cracked during transport in 1926 — Duchamp declared the cracks "fortuitous" and part of the work.
powerAccompanied by the Green Box (1934) — 94 facsimile notes Duchamp wrote while planning the work, intended to be read alongside the glass. Title is also written "La mariée mise à nu par ses célibataires, même"; the "even" is untranslatable and deliberate non-sense.
wiki ↗
Bicycle Wheel
1913 (original, lost) · readymade: bicycle wheel on a kitchen stool · replicas at MoMA and elsewhere
recogA bicycle wheel mounted upside-down on a white kitchen stool — the first readymade Duchamp made, though he did not use the term "readymade" until 1915.
recogDuchamp said he made it purely for visual pleasure — watching it spin; he did not initially consider it art.
powerPredates all other readymades; Duchamp later said it was like a "fireplace one contemplates." The 1951 third version (at MoMA) is the most reproduced; original Paris version destroyed. Sets up the "assisted readymade" subcategory.
wiki ↗
L.H.O.O.Q.
1919 · pencil on postcard reproduction · multiple versions
recogA cheap postcard reproduction of the Mona Lisa to which Duchamp drew a mustache and goatee in pencil.
recogTitle is a French phonetic pun: when the letters are read aloud in French ("elle a chaud au cul"), it means "she has a hot ass."
powerA prime example of a "rectified readymade" — a mass-produced image slightly altered. Duchamp made a follow-up in 1965 called L.H.O.O.Q. Rasée (the same postcard without the mustache, labeled "shaved").
wiki ↗
Étant donnés (Given: 1. The Waterfall, 2. The Illuminating Gas)
1946–1966 · mixed media assemblage · Philadelphia Museum of Art
recogViewers peek through two small holes in a wooden door to see a nude female figure lying on twigs, holding a gas lamp, with a painted landscape behind.
recogWorked on secretly for 20 years while the world believed Duchamp had abandoned art; revealed only after his death in 1968.
powerBased on a Spanish wooden door Duchamp had shipped from Cadaqués; the nude figure is modeled on Maria Martins (his lover, a Brazilian sculptor). The title mirrors the subtitle of The Large Glass, connecting the two as a diptych.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Readymade vocabulary

  • Readymade — unmodified found/manufactured object declared as art
  • Assisted readymade — slightly modified (Bicycle Wheel, L.H.O.O.Q.)
  • Rectified readymade — mass reproduction with added marks (L.H.O.O.Q.)
  • Term "readymade" coined by Duchamp ~1915 in New York
  • Bottle Rack (1914) — first unassisted readymade

Key people & connections

  • Rrose Sélavy — female alter ego; photographed by Man Ray
  • Brothers: painters Jacques Villon and sculptor Raymond Duchamp-Villon
  • Companion and collaborator: Man Ray, Francis Picabia
  • Patron: Katherine Dreier (co-founded the Société Anonyme with Duchamp)
  • Walter Arensberg — major collector; his collection now at Philadelphia
  • Lover and model for Étant donnés: Maria Martins

Concepts & terms

  • Anti-retinal — art that engages intellect, not just the eye
  • The Green Box (1934) — 94 notes explaining The Large Glass
  • Infrathin (inframince) — imperceptible difference (smoke smell on a coat)
  • "R. Mutt" pseudonym on Fountain
  • Society of Independent Artists — rejected Fountain in 1917
  • Armory Show, 1913 — Nude Descending causes sensation

"Also did" trivia

  • Became a professional chess player after ~1923; represented France in Chess Olympiads
  • Co-founded the Société Anonyme (first American museum of modern art) with Katherine Dreier and Man Ray, 1920
  • Compiled the Boîte-en-valise ("Box in a Suitcase," 1935–41) — portable museum of 68 miniature replicas of his own works
  • Famous last words via telegram: "Always, always towards the future. The return to the past is impossible." (disputed apocryphal)
  • Notable quote: "I have forced myself to contradict myself in order to avoid conforming to my own taste."

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Man RayMan Ray is the photographer / Dadaist famous for Rayographs (cameraless photograms), the flatiron with tacks (Gift), and the Lips / Observatory Time. He was Duchamp's close collaborator and photographer of Rrose Sélavy — a collaborator, not the same person.
Francis PicabiaAlso Dada/anti-art; known for machine drawings (spark plugs as portraits) and the journal 291. Picabia has a wilder, more decorative surface; Duchamp is drier and more conceptual.
John CageCage applied Duchamp's "chance operations" philosophy to music (4′33″). If the question is about sound or silence, it's Cage; if about visual objects or chess, it's Duchamp.
Kurt SchwittersMerz collage — assemblages of urban debris, trash, and ticket stubs. More painterly and emotional than Duchamp's cool conceptualism; Schwitters was excluded from Berlin Dada.
Joseph CornellBoxes with found objects (stars, maps, birds) arranged in shadow boxes — lyrical, nostalgic. Cornell's boxes are aesthetically beautiful; Duchamp's readymades are intentionally indifferent to aesthetics.
Jasper JohnsJohns admired Duchamp and used found/everyday subjects (flags, targets, numbers), but his works are painted in thick encaustic — not readymades. Johns is Neo-Dada / early Conceptual; he was also Duchamp's chess opponent.

♟️ Cross-discipline big picture Duchamp's legacy branches into every category: chess (he became a master-level player, a recurring trivia point in sports/games rounds); philosophy of art (his "institutional theory" — art is whatever the art world designates — underpins analytic aesthetics); music (John Cage's 4′33″ is the direct sonic equivalent of the readymade); and literature (his wordplay, puns, and the "infrathin" concept influenced Oulipo and language poetry). On a visual-arts tossup, Duchamp almost always comes via a concept (readymade, anti-retinal) rather than a visual image — train on the ideas, not just the pictures.
