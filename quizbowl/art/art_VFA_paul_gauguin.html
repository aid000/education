Quick Overview

📷 Image note: Gauguin died in 1903; his works are in the public domain and available on Wikimedia Commons. Images are loaded via Wikipedia's Special:FilePath redirect and require an internet connection. If an image fails to load, the filename likely differs slightly on Wikimedia — titles are best-guess plausible names.

NationalityFrench (born Paris, 1848–1903)
PeriodPost-Impressionism; active 1870s–1903
MovementPost-Impressionism; founder of Synthetism / Cloisonnism
Key localesPont-Aven (Brittany), Arles (with Van Gogh), Tahiti, Marquesas Islands
Signature styleBold outlines (cloisonnism), flat color areas, primitivism, spiritual symbolism
Key influenceJapanese woodblock prints, Polynesian art; influenced Fauvism and Expressionism

Defining traits

  • Cloisonnism / Synthetism: thick dark outlines enclosing flat, unmodulated color fields — like stained glass or enamel cloisonné.
  • Anti-naturalist color: grass pink, sea red, skin yellow-orange — color chosen for emotional/symbolic resonance, not optical accuracy.
  • Primitivist ideology: fled "civilized" Paris for Brittany, then Tahiti, seeking a purer spiritual world; paintings blend Polynesian subjects with Western allegory.
  • Recurring figures: Tahitian women in landscape, animals (dogs, horses), Polynesian idols (often the fictional "Hina" or "Oviri" goddess).
  • Biographical legend: abandoned a successful stockbroker career and his Danish wife Mette-Sophie to paint; died poor and syphilitic in the Marquesas.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Any painting of Tahitian or Polynesian women with flat color + dark outlines → likely Gauguin.
  • "Where Do We Come From? What Are We? Where Are We Going?" — his monumental philosophical frieze; title alone is the giveaway.
  • The ear-cutting incident with Van Gogh in Arles — both men are co-answers; Gauguin was present, Van Gogh did it.
  • The fictional ceramic sculpture Oviri (the "Savage") and the concept of "primitivism."

Vision After the Sermon (Jacob Wrestling with the Angel)
1888 · oil on canvas · Scottish National Gallery, Edinburgh
recogBreton women in white headdresses (coiffes) pray in the foreground; in the background, Jacob and the angel wrestle on a flat vermilion red field.

powerKey early statement of Synthetism / Cloisonnism — the red ground is anti-naturalistic; Gauguin offered it to two Breton churches, both refused it. Painted at Pont-Aven, Brittany.

The Yellow Christ (Le Christ jaune)
1889 · oil on canvas · Albright-Knox Art Gallery, Buffalo
recogA bright yellow Christ on a crucifix in an autumn Breton landscape, flanked by three Breton peasant women in coiffes.
recogThe autumn landscape uses flat zones of orange, yellow, and green; no shadows; hills and houses in background.
powerBased on a polychrome wooden crucifix in the Tremalo chapel near Pont-Aven; the yellow skin is a symbolic rather than naturalistic choice — Gauguin included it in his own Self-Portrait with Yellow Christ.

Spirit of the Dead Watching (Manao Tupapau)
1892 · oil on burlap · Albright-Knox Art Gallery, Buffalo
recogA young Tahitian woman (Teha'amana, Gauguin's vahine) lies prone and nude on a bed, staring out in fear.
recogA hooded dark spirit (tupapau) sits at the foot of the bed; background filled with purple and white floral motifs.
powerDirectly quotes Manet's Olympia (prone nude + dark background figure) but inverts the power dynamic — the model is terrified, not composed. Gauguin described it in his memoir Noa Noa.

Ia Orana Maria (Hail Mary)
1891 · oil on canvas · Metropolitan Museum of Art, New York
recogA Tahitian Madonna and Child — the Virgin in yellow pareo, Child on her shoulder; two Tahitian women pray in the foreground.
recogAn angel with golden wings stands at left; the lush background is tropical foliage.
powerTitle in Tahitian = "Hail Mary"; Gauguin adapted the composition from a Javanese temple frieze photograph he owned — one of his first major Tahitian paintings, now at the Met.

Nevermore
1897 · oil on canvas · Courtauld Gallery, London
recogA nude Tahitian woman on a bed; a dark bird (raven) perches in the upper left, alluding to Poe's poem.
recogThe title "Nevermore" is inscribed at upper left; two figures whisper in the background.
powerGauguin wrote: "I wished to suggest by means of a simple nude a certain long-lost barbarian luxury." The raven is not Poe's literal raven but a "bird of the devil that keeps watch" — held at the Courtauld.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Visual motifs

  • Flat color zones + bold dark outlines (cloisonnism)
  • Anti-naturalistic color: red grass, pink sand, yellow skin
  • Breton coiffes (white headdresses) in pre-Tahiti works
  • Tahitian women — seated, reclining, profile, or frontal
  • Polynesian idols (Hina, Oviri) in landscape background
  • Diagonal tree / branch dividing pictorial planes

Key people & places

  • Pont-Aven, Brittany — first "primitivist" retreat, 1886–91
  • Arles — lived with Van Gogh Oct–Dec 1888; ear-cutting incident
  • Tahiti (Papeete → Mataiea) — 1891–93, 1895–1901
  • Marquesas Islands (Atuona, Hiva Oa) — died 1903
  • Teha'amana — 13-year-old Tahitian vahine; model for Manao Tupapau
  • Emile Bernard — fellow Synthetist at Pont-Aven; joint-credited cloisonnism

Techniques & terms

  • Cloisonnism — outlines + flat enamel-like color fields
  • Synthetism — fusing nature, artist's feelings, form
  • Primitivism — deliberate rejection of "civilization"
  • Influenced by Japanese woodblocks (ukiyo-e), Javanese art
  • Influenced later: Fauvism (Matisse, Derain) and German Expressionism
  • Medium variety: oil, woodblock prints (Noa Noa suite), ceramics (Oviri)

Biographical hard clues

  • Born Paris, 1848; Peruvian mother; spent childhood in Lima, Peru
  • Worked as a Paris stockbroker until ~1883; abandoned family to paint
  • Wife: Mette-Sophie Gad (Danish); five children
  • Memoir: Noa Noa (Tahitian journal/collaboration with Charles Morice)
  • Died May 8, 1903, Atuona, Marquesas, of syphilis and heart failure
  • Late series of anti-colonial newspaper: Le Sourire in Marquesas

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Paul CézanneBoth Post-Impressionists, both friends with Pissarro. But Cézanne = geometric, structural facets (Mont Sainte-Victoire, Card Players), Provence landscapes — no Polynesian subjects, no symbolic color. Gauguin = flat outline + symbolic primitivism.
Vincent van GoghBoth "Post-Impressionists," lived together in Arles 1888. Van Gogh = swirling brushstroke, impasto, emotional Northern European subjects (Starry Night, Sunflowers, Potato Eaters). Gauguin = smooth flat zones, Tahitian figures, dark outlines. Van Gogh cut the ear; Gauguin was the one who left.
Henri MatisseMatisse is a later Fauvist directly influenced by Gauguin's flat color. Tell apart by era (Matisse 1905+) and subject (European interiors, odalisques, cut-outs). If the question says "influenced by Gauguin" → Matisse is the answer, not Gauguin himself.
Emile BernardCo-inventor of cloisonnism at Pont-Aven — disputed priority with Gauguin. Bernard = less famous, same style. Gauguin tended to overshadow Bernard; if the question asks who moved to Tahiti or who made the Vision After the Sermon, it's Gauguin.
Henri Rousseau (Le Douanier)Both painted "primitive" lush jungle scenes. Rousseau = self-taught naïf, flat linear style, Parisian fantasies of jungle (never left Europe). Gauguin = formally trained, actually lived in Polynesia. Rousseau's jungles have more uniform green palette and folk-art stiffness.
Édouard ManetGauguin deliberately quoted Olympia in Manao Tupapau. Manet = Impressionism precursor, Parisian modernity, crisp paint handling. Gauguin's "Tahitian Olympia" is the answer when the clue says "responds to Manet's nude."
