Quick Overview

Unfinished portrait by Joseph Lange, c. 1789–90
Full NameJohannes Chrysostomus Wolfgangus Theophilus Mozart
Born / DiedSalzburg, 1756 – Vienna, 1791 (age 35)
PeriodClassical period (ca. 1750–1820)
Patron / employerArchbishop Colloredo of Salzburg (fled 1781); then Emperor Joseph II in Vienna
Output626 works catalogued by Ludwig von Köchel (K. / KV numbers)
FamilyFather Leopold Mozart (violinist/teacher); sister Nannerl (also prodigy)

Defining traits

  • Child prodigy: composing minuets at age 5, performing for European courts on tour with Leopold.
  • Mastered virtually every genre: opera, symphony, concerto, chamber music, sacred music, keyboard works.
  • Melodies of extraordinary clarity and naturalness combined with sophisticated counterpoint.
  • Died at 35 leaving the Requiem unfinished; legend of a "gray messenger" commissioning it anonymously (Count Walsegg).
  • Buried in a common grave in Vienna; exact location unknown.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "This Salzburg-born composer" or "this composer born in 1756" — points to Mozart.
  • Köchel numbers (K.) attached to any work — immediately Mozart.
  • Unfinished Requiem and the mysterious gray-clad messenger.
  • Operas with librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte (Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così).

Don Giovanni, K. 527
1787 · opera in two acts · Prague première; libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte
recogLibertine Don Juan is dragged to hell by the stone statue of the Commendatore he murdered in Act I.
recogServant Leporello sings the "Catalogue Aria" listing Don Giovanni's 1,003 conquests in Spain alone.
powerPremièred in Prague, 1787; called a dramma giocoso mixing comedy and tragedy. The overture opens with the D-minor chord later associated with the statue scene. One of Da Ponte's three Mozart operas.

The Marriage of Figaro (Le nozze di Figaro), K. 492
1786 · opera buffa · Vienna; libretto by Lorenzo Da Ponte after Beaumarchais
recogServant Figaro and Susanna outwit Count Almaviva, who tries to exercise the droit du seigneur on Susanna.
recogContains the aria "Non più andrai" (Figaro to Cherubino) and the Countess's "Porgi, amor" and "Dove sono."
powerBased on Beaumarchais's controversial play (politically censored in Vienna); Mozart convinced Emperor Joseph II to allow it. The overture is a standard orchestral showpiece. Part of Da Ponte trilogy.

Symphony No. 40 in G minor, K. 550
1788 · one of the "final three" symphonies written in six weeks
recogInstantly recognizable G-minor opening theme in the violins — the most famous symphonic incipit in Classical music.
recogOne of only two Mozart symphonies in a minor key; the other is No. 25 (also G minor).
powerWritten in the summer of 1788 together with Nos. 39 and 41 (Jupiter) — Mozart's last three symphonies, all composed within six weeks, with no known première during his lifetime for No. 40.

Symphony No. 41 in C major ("Jupiter"), K. 551
1788 · last symphony Mozart ever wrote
recog"Jupiter" nickname (coined by impresario Johann Peter Salomon, not Mozart); finale features a five-subject fugue combining four themes simultaneously.
recogFinale's main theme uses the four-note "Jupiter motif" (C–D–F–E), which appears throughout Mozart's output and in earlier works by others.
powerThe finale's stretto combining five themes is considered one of the greatest feats of Classicalcounterpoint. Nickname bestowed in London; Mozart never heard the piece performed (possibly).
wiki ↗
Requiem in D minor, K. 626
1791 (unfinished) · completed by Franz Xaver Süssmayr
recogLeft unfinished at Mozart's death; completed by his pupil Franz Xaver Süssmayr. Movements from the Lacrimosa onward are mostly Süssmayr.
recogCommissioned anonymously by Count Franz von Walsegg, who intended to pass it off as his own. The "gray messenger" legend grew from Mozart's anxiety about the anonymous patron.
powerThe Lacrimosa breaks off after eight bars in Mozart's hand. The Introitus and Kyrie fugue are entirely Mozart's; the Dies Irae sequence and Confutatis are substantially his. Depicted in Miloš Forman's film Amadeus (1984).
wiki ↗
Piano Concerto No. 21 in C major, K. 467
1785 · nicknamed "Elvira Madigan" after a 1967 Swedish film
recogSlow movement (Andante in F major) used as the soundtrack to the 1967 film Elvira Madigan — hence the popular nickname.
recogThe Andante's triplet-figure accompaniment under a singing solo line is one of the most recognizable moments in any piano concerto.
powerWritten in February 1785 — the same year as the Marriage of Figaro. Mozart performed the première himself in Vienna; his father Leopold was in the audience and wrote admiringly home about it.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Biographical hard clues

  • Born 27 January 1756, Salzburg (then Prince-Archbishopric of Salzburg)
  • Father Leopold Mozart — author of Versuch einer gründlichen Violinschule (violin method)
  • Resigned (was "kicked out") from service of Archbishop Hieronymus Colloredo, 1781
  • Married Constanze Weber, 1782 (sister of soprano Aloysia, whom Mozart had loved first)
  • Died 5 December 1791; rival composer Antonio Salieri falsely accused in later legend (no evidence)
  • Buried in St. Marx Cemetery, Vienna, in a common grave
  • Depicted in Miloš Forman's Amadeus (1984), screenplay by Peter Shaffer

Key collaborators & patrons

  • Librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte — Figaro, Don Giovanni, Così fan tutte (the "Da Ponte trilogy")
  • Clarinetist Anton Stadler — wrote Clarinet Concerto and Clarinet Quintet for him
  • Librettist Emanuel Schikaneder — The Magic Flute (also the original Papageno)
  • Patron Baron van Swieten — introduced Mozart to Bach and Handel counterpoint
  • Teacher Joseph Haydn — mutual admiration; Mozart dedicated six quartets to Haydn

Key terms & numbers

  • Köchel catalogue (K. / KV) — Ludwig von Köchel's 1862 chronological catalogue; 626 works
  • Opera seria vs. opera buffa vs. Singspiel — Mozart mastered all three
  • "Too many notes" — apocryphal Joseph II quip about Die Entführung
  • Masonic symbolism — Magic Flute; Mozart was a Freemason (lodge "Zur Wohltätigkeit")
  • Sturm und Drang influence — early G-minor works (Syms. 25 & 40)
  • "Final three symphonies" — Nos. 39, 40, 41 (all 1788)

Opera characters (frequent clues)

  • Leporello — Don Giovanni's servant; sings "Catalogue Aria" (Madamina)
  • Figaro / Susanna — servant couple, Marriage of Figaro
  • Cherubino — young page, sung by mezzo-soprano (trouser role)
  • Queen of the Night — Magic Flute; sings two famous high coloratura arias
  • Pamina / Tamino / Papageno — Magic Flute main trio
  • Donna Anna / Donna Elvira / Zerlina — Don Giovanni women trio

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Joseph HaydnHaydn is older (born 1732) and lived to 1809; mentor to Mozart. Symphonies Nos. 1–104 are Haydn's ("London" and "Paris" symphonies, "Surprise," "Clock," "Drumroll"). No operas rivaling Mozart's. String quartet form largely Haydn's invention.
Ludwig van BeethovenBeethoven starts where Mozart ends — born 1770, briefly studied with Haydn. First Symphony 1800; heroic middle period (Eroica, Fifth, Ninth), late quartets. Much more dramatic, developmental, larger-scale than Mozart.
Antonio SalieriSame era, same Vienna court; wrote operas (including Falstaff). Salieri is the narrative villain in Amadeus but the poisoning story is fictional. Salieri's music is competent but less canonical. Clue: Salieri vs. Mozart rivalry is itself a frequent tossup answer.
Christoph Willibald GluckGluck reformed opera seria in the 1760s–70s (Orfeo ed Euridice, Alceste); older (1714–1787). Mozart admired him. Gluck's operas are leaner, Classical-reform; not the Da Ponte comedies.
George Frideric HandelHandel is Baroque (1685–1759), died before Mozart composed anything significant. Oratorios (Messiah), Italian operas, English anthems — not Classical symphonies or concertos.
Johann Sebastian BachBaroque (1685–1750); Bach died when Mozart was 6. Counterpoint king, but complex, polyphonic. Baron van Swieten introduced Mozart to Bach; Mozart arranged Bach fugues. Answer "Bach" = Well-Tempered Clavier, St. Matthew Passion, Brandenburg Concertos.

🎵 Cross-discipline big picture Mozart sits at the center of several tossup webs: his Da Ponte operas connect to literary history (Beaumarchais, Don Juan legend, commedia dell'arte); the Requiem connects to sacred choral music and the competing Süssmayr attribution debate; Salieri is both a musical answer and a historical-drama one (Amadeus); the Köchel catalogue connects to musicology. The "Classical period" framing (ca. 1750–1820) is itself a periodization clue — Mozart bridges the late Baroque via Haydn and anticipates Beethoven's Romantic gestures in works like the D-minor Piano Concerto (K. 466).
