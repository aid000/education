Quick Overview

TraditionNorse / Germanic mythology
RoleAllfather; chief of the Aesir gods
Germanic nameWoden / Wotan (Old English / Old High German)
Primary sourcesPoetic Edda, Prose Edda (Snorri Sturluson, c. 1220)
Calendar linkWednesday = "Woden's day" (Old English Wodnesdæg)
HallValhalla in Asgard; throne Hlidskjalf

Defining traits

  • One-eyed — sacrificed an eye at Mimir's well for cosmic wisdom.
  • God of wisdom, war, death, poetry, magic, and runic knowledge.
  • Wanders disguised as a hooded, one-eyed old man with a wide-brimmed hat and staff.
  • Hanged himself on Yggdrasil for nine nights, wounded by his own spear, to discover the runes.
  • Collects the heroically slain (Einherjar) via the Valkyries to prepare for Ragnarok.
  • Dies at Ragnarok swallowed by the wolf Fenrir; avenged by his son Vidar.

Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • One eye + the wide-brimmed hat — the single most given-away visual pair.
  • Ravens Huginn and Muninn ("Thought" and "Memory") perched on his shoulders.
  • Spear Gungnir; eight-legged horse Sleipnir.

High-Yield Myths & Episodes

The myths most likely to be the question's subject. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / mid-level clue.

The Sacrifice at Mimir's Well
Source: Völuspá (Poetic Edda) · Domain: wisdom, sacrifice
powerOdin approaches Mimir, guardian of the well beneath Yggdrasil's second root, and offers his left eye as the price for a single drink of wisdom. Mimir preserves the eye in the well.
recogMimir's head is later preserved by Odin after the Aesir–Vanir War; Odin continues consulting it for counsel.
recogThe act epitomizes Odin as the god willing to pay any cost for knowledge — one eye trades for universal wisdom.
Hanging on Yggdrasil / Discovery of the Runes
Source: Hávamál (Poetic Edda, stanzas 138–141) · Domain: runes, self-sacrifice
powerThe poem Hávamál ("Sayings of the High One") contains Odin's first-person account: he hung on Yggdrasil for nine nights, wounded by his own spear Gungnir, neither given food nor drink, as a sacrifice "offered to himself."
recogAt the end of nine nights he cries out, seizes the runes, and falls — gaining mastery of runic magic (galdr) and the eighteen spells listed afterward.
recogOften compared to Christ's crucifixion in academic writing; key distinction is Odin's act is self-directed and for knowledge, not atonement.
Creation of the World / Slaying Ymir
Source: Gylfaginning (Prose Edda); Völuspá · Domain: cosmogony
powerOdin and his brothers Vili and Ve slay the primordial frost-giant Ymir, whose body becomes the world: flesh → earth, blood → seas, bones → mountains, skull → sky, brain → clouds.
recogThey then shape the first humans, Ask and Embla, from two trees (ash and elm), with Odin giving önd (breath/spirit), Vili giving sense, Ve giving appearance.
recogYmir's maggots become the dwarves; the world-tree Yggdrasil is the cosmic axis connecting nine realms.
The Mead of Poetry
Source: Skáldskaparmál (Prose Edda) · Domain: poetry, cunning
powerThe mead is made from the blood of Kvasir (a being of supreme wisdom, born from the Aesir–Vanir peace agreement) mixed with honey by the dwarves Fjalarr and Galarr. It grants poetic inspiration to all who drink it.
recogOdin disguises himself, seduces the giant Gunnlod (daughter of Suttungr) over three nights, drains all three vats (Odroerir, Bodn, Son), then escapes as an eagle, spitting the mead into vessels in Asgard.
recogThe mead he spills while fleeing becomes bad poetry — "the rhymester's share." His gift of poetry to humanity is called the Odroerir (or the "mead's gift").
Ragnarok — Death by Fenrir
Source: Völuspá; Gylfaginning · Domain: eschatology, fate
powerVöluspá prophesies that at Ragnarok, Odin is swallowed whole by the wolf Fenrir (son of Loki and the giantess Angrboda). His son Vidar avenges him by either stabbing Fenrir in the palate with a massive shoe/boot or tearing its jaws apart.
recogOdin's foreknowledge of Ragnarok (obtained from the völva in Völuspá and from Mimir's head) drives his obsessive collection of warriors (Einherjar) in Valhalla — all preparation for the final battle.
recogOther key Ragnarok pairings: Thor vs. Jormungandr (Midgard Serpent); Freyr vs. Surt; Tyr vs. Garm; Loki vs. Heimdall (mutual death).
The Völuspá ("Prophecy of the Völva")
Source: Völuspá (Poetic Edda, first poem) · Domain: cosmogony, eschatology
powerOdin raises a dead völva (seeress) and compels her to recount the world's history from creation to Ragnarok and the eventual renewal (ginnunga). The poem is the single most important primary source for Norse cosmology.
recogThe Völuspá opens with the seeress demanding silence: "Hljóðs bið ek allar..." ("Silence I ask of all..."). The refrain "Vituð ér enn, eða hvat?" ("Do you know yet, or what?") punctuates the prophecy.
recogOdin's name for himself in the Völuspá is Valföðr ("Father of the Slain") — a key epithet clue.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Less-common myths and episodes that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

Odin & Frigg's sons
Sons include Thor (with Jord), Baldr and Hodr (with Frigg), Vidar (with Grid), Vali (with Rind).
The Lay of Vafthrudnir
Odin contests a wisdom-flyting with the giant Vafthrudnir; wins by asking what Odin whispered to Baldr on his funeral pyre — the giant cannot know.
Grímnismál ("Sayings of Grimnir")
Odin, disguised as Grimnir, is tortured between two fires by King Geirrod; recites cosmological knowledge before revealing himself.
The Hávamál wisdom stanzas
Lengthy collection of gnomic verse attributed to Odin; includes the Loddfáfnismál and the famous stanza on death ("cattle die, kinsmen die…").
Aesir–Vanir War
Odin initiates the first war by hurling his spear Gungnir over the Vanir host; ends with hostage exchange of Mimir and Hoenir.
Odin and Rind (Saxo Grammaticus)
Odin pursues and deceives the mortal princess Rind to beget Vali, the avenger of Baldr's death.
The Wild Hunt
In Germanic folk tradition, Odin (Woden) leads the phantom Wild Hunt across the sky in winter; precursor to Santa Claus lore (eight-legged Sleipnir → reindeer).
Seiðr magic
Odin learned seiðr (shamanic magic involving fate-manipulation) from the Vanir goddess Freyja; considered unmanly (ergi) for a male god.
Odin's names/heiti
The Prose Edda lists over 200 names; common in tossups: Allfather, Ygg, Har, Jafnhar, Thridi, Grimnir, Wanderer, Bolverk, Oski.
The Lay of Sigrdrifa
Odin places a sleep-thorn in the Valkyrie Brynhildr/Sigrdrifa and surrounds her with a fire-wall; she teaches Sigurd the hero runic lore.
The Sword Gram / Volsung Saga
Odin appears disguised at the Volsung feast and drives a sword (Gram) into the tree Barnstokkr; only Sigmund can pull it free.
Odin and Loki
Odin and Loki are sworn blood-brothers; Odin vowed never to drink unless Loki was served too — referenced in Lokasenna.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Possessions & companions

  • Gungnir — his never-missing spear (made by the dwarves Sons of Ivaldi)
  • Sleipnir — eight-legged horse, son of Loki and the stallion Svadilfari
  • Huginn & Muninn — ravens "Thought" and "Memory"; fly the world daily, report to Odin
  • Geri & Freki — his two wolves; he feeds them all his food (drinks only wine)
  • Draupnir — gold arm-ring that drips eight identical rings every ninth night
  • Hlidskjalf — throne from which he sees all nine worlds

Key epithets (heiti)

  • Allfather (Alfaðir) — father of gods and men
  • Valföðr — "Father of the Slain"
  • Ygg / Yggr — "The Terrible"; gives Yggdrasil its name
  • Har, Jafnhar, Thridi — "High, Just-As-High, Third" (three aspects in Prose Edda)
  • Bolverk — "Evil-Doer" (disguise during Mead of Poetry quest)
  • Grimnir — "The Hooded/Masked One"
  • Wanderer / Gangleri — wandering disguise

Key places & concepts

  • Valhalla — hall of the slain; roof of shields, rafters of spears
  • Valkyries — choosers of the slain (notable: Brynhildr, Göndul, Skuld)
  • Einherjar — the warrior dead who feast and fight in Valhalla
  • Yggdrasil — world-tree (ash); "Ygg's horse" = gallows = Odin's hanging
  • Mimir's well — at Yggdrasil's root beneath Jotunheim; well of wisdom
  • Nine realms — Asgard, Midgard, Jotunheim, Helheim, Niflheim, Muspelheim, Vanaheim, Alfheim, Svartalfheim

Primary sources & modern reception

  • Poetic Edda — Völuspá, Hávamál, Vafþrúðnismál, Grímnismál, Sigrdrífumál (most Odin-heavy poems)
  • Prose Edda — Snorri Sturluson, c. 1220 CE; Gylfaginning is the primary cosmological narrative
  • Gesta Danorum — Saxo Grammaticus (Latin; Odin = euhemerized king)
  • Wagner's Der Ring des Nibelungen — Odin as Wotan; major opera-category crossover
  • Neil Gaiman's American Gods (2001) — Odin as "Mr. Wednesday" (Wednesday = Woden's day)
  • J.R.R. Tolkien's Gandalf — grey wanderer with staff and hat, modeled on Odin

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Zeus / JupiterBoth are king of the gods, but Zeus is never one-eyed or self-sacrificial. Zeus rules via thunderbolt and authority; Odin rules via accumulated wisdom. Zeus doesn't hang on a tree. Zeus → Greek/Roman; Odin → Norse/Germanic.
ThorThor is Odin's son, not his equal. Thor = red-bearded, hammer Mjolnir, god of thunder, storms, strength; fights giants directly. Odin is subtle and intellectual; Thor is brute force. Thor is more popular among commoners; Odin is the aristocratic/poetic god.
TyrTyr is the Norse god of law and justice (cognate with Latin Deus / Greek Zeus etymologically). Tyr sacrificed his right hand to bind Fenrir. Odin is associated with war as fate/cunning; Tyr is associated with war as law/oaths.
LokiLoki is a trickster and shapeshifter, not a chief god. Loki and Odin are blood-brothers. Loki is the father of Fenrir, Hel, and Jormungandr (and the mother of Sleipnir). Loki ends up bound until Ragnarok; Odin remains chief of Asgard.
MimirMimir is the guardian of the well of wisdom — a separate figure whom Odin consults. Mimir is not Odin; Odin is the supplicant. Do not confuse Mimir's well (wisdom) with Urd's well (fate, beneath Yggdrasil's first root).
Freyr / FreyFreyr is a Vanir god (not Aesir), associated with fertility, sunshine, rain, and the harvest. His sister is Freyja. He dies at Ragnarok fighting Surt, having given away his magic sword. Completely different domain from Odin's.
BaldrBaldr is Odin's beloved son — the god of beauty, light, and purity. He is killed by the mistletoe dart guided by Loki through the blind god Hodr. Baldr's death triggers the chain leading to Ragnarok. Do not confuse Baldr with Odin.
Woden vs. WotanSame deity: Woden = Old English form (Anglo-Saxon England); Wotan = Old High German / Wagner's Ring cycle. All are the same as Norse Odin (Old Norse).

Cross-discipline connections Odin bridges multiple tossup categories: Germanic linguistics (Wednesday = Wodnesdæg; the word "berserk" comes from the animal-spirit warriors/berserkers who fought under Odin's protection); literature (Tolkien's Gandalf, Gaiman's Mr. Wednesday, Longfellow's Tegnér's Drapa); opera (Wagner's Wotan in the Ring cycle — Odin can be the correct answer in music tossups); anthropology (Odin / Mercurius equation: Caesar and Tacitus identify Woden with Mercury because both are associated with eloquence and the dead — a cross-cultural syncretism clue); and comparative religion (Odin's self-hanging is frequently juxtaposed with shamanic initiation and with Christ's crucifixion in religion/mythology courses).
