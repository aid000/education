Quick Overview

Full nameJohn Maynard Keynes, 1st Baron Keynes
Dates1883–1946; died of heart disease
NationalityBritish (Cambridge, England)
Core ideaAggregate demand drives output; government spending can stabilize recessions
Magnum opusThe General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money (1936)
SchoolKeynesian economics (macroeconomics; breaks with classical laissez-faire)

Defining traits

  • Aggregate demand — total spending (C + I + G + NX) determines output; recessions = demand shortfalls, not natural equilibria.
  • The multiplier effect — a unit of government spending ripples through the economy, creating more than one unit of GDP.
  • "Animal spirits" — his term for irrational waves of investor confidence that drive booms and busts.
  • Liquidity trap — at very low interest rates, monetary policy loses traction; only fiscal policy works.
  • Sticky wages & prices — wages resist downward movement, so markets do not self-correct as classical theory predicts.
  • Member of the Bloomsbury Group (Virginia Woolf, Lytton Strachey); married ballet dancer Lydia Lopokova.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "In the long run, we are all dead" — most quoted Keynes line.
  • Author of The General Theory (1936) — the single most given-away identifier.
  • Led the British delegation at Bretton Woods (1944).
  • Criticized the Versailles peace terms in The Economic Consequences of the Peace (1919).

High-Yield Ideas & Works (Very Common)

The key theories, texts, and events most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early clue. recog = recognition / mid-clue.

The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money
1936 · book · Cambridge / Macmillan
recogArgues that aggregate demand, not supply, determines overall output and employment levels.
recogIntroduces the consumption function, the multiplier, and the concept of liquidity preference.
powerAttacks the Treasury View and Say's Law ("supply creates its own demand"); argues markets need not clear in the short run. Dedicated to "the struggle of escape from habitual modes of thought."

The Economic Consequences of the Peace
1919 · book
recogCondemns the reparations clauses of the Treaty of Versailles as ruinously large, predicting European economic collapse.
recogFeatures devastating character sketches of Wilson, Lloyd George, and Clemenceau at the Paris Conference.
powerKeynes resigned as Treasury representative to the Paris Peace Conference in protest; this book made him internationally famous. Later seen as prophetic after the rise of Nazism.
wiki ↗
The Multiplier Effect
concept · formalized with Richard Kahn, 1931
recogEach dollar of government spending creates more than one dollar of GDP via rounds of consumption; the fiscal multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC).
recogMPC (marginal propensity to consume) — the fraction of additional income spent, not saved.
powerFirst clearly formulated by Keynes's student Richard Kahn in 1931 as the "employment multiplier"; Keynes incorporated it centrally into the General Theory. The paradox of thrift follows: individual saving is collectively harmful if everyone does it.
wiki ↗
Bretton Woods Conference
1944 · Bretton Woods, New Hampshire
recogKeynes led the UK delegation; his counterpart was US Treasury official Harry Dexter White.
recogCreated the IMF and the World Bank; established the postwar dollar-linked exchange-rate system.
powerKeynes proposed an international clearing union using a new currency called the bancor; the US rejected it in favor of a dollar-centric system. The Keynes plan would have penalized surplus countries, not just debtors.
wiki ↗
Liquidity Preference & the IS-LM Model
concept · IS-LM formalized by John Hicks, 1937
recogLiquidity preference: people demand money for transactions, precaution, and speculation; the speculative motive links money demand to interest rates.
recogLiquidity trap: at very low interest rates, further monetary easing is ineffective — fiscal policy is the only lever.
powerJohn Hicks formalized the General Theory into the IS-LM model (1937) — the workhorse of Keynesian macro. IS = investment-saving equilibrium; LM = liquidity preference-money supply equilibrium.
wiki ↗
"Animal Spirits" & Keynesian Psychology
concept · introduced in the General Theory, 1936
recog"Animal spirits" — Keynes's term for the spontaneous urge to act (invest, spend) driven by optimism or pessimism, not rational calculation.
recogExplains why investment is volatile and recessions are self-reinforcing: pessimism kills investment, which destroys jobs, which deepens pessimism.
powerTerm borrowed from Latin spiritus animalis; used alongside his argument that long-run economic forecasting requires "our innate urge to activity" since the mathematical expectation of the future is unknowable. George Akerlof and Robert Shiller later wrote Animal Spirits (2009) extending this.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key quotations

  • "In the long run, we are all dead." — Tract on Monetary Reform, 1923
  • "Animal spirits" — General Theory, 1936
  • "Practical men who believe themselves to be quite exempt from any intellectual influences are usually the slaves of some defunct economist." — General Theory
  • "The market can remain irrational longer than you can remain solvent." (commonly attributed; likely apocryphal)
  • "Words ought to be a little wild, for they are the assaults of thought on the unthinking." — Essays in Persuasion

Key concepts & terms

  • Aggregate demand (AD) = C + I + G + NX
  • Multiplier = 1 / (1 − MPC)
  • MPC (marginal propensity to consume)
  • MPS (marginal propensity to save) = 1 − MPC
  • Liquidity preference — demand for money over bonds
  • Liquidity trap — zero lower bound; monetary policy ineffective
  • Sticky wages — wages resist downward adjustment
  • Say's Law — what Keynes explicitly refuted
  • Effective demand — realized demand at output equilibrium

Institutions & associations

  • King's College, Cambridge — lifelong affiliation; bursar
  • Cambridge Apostles — elite intellectual secret society
  • UK Treasury — served WWI and WWII
  • IMF & World Bank — Bretton Woods institutions he helped create
  • Bloomsbury Group — literary-intellectual circle
  • The Economic Journal — edited 1911–1944
  • Royal Economic Society — secretary/president

Collaborators & interlocutors

  • Alfred Marshall — Cambridge mentor; Keynes diverged from him
  • A. C. Pigou — Cambridge colleague; Keynes criticized his "classical" theory
  • Richard Kahn — Cambridge student; formalized the multiplier
  • John Hicks — formalized General Theory as IS-LM (1937)
  • Alvin Hansen — American Keynesian; popularized liquidity trap / secular stagnation
  • Harry Dexter White — US counterpart at Bretton Woods
  • Friedrich Hayek — chief intellectual adversary; advocated laissez-faire over stimulus
  • Milton Friedman — later monetarist critic of Keynesian policy

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Friedrich HayekKeynes's great antagonist. Hayek: free markets self-correct; central planning leads to serfdom; business cycles from credit expansion. If the clue praises markets or attacks government intervention, it's Hayek, not Keynes. The two had a famous public debate in 1931–32.
Milton FriedmanFriedman: monetarist — the quantity of money, not fiscal policy, drives output. Blames the Great Depression on Fed monetary contraction. If the answer involves M1/M2, velocity of money, or the Phillips curve long-run vertical, lean Friedman.
Adam SmithSmith: 18th-century; founder of classical economics; Wealth of Nations (1776); invisible hand; division of labor. Keynes explicitly built against the classical tradition Smith founded.
Paul SamuelsonSamuelson: "neo-Keynesian synthesis" — merged Keynes with neoclassical micro; wrote the dominant postwar economics textbook (Economics, 1948). If clue mentions neoclassical synthesis or the standard undergraduate textbook, it's Samuelson.
Joseph SchumpeterSchumpeter: Austrian; "creative destruction"; entrepreneur-driven innovation cycles; contemporary of Keynes but separate tradition. If the answer involves innovation, entrepreneurs, creative destruction, it's Schumpeter.
Irving FisherFisher: American; quantity theory of money; debt-deflation theory; famous for predicting market stability just before 1929 crash. If clue mentions debt-deflation or the Fisher equation (i = r + π), lean Fisher.
John Kenneth GalbraithGalbraith: 20th-century American institutional economist; The Affluent Society (1958); critiqued consumer culture and corporate power. More sociological than Keynes; no formal model.

Cross-discipline big-picture Keynes sits at the intersection of economics, history, and political theory. In tossups on the Great Depression or New Deal, Keynesian fiscal stimulus is the policy answer. In questions on the post-WWII international order, Bretton Woods and the IMF trace back to Keynes. In philosophy of probability, his early A Treatise on Probability (1921) anticipates his later epistemic humility about unknowable long-run futures — the same uncertainty that underlies "animal spirits." His feud with Hayek defines the central axis of 20th-century macro debate, with Milton Friedman as a third vertex. Keynes is also a common answer in British literature / Bloomsbury bonus parts.
