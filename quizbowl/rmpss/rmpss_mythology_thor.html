Quick Overview

PantheonNorse (Germanic); Aesir tribe of gods
DomainThunder, storms, strength, oak trees, protection of mankind
ParentsSon of Odin (father) and Jord (earth goddess, mother)
WeaponMjolnir — short-handled hammer; returns when thrown
RealmThrudheim (also called Thrudvangar); hall named Bilskirnir
SourcesProse Edda (Snorri Sturluson), Poetic Edda (Thrymskviða, Hymiskviða, Lokasenna)
Roman equivalentJupiter / Iuppiter (Thursday = Þórsdagr → "Thor's day")
NemesisJormungandr (the Midgard Serpent) — fated to kill each other at Ragnarok

Defining traits

  • Red beard; immense physical size and appetite (eats whole oxen).
  • Rides a chariot pulled by two goats: Tanngrisnir ("teeth-barer") and Tanngnjóstr ("teeth-grinder") — thunder is the sound of the wheels.
  • Wears iron gauntlets (Járngreipr) to grip Mjolnir, and a strength-doubling belt (Megingjord).
  • Despite Aesir status, not a god of war — he protects farmers and common people, not warriors (Odin takes fallen warriors).
  • Wife: Sif (golden hair); stepson Ullr; sons Modi and Magni by giantess Jarnsaxa.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "This god's hammer" / "Mjolnir" — near-instant giveaway.
  • "This deity's name gives us Thursday (Þórsdagr)."
  • "This god killed the Midgard Serpent but staggered nine steps and died" (Ragnarok).

High-Yield Myths (Very Common)

The 5 myths / episodes most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / mid-question clue.

The Theft of Mjolnir — Thrymskviða
Source: Poetic Edda · Thor dressed as a bride
recogThrym, king of the giants (Jotnar), steals Mjolnir and demands Freya as a bride for its return.
recogLoki suggests Thor disguise himself as Freya; Thor attends the wedding feast dressed as a bride, with Loki as bridesmaid.
powerThor eats an entire ox, eight salmon, and three casks of mead at the feast; Loki explains "the bride's" appetite by saying Freya had not eaten in eight days from longing. When Mjolnir is placed in his lap as the bridal blessing, Thor seizes it and kills all the giants.

Thor vs. Jormungandr (Midgard Serpent)
Sources: Prose Edda, Poetic Edda — recurs in multiple episodes + Ragnarok
recogThor and Jormungandr are fated enemies — Ragnarok prophecy: Thor kills the serpent with Mjolnir but takes nine steps and falls dead from its venom.
recogEarlier encounter: fishing with the giant Hymir — Thor uses an ox head as bait, hauls up Jormungandr; Hymir cuts the line in fear before Thor can kill it.
powerIn the Hymiskviða, Thor rows Hymir's boat out past the point Hymir will allow, casts his line to the ocean floor; the serpent bites. Thor's foot goes through the boat bottom as he pulls. Also appears in the Gylfaginning account of Utgard-Loki's hall, where Thor is tricked into trying to lift the serpent disguised as a giant cat.

Journey to Utgard (Utgard-Loki's Hall)
Source: Prose Edda, Gylfaginning (Snorri Sturluson)
recogThor, Loki, and companions travel to Utgard; giant king Utgard-Loki sets impossible contests — Thor is humiliated in three challenges.
recogThor cannot drain the drinking horn (one end connects to the sea), cannot lift the cat (Jormungandr), cannot pin the old woman (who is Elli = Old Age) in wrestling.
powerOn departure, Utgard-Loki reveals the illusions: the horn's other end was in the sea (Thor lowered sea levels visibly); the cat was actually the Midgard Serpent; Elli was personified Old Age, which nothing can overcome. Thor raises his hammer to destroy Utgard but Utgard-Loki vanishes. The episode is the source for the "cat" and "sea" clues in tossups.

Thor and the Giant Hrungnir
Source: Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál
recogHrungnir, the strongest of all giants, boasts he will sink Asgard and carry off Freya and Sif; challenged to a duel by Thor.
recogGiants create a clay giant named Mokkurkalfi as Hrungnir's second; Mokkurkalfi trembles with fear (given a mare's heart) and is slain by Thor's companion Thjalfi.
powerHrungnir's head and heart are of stone; he holds a whetstone (hein) as weapon. Thor throws Mjolnir; Hrungnir throws the whetstone — they collide, a whetstone shard lodges in Thor's skull (removed only partway by seeress Groa). Thor kills Hrungnir but is pinned under the giant's leg until his three-year-old son Magni lifts it.

Thor and the Giantess Skrymir / Utgard Journey
Source: Prose Edda — the giant Skrymir episode
recogOn the way to Utgard, the party shelters in what turns out to be a giant's glove (Skrymir's glove); Thor strikes Skrymir three times with Mjolnir during the night.
recogEach blow is explained away by Skrymir as a leaf, acorn, or bird dropping on his face — illustrating that Skrymir (= Utgard-Loki) uses illusion to negate Thor's strength.
powerThe craters left by Mjolnir blows become valleys in the landscape — a etiological narrative explaining geographic features. Skrymir is revealed post-journey to be Utgard-Loki himself in disguise.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Epithets & kennings

  • Asa-Thor — "Thor of the Aesir"
  • Vingthor — "Thor the Charioteer"
  • Hlorridi — heat-rider; old poetic name
  • Einriði — "lone rider"
  • Bjorn (bear) — not Thor but sometimes confused
  • Thunderer — common prose Edda gloss

Key objects & symbols

  • Mjolnir — the hammer; short-handled (Loki's sabotage)
  • Megingjord — belt that doubles his strength
  • Járngreipr — iron gauntlets to grasp Mjolnir
  • Oak tree — sacred to Thor in Germanic tradition
  • Red beard — consistent iconographic marker
  • Lightning bolt / thunder — hammer throw makes thunder

Key figures in Thor myths

  • Loki — frequent companion, sometimes antagonist
  • Sif — wife; golden hair (wheat symbolism)
  • Jormungandr — serpent nemesis; Loki's son
  • Hymir — giant; fishing companion
  • Thrym — giant who steals Mjolnir
  • Utgard-Loki — illusionist giant king
  • Hrungnir — stone-giant; duel opponent
  • Magni — son; lifts Hrungnir's body
  • Grid — giantess who helps Thor vs. Geirrod

Source texts

  • Prose Edda — Snorri Sturluson, c. 1220; sections: Gylfaginning, Skáldskaparmál
  • Poetic Edda — older poems; key Thor poems: Þrymskviða, Hymiskviða, Lokasenna, Hárbarðsljóð
  • Hárbarðsljóð — flyting match between Thor and Odin in disguise (Harbard)
  • Skaldic verse — kennings like "Sif's husband," "Hrungnir's slayer"

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Odin (Norse)Odin = wisdom, war, magic, ravens, spear (Gungnir), one eye, hanged himself on Yggdrasil. Thor = brute strength, thunder, hammer, red beard, protects common people. Both are Aesir; Thor is Odin's son.
Zeus (Greek)Both rule thunder/sky, but Zeus is king of the gods (Thor is not — Odin is). Zeus's weapon = lightning bolt; Thor's = hammer. Zeus is Thor's Roman-equivalent Jupiter's Greek equivalent.
Jupiter (Roman)Thursday = Þórsdagr = dies Iovis (Jupiter's day) — the names are interpretatio romana equivalents. Jupiter = sky-king + civic order; Thor = storm fighter + defender of mankind. Same sky domain, different social roles.
Indra (Hindu)Indra and Thor are cognate Proto-Indo-European thunder gods; both wield a weapon against a serpent (Vritra/Jormungandr). Indra = vedic texts (Rigveda), soma-drinking, storm-warrior; Thor = Norse Eddas. Compare on PIE mythology questions.
Tyr (Norse)Tyr = Norse god of law, justice, single combat; one-handed (sacrificed to bind Fenrir). Tyr's day = Tuesday. Thor = thunder; Tyr = law. Both Aesir, often lumped — differentiate by domain.
Freyr (Norse)Freyr = Vanir god of fertility, sunshine, prosperity; uses a magic sword; associated with Yule. Not a fighter in the Thor sense. Freyr's sister Freya is confused with Thor's wife Sif — they are distinct.
Hercules (Greek/Roman)Both are immensely strong heroes/gods who fight monsters; Roman writers (Tacitus) identified Thor with Hercules (Herculem ante alios venerantur). But Hercules completes 12 Labors (mortal hero deified); Thor is a full god with cosmic role from birth.

🌐 Cross-discipline big picture Thor is a Proto-Indo-European thunder-god cognate: compare him to Indra (Vedic, slays serpent Vritra with thunderbolt), Zeus/Jupiter (Greek/Roman sky-kings), and Perun (Slavic thunder deity). The shared pattern — strong male deity, weapon associated with thunder, fights a chaos-serpent — is the core PIE mythological structure studied in comparative religion. For linguistics questions: Þunor (Old English) → Modern English "thunder"; his name derives from Proto-Germanic *Þunraz. For history questions: Viking-age Mjolnir amulets (archaeological record) spike in the 10th century, possibly as a deliberate counter-symbol to the Christian cross.
