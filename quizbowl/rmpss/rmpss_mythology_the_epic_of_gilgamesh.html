Quick Overview

OriginSumerian/Akkadian; ancient Mesopotamia (modern Iraq)
DateOldest versions ~2100 BCE; Standard Babylonian version ~1200 BCE
LanguageAkkadian (Standard Version); earlier fragments in Sumerian
Format12 tablets; cuneiform script; ~3,000 lines in Standard Version
RediscoveryGeorge Smith, British Museum, 1872 — found the flood tablet
Claim to fameOldest surviving major work of world literature

Essential narrative arc

  • Gilgamesh, king of Uruk, is two-thirds divine, one-third mortal; tyrannizes his people.
  • Gods create Enkidu — a wild man raised by animals — to balance Gilgamesh; they wrestle then become inseparable friends.
  • Together they slay the monster Humbaba in the Cedar Forest and kill the Bull of Heaven.
  • Gods decree Enkidu must die; his death shatters Gilgamesh, who embarks on a quest for immortality.
  • Gilgamesh seeks Utnapishtim — the only mortal granted eternal life — who tells him the flood story and reveals a plant of rejuvenation.
  • A serpent steals the plant; Gilgamesh returns to Uruk, accepting mortality.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Two-thirds divine and one-third mortal" — almost always Gilgamesh.
  • "Wild man raised by animals" or "hair like grain" — Enkidu.
  • "Plant of immortality stolen by a serpent" — the epic's climax.
  • "George Smith" or "flood tablet" — the 1872 rediscovery angle.

High-Yield Figures, Episodes & Themes

The episodes and figures most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = middle/recognition clue.

Enkidu
Gilgamesh's companion · created by the goddess Aruru
recogWild man raised among animals; body covered in hair like grain; drinks with gazelles at a watering hole.
recogCivilized by the harlot/priestess Shamhat over six days and seven nights; later dies by divine decree.
powerCreated by the goddess Aruru from clay and spittle specifically to counterbalance Gilgamesh's excesses; his name may mean "lord of the good place." His death — and Gilgamesh's grief — is the pivot that drives the second half of the epic.
Humbaba (Huwawa) and the Cedar Forest
Tablets III–V · guardian appointed by Enlil
recogMonstrous guardian of the Cedar Forest; his face said to resemble a coil of intestines; his roar is a flood, breath is fire.
recogGilgamesh and Enkidu slay him despite Enkidu urging mercy; the sun god Shamash helps by trapping Humbaba with winds.
powerHumbaba is described as possessing seven "splendors" (melammu) — terrifying auras; he pleads for his life before Enkidu convinces Gilgamesh to kill him. His death angers the god Enlil, setting in motion Enkidu's fate.
Ishtar's Proposal & the Bull of Heaven
Tablet VI · Gilgamesh's great insult
recogIshtar (goddess of love/war) proposes marriage to Gilgamesh; he rejects her by listing all her previous lovers she destroyed.
recogIshtar asks her father Anu for the Bull of Heaven; the bull causes famine and kills hundreds with its snorts; Enkidu tears off its hindquarter and flings it at Ishtar's face.
powerGilgamesh's insult speech lists former consorts: Tammuz (Dumuzi), the bird, lion, horse, shepherd, and gardener Ishullanu — all harmed by Ishtar. This earns the gods' wrath and seals Enkidu's death sentence.
Death of Enkidu & Gilgamesh's Lament
Tablets VII–VIII · the pivot of the epic
recogEnkidu falls ill after cursing the cedar door, Shamhat, and the trapper; he dies after a twelve-day illness.
recogGilgamesh refuses to bury Enkidu for seven days and seven nights, hoping he will wake; a maggot falls from Enkidu's nose before Gilgamesh accepts death.
powerGilgamesh covers Enkidu's face like a bride; he tears his clothes and puts on a lion skin. The lament on Tablet VIII is one of the earliest literary elegies — Gilgamesh compares his grief to that of a lioness robbed of cubs.
The Journey: Scorpion-People, Mount Mashu, Siduri
Tablets IX–X · the quest for immortality
recogScorpion-people (aqrabuamelu) guard the twin-peaked mountain Mashu, through which the sun passes; they warn Gilgamesh the passage is impossible.
recogSiduri, the ale-wife/tavern-keeper, gives Gilgamesh the famous speech advising him to enjoy life: "When the gods created mankind, they allotted death… fill your belly, make merry day and night."
powerSiduri's speech is often cited as the world's first carpe diem passage and anticipates Ecclesiastes. The ferryman Urshanabi then takes Gilgamesh across the Waters of Death to reach Utnapishtim.
Utnapishtim and the Flood
Tablet XI · the most-asked episode
recogUtnapishtim (Sumerian: Ziusudra) is the sole mortal granted immortality by the gods; he and his wife live at the mouth of the rivers.
recogThe god Ea (Enki) warns Utnapishtim of the flood through a reed wall; he builds a boat, loads animals and craftsmen, survives seven days of storm; sends out a dove, swallow, and raven to test for land.
powerThe flood narrative parallels Genesis almost exactly — boat, animals, birds, landing on a mountain (Mount Nimush / Nisir), and a sacrifice afterward. George Smith identified this parallel in 1872; it caused a sensation in Victorian England. The parallel source is the Atrahasis Epic.
The Plant of Immortality and the Serpent
Tablet XI · climax and resolution
recogUtnapishtim tells Gilgamesh of a thorny plant at the bottom of the sea called "The Old Man Becomes Young Again" (or "Heartbeat of the Old").
recogGilgamesh dives, retrieves the plant, but a serpent steals it while he sleeps; it sheds its skin — explaining why snakes are immortal — and Gilgamesh weeps.
powerThe serpent's skin-shedding is the epic's etiological payoff for snake immortality. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk and the poem ends with him pointing to the city walls — the enduring legacy that is his true immortality. The walls of Uruk are said to be one square mile each of city, orchards, and clay pits.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Epithets & descriptions

  • "Two-thirds divine, one-third mortal" — Gilgamesh
  • "He who saw the deep" (sha nagba imuru) — opening epithet
  • "Bull of Uruk" — Gilgamesh's epithet
  • "Wild man of the steppe" — Enkidu
  • "He who found the plant of life" — Utnapishtim
  • "Far-away" (Uta-napishti) — Utnapishtim's epithet/meaning

Key symbols & objects

  • Cedar wood door — Enkidu curses it (made from the Cedar Forest cedar)
  • Plant of immortality — thorny, grows at sea bottom, stolen by serpent
  • Lion skin — worn by Gilgamesh after Enkidu's death
  • Stone oars / stone men — used by Urshanabi for the Waters of Death
  • Walls of Uruk — three leagues of city; Gilgamesh's lasting legacy
  • Dreams — recur throughout; interpreted by Ninsun and Enkidu

Key numbers & locations

  • Uruk — Gilgamesh's city (modern Warka, Iraq)
  • Cedar Forest — home of Humbaba; possibly Lebanon or Zagros Mts.
  • Waters of Death — the sea Gilgamesh must cross
  • 12 tablets — structure of the Standard Babylonian version
  • Seven days and seven nights — recurring unit (flood, vigil over Enkidu)
  • Six days and seven nights — Shamhat civilizes Enkidu

Themes & cross-discipline links

  • Mortality vs. immortality — central quest; answer is acceptance
  • Civilization vs. nature — Enkidu's arc from wild to civilized
  • Friendship / bromance — Gilgamesh & Enkidu; ancient literary motif
  • Flood parallel — links to Genesis (Noah), Hindu (Manu), Greek (Deucalion)
  • Carpe diem — Siduri's speech anticipates Ecclesiastes/Epicureanism
  • Etiological myth — explains why snakes shed skin (immortality)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Noah / Genesis floodBoth send out birds and land on a mountain, but Utnapishtim is Mesopotamian and predates Genesis. The Gilgamesh flood is on Tablet XI; Noah is in Genesis 6–9. Gilgamesh is the source, not the Biblical version.
AtrahasisAnother Akkadian flood hero in his own epic (Atrahasis Epic, ~1700 BCE). Utnapishtim is Atrahasis in function; the Gilgamesh flood narrative borrows from Atrahasis. If asked about the flood story as a standalone work, the answer may be Atrahasis, not Gilgamesh.
Odysseus / OdysseyBoth are hero-journey epics involving monsters and the sea, but Odysseus returns home to a wife; Gilgamesh quests for immortality and fails. The Odyssey is Greek, ~800 BCE; Gilgamesh is Akkadian, ~1200 BCE in standard form.
Heracles (Hercules)Both kill monsters and descend to the underworld, but Heracles is Greek and his labors are assigned as penance. Gilgamesh's quests are chosen and driven by friendship/grief, not punishment.
Marduk / Enuma ElishEnuma Elish is the Babylonian creation myth (Marduk slays Tiamat to create the world). Gilgamesh is a hero epic, not a cosmogony. Both are Mesopotamian/Akkadian — read the clue for "creation" vs. "hero."
Enkidu vs. Enkidu (Tablet XII)In Tablets I–XI, Enkidu is Gilgamesh's beloved companion who dies. Tablet XII is a separate Sumerian poem tacked on where Enkidu descends to the underworld for a lost pukku (ball/drum) and cannot return — a different tradition grafted onto the epic.
Shamash vs. ShamhatShamash = sun god who helps in battle; Shamhat = the woman (priestess/harlot) who civilizes Enkidu. Easy to mix up in audio tossups — listen for context (divine vs. human).

🌐 Cross-discipline big picture The Epic of Gilgamesh is one of quizbowl's most cross-disciplinary topics: it bridges mythology (Mesopotamian pantheon: Anu, Enlil, Ea, Ishtar, Shamash), religion (the flood parallel connects to Genesis/Noah, Hindu Manu, and Greek Deucalion — a recurring comparative-religion clue), literature (oldest epic; carpe diem; elegy; hero's journey), history/archaeology (Nineveh library, George Smith's 1872 discovery, cuneiform tablets), and even philosophy (the first sustained meditation on mortality in world literature). When a tossup opens on "a Mesopotamian king" or "cuneiform tablets" or "the oldest written story," think Gilgamesh first.
