Quick Overview

Four interlocking concepts — karma, dharma, samsara, and moksha — form the cosmological and ethical backbone of Hinduism, Buddhism, Jainism, and Sikhism. They appear constantly as quizbowl tossup answers and as defining clue clusters in questions about specific texts, figures, and traditions.

Tradition scopeHindu, Buddhist, Jain, Sikh — each redefines the terms differently
Language originSanskrit (all four terms); Pali equivalents used in Theravada Buddhism
Core logicKarma → Samsara → goal of Moksha; Dharma governs how to act within Samsara
Key textsUpanishads, Bhagavad Gita, Dhammapada, Jain Agamas, Guru Granth Sahib
Difficulty★★★ Core / highest-frequency religion category

The four concepts at a glance

  • Karma — action and its moral consequences; good acts accumulate good karma, bad acts accumulate bad karma; propels the cycle of rebirth.
  • Dharma — cosmic order, moral law, duty; in Hinduism it is caste-specific (svadharma); in Buddhism it is the Buddha's teaching; in Jainism it is also a substance enabling motion.
  • Samsara — the beginningless cycle of death and rebirth (reincarnation / transmigration); driven by karma; encompasses all suffering (dukkha in Buddhism).
  • Moksha / Nirvana / Mukti — liberation from samsara; the ultimate soteriological goal; called nirvana in Buddhism, moksha or mukti in Hinduism, moksha in Jainism, mukti in Sikhism.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Any question asking about "the cycle of death and rebirth" → samsara.
  • "Liberation from the cycle of rebirth" → moksha (Hindu/Jain) or nirvana (Buddhist).
  • "The law of cause and effect governing rebirth" → karma.
  • "One's duty or cosmic law" in a Hindu context → dharma; in a Buddhist context → the Dharma (the Buddha's teaching).

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

The four core concepts as quizbowl answers, with pyramidal clue ladders. power = hard early clue. recog = mid/recognition clue.

Karma (कर्म)
Sanskrit: "action, deed" · Shared by Hindu Buddhist Jain Sikh
power In Jainism, karma is conceived as a literal physical substance — fine particles (karman) that cling to the soul (jiva) and weigh it down, preventing liberation.
recog In the Bhagavad Gita, Krishna distinguishes three paths: karma yoga (action), jnana yoga (knowledge), bhakti yoga (devotion) — karma yoga is acting without attachment to results.
recog Intentional action (cetana) is karma in Buddhism (Pali: kamma); unintentional acts do not generate karma.
recog Good karma = punya; bad/demerit karma = papa (Sanskrit). The law operates automatically — no divine judge required.

Dharma (धर्म)
Sanskrit root: dhṛ — "to hold, uphold, sustain" · Shared by Hindu Buddhist Jain
power In Jainism, dharma (alongside adharma, akasha, kala, and pudgala) is one of the five or six fundamental substances (dravyas) — the medium that enables motion, not an ethical concept.
power The Dharmashastra texts (including Manu Smriti) codify dharma by varna (caste) and ashrama (life stage); Manu Smriti is the most cited in quizbowl.
recog In the Bhagavad Gita, Arjuna's crisis is resolved by Krishna explaining svadharma — one's own caste/social duty must be performed even if imperfect.
recog In Buddhism, Dharma (capital D) = the teaching of the Buddha; one of the Three Jewels (Buddha, Dharma, Sangha) that Buddhists take refuge in.
recog The Dharmachakra (Wheel of Dharma), depicted on the Indian flag, represents the Buddha's first sermon at Sarnath — "setting the wheel of Dharma in motion."
wiki ↗
Samsara (संसार)
Sanskrit: "wandering through" · Shared by Hindu Buddhist Jain Sikh
power In Buddhism, samsara is structured into six realms: gods, demi-gods, humans, animals, hungry ghosts (pretas), and hell beings — depicted in the Wheel of Life (Bhavachakra) held by Yama.
power The Jain cosmology depicts samsara as a cosmic ocean (samsara-sagara); the 24 Tirthankaras are "ford-finders" (the literal meaning of tirthankara) who have crossed it to liberation.
recog Samsara is driven by the Three Poisons in Buddhism: greed (raga), hatred (dvesha), and delusion (moha) — depicted at the hub of the Wheel of Life as a pig, snake, and rooster.
recog The atman (eternal soul) transmigrates in Hindu thought; Buddhism denies a permanent self (anatman / anatta) yet maintains the continuity of karma across rebirths.
recog Rebirth in samsara is conditioned by the Twelve Nidanas (chain of dependent origination, pratityasamutpada) in Buddhism — ignorance → formations → consciousness → … → birth → suffering → death.
wiki ↗
Moksha / Nirvana / Mukti (मोक्ष · निर्वाण)
Sanskrit mokṣa: "release, liberation" · Shared by Hindu Buddhist Jain Sikh
power In Advaita Vedanta (Shankara), moksha is jnana: realizing that the individual atman is identical to Brahman — the distinction is only maya (illusion). Contrast: Ramanuja's Vishishtadvaita says the released soul joins Brahman but remains distinct.
power In Jainism, a liberated soul is a Siddha — formless, omniscient, at the apex of the universe (Siddhashila); the 24 Tirthankaras (including Mahavira and legendary Rishabhanatha) achieved this.
recog Buddhist nirvana (Pali: nibbana) literally means "blowing out" — the extinguishing of the Three Poisons. The Buddha's nirvana under the Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya is its paradigm case.
recog The four paths to moksha in Hinduism: jnana (knowledge), bhakti (devotion), karma (righteous action), raja (meditation / yoga). Outlined in the Bhagavad Gita and the Upanishads.
recog In Sikhism, liberation (mukti) comes through Nam Simran (meditation on God's Name) and Guru's grace (nadar), not caste-based ritual — a deliberate break with Brahmanical dharma.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related concepts, texts, and figures that appear as hard early clues or as the answer itself in specialized tossups.

Pratityasamutpada Buddhist
Dependent Origination
Twelve-link chain explaining how karma fuels rebirth; ignorance is the first link; key doctrine of the Buddha.
Ahimsa & Jain Karma Jain
Non-violence as karma-prevention
Every act of violence inflicts karmic particles; Jain monks sweep the path and wear mouth-masks to avoid harming insects.
Bhagavad Gita ch. 2–3 Hindu
c. 1st–2nd c. BCE (composition)
Krishna's teaching of nishkama karma — desireless action, renouncing fruits; central to karma yoga.
Brihadaranyaka & Chandogya Upanishads Hindu
c. 800–600 BCE
Earliest texts explicitly connecting karma to rebirth; also introduce atman = Brahman (tat tvam asi).
Bhavachakra (Wheel of Life) Buddhist
Tibetan iconography
Yama holds six realms of samsara; three hub animals (pig, snake, rooster) = three poisons; outer ring = 12 nidanas.
Ashrama System Hindu
Four life stages
Student (brahmacharya), householder (grihastha), forest dweller (vanaprastha), renunciant (sannyasa) — dharma differs by stage.
Varna-Dharma & Manu Smriti Hindu
c. 2nd c. BCE – 3rd c. CE
Laws of Manu codify dharma for four varnas (Brahmin, Kshatriya, Vaishya, Shudra); controversial for caste hierarchy.
Parinirvana Buddhist
Final nirvana at death
Complete nirvana at death with no further rebirth; the Buddha's parinirvana at Kushinagar (c. 483 BCE) is canonical.
Bodhisattva Ideal & Samsara Buddhist
Mahayana tradition
Bodhisattvas postpone their own nirvana to help all beings escape samsara; contrast Theravada arhat ideal.
Jnana-Bhakti-Karma Marga Hindu
Three paths to moksha
Jnana = Shankara's path; Bhakti = Ramanuja/Chaitanya; Karma = Gita's nishkama karmayoga. Each a distinct school.
Tirthankara & Siddha Jain
24 ford-finders
Rishabhanatha (first), Parshvanatha (23rd), Mahavira (24th / last) — all achieved moksha and reside at Siddhashila.
Anatta / Anatman Buddhist
No-self doctrine
Buddhism rejects a permanent atman; karma persists across rebirths without a soul — the "flame passing to a new candle" metaphor.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key Sanskrit / Pali terms

  • Karma / Kamma — action; punya = merit; papa = demerit
  • Nishkama karma — desireless action (Gita)
  • Svadharma — one's own duty (caste-specific)
  • Rita — Vedic cosmic order (precursor to dharma)
  • Atman — individual soul; Brahman — ultimate reality
  • Maya — illusion (Advaita Vedanta)
  • Nirvana / Nibbana — "blowing out" (Buddhist liberation)
  • Mukti — liberation (Sikh/Hindu synonym for moksha)

Key figures & texts

  • Shankara — 8th c. Advaita Vedanta; jnana path to moksha
  • Ramanuja — 11th–12th c. Vishishtadvaita; bhakti path
  • Arjuna & Krishna — Bhagavad Gita protagonists; dharma crisis
  • Mahavira — 24th Tirthankara; Jain karma as matter
  • Nagarjuna — Madhyamaka; nirvana = samsara (identity thesis)
  • Yama — god of death; holds Bhavachakra (Wheel of Life)
  • Bhagavad Gita — principal dharma/karma/moksha text
  • Dhammapada — "mind precedes all actions" opens chs 1–2

Symbols & images

  • Dharmachakra — Wheel of Dharma; 8 spokes = Eightfold Path; on Indian flag
  • Bhavachakra — Wheel of Life; Yama clutches it; 6 realms visible
  • Lotus — moksha symbol; rises pure from muddy samsara
  • Om / Aum — sacred syllable representing Brahman
  • Bodhi tree — site of Buddha's nirvana, Bodh Gaya
  • Jain hand symbol with ahimsa in the palm

Cross-tradition contrasts

  • Hindu: karma requires an atman to transmigrate
  • Buddhist: karma works without a permanent self (anatta)
  • Jain: karma is literally material — unique to Jainism
  • Sikh: karma exists but can be overridden by God's grace (nadar)
  • Mahayana: nirvana and samsara are ultimately identical (Nagarjuna)
  • Theravada: nirvana is the unconditioned — absolutely distinct from samsara

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse with / common trapHow to tell them apart
Karma (Hindu) vs. Karma (Jain)Hindu karma is metaphysical/ethical; Jain karma is a physical substance (fine particles) that literally weighs down the soul. If the question mentions "particles clinging to the soul," it's Jain.
Moksha (Hindu) vs. Nirvana (Buddhist)Both = liberation, but moksha typically implies the atman merging with or joining Brahman; nirvana is the extinguishing of craving with no self/atman assumed. Jain moksha = formless Siddha at universe-apex.
Dharma (Hindu duty) vs. Dharma (Buddhist teaching)In Hindu contexts, dharma = moral/social law, caste duty. In Buddhist contexts, Dharma (capitalized) = the Buddha's teaching, one of the Three Jewels. Context determines which is meant.
Samsara vs. Reincarnation (Western)Samsara is the entire cycle including all six realms (gods, animals, hell, etc.), not just human rebirth. "Transmigration of souls" is the Hindu framing; Buddhism emphasizes continuity of karma without a fixed soul.
Arhat (Theravada) vs. Bodhisattva (Mahayana)Both are on paths to liberation, but an arhat achieves personal nirvana and exits samsara; a bodhisattva stays within samsara to help all beings. Key Mahayana vs. Theravada marker.
Advaita (Shankara) vs. Vishishtadvaita (Ramanuja)Shankara: moksha = atman IS Brahman (non-dual); difference is maya. Ramanuja: souls are real but part of Brahman as its body — devotional union, not identity.
Rita (Vedic) vs. DharmaRita is the earlier Vedic cosmic order maintained by the gods; dharma emerges in the Upanishads and Epics as a more personal ethical/social concept. Rita is not usually the answer in a dharma tossup.
Moksha vs. MuktiThese are synonyms in most contexts (mukti is more common in northern Bhakti traditions and Sikhism; moksha in Sanskrit philosophy). Do not treat them as different concepts.

Cross-discipline "big picture" These four concepts connect across quizbowl categories: karma links to ethics (consequentialism vs. deontology debates) and sociology (Weber's analysis of Indian religion in The Religion of India); dharma links to political philosophy (Ashoka's dhamma edicts, rock edicts at Kalsi and Girnar); samsara links to comparative religion (Plato's metempsychosis in the Phaedo and Republic; Pythagorean transmigration); and moksha/nirvana links to philosophy of mind (the self, personal identity) and literature (Hermann Hesse's Siddhartha, which dramatizes the search for liberation). The Indian flag's Dharmachakra also bridges history (Ashoka, Maurya Empire) and religion tossups.
