Quick Overview

Datesc. 428–348 BCE (Athens)
Teacher / StudentStudent of Socrates; teacher of Aristotle
School foundedThe Academy in Athens (c. 387 BCE) — first Western university
FormVirtually all works as philosophical dialogues
Central doctrineTheory of Forms (eternal, perfect Ideas behind appearances)
Political visionRule by philosopher-kings; aristocratic ideal state

Defining traits

  • Dialogue form: philosophy dramatized as conversation, almost always starring Socrates as main interlocutor.
  • Theory of Forms (Ideas): the physical world is a shadow of perfect, eternal, immaterial Forms (e.g., the Form of the Good, the Form of Beauty).
  • Recollection / Anamnesis: learning is remembering truths the immortal soul knew before birth.
  • Tripartite soul: reason (logistikon), spirit (thymoeides), appetite (epithymetikon).
  • Philosopher-kings: only those who know the Forms are fit to rule — the central political claim of the Republic.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The Allegory of the Cave — prisoner sees only shadows on a wall; the single most-given Plato clue.
  • The Theory of Forms / ideal world of Ideas.
  • The Academy founded in Athens.
  • Student of Socrates; teacher of Aristotle — the most common "chain" clue.

High-Yield Works / Ideas (Very Common)

The dialogues and doctrines most likely to be the tossup answer. power = hard early-clue. recog = recognition / mid-clue.

The Republic (Politeia)
c. 375 BCE · longest and most-studied dialogue · 10 books
recogDefines justice in the soul and in the city; argues philosophers must rule as philosopher-kings.
recogContains the Allegory of the Cave (Book VII): prisoners chained, see only shadows — freed prisoner ascends to the sun (the Form of the Good).
recogContains the Allegory of the Sun and the Divided Line (Books VI–VII): four levels of cognition (shadows → images → objects → Forms).
powerDescribes the Ship of State analogy; dismisses poetry and imitative arts from the ideal city (imitates appearances, not truth); proposes Noble Lie (gennaion pseudos) to maintain social order.
wiki ↗
Symposium
c. 385–370 BCE · dramatic setting: a drinking party
recogA series of speeches on the nature of Eros (Love) at a banquet; attendees include Aristophanes, Agathon, Socrates.
recogAristophanes' myth: humans were originally double-bodied (three sexes) and split by Zeus; love is the search for one's other half.
recogDiotima's ladder: Socrates reports the priestess Diotima's teaching — love ascends from a beautiful body → all bodies → souls → knowledge → the Form of Beauty itself ("Platonic love").
powerLate arrival Alcibiades bursts in drunk and delivers a eulogy of Socrates himself, comparing him to a Silenus figure — ugly outside, divine inside. The dialogue is narrated by Apollodorus (a second-hand account).
wiki ↗
Phaedo
c. 360 BCE · setting: Socrates' last day in prison
recogSocrates argues for immortality of the soul in his final hours before drinking hemlock.
recogFour arguments: Argument from Opposites, Argument from Recollection (anamnesis), Affinity Argument (soul like Forms = simple, unchanging), Final Argument (soul is life-itself, cannot admit death).
powerNarrated by Phaedo of Elis to Echecrates; interlocutors are Simmias and Cebes (Pythagorean-influenced Thebans). The dialogue ends with Socrates' calm death — last words: "We owe a rooster to Asclepius."
wiki ↗
Meno
c. 380 BCE · interlocutors: Meno (Thessalian aristocrat), a slave boy
recogOpens with "Can virtue be taught?" — classic Socratic inquiry into the nature of arete.
recogDemonstrates anamnesis (recollection): Socrates guides an uneducated slave boy to prove a geometric theorem — showing knowledge is remembered, not learned.
powerIntroduces the "Meno's paradox" (paradox of inquiry): you can't seek what you don't know, and you don't need to seek what you already know. Resolved by the doctrine of recollection. Also features brief appearance of Anytus, one of Socrates' future accusers.
wiki ↗
Apology (Apologia Sokratous)
c. 399 BCE (events) · Socrates' trial defense speech
recogSocrates defends himself against charges of impiety and corrupting the youth of Athens; votes 280–220 to convict.
recogThe Oracle at Delphi proclaimed Socrates the wisest man; Socrates interpreted this to mean only he knows he knows nothing ("Socratic wisdom").
powerContains the phrase "the unexamined life is not worth living" (ho anexetastos bios ou biotos). Accusers are Meletus, Anytus, and Lycon. Not a speech of apology — apologia means defense.
wiki ↗
Phaedrus
c. 370 BCE · setting: outside Athens by a stream
recogOn love, rhetoric, and the soul; set outside Athens — unusually pastoral for Plato.
recogChariot Allegory (Phaedrus myth): the soul as a charioteer driving two horses — one noble (spirit), one base (appetite) — toward the realm of Forms.
powerContains Plato's critique of writing (the myth of Theuth / Thamus): Theuth invents writing; Thamus (king of Egypt) argues it will weaken memory and give only the appearance of wisdom. Ironically, Plato himself writes this critique down.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-cited dialogues and doctrines that open hard tossups. One-line ID each.

Timaeus
c. 360 BCE
Plato's cosmology; the Demiurge fashions the world from eternal Forms; introduces Atlantis briefly.
Critias
c. 360 BCE (unfinished)
Full account of Atlantis — the sunken island empire; only Plato mentions it.
Theaetetus
c. 369 BCE
Investigates the nature of knowledge; rejects "knowledge = perception" (vs. Protagoras' relativism).
Parmenides
c. 370 BCE
Young Socrates meets Parmenides and Zeno; Theory of Forms subjected to rigorous self-critique ("Third Man" objection).
Crito
c. 399 BCE
Crito urges Socrates to escape prison; Socrates refuses — duty to obey the Laws of Athens.
Euthyphro
c. 399 BCE
Asks "What is piety?"; poses the Euthyphro dilemma: is the pious loved by gods because it is pious, or pious because the gods love it?
Protagoras
c. 390 BCE
Socrates debates the sophist Protagoras on whether virtue is teachable; famous paradox reversal at end.
Gorgias
c. 380 BCE
Attacks rhetoric as flattery; argues justice beats injustice even for the wrongdoer; interlocutor Callicles advocates might-makes-right.
Laws
c. 350 BCE · last and longest dialogue
Practical second-best city governed by laws, not philosopher-kings; Socrates absent — replaced by "the Athenian Stranger."
Sophist
c. 360 BCE
Defines the sophist as image-maker; wrestles with non-being and the problem of false statements.
Laches
c. 380 BCE
What is courage? Socrates and two generals (Laches, Nicias) fail to define it.
Ion
c. 390 BCE
Rhapsode Ion claims expertise; Socrates argues poets compose by divine inspiration (mania), not knowledge.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & doctrines

  • Theory of Forms (Eidos / Idea) — eternal perfect archetypes
  • Anamnesis — knowledge as recollection of pre-birth truths
  • Demiurge — divine craftsman who creates the world (Timaeus)
  • Platonic love — spiritual, non-physical love ascending to the Good
  • Noble Lie (gennaion pseudos) — founding myth to stabilize the ideal city
  • Philosopher-king — the only just ruler

Allegories & myths

  • Allegory of the Cave (Republic VII) — shadows, prisoners, the sun
  • Allegory of the Sun (Republic VI) — the Good = the sun of the intelligible realm
  • Divided Line (Republic VI) — four epistemic levels
  • Chariot Allegory (Phaedrus) — soul with two horses
  • Aristophanes' Split Humans (Symposium) — origin of erotic longing
  • Myth of Er (Republic X) — soldier's afterlife vision; souls choose new lives
  • Atlantis myth (Timaeus / Critias)

Figures & interlocutors

  • Socrates — main speaker in most dialogues (never writes)
  • Glaucon & Adeimantus — Plato's brothers; main interlocutors in Republic
  • Diotima of Mantinea — priestess who teaches Socrates about love (Symposium)
  • Alcibiades — praises Socrates in Symposium
  • Thrasymachus — sophist who defines justice as "advantage of the stronger" (Republic)
  • Meno, Cebes, Simmias, Euthyphro, Crito — named dialogue interlocutors

Biography & context

  • Born c. 428 BCE, died c. 348 BCE; Athenian aristocrat
  • Witnessed Socrates' trial and execution (399 BCE)
  • Founded the Academy (c. 387 BCE) — operated ~900 years
  • Three visits to Syracuse — attempted to educate tyrant Dionysius II
  • Whitehead: all Western philosophy is "footnotes to Plato"
  • "Plato" = broad-shouldered — a wrestling nickname

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Aristotle Plato's student; rejects the separate world of Forms — universals exist in particulars (immanent vs. transcendent). Aristotle writes Nicomachean Ethics, Politics, Poetics. If the clue mentions empirical observation, teleology, or the four causes, answer Aristotle. If it mentions the Cave, philosopher-kings, or eternal Forms, answer Plato.
Socrates Socrates wrote nothing — all we know comes from Plato, Xenophon, Aristophanes. If the tossup asks "this philosopher who wrote the Apology," the answer is Plato (the author), not Socrates (the subject). Socrates is the character in Plato's dialogues.
Plotinus / Neo-Platonism Plotinus (3rd CE) develops Neo-Platonism: the One, Nous (Intellect), Soul. Heavily Platonic but centuries later; clue words: Enneads, the One, emanation. Don't answer Plato if the date is CE.
Protagoras / Sophists Sophists charge fees, teach rhetoric, embrace relativism ("man is the measure of all things" — Protagoras). Plato attacks them (especially in Gorgias and Protagoras). If the clue is about a sophist's relativism, don't answer Plato.
Republic vs. Utopia / Leviathan All are ideal-state texts — but More's Utopia is Renaissance fiction; Hobbes's Leviathan argues for absolute sovereign from a social contract; Plato's Republic is ancient Greek and argues philosopher rule. Distinguish by date, author name, and key concepts.
Plato's Republic vs. Aristotle's Politics Republic = idealist, abstract; philosopher-kings rule by knowledge of the Good. Politics = empirical; classifies actual constitutions; says man is a political animal (zoon politikon).

Cross-discipline "answer web" Plato connects across quizbowl categories: the Cave and Theory of Forms anchor Philosophy questions; Atlantis pulls in Mythology/Folklore; Diotima's ladder and Aristophanes' myth link to the Symposium as Literature; the Academy connects to History of Education; and Plato's influence on early Christian theology (Augustine, Origen absorbed Neoplatonism) links to Religion questions.
