Quick Overview

TraditionNorse / Old Norse mythology (Scandinavia)
RoleTrickster god; shapeshifter; "blood-brother" of Odin
ParentageFather Fárbauti (giant); mother Laufey (also called Nál)
Primary sourcesProse Edda (Snorri Sturluson) & Poetic Edda (esp. Lokasenna)
StatusAesir by adoption; not a Vanir; of jotun (giant) descent
Role at RagnarökEscapes his binding; captains ship Naglfar; kills & is killed by Heimdall

Defining traits

  • Shapeshifter — transforms into mare, salmon, fly, seal, old woman, and more; the only god with confirmed sex-change transformations.
  • Schemer & problem-solver — engineers crises but often must fix them, sometimes under duress.
  • Mixed loyalties — begins as Odin's companion; grows increasingly antagonistic; ultimately enemy of the gods.
  • Father of monsters by the giantess Angrboda: Fenrir (wolf), Jormungandr (Midgard Serpent), Hel (queen of the dead). Also mother (yes, mother) of Sleipnir, Odin's 8-legged horse.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Blood-brother of Odin" or "the trickster of Asgard" — near-universal giveaway.
  • Bound beneath a mountain with serpent venom dripping on his face; wife Sigyn holds a bowl.
  • Responsible for the death of Baldr via the mistletoe dart thrown by blind Hodr.

High-Yield Myths & Episodes (Very Common)

The 6 stories / roles most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Death of Baldr
Key myth · Prose Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 49 · Poetic Edda, Baldrs draumar
powerLoki disguises himself as an old woman to learn from Frigg that she forgot to make mistletoe swear an oath. He cuts a dart from mistletoe and guides blind Hodr's throw.
recogBaldr (son of Odin and Frigg), beloved and invulnerable, is struck dead; the gods fail to retrieve him from Hel because a single giantess (Thokk, Loki in disguise) refuses to weep for him.
recogLoki is later captured and bound as punishment for Baldr's death and his subsequent taunting of the gods in Lokasenna.

Loki's Binding (Lokasenna & aftermath)
Key myth · Poetic Edda, Lokasenna; Prose Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 50
powerAt a feast, Loki kills the servant Fimafeng; when expelled, he returns to accuse every god of cowardice or sexual misconduct — the poem Lokasenna ("Loki's Quarrel").
recogCaptured and bound in a cave with the entrails of his son Narfi (or Nari); a serpent drips venom on his face; wife Sigyn holds a bowl — when she empties it, his writhing causes earthquakes.
recogHe remains bound until Ragnarök, when he breaks free and steers the ship Naglfar (made of dead men's nails) against the gods.

Birth of Sleipnir (The Builder Myth)
Key myth · Prose Edda, Gylfaginning ch. 42
powerA giant offers to build Asgard's walls; Loki advises the gods to accept with the condition it be done in one winter using only his horse Svadilfari. The stallion does too much of the work, so Loki transforms into a mare to lure Svadilfari away.
recogLoki becomes pregnant by Svadilfari and gives birth to the eight-legged horse Sleipnir, which he presents to Odin — making Loki the mother of Odin's steed.
recogThe outraged builder is revealed as a jotun (frost giant) and killed by Thor.

Theft of Thor's Hammer (Þrymskviða)
Key myth · Poetic Edda, Þrymskviða
powerGiant Thrym steals Mjolnir and demands Freyja as his bride to return it; it is Loki who devises the plan and accompanies Thor to Thrym's hall.
recogLoki and Thor dress as the bride and her handmaid; at the wedding feast Thor is unmasked when he devours whole oxen, but Loki covers by saying the "bride" has not eaten in eight days from longing.
recogThor retrieves Mjolnir placed in his lap, kills Thrym and the entire wedding party.

Sif's Hair & the Dwarven Treasures
Key myth · Prose Edda, Skáldskaparmál
powerLoki cuts off Sif's (Thor's wife) golden hair while she sleeps; threatened by Thor, he travels to Svartalfheim (the realm of the dwarves) to have new golden hair made.
recogThe dwarves Sons of Ivaldi craft Sif's hair, Odin's spear Gungnir, and Freyr's ship Skidbladnir; Loki then bets his own head that brothers Brokkr and Sindri cannot match them.
recogBrokkr and Sindri forge Mjolnir, Odin's ring Draupnir, and Freyr's boar Gullinbursti; Loki (who interfered as a fly) loses the bet but saves himself on the technicality that his neck was not wagered, so Brokkr sews his lips shut.

Children of Loki & Angrboda
Key figures · Prose Edda, Gylfaginning
powerThe giantess Angrboda ("she who brings grief") is Loki's consort in Jotunheim; the gods learn a prophecy that her children will bring ruin and forcibly retrieve them.
recogThree offspring: Fenrir (wolf bound by the ribbon Gleipnir; will swallow Odin at Ragnarök), Jormungandr (the Midgard Serpent cast into the ocean encircling all land; kills Thor at Ragnarök), Hel (half-living, half-dead; rules the realm of the dead also called Hel/Niflheim).
recogA fourth child by his wife Sigyn: Narfi/Nari, whose entrails bind Loki after Baldr's death.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Epithets & kennings

  • Lopt — alternate name (possibly "air" or "loft")
  • Logi — fire personification, often confused with Loki
  • Sky-treader, crafty one — common kenning descriptors
  • "Blood-brother of Odin" — the canonical relationship marker
  • Fárbauti's son — patronymic giveaway
  • Laufeyjarson — rare matronymic (son of Laufey)

Key relationships

  • Odin — sworn blood-brother; frequent companion
  • Thor — close ally in adventures; Loki often causes and resolves Thor's problems
  • Sigyn — loyal wife; holds bowl over bound Loki
  • Angrboda — giantess consort; mother of the three monsters
  • Heimdall — mortal enemy; mutual slayers at Ragnarök
  • Baldr — victim of Loki's plot; most pivotal act

Children & creations

  • Fenrir — wolf son; bites off Tyr's hand while being bound
  • Jormungandr — Midgard Serpent; encircles world; kills Thor
  • Hel — daughter; rules realm of the honored dead; half black/half flesh-colored
  • Sleipnir — 8-legged horse; Loki is the mother; given to Odin
  • Narfi/Nari — son by Sigyn; killed so his guts bind Loki

Primary source markers

  • Prose Edda by Snorri Sturluson (~1220 CE) — main narrative source
  • Poetic Edda — Lokasenna ("Loki's quarrel"), Þrymskviða, Völuspá
  • Völuspá — the "Prophecy of the Völva"; describes Loki's binding and Ragnarök role
  • Saxo Grammaticus — Gesta Danorum; Loki appears as Utgarthilocus
  • Lokasenna — Loki insults every god in turn; key for character study

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Utgard-LokiA completely separate giant king who rules Utgard; humiliates Thor's party with illusions. The name similarity is the trap. Utgard-Loki does not shapeshift or cause Ragnarök.
LogiPersonification of fire (wildfire); defeats Loki in an eating contest in the Utgard visit. Logi ≠ Loki despite near-identical names.
Odin (Trickster)Odin also uses disguise and cunning, but Odin is All-Father / king of gods. Loki is the trickster specifically — if the clue says "blood-brother," think Loki, not Odin.
Prometheus (Greek)Frequent comparison: both trickster figures, both bound as punishment (eagle vs. serpent venom), both associated with beneficial gifts to gods/humans. Key difference: Prometheus is Titan, not a god; Loki's binding is for sabotage, not beneficence.
Hermes / MercuryBoth trickster messengers, both aid heroes in quests. Hermes is an Olympian; Loki is Norse. Loki shapeshifts much more radically and is explicitly a villain by myth's end.
Coyote (Native American)Trickster archetype comparison in academic contexts; Coyote myths are Pan-Native American (many traditions), not Norse — if the clue says "Old Norse" or "Edda," it's Loki.
FenrirFenrir is Loki's son, not Loki himself. If the clue says "wolf bound by Gleipnir who bit off Tyr's hand," that's Fenrir.

🌍 Cross-discipline "big picture" Loki is the paradigm case of the trickster archetype in comparative mythology (Raven, Coyote, Hermes, Anansi) — a figure who exists at the boundary of order and chaos, inside and outside the community. His trajectory from useful ally to bound prisoner to Ragnarök captain maps onto eschatological narratives worldwide. In literature, Loki connects to questions on Neil Gaiman (American Gods, Norse Mythology), Richard Wagner (Loge in the Ring Cycle), and the scholarly debate over whether he was an ancient fire deity (Bugge, Dumézil) or a purely literary creation. For comparative religion, pair with Prometheus (Greek) and Coyote (Pan-Indigenous North American) as cross-cultural trickster types.
