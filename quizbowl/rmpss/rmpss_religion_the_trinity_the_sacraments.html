Quick Overview

Core doctrineTrinity: one God, three co-equal persons — Father, Son (Jesus Christ), Holy Spirit
Key termhomoousios ("of the same substance") — Nicene formula, 325 CE
Sacraments (RC)Seven: Baptism, Eucharist, Confirmation, Penance, Anointing, Holy Orders, Matrimony
Sacraments (Protestant)Most Protestant traditions: two — Baptism and Eucharist (Lord's Supper)
Primary textsNicene Creed (381), Apostles' Creed; Council of Trent (1545–63) for sacramental theology
Key thinkersTertullian, Athanasius, Augustine, Aquinas, Luther, Calvin, Zwingli

The Trinity — core claims

  • Three persons (hypostases), one substance (ousia) — the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit are distinct but not separate gods.
  • Nicene Creed (325/381 CE): the Son is homoousios with the Father — against Arianism (Son is subordinate/created).
  • Filioque controversy: Western (Catholic) creed adds that the Spirit proceeds "from the Father and the Son" (filioque) — rejected by Eastern Orthodoxy; a root cause of the Great Schism (1054).
  • Perichoresis (Greek) / circumincession (Latin): mutual indwelling of the three persons.
  • Modalism (Sabellianism): heresy that the three are modes of one person, not three distinct persons.

The sacraments — core frame

  • A sacrament is an outward sign instituted by Christ to give grace (classic Catholic definition).
  • Augustine: "visible word" (verbum visibile); sacrament = sign that effects what it signifies.
  • Thomas Aquinas: sacraments cause grace ex opere operato (by the act performed), not by the minister's merit.
  • Number fixed at seven by the Council of Florence (1439) and confirmed at Trent (1547).
  • Luther: only two true sacraments (Baptism and the Lord's Supper); Calvin agrees on two; some traditions add Penance as a third.

The figures, controversies, and doctrines most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard, early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Council of Nicaea (325 CE) and the Nicene Creed
Key doctrine: homoousios · anti-Arian · convened by Constantine I
recogFirst ecumenical council; debated the nature of Christ — subordinate to God (Arius) vs. co-equal/same substance (Athanasius).
recogProduced the Nicene Creed (expanded at Constantinople, 381) — declares Son is homoousios (consubstantial) with the Father.
powerArius held the Son was the first and highest creation (homoiousios — "of similar substance," not identical). The one-iota difference (homoousios vs. homoiousios) is a classic hard clue. Athanasius ("Athanasius contra mundum") defended the Nicene formula through multiple exiles.

Baptism
First sacrament · "new birth" · water + Trinitarian formula
recogRite of initiation using water in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit (Matt. 28:19 — the "Great Commission").
recogRegeneration / forgiveness of original sin — Catholic and Lutheran theology hold baptism regenerates; Calvinist/Reformed hold it is a covenant sign.
powerInfant vs. believer's baptism: Catholics, Orthodox, Lutherans, Anglicans practice infant baptism (paedobaptism); Anabaptists and Baptists insist on adult/believer's baptism (credobaptism). The Anabaptist insistence on re-baptism was the cause of their persecution. John the Baptist prefigures Christian baptism; Jesus's baptism at the Jordan by John is the prototype event.

Eucharist (Lord's Supper / Holy Communion)
Central sacrament · bread + wine · institution narrative
recogRite re-enacting the Last Supper; based on the words of institution: "This is my body… this is my blood" (synoptic gospels + 1 Cor. 11:23–26).
recogTransubstantiation: Catholic doctrine that the bread and wine become the actual body and blood of Christ (substance changes, accidents remain) — defined at the Fourth Lateran Council (1215) and confirmed at Trent.
powerThree classic Protestant positions: (1) Luther's consubstantiation — body and blood are "in, with, and under" the bread/wine (real presence maintained); (2) Calvin's spiritual presence — Christ is truly present but spiritually/by faith; (3) Zwingli's memorialism — the elements are purely symbolic, a memorial. The Marburg Colloquy (1529) between Luther and Zwingli failed to reconcile positions (1) and (3). The term Eucharist derives from Greek "thanksgiving" (eucharistia).

Tertullian and Augustine — Trinitarian Theology
Tertullian c. 200 CE · Augustine c. 400 CE
recogTertullian first formulated "one substance, three persons" (una substantia, tres personae) in Latin — coined the Latin term Trinitas.
recogAugustine's De Trinitate (On the Trinity, ~400–416 CE): psychological analogy — the Trinity mirrors the human mind's memory, understanding, and will.
powerAugustine: "Our heart is restless until it rests in Thee" (Confessions I.1). His De Trinitate is the foundational Western treatise. Tertullian also coined sacramentum as a Latin theological term. The Cappadocian Fathers (Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa, Gregory Nazianzus) developed Eastern Trinitarian theology using hypostasis.

Filioque Controversy and the Great Schism (1054)
Filioque added to Western creed c. 589 · formal schism 1054
recogLatin filioque ("and from the Son") inserted into the Nicene Creed by Western churches: the Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son.
recogEastern Orthodoxy rejected filioque as an unauthorized addition; this was a key theological flashpoint in the 1054 Great Schism (mutual excommunications of Pope Leo IX's legate Humbert and Patriarch Cerularius).
powerThe filioque was first formally added at the Third Council of Toledo (589) in Visigothic Spain. The Eastern position holds that the Spirit proceeds from the Father through the Son (not "and" the Son), preserving the Father as sole source (monarchia). Photius of Constantinople challenged it in the Photian Schism (863–867), foreshadowing 1054.
wiki ↗
Transubstantiation — Aquinas and Aristotelian Theology
Term coined 12th c. · defined Lateran IV 1215 · Aquinas Summa III q.75
recogThomas Aquinas used Aristotle's substance/accident distinction: the substance of bread and wine changes to body and blood; the accidents (taste, shape, smell) remain.
recogAquinas composed the Corpus Christi liturgy (hymns Pange Lingua / Tantum Ergo) celebrating the Eucharist — a frequent clue linking him to the sacrament.
powerThe feast of Corpus Christi (Body of Christ) was established by Pope Urban IV in 1264 at Aquinas's instigation. Luther's term for his alternative was sacramental union (not "consubstantiation" — which Luther never used). Wycliffe and Hus attacked transubstantiation before Luther, foreshadowing the Reformation.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Trinity — key terms & epithets

  • homoousios — "of the same substance" (Nicene, pro-orthodox)
  • homoiousios — "of similar substance" (Arian compromise)
  • hypostasis — individual person/subsistence within the Trinity
  • ousia — the shared divine essence/substance
  • Trinitas — coined by Tertullian in Latin
  • filioque — "and from the Son" (Western creed addition)
  • perichoresis — mutual indwelling of the three persons
  • monarchia — Eastern insistence on Father as sole source

Eucharist — key terms

  • transubstantiation — substance of bread/wine becomes Christ's body/blood
  • accidents — sensory properties that remain unchanged
  • ex opere operato — grace given by the act itself, regardless of minister
  • Real Presence — Lutheran term for Christ's bodily presence in elements
  • memorialism — Zwinglian view: elements are purely symbolic
  • spiritual presence — Calvinist view: Christ present by faith, not physically
  • eucharistia — Greek "thanksgiving" (source of the word)
  • anamnesis — "memorial/remembrance" (Greek term from 1 Cor. 11:24–25)

Baptism — key terms

  • paedobaptism — infant baptism
  • credobaptism — believer's baptism (adult)
  • regeneration — spiritual new birth through baptism
  • original sin — what baptism removes (Catholic/Lutheran)
  • immersion / aspersion / affusion — modes: full immersion, sprinkling, pouring
  • chrismation — Eastern Orthodox counterpart to Confirmation, done at baptism
  • Great Commission — Matt. 28:19, Trinitarian baptismal formula

Key figures & councils

  • Athanasius — champion of homoousios; exiled five times
  • Arius — Son is first created being; condemned 325
  • Tertullian — coined Trinitas, una substantia tres personae
  • Augustine — De Trinitate; psychological analogy; verbum visibile
  • Basil of Caesarea — Cappadocian; "the Spirit is not a creature"
  • Aquinas — transubstantiation via Aristotle; Corpus Christi liturgy
  • Luther — two sacraments; real presence; Marburg 1529
  • Zwingli — memorialism; died at Battle of Kappel (1531)
  • Calvin — spiritual presence; sacraments as covenant signs
  • Council of Nicaea I (325) — homoousios, anti-Arian
  • Council of Constantinople I (381) — expanded creed; Spirit's divinity
  • Fourth Lateran Council (1215) — transubstantiation defined
  • Council of Trent (1545–63) — seven sacraments confirmed

Comparison Traps

Don't confuseHow to tell them apart
homoousios vs. homoiousiosOne iota of difference: homoousios = "same substance" (Nicene/orthodox); homoiousios = "similar/like substance" (Arian compromise). Arius himself used homoios ("like"). The stakes were eternal — hence "not an iota's difference."
Transubstantiation vs. consubstantiation vs. memorialismTransubstantiation (Catholic): substance changes, accidents remain. "Sacramental union" (Luther): both body/blood and bread/wine fully present. Memorialism (Zwingli): pure symbol, no real presence. Spiritual presence (Calvin): real but not physical, received by faith.
Arianism vs. Modalism (Sabellianism)Arianism: three distinct beings, Son subordinate/created. Modalism: one person in three sequential modes (Father in OT, Son in incarnation, Spirit after). Both are heresies; opposites in their error — one has too many gods, the other too few persons.
Paedobaptism vs. CredobaptismPaedobaptism: infants baptized into the covenant (Catholic, Orthodox, Lutheran, Anglican). Credobaptism: only conscious believers baptized on profession of faith (Baptist, Anabaptist, most Evangelical). The Anabaptists were killed partly for "rebaptizing" adults already baptized as infants.
Filioque (West) vs. Eastern Orthodox positionWest: Spirit proceeds from Father and the Son (filioque). East: Spirit proceeds from the Father through or alone from the Father, not "and" the Son — to protect the Father's monarchia. East sees filioque as unauthorized unilateral insertion into an ecumenically ratified creed.
Council of Nicaea (325) vs. Council of Constantinople (381)Nicaea 325: resolved the Arian controversy; produced original Nicene Creed (focused on Son's nature). Constantinople 381: expanded creed; affirmed Holy Spirit's divinity against Macedonians/Pneumatomachians; added filioque-free Spirit clause. The combined text is the Niceno-Constantinopolitan Creed, still used liturgically.
Augustine's Trinity analogy vs. Aquinas's analogyAugustine: psychological analogy — memory/understanding/will mirrors Father/Son/Spirit. Aquinas: procession analogy — Son proceeds as Word (intellectual act), Spirit proceeds as Love (volitional act). Both are Latin-tradition analogies; Eastern theology (Cappadocians) emphasizes persons-in-communion, not psychological model.
Marburg Colloquy (1529) vs. Diet of Worms (1521)Marburg: Luther vs. Zwingli on the Eucharist — failed to unify Lutherans and Reformed. Diet of Worms: Luther before Emperor Charles V — "Here I stand" speech; Luther declared an outlaw. Both 1520s Reformation events but entirely different in topic and stakes.
