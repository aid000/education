Quick Overview

NationalityScottish (born Kirkcaldy, Fife, 1723–1790)
FieldMoral philosophy and political economy
Epithet"Father of modern economics" / "Father of capitalism"
Major institutionProfessor at University of Glasgow; later Edinburgh
Intellectual circleScottish Enlightenment — close friend of David Hume
Key datesTMS 1759 · WN 1776 · died Edinburgh 1790

Defining ideas (giveaway level)

  • Invisible hand — individuals pursuing self-interest inadvertently benefit society.
  • Division of labor — pin-factory example; specialization raises productivity enormously.
  • Free markets over mercantilism — opposes monopolies, guilds, tariffs, and the Navigation Acts.
  • Labor theory of value — the value of a commodity derives from the labor required to produce it.
  • Sympathy / moral sentiments — ethics grounded in fellow-feeling and the "impartial spectator."

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "The Wealth of Nations" — single most common Smith answer.
  • "invisible hand" — the phrase alone almost always answers to Smith.
  • "pin factory" — famous opening illustration of division of labor.
  • "impartial spectator" — key term from The Theory of Moral Sentiments.

High-Yield Works & Ideas (Very Common)

The works and concepts most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations
1776 · full 5-book treatise · published same year as U.S. Declaration of Independence
powerArgues against the mercantilist doctrine (trade as zero-sum); rejects the Physiocratic view that only agriculture creates wealth. Book IV systematically dismantles mercantile system policies.
recogOpens Book I with the pin factory: one worker makes ~20 pins/day alone; ten specialized workers produce 48,000/day — definitive illustration of division of labor.
recogContains the phrase "invisible hand" (used only once in WN, in Book IV) to describe how self-interest guides resources.
recogIntroduces the distinction between value in use vs. value in exchange (water–diamond paradox).
wiki ↗
The Theory of Moral Sentiments
1759 · Smith's first major book · based on his Glasgow lectures
powerSmith's ethical foundation: moral judgments arise from sympathy — imagining how an impartial spectator (a reasonable third party) would view an action. Predates and underpins WN.
recogThe "impartial spectator" is the internal moral check — a conscience figure that governs self-interested behavior.
recogThe apparent tension between self-interest (WN) and sympathy (TMS) is called the "Adam Smith problem" (a 19th-c. German framing, largely considered a false dichotomy today).
wiki ↗
Division of Labor
Core doctrine · WN Books I–III
powerSmith identifies three sources of productivity gain: (1) dexterity from specialization, (2) saved time from not switching tasks, (3) invention of machines by focused workers.
recogPin factory (Book I, Chapter 1) — the single most quoted passage in all of economics.
recog"The extent of the market" limits division of labor — larger markets allow deeper specialization (Book I, Chapter 3).
wiki ↗
Invisible Hand
Concept from TMS (1759) and WN (1776)
powerAppears three times in Smith's writings: once in The History of Astronomy (early ms.), once in TMS (Part IV), once in WN (Book IV, Ch. 2). Each context differs; the market-mechanism reading is the canonical modern one.
recogIn WN: a merchant preferring domestic to foreign investment is "led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention."
recogBecame the defining metaphor for laissez-faire / free-market economics and was adopted by later economists to summarize competitive markets.
wiki ↗
Critique of Mercantilism & Monopoly
WN Books IV–V
powerAttacks the Navigation Acts and colonial trade monopolies (especially the East India Company) as wealth-extracting rent-seeking. Argues British consumers pay higher prices to enrich merchants.
recogFamous line: "People of the same trade seldom meet together … but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public." — warns that merchants lobby for anticompetitive rules.
recogFavors free trade on absolute-advantage grounds; Ricardo later extended this to comparative advantage.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known works, lectures, and concepts — show up as early/power clues.

Lectures on Jurisprudence
delivered 1762–63; reconstructed from student notes
Smith's Glasgow lectures on justice, law, and governance — source material for ideas later in WN.
Essays on Philosophical Subjects
posthumous, 1795
Includes History of Astronomy — early ms. where "invisible hand" first appears in astronomical context.
The Water–Diamond Paradox
WN Book I, Chapter 4
Water has high use-value but low exchange-value; diamonds the reverse — sets up labor theory of value problem.
Natural vs. Market Price
WN Book I, Chapter 7
Natural price = cost of production; market price gravitates toward it via competition.
Productive vs. Unproductive Labor
WN Book II, Chapter 3
Productive labor adds to stock; unproductive labor (servants, soldiers, clergy) does not — controversial distinction.
Three Component Theory of Price
WN Book I, Chapter 6
Price resolves into wages + profit + rent — three orders (workers, capitalists, landlords).
Canons of Taxation
WN Book V, Chapter 2
Four principles: equity, certainty, convenience, economy — still cited in public finance.
Sympathy & Propriety (TMS)
TMS Part I
Virtue = matching emotion to what an impartial spectator would feel — not mere utility maximization.
Tutoring the Duke of Buccleuch
1764–66; Grand Tour of France
Met Voltaire, Quesnay, Turgot in Paris — Physiocrats influenced his agrarian analysis; provided financial security to write WN.
Commissioner of Customs
Edinburgh, 1778–1790
Ironic: the anti-tariff economist spent his last years enforcing customs duties.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key phrases & quotes

  • "invisible hand" — self-interest promotes social good
  • "pin factory" — division of labor example
  • "impartial spectator" — internal moral judge (TMS)
  • "People of the same trade…conspiracy against the public" — anti-monopoly warning
  • "water–diamond paradox" — use-value vs. exchange-value
  • "butcher, brewer, baker" — self-interest passage (WN I.2)

Biographical anchors

  • Born Kirkcaldy, Fife, Scotland, 1723
  • Studied at Glasgow (under Francis Hutcheson) then Oxford (Balliol; hated it)
  • Chair of Moral Philosophy, University of Glasgow
  • Close friend of David Hume (Hume's literary executor)
  • Met Voltaire, Quesnay, Turgot in Paris, 1764–66
  • Died Edinburgh, 1790; reportedly destroyed most manuscripts

Intellectual context

  • Scottish Enlightenment — Hutcheson, Hume, Ferguson
  • Physiocrats (Quesnay, Turgot) — key French influence; Smith critiques their agriculture-only view
  • Francis Hutcheson — Smith's teacher; originated "greatest happiness" idea
  • Published WN same year as U.S. Declaration of Independence (1776)
  • Classical economics lineage: Smith → Ricardo → Mill → Marx (critique)

Common answer-web links

  • WN ↔ laissez-faire ↔ free trade ↔ Ricardo's comparative advantage
  • TMS ↔ moral philosophy ↔ Hutcheson ↔ Hume
  • "invisible hand" ↔ general equilibrium theory ↔ Walras
  • Division of labor ↔ Marx's alienation (critique)
  • East India Company critique ↔ colonialism / imperialism tossups

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
David RicardoRicardo = comparative advantage (not absolute), Principles of Political Economy and Taxation (1817), iron law of wages, Ricardo equivalence. Smith precedes Ricardo; Ricardo extends and critiques him.
John Stuart MillMill = utilitarianism, Principles of Political Economy (1848), On Liberty, The Subjection of Women. Also classical economics but later and more liberal/reform-minded.
Thomas MalthusMalthus = population theory (An Essay on the Principle of Population, 1798) — population grows geometrically, food arithmetically. Pessimist; friend and rival of Ricardo, not Smith.
David HumeHume = empiricist philosopher (Treatise of Human Nature, Enquiries), also wrote on economics (quantity theory of money). Smith's close friend but Hume is philosopher, not economist.
Karl MarxMarx built on Smith's labor theory of value to argue for exploitation; his answer = Capital, Communist Manifesto, dialectical materialism, surplus value. Anti-capitalist; Smith is pro-market.
François QuesnayQuesnay = French Physiocrat, Tableau économique (1758), argues only agriculture creates wealth. Smith met him in Paris and was influenced but disagreed on manufacturing.
Jeremy BenthamBentham = utilitarianism founder, Introduction to the Principles of Morals and Legislation (1789), felicific calculus, panopticon. Contemporary of Smith but different ethical framework.

🌐 Cross-discipline big picture Smith bridges moral philosophy, economics, and political theory. His TMS grounds markets in human psychology (sympathy, conscience), his WN makes the empirical case for free markets, and his attack on monopolies connects to political debates on colonialism and state power. The invisible hand metaphor anchors tossups across economics, philosophy, and history of ideas; the pin factory anchors tossups on industrialization and division of labor. Smith is also a bridge answer to David Hume (philosophy), David Ricardo (classical economics), and Karl Marx (critique of capitalism).
