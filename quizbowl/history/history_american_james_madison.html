Quick Overview

Presidency4th President, 1809–1817 (two terms)
PartyDemocratic-Republican
Nickname"Father of the Constitution" · "Little Jemmy"
CollegeCollege of New Jersey (now Princeton); studied under John Witherspoon
SpouseDolley Payne Todd (married 1794)
PlantationMontpelier, Orange County, Virginia
DiedJune 28, 1836 — last surviving Founder / Framer

Defining roles

  • Principal drafter of the Constitution (1787 Philadelphia Convention); kept the most detailed notes.
  • Co-author (with Hamilton and Jay) of The Federalist Papers — wrote 29 of 85 essays.
  • Drafted the Bill of Rights (first ten amendments, 1789–1791) as a House member.
  • Co-founded (with Jefferson) the Democratic-Republican Party to oppose Hamiltonian Federalism.
  • Led the nation through the War of 1812 — the first declared war under the Constitution.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Father of the Constitution" — the single most-given-away descriptor.
  • Federalist No. 51 ("If men were angels…") and Federalist No. 10 (factions / extended republic).
  • Dolley Madison saving the Lansdowne portrait of Washington during the burning of D.C. (1814).
  • War of 1812 + White House torched by the British.

High-Yield Events & Works

The topics most likely to anchor a tossup about Madison. power = hard/early clue. recog = recognition/giveaway.

The Federalist Papers (esp. Nos. 10 & 51)
1787–1788 · Pseudonym "Publius" · co-authors Hamilton & Jay
powerMadison wrote 29 of the 85 essays. Federalist No. 10 argues that a large republic best controls factions — more sphere, more factions, none dominant. Federalist No. 51 argues for separation of powers / checks and balances: "If men were angels, no government would be necessary."
recogFederalist No. 10 = factions / extended republic. Federalist No. 51 = "If men were angels" / ambition counteracting ambition.
recogPublished in New York newspapers under "Publius"; collected as The Federalist. Hamilton wrote 51, Madison 29, Jay 5.
Constitutional Convention / Virginia Plan
Summer 1787 · Philadelphia · "Father of the Constitution"
powerMadison drafted the Virginia Plan — a bicameral legislature with proportional representation, a national executive, and a national judiciary — submitted by Edmund Randolph on opening day. Madison kept the most detailed surviving notes of the Convention debates.
recogEarned the title "Father of the Constitution", though he rejected it, saying the Constitution was "the work of many heads and many hands."
recogThe Virginia Plan was countered by the New Jersey Plan (Patterson); the Connecticut Compromise (Sherman) split the difference with the modern Senate/House structure.
Bill of Rights (First Ten Amendments)
Proposed 1789 · Ratified 1791 · House of Representatives
powerMadison initially opposed a bill of rights (thought it a "parchment barrier"), but promised one during Virginia's ratification debate to win over Anti-Federalists (including George Mason). He introduced 12 amendments; 10 were ratified.
recogDrafted while serving in the First Congress; drew heavily on George Mason's Virginia Declaration of Rights (1776).
recogThe 2nd of Madison's 12 proposed amendments (congressional pay) was finally ratified in 1992 as the 27th Amendment.
Marbury v. Madison (1803)
U.S. Supreme Court · Chief Justice John Marshall
powerMadison (as Secretary of State) refused to deliver William Marbury's commission as justice of the peace — a midnight appointment by outgoing President Adams. Marshall's ruling established judicial review (power to strike down federal laws as unconstitutional) while ruling against Marbury on narrow grounds.
recogMadison did not write the opinion — Chief Justice John Marshall did. Madison is the defendant in the case title.
recogEstablished that the Judiciary Act of 1789 (Sec. 13) unconstitutionally expanded the Court's original jurisdiction; first use of judicial review of federal law.
War of 1812
June 1812 – February 1815 · Madison's presidency
powerMadison asked Congress for a declaration of war citing impressment of U.S. sailors, interference with U.S. shipping, and British support for Native American resistance ("War Hawks" — Henry Clay, John C. Calhoun). First declared war under the Constitution.
recogAugust 1814: British forces under General Robert Ross burned the Capitol and the White House; Dolley Madison famously rescued the Lansdowne portrait of Washington and the cabinet documents.
recogEnded by the Treaty of Ghent (Dec. 24, 1814) — status quo ante bellum. Battle of New Orleans (Andrew Jackson, Jan. 1815) was fought after the treaty but boosted U.S. morale.
Virginia and Kentucky Resolutions (1798)
1798 · Madison (Virginia) + Jefferson (Kentucky)
powerWritten in secret response to Adams's Alien and Sedition Acts. Madison's Virginia Resolutions argued that states could "interpose" between citizens and unconstitutional federal laws — but stopped short of nullification. Jefferson's Kentucky Resolutions used the stronger word "nullification."
recogLater invoked by South Carolina in the Nullification Crisis (1832) — Madison explicitly disowned that reading, insisting he never meant unilateral nullification.
recogThe Acts targeted French immigrants and criticism of the Adams administration; Madison and Jefferson feared the acts as an attack on First Amendment freedoms.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-cited events and facts that appear as hard/early clues. One-line IDs.

  • Virginia Plan (1787)
    Philadelphia Convention
    Madison's blueprint; submitted by Edmund Randolph on opening day.
  • Federalist No. 39 & No. 46
    1788
    No. 39 defines "republic"; No. 46 argues states can resist federal overreach.
  • Memorial and Remonstrance Against Religious Assessments
    1785 · Virginia
    Petition opposing Patrick Henry's bill taxing citizens for churches; underpins First Amendment.
  • Friendship & Alliance with Thomas Jefferson
    1776–1836
    Closest political partnership in Founding era; neighbors in Virginia.
  • Macon's Bill No. 2 (1810)
    Madison presidency
    Reopened trade with Britain and France; whichever repealed restrictions, U.S. would embargo the other.
  • Non-Intercourse Act (1809)
    Replaced Embargo Act
    Banned trade specifically with Britain and France; less damaging than Jefferson's total embargo.
  • Re-charter of the Second Bank of the United States
    1816
    Madison reversed his earlier anti-Bank stance; signed its 20-year charter.
  • Bonus Bill Veto (1817)
    Last act as president
    Vetoed internal improvements bill — said Constitution gave no such power; ironic given nationalist turn.
  • Battle of Bladensburg (1814)
    War of 1812
    Disastrous U.S. defeat opened road to Washington; Madison briefly near the front lines.
  • Hartford Convention (1814–15)
    New England Federalists
    Federalist opposition to the war; proposed constitutional amendments; effectively ended Federalist Party.
  • Gerry-mandering (1812)
    Elbridge Gerry / Massachusetts
    Madison's VP Elbridge Gerry signed a redistricting bill that gave "gerrymander" to the language.
  • Notes of Debates in the Federal Convention of 1787
    Published posthumously 1840
    Madison's meticulous daily notes — the primary source on the Constitutional Convention.

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Thomas Jefferson Jefferson = Declaration of Independence, Louisiana Purchase, Lewis & Clark, 3rd president. Madison = Constitution, Bill of Rights, Federalist Papers, 4th president. Both co-founded Democratic-Republican Party and wrote the 1798 resolutions, but Kentucky Resolutions = Jefferson; Virginia Resolutions = Madison.
Alexander Hamilton Both wrote Federalist Papers, but Hamilton wrote 51 essays; Madison wrote 29. Hamilton favored a strong central bank and commercial economy; Madison eventually opposed both. Hamilton = Federalist Party; Madison = Democratic-Republican Party.
John Adams Adams signed the Alien and Sedition Acts (which Madison opposed). Adams was 2nd president; Madison was 4th. The Marbury commission was Adams's midnight appointment — Madison (as Secretary of State) refused to deliver it.
James Monroe Monroe = 5th president, "Era of Good Feelings," Monroe Doctrine. He succeeded Madison. Both were Democratic-Republicans from Virginia, but Monroe is not associated with the Constitution or Federalist Papers.
George Mason Mason drafted the Virginia Declaration of Rights (Madison used it for the Bill of Rights) and refused to sign the Constitution (Anti-Federalist). Madison is often confused with Mason on religious liberty questions — but Madison wrote the Memorial and Remonstrance; Mason wrote the Virginia Declaration.
War of 1812 vs. other early wars War of 1812 = Madison + British impressment + Hartford Convention + Dolley saves portrait + Battle of New Orleans (Jackson). Do not confuse with XYZ Affair / Quasi-War (Adams) or Louisiana Purchase era conflicts (Jefferson).
Nullification Crisis (1832) The Nullification Crisis is Jackson vs. Calhoun / South Carolina — not Madison. Madison's 1798 Virginia Resolutions used interposition, not nullification. Madison himself in 1832 wrote against South Carolina's use of his argument.

Big Picture: Federalism as a System Madison is the pivot point of the Founding era's central intellectual problem: how to balance liberty against order, and states against the nation. Federalist No. 10 solves the faction problem through scale; No. 51 solves the tyranny problem through institutional rivalry. The Bill of Rights addresses the same tension from below. The Virginia Resolutions introduce the interposition idea that haunts states' rights debates through the Civil War and into the 20th century. Tossup writers use Madison to test whether competitors understand these theoretical fault lines, not just the biographical facts.
