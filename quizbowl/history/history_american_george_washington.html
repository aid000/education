Quick Overview

Born / Died1732, Westmoreland Co., Virginia — 1799, Mount Vernon, Virginia
Presidency1st President, 1789–1797; two terms; inaugurated in New York City
Military roleCommander-in-Chief, Continental Army, 1775–1783
Key epithet"Father of His Country"; "First in war, first in peace, first in the hearts of his countrymen"
Home / estateMount Vernon, on the Potomac River, Virginia
Vice PresidentJohn Adams (both terms)

Life phases — at a glance

1732–1752
Virginia planter / surveyor
Grew up in Westmoreland and later Fredericksburg; became a surveyor of Virginia's western frontier at age 16.
1753–1758
French and Indian War
Sent by Virginia governor Robert Dinwiddie to warn the French; survived Fort Necessity (1754) and Braddock's defeat at the Monongahela (1755); rose to colonel commanding Virginia Regiment.
1759–1774
Burgess & planter
Married Martha Dandridge Custis (1759); member of Virginia House of Burgesses; grew tobacco at Mount Vernon; shifted to wheat and diversified economy after frustrations with British merchants.
1775–1783
Revolutionary War command
Appointed Commander-in-Chief by the Second Continental Congress; led army through defeats and victories; accepted Cornwallis's surrender at Yorktown, Oct 19, 1781.
1787–1788
Constitutional Convention
Presided over the Philadelphia Convention; his presence gave the Constitution legitimacy; it was widely assumed he would be the first president.
1789–1797
Presidency
Two terms; set major precedents; managed Hamilton–Jefferson cabinet rivalry; issued Farewell Address (1796) warning against "permanent alliances" and factionalism.
Dec 14, 1799
Death at Mount Vernon
Died of acute epiglottitis (likely aggravated by bloodletting); physicians James Craik and Elisha Dick attended him.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Crossing the Delaware (Christmas night 1776) and the attack on Trenton.
  • Farewell Address warning against entangling alliances and political parties.
  • Valley Forge winter encampment, 1777–78.

High-Yield Events & Acts

The 7 topics most likely to anchor a tossup about Washington. power = early/hard clue. recog = recognition / mid-question clue.

Crossing of the Delaware & Battle of Trenton
Night of Dec 25–26, 1776 · Trenton, New Jersey
powerWashington's army crossed in Durham boats through ice floes; Col. Johann Rall commanded the Hessian garrison at Trenton and was mortally wounded.
recog900 Hessian prisoners taken; no American fatalities in combat; the attack came at dawn after Christmas when the garrison was reportedly off-guard.
recogFamously depicted in Emanuel Leutze's 1851 painting — Washington standing in a boat, flag flying, ice floes in the river.
wiki ↗
Valley Forge Encampment
Dec 1777 – Jun 1778 · Chester County, Pennsylvania
powerBaron Friedrich von Steuben arrived in February 1778 and drilled the army into a professional force; Steuben's Regulations for the Order and Discipline of the Troops ("the Blue Book") became the standard manual.
recog~2,500 soldiers died from cold, disease, and starvation; the army nearly dissolved; Nathanael Greene took over as Quartermaster General to restore supply lines.
recogIconic image: Washington kneeling in prayer in the snow — widely reproduced (though likely apocryphal).
wiki ↗
Siege of Yorktown
Sep 28 – Oct 19, 1781 · Yorktown, Virginia
powerFrench fleet under Admiral de Grasse blocked British relief at the Battle of the Chesapeake (Sept 5); French General Rochambeau co-commanded ground forces alongside Washington.
recogBritish General Lord Cornwallis surrendered; his subordinate Charles O'Hara presented the sword (Cornwallis claimed illness) — to Benjamin Lincoln, Washington's subordinate, in a deliberate protocol snub.
recogThe band allegedly played "The World Turned Upside Down"; this battle effectively ended major fighting in the Revolution.
wiki ↗
Constitutional Convention
May–Sep 1787 · Philadelphia (Independence Hall)
powerWashington presided but spoke almost nothing during debates; his silent authority was described by delegates as essential to the Convention's legitimacy; James Madison called it the "Virginia Plan" convention.
recogElected president of the Convention unanimously; his willingness to serve as president signaled the executive article was safe to make strong.
wiki ↗
Farewell Address
Sep 19, 1796 · Published in Philadelphia Daily American Advertiser
powerDrafted with major input from Alexander Hamilton (who wrote the final version from Washington's earlier Madison draft of 1792); never delivered as a speech — published as an open letter.
recogThree main warnings: against permanent foreign alliances, against political parties / factionalism, and against sectionalism.
recogQuoted in Senate debates about foreign policy to the present day; Congress reads it aloud each February.
wiki ↗
Whiskey Rebellion
1794 · Western Pennsylvania
powerThe 1791 excise tax on distilled spirits (part of Hamilton's financial program) sparked the revolt; Washington personally led ~13,000 militia west — the only time a sitting U.S. president led troops in the field.
recogRebellion collapsed without major bloodshed; two leaders convicted of treason were later pardoned by Washington; it demonstrated federal authority over the states.
wiki ↗
Resignation of Command / Voluntary Retirement
Dec 23, 1783 · Annapolis, Maryland (Congress) · 1797 after presidency
powerWashington resigned his commission to the Continental Congress in Annapolis — stunning European observers who expected him to seize power; King George III reportedly called him "the greatest man in the world" if true.
recogHis decision to leave after two presidential terms — though eligible for a third — set the two-term tradition codified only by the 22nd Amendment (1951).

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known episodes and facts that appear as early / power-mark clues. One-line ID each.

1753
Mission to Fort Le Boeuf
Washington (21) delivered Dinwiddie's ultimatum to French; his published journal made him famous in Britain.
1754
Jumonville Glen / Fort Necessity
Ambush of French officer Jumonville sparked the French & Indian War; Washington surrendered Fort Necessity shortly after.
1755
Braddock's Defeat (Monongahela)
Washington's horses shot out; he rallied survivors; legend of battlefield protection grew from this fight.
1776
Battle of Long Island
Worst American defeat; Washington evacuated 9,000 troops overnight across the East River in fog — the "Brooklyn Miracle."
1777
Battle of Brandywine
British flanked through Osborne's Hill; Washington defeated; led to British occupation of Philadelphia.
1777–78
Conway Cabal
Suspected plot by Gen. Horatio Gates, Thomas Conway, and others to replace Washington with Gates; Washington survived politically.
1779
Sullivan-Clinton Campaign
Washington ordered Gen. John Sullivan to devastate Iroquois Confederacy in western NY/PA; called "Town Destroyer" by Haudenosaunee.
1783
Newburgh Conspiracy / Address
Officers threatened mutiny over back pay; Washington personally appealed — put on spectacles, saying "I have grown gray and nearly blind"; officers wept.
1789
Judiciary Act / First Cabinet
Cabinet: Jefferson (State), Hamilton (Treasury), Knox (War), Randolph (AG); Judiciary Act created federal court system.
1793
Proclamation of Neutrality
Declared U.S. neutral in French Revolutionary Wars; angered Jeffersonians who backed France; set precedent for executive foreign-policy power.
1794–95
Jay Treaty
Negotiated by Chief Justice John Jay; settled issues with Britain post-Revolution; deeply unpopular with Democratic-Republicans; Washington's most controversial act.
1795
Pinckney's Treaty
With Spain; gave U.S. navigation rights on the Mississippi and the right of deposit at New Orleans; much more popular than the Jay Treaty.
1799
Death & Physicians
Died after being bled four times in 12 hours; doctors included James Craik (lifelong friend) and Elisha Dick, who opposed aggressive bloodletting.

Comparison Traps

Events and figures frequently confused with Washington in buzzer competition.

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
John Adams (2nd President)Adams fought the Quasi-War with France (1798–1800), signed the Alien & Sedition Acts, and lost re-election to Jefferson. Washington never faced a French quasi-war; he issued the Neutrality Proclamation (1793) instead.
Battle of Saratoga (1777)Horatio Gates commanded at Saratoga, not Washington. Benedict Arnold's heroic charge is the power clue. Washington was at Brandywine / Germantown. Saratoga brought France into the war.
Battle of Bunker Hill (1775)Washington was not yet in command — he was en route to Cambridge. William Prescott and Israel Putnam led the Americans; British under Howe attacked.
Thomas Paine's Common SensePaine wrote Common Sense; Washington had it read to his troops but did not write it. Common Sense / The American Crisis ("These are the times that try men's souls") = Paine, not Washington.
Jay Treaty vs. Pinckney's TreatyJay Treaty = with Britain, unpopular, negotiated by Chief Justice John Jay (1794–95). Pinckney's Treaty = with Spain, popular, opened Mississippi navigation (1795). Both under Washington.
The Constitutional Convention vs. the Continental CongressWashington presided at the Constitutional Convention (Philadelphia, 1787) but served as a delegate in the Continental Congress (1774–75). He did not preside at any Congress session.
Benedict Arnold's treasonArnold's treason (1780, West Point) happened under Washington's command — Washington discovered it. His co-conspirator Major John André was captured and executed. Arnold fled to the British.

Big Picture: Washington's Constitutional Legacy Washington is less a subject of military history than of precedent-setting constitutional governance. Nearly every quizbowl tossup about him touches something he did first and thereby defined: the two-term limit (codified only by the 22nd Amendment in 1951); the cabinet as an advisory body; the Proclamation of Neutrality as an assertion of executive foreign-policy authority; and the voluntary resignation of power — compared to ancient Rome's Cincinnatus and contrasted with every European military strongman of the era. The meta-clue is: if a question is about "establishing precedents" or "what no other leader did," the answer is almost always Washington.
