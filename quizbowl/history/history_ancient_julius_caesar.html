Quick Overview

Full nameGaius Julius Caesar
Born / Died100 BCE – 44 BCE (Ides of March)
Class / gensPatrician; gens Julia (claimed descent from Venus via Aeneas)
Final titleDictator perpetuo (dictator in perpetuity), Feb 44 BCE
Key relationshipsAlly/rival: Pompey, Crassus; lover: Cleopatra VII; great-nephew/heir: Octavian
WritingsCommentarii de Bello Gallico; Commentarii de Bello Civili

Why he matters for quizbowl

  • Central figure bridging the Roman Republic and Empire — his murder triggers the sequence ending in Augustus's principate.
  • Appears in tossups on: Roman history, Shakespeare's Julius Caesar, Plutarch's Lives, military history (Gallic Wars, Civil War), calendar reform, rhetoric, and classical biography.
  • Classic giveaway: "Et tu, Brute?" (Shakespeare's line) and Ides of March (March 15, 44 BCE) assassination.

Career arc (one-line each)

  • Populares politician — allied with Marius's faction; nephew of Marius; survived Sulla's proscriptions.
  • Pontifex Maximus (63 BCE) — won the chief religious office, key power move.
  • First Triumvirate (60 BCE) — informal alliance with Pompey and Crassus.
  • Gallic Wars (58–50 BCE) — conquered all of Gaul; crossed Rhine; invaded Britain twice.
  • Civil War (49–45 BCE) — crossed the Rubicon; defeated Pompey at Pharsalus; campaigns in Egypt, Africa, Spain.
  • Dictator perpetuo and subsequent reforms before assassination.

High-Yield Events (Very Common)

The 6 events / topics most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / giveaway clue.

Crossing the Rubicon
January 49 BCE · Rubicon River, northeastern Italy
recogCaesar led the 13th Legion south across the Rubicon, the boundary between his province and Italy proper — making his return to Rome a declaration of war.
recogThe Senate had ordered him to disband his army; crossing it was high treason. Pompey and the Senate fled Rome.
powerAncient sources (Suetonius) give his reported words as "Alea iacta est" ("The die is cast"). The phrase and idiom "crossing the Rubicon" both appear as quizbowl clues. The river was likely the Fiumicino or Pisciatello.
wiki ↗
Battle of Pharsalus
48 BCE · Pharsalus, Thessaly (Greece)
recogCaesar's outnumbered force crushed Pompey's larger army; Pompey fled to Egypt, where he was assassinated on arrival.
recogCaesar was significantly outnumbered (approx. 22,000 vs. 45,000) but won decisively through superior tactics.
powerCaesar anchored his right flank with a hidden fourth cavalry line that surprised and routed Pompey's cavalry. Pompey escaped to Egypt, where Ptolemy XIII had him killed as a gift — Caesar wept at Pompey's severed head.
wiki ↗
Assassination on the Ides of March
March 15, 44 BCE · Theatre of Pompey, Rome
recogStabbed 23 times by a group of senators called the Liberatores, led by Cassius Longinus and Marcus Junius Brutus.
recogKilled at a meeting of the Senate in the Theatre of Pompey (the Curia Pompeia); fell at the base of Pompey's statue.
powerA physician later concluded only one wound was fatal. Caesar had been warned by the soothsayer Spurinna to "beware the Ides of March." His last words are disputed: Suetonius says he pulled his toga over his face; only Shakespeare gives him "Et tu, Brute?"
wiki ↗
Gallic Wars
58–50 BCE · Gaul (modern France, Belgium, Switzerland)
recogCaesar's conquest of Gaul, documented in Commentarii de Bello Gallico, opened with the migration of the Helvetii and ended with Vercingetorix's revolt.
recogSiege of Alesia (52 BCE) — Caesar built a double wall system (circumvallation and contravallation) to trap Vercingetorix; Vercingetorix surrendered and was later executed in Rome.
powerBello Gallico opens: "Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres" ("All of Gaul is divided into three parts"). Caesar crossed the Rhine on a bridge built in just 10 days and invaded Britain in 55 and 54 BCE — primarily a propaganda exercise.
wiki ↗
First Triumvirate
60 BCE · Rome (informal; no legal standing)
recogPrivate political alliance of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus; Pompey provided military glory, Crassus provided wealth, Caesar provided political skill.
recogRenewed at the Conference of Luca (56 BCE); collapsed after Crassus died at the Battle of Carrhae (53 BCE) fighting the Parthians.
powerCaesar sealed the alliance by giving his daughter Julia to Pompey in marriage. Julia's death (54 BCE) and Crassus's death broke the personal ties keeping peace. Contemporaries called it the "three-headed monster" (τρικάρανον, in Varro's phrase).
wiki ↗
Julian Calendar Reform
46 BCE (adopted 45 BCE) · Rome
recogCaesar replaced the chaotic Roman lunisolar calendar with a 365-day solar calendar with a leap year every 4 years, devised with the Alexandrian astronomer Sosigenes.
recogTo realign the calendar, 46 BCE became the "Year of Confusion" — 445 days long.
powerThe Julian Calendar remained dominant until the Gregorian reform of 1582 (off by ~11 minutes/year, adding up to 10 days). The month of July is named after Caesar. This reform is a canonical clue in both history and science/astronomy tossups.
wiki ↗

Deeper Cuts — Timeline Strip

Lesser-known events and sub-clues that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

~83 BCE
Marriage to Cornelia; refusal to divorce under Sulla
Caesar defied dictator Sulla's order to divorce Cornelia; lost his priesthood and property; fled Rome; Sulla relented, reportedly saying he saw "many Mariuses" in Caesar.
75 BCE
Capture by Cilician pirates
Pirates demanded 20 talents ransom; Caesar laughed, insisted he was worth 50; after release, he raised a fleet, recaptured the pirates, and crucified them — as he had promised while captive.
63 BCE
Elected Pontifex Maximus; Catilinarian Conspiracy
Won chief priesthood over Catulus. Also argued against executing Catiline's conspirators (vs. Cicero and Cato); Sallust quotes his speech in the Catilinarian debate.
61 BCE
Bona Dea scandal / divorce of Pompeia
Clodius Pulcher disguised himself as a woman and infiltrated the women-only Bona Dea rites held at Caesar's house. Caesar divorced his wife Pompeia — "Caesar's wife must be above suspicion."
59 BCE
Consulship; lex Iulia agraria
As consul, Caesar passed land reform giving land to Pompey's veterans and the poor, bypassing obstruction from his co-consul Bibulus; called a tyrannical year by opponents.
55–54 BCE
Invasions of Britain
Two expeditions; no permanent conquest; notable for being the first Roman military presence in Britain. Cassivellaunus led British resistance.
49–48 BCE
Siege of Massilia; Spanish campaign
Caesar besieged Pompey-loyal Massilia (Marseille) simultaneously with fighting Pompeian forces in Spain at Ilerda — "war without blood" by maneuver and supply-cutting.
48 BCE
Alexandrine War; affair with Cleopatra
Caesar was trapped in Alexandria, burnt (or damaged) the Library of Alexandria accidentally, installed Cleopatra VII as queen alongside her brother. Son Caesarion claimed to be Caesar's.
47 BCE
Battle of Zela — "Veni, vidi, vici"
Swift defeat of Pharnaces II of Pontus in just 5 days; Caesar reportedly wrote to Amantius: "Veni, vidi, vici" — the most-quoted quizbowl Latin phrase after "Et tu."
46 BCE
Battle of Thapsus; death of Cato the Younger
Final defeat of Pompeian forces in North Africa. Cato committed suicide rather than accept Caesar's pardon — a defining act of Stoic principle; Caesar wrote an anti-Cato pamphlet in response.
45 BCE
Battle of Munda — last battle of the Civil War
Defeated Pompey's sons Gnaeus and Sextus in Spain; Caesar said it was the first battle in which he fought for his life, not for victory.
44 BCE (Feb)
Offered the crown (Lupercalia); refused three times
At the Lupercalia festival, Mark Antony offered Caesar a royal diadem three times; Caesar refused each time to popular acclaim — but the gesture inflamed senatorial fears of kingship.

Clue Bank

Famous quotes / phrases

  • "Alea iacta est" — "The die is cast" (crossing the Rubicon)
  • "Veni, vidi, vici" — "I came, I saw, I conquered" (Battle of Zela, 47 BCE)
  • "Et tu, Brute?" — Shakespeare only; Suetonius gives Greek: "καὶ σύ, τέκνον;" ("You too, child?")
  • "Gallia est omnis divisa in partes tres" — opening of Bello Gallico
  • "Caesar's wife must be above suspicion" — re: Pompeia / Bona Dea scandal
  • "Beware the Ides of March" — Spurinna the soothsayer's warning

Key numbers & dates

  • 100 BCE — birth
  • 44 BCE, March 15 — assassination (Ides of March)
  • 23 — stab wounds at assassination
  • 58–50 BCE — Gallic Wars
  • 52 BCE — Siege of Alesia
  • 49 BCE — crosses the Rubicon
  • 48 BCE — Battle of Pharsalus
  • 445 days — length of the Year of Confusion (46 BCE)
  • July — month named for Caesar (born in it)

Named actors / associates

  • Pompey the Great — ally then enemy; killed in Egypt
  • Crassus — third triumvir; died at Carrhae (53 BCE)
  • Mark Antony (Marcus Antonius) — loyal ally; gave the funeral oration
  • Brutus (Marcus Junius Brutus) — key assassin; Caesar's former friend
  • Cassius Longinus — organizer of the conspiracy; Brutus's brother-in-law
  • Cleopatra VII — Egyptian queen; lover; mother of Caesarion
  • Octavian (Augustus) — great-nephew; named sole heir in Caesar's will
  • Cicero — opposed Caesar; gave Philippics against Antony after the murder
  • Vercingetorix — Gallic chieftain; surrendered at Alesia
  • Sosigenes — Alexandrian astronomer who designed the Julian calendar

Titles, offices & terms

  • Dictator perpetuo — dictator in perpetuity (Feb 44 BCE)
  • Pontifex Maximus — chief Roman priest; won 63 BCE
  • Imperator — military title; root of "emperor"
  • Populares — faction Caesar allied with (vs. Optimates)
  • Liberatores — conspirators' self-designation
  • Gens Julia — his clan, claiming divine descent via Venus/Aeneas/Iulus
  • Deified: Divus Julius — officially deified 42 BCE by the Senate; comet seen at games following his death interpreted as his soul ascending

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Augustus (Octavian)Augustus was Caesar's great-nephew and heir, not Caesar himself. Augustus became the first emperor; Caesar was dictator, never officially emperor. If a question says "first emperor," it's Augustus. If it says "crossed the Rubicon" or "Ides of March," it's Caesar.
Pompey the GreatPompey also conquered widely (cleared the Mediterranean of pirates; subjugated the East). But Caesar defeated Pompey at Pharsalus. Pompey's theater, Pompey's column — clues about Pompey's monument in Rome or Egypt point to Pompey, not Caesar.
Marcus BrutusBrutus was an assassin, not the victim. If a question mentions "stabbing" or "Ides of March" from the killer's perspective, it's Brutus. Brutus also appears in Dante's Inferno (with Cassius and Judas in Satan's three mouths).
Mark AntonyAntony gave the funeral oration for Caesar and was Caesar's loyal general — distinct answers. Antony's affair with Cleopatra comes after Caesar's death. If the question links Cleopatra to Roman civil war and Actium, it's Antony (and Octavian/Augustus).
SullaSulla was an earlier Roman dictator who proscribed enemies and then resigned the dictatorship (unlike Caesar). Both marched on Rome with armies; the key distinction is that Sulla resigned and Caesar did not.
Caesar (Shakespeare play) vs. Caesar (historical figure)Shakespeare's Julius Caesar is also a major literature tossup. Clues from the play: "Friends, Romans, countrymen" (Antony's speech), Brutus's "It must be by his death" soliloquy, the ghost scene, Portia (Brutus's wife). Clues from history: Rubicon, Pharsalus, calendar reform.
Second TriumvirateThe Second Triumvirate (Octavian, Antony, Lepidus) was formed after Caesar's assassination, in 43 BCE, and had official legal standing — unlike Caesar's First Triumvirate (60 BCE, Pompey/Crassus/Caesar) which was purely informal.
