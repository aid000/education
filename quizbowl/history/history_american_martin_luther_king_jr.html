High-Yield Events (Very Common)

Key events most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Montgomery Bus Boycott
December 5, 1955 – December 20, 1956 · Montgomery, Alabama
recogRosa Parks arrested Dec 1, 1955 for refusing to give up her seat — the precipitating event.
recogLasted 381 days; Black residents walked or carpooled, costing the bus company ~65% of ridership.
recogEnded when the Supreme Court ruled in Browder v. Gayle (1956) that bus segregation is unconstitutional.
powerOrganized in part through the Women's Political Council led by Jo Ann Robinson, who printed and distributed 52,000 leaflets the night of Parks' arrest. MIA (Montgomery Improvement Association) chose King as president precisely because he was new to town and had no political enemies.
Birmingham Campaign (Project C)
April 3 – May 10, 1963 · Birmingham, Alabama
recogBull Connor (Public Safety Commissioner) ordered fire hoses and police dogs turned on peaceful protesters — broadcast nationwide.
recogKing arrested April 12; wrote the "Letter from Birmingham Jail" in the margins of a newspaper.
recogChildren's Crusade (May 2–7): schoolchildren marched; over 1,000 arrested in a single day.
power"Project C" = Project Confrontation; designed by Wyatt Tee Walker (SCLC executive director) to provoke a visible response. The "Letter" rebukes eight white Alabama clergymen who called King's methods "unwise and untimely." A settlement was reached May 10 — businesses agreed to desegregate.
March on Washington for Jobs and Freedom
August 28, 1963 · Lincoln Memorial, Washington D.C.
recogKing's "I Have a Dream" speech — the famous improvised ending ("I have a dream…") was added after Mahalia Jackson called out "Tell them about the dream, Martin!"
recog~250,000 attendees; the largest political rally in U.S. history to that date.
recogOrganized by A. Philip Randolph (who had proposed a similar march in 1941) and Bayard Rustin (logistics chief).
powerThe full prepared text did not include "I have a dream" — King departed from his script. The march demanded six goals, including a $2/hour minimum wage and an end to discrimination in public accommodations. Closing speaker was King; opening prayer was by Cardinal Patrick O'Boyle.
Selma to Montgomery Marches
March 7–25, 1965 · Selma and Montgomery, Alabama
recog"Bloody Sunday" (March 7): marchers crossing the Edmund Pettus Bridge attacked by state troopers with clubs and tear gas; broadcast on national TV.
recogThe bridge was named after Edmund Pettus, a former KKK Grand Dragon and U.S. Senator.
recogLed directly to LBJ's address to Congress: "We Shall Overcome" — and to the Voting Rights Act of 1965.
powerMarch 7 was led by John Lewis (SNCC) and Hosea Williams (SCLC), not King (who was in Atlanta). King led the second attempt (March 9 — "Turnaround Tuesday," halted by a court injunction) and the final successful march March 21–25. Jimmie Lee Jackson's death in Marion (Feb 26) was the precipitating event for the Selma marches.
"Letter from Birmingham Jail"
April 16, 1963 · written in Birmingham City Jail
recogDefends nonviolent civil disobedience and the moral duty to disobey unjust laws — drawing on Aquinas, Augustine, Thoreau.
recogExplicitly criticizes the "white moderate" who prefers a negative peace (absence of tension) to a positive peace (presence of justice).
recogWritten on margins of newspapers and on toilet paper, then smuggled out; addressed to eight white Alabama clergymen.
powerThe letter distinguishes just vs. unjust laws: "An unjust law is a code that a numerical or power majority group compels a minority group to obey but does not make binding on itself." Published in Liberation, The Christian Century, and in King's 1964 book Why We Can't Wait.
"I Have a Dream" Speech
August 28, 1963 · Washington, D.C.
recogDelivered at the steps of the Lincoln Memorial; closing speech of the March on Washington.
recogKey phrases: "free at last, free at last, thank God Almighty, we are free at last" (from a Negro spiritual); "I have a dream that my four little children…"
recogQuotes the Declaration of Independence ("all men are created equal") and alludes to Amos 5:24 ("let justice roll down like waters").
powerThe phrase "I have a dream" appears multiple times but was not in the prepared text. King had used the "dream" motif at earlier speeches in Detroit (June 1963) and Rocky Mount (1962). The speech was registered copyright; King's estate later litigated unauthorized reproductions.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known events and writings that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

Albany Movement
1961–62 · Albany, Georgia
King's first major campaign; considered a failure — Albany Police Chief Laurie Pritchett countered nonviolence by avoiding brutality.
Freedom Rides
1961
CORE-organized bus rides testing desegregated interstate travel; King supported but did not ride; firebombed at Anniston, AL.
St. Augustine Campaign
1964 · St. Augustine, Florida
Wade-ins at a segregated beach; King arrested; helped pressure passage of Civil Rights Act 1964.
Chicago Open Housing Campaign
1966
King moved to a slum apartment in North Lawndale; marched through Marquette Park; met with stone-throwing white mobs.
"Beyond Vietnam: A Time to Break Silence"
April 4, 1967 · Riverside Church, NYC
King publicly opposes the Vietnam War; calls U.S. "the greatest purveyor of violence in the world today."
Poor People's Campaign
1968
Planned multiracial march on Washington demanding economic rights; King assassinated before its completion; led by Ralph Abernathy.
Stride Toward Freedom
1958 (book)
King's memoir of the Montgomery Boycott; he was stabbed in the chest by Izola Ware Curry while signing copies in NYC.
Why We Can't Wait
1964 (book)
Contains the "Letter from Birmingham Jail"; argues for immediate civil rights action, not gradualism.
Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community?
1967 (book)
King's last book; calls for guaranteed annual income; criticizes Black Power rhetoric while affirming Black pride.
"I've Been to the Mountaintop"
April 3, 1968 · Mason Temple, Memphis
Final speech the night before his assassination; "I may not get there with you" — prophetically alludes to his own death.
FBI Surveillance (COINTELPRO)
1956–68
J. Edgar Hoover wiretapped King; called him "the most dangerous Negro in America"; sent an anonymous letter urging suicide.
Dexter Avenue Baptist Church / Ebenezer Baptist Church
Montgomery / Atlanta
Dexter Avenue: his first pastorship (1954). Ebenezer: his father's church, where he co-pastored after 1960.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key quotes & slogans

  • "I Have a Dream" — March on Washington, Aug 28, 1963
  • "Free at last, free at last…" — closes "I Have a Dream"
  • "Letter from Birmingham Jail" — "injustice anywhere is a threat to justice everywhere"
  • "I've Been to the Mountaintop" — final speech, night before death
  • "Beyond Vietnam" — "greatest purveyor of violence in the world today"
  • "We Shall Overcome" — movement anthem (LBJ used it in Congress speech)

Key organizations & acts

  • SCLC — Southern Christian Leadership Conference; King president 1957–68
  • NAACP — was involved in Parks case; precedes SCLC
  • SNCC — Student Nonviolent Coordinating Committee; more radical wing
  • Civil Rights Act of 1964 — bans segregation in public places
  • Voting Rights Act of 1965 — outlaws literacy tests and poll taxes
  • Fair Housing Act of 1968 — signed 7 days after King's death

Key allies & colleagues

  • Ralph Abernathy — closest associate; succeeded as SCLC president
  • Bayard Rustin — chief organizer of March on Washington
  • Andrew Young — SCLC aide; later congressman and Atlanta mayor
  • John Lewis — SNCC chairman; led "Bloody Sunday"
  • Fred Shuttlesworth — Birmingham ACMHR leader; invited King to Birmingham
  • Coretta Scott King — wife; civil rights leader in her own right

Numbers & dates to know

  • 381 days — Montgomery Boycott duration
  • 250,000 — marchers at March on Washington
  • 35 — King's age when awarded Nobel Peace Prize (youngest at the time)
  • April 4, 1968 — assassination date; Lorraine Motel, Memphis
  • James Earl Ray — assassin; convicted 1969
  • January 15 — King's birthday; federal holiday since 1983 (signed by Reagan)
  • Boston University, 1955 — Ph.D. in Systematic Theology

Intellectual influences

  • Mahatma Gandhi — satyagraha (nonviolent resistance); King visited India 1959
  • Henry David Thoreau — Civil Disobedience
  • Reinhold Niebuhr — Christian realism; influence on King's theology
  • Walter Rauschenbusch — Social Gospel movement
  • Hegel — dialectics; central to King's Boston doctoral work
  • Amos (biblical prophet) — "let justice roll down like waters"

Books by King

  • Stride Toward Freedom (1958) — Montgomery memoir
  • Strength to Love (1963) — sermons
  • Why We Can't Wait (1964) — includes Birmingham Letter
  • Where Do We Go from Here: Chaos or Community? (1967)
  • The Trumpet of Conscience (1968, posthumous) — Massey Lectures

Comparison Traps

Adjacent figures, events, and overlapping clues most likely to cause mis-buzzes.

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Malcolm XMalcolm X = Black nationalism, "by any means necessary," Nation of Islam (until 1964 break), Audubon Ballroom assassination (Feb 21, 1965). King = nonviolent integration. Both were FBI targets and both assassinated — know dates and venues.
Medgar EversEvers = NAACP field secretary in Mississippi; assassinated June 12, 1963 outside his Jackson home by Byron De La Beckwith. Comes before the March on Washington. Different org, different state, different assassin.
John LewisLewis = SNCC (not SCLC); led Bloody Sunday (King was in Atlanta that day); Lewis's skull was fractured on the Edmund Pettus Bridge. Lewis later served as U.S. Representative from Georgia for 33 years.
Bayard RustinRustin = behind-the-scenes chief organizer of March on Washington; gay Black activist marginalized within the movement. Rarely the answer in a King tossup but frequent in "organized the March" questions.
Ralph AbernathyAbernathy = King's closest friend; co-founded SCLC; was present at King's assassination; succeeded him as SCLC president. Not the leader of the movement; frequently a wrong-answer trap.
Selma vs. BirminghamBirmingham 1963 = Bull Connor + fire hoses + "Letter from Birmingham Jail." Selma 1965 = Edmund Pettus Bridge + Bloody Sunday + Voting Rights Act. Wrong year or wrong city kills the buzz.
Civil Rights Act 1964 vs. Voting Rights Act 1965CRA 1964 = public accommodations, employment; triggered by Birmingham Campaign. VRA 1965 = voting; triggered by Selma marches. Both signed by LBJ.
Rosa Parks vs. Claudette ColvinClaudette Colvin refused to give up her seat nine months before Parks (March 2, 1955) but was 15 and pregnant — NAACP chose Parks as a more strategically viable plaintiff. Colvin was also a plaintiff in Browder v. Gayle.

Big Picture — Cross-Discipline Connections
  • Literature: King's speeches draw on the Bible (Amos, Psalms, Galatians), the Declaration of Independence, and Langston Hughes's "America" imagery — common in lit tossups about American rhetoric.
  • Philosophy/religion: His theology engages Aquinas, Augustine, Niebuhr, Rauschenbusch — relevant in ethics and theology categories. His Ph.D. from Boston University was in Systematic Theology (Hegel and Personalism).
  • World history: Nonviolent direct action links to Gandhi's Indian Independence movement and to Thoreau's essay on civil disobedience — a recurring cross-category thread.
  • Music: "We Shall Overcome" (folk hymn adapted by Pete Seeger) and "Ain't Gonna Ride No More" (boycott song) appear in music history tossups.
  • Law: Browder v. Gayle (1956 bus desegregation), Heart of Atlanta Motel v. United States (CRA 1964 upheld), and South Carolina v. Katzenbach (VRA 1965 upheld) are the landmark cases.
