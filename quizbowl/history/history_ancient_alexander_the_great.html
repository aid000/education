Quick Overview

Full nameAlexander III of Macedon
Lived356–323 BCE (died age 32, Babylon)
Father / tutorPhilip II of Macedon / Aristotle
CapitalPella (Macedon); later Babylon as imperial seat
Peak empire~5 million km² — Greece to Punjab (India)
Dynasty afterDiadochi ("successors") war over his empire

Defining traits

  • Never lost a battle in 15 years of campaigning — died undefeated.
  • Tutored by Aristotle at Mieza from age 13–16; carried a copy of The Iliad annotated by Aristotle throughout campaigns.
  • Admired Achilles as his personal hero; visited Troy before his Persian campaign.
  • Founded over 20 cities named Alexandria; the most important is Alexandria, Egypt.
  • Promoted proskynesis (prostration before the king) and adopted Persian dress — caused friction with Macedonian officers.
  • Died in Babylon, June 323 BCE; cause of death disputed (fever, typhoid, poisoning, or alcohol).

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Horse named Bucephalus — tamed as a boy; died at Hydaspes; city named Bucephala in his honor.
  • Tutor Aristotle; father Philip II; childhood friend and general Hephaestion.
  • Gordian Knot — cut it rather than untied it at Gordium, Phrygia.
  • Battle of Gaugamela (331 BCE) — decisive defeat of Darius III.

High-Yield Events & Moments

Key sub-events most likely to appear as the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Battle of Granicus — Opening of Asia Minor
334 BCE · Granicus River, northwest Asia Minor
recogFirst major battle against Persia; Alexander personally crossed the river and fought hand-to-hand.
recogPersian satrapal cavalry routed; opened all of Asia Minor to Macedonian advance.
powerAlexander's life was saved by Cleitus the Black, who killed a Persian about to strike him from behind — the same Cleitus whom Alexander later killed in a drunken rage at Maracanda (Samarkand).
Battle of Issus
333 BCE · Issus, southern Anatolia (modern Turkey–Syria border)
recogAlexander defeated Darius III personally; Darius fled, abandoning his mother, wife, and daughters.
recogCommemorated in the Alexander Mosaic from Pompeii (House of the Faun), showing Alexander charging Darius on horseback.
powerAfter Issus, Darius offered Alexander all land west of the Euphrates and a ransom for his family; Alexander refused. General Parmenion reportedly said he'd accept "if I were Alexander" — Alexander replied "so would I, if I were Parmenion."
Siege of Tyre
332 BCE · Tyre (modern Lebanon) · 7-month siege
recogIsland city; Alexander built a mole (causeway) from the mainland to reach it — the mole eventually became a peninsula.
recogAfter capture, Alexander crucified 2,000 survivors and sold 30,000 into slavery.
powerTyre had refused to let Alexander sacrifice at the temple of Melqart (whom he equated with Heracles). The siege demonstrated his engineering determination and use of ship-mounted siege towers.
Visit to the Oracle at Siwa
332/331 BCE · Siwa Oasis, Egypt (Libya border)
recogOracle of Ammon (Zeus-Ammon) reputedly confirmed Alexander as son of a god; he thereafter promoted his divine descent.
recogOnly the pharaoh traditionally received such recognition; Alexander had already been crowned pharaoh.
powerAlexander reportedly asked whether all his father's murderers had been punished; the priest corrected him — his father was divine, not Philip. This exchange is a common quizbowl power clue.
Battle of Gaugamela (Arbela)
October 1, 331 BCE · near Mosul, modern Iraq · decisive world-historical battle
recogDarius assembled a huge force including scythed chariots and war elephants; Alexander used an oblique cavalry charge on the right flank to rout Darius again.
recogDarius fled and was later killed by his own satrap Bessus; Alexander then took the title King of Asia.
powerFought on level ground Darius specifically chose and leveled for his scythed chariots; Alexander used a feigned gap in his line to draw them into a killing zone. Also known as the Battle of Arbela (nearby town).
Burning of Persepolis
330 BCE · Persepolis, Persia (modern Iran)
recogPalace of the Persian kings burned — traditionally attributed to Thaïs, an Athenian courtesan, who allegedly urged Alexander to torch it during a drunken party.
recogAlexander later said he regretted it; historians debate whether it was deliberate revenge for the Persian burning of Athens in 480 BCE or a drunken accident.
powerThe story of Thaïs appears in Diodorus Siculus and was dramatized by Dryden in the poem Alexander's Feast — a common cross-discipline link.
Battle of the Hydaspes
326 BCE · Hydaspes River (Jhelum), Punjab (modern Pakistan)
recogFought against Indian king Porus (Paurava), who used war elephants; Alexander's tactical night crossing of the flooded river was decisive.
recogAfter defeating Porus, Alexander was so impressed he reinstated Porus as a vassal king — one of his most famous acts of magnanimity.
powerAlexander's beloved horse Bucephalus died after this battle (of wounds and old age at ~30); Alexander founded the city Bucephala on the site in the horse's honor.
Mutiny at the Hyphasis (Beas River)
326 BCE · Hyphasis River, Punjab — easternmost point of the campaign
recogMacedonian troops refused to march further east; Coenus spoke for the army — Alexander sulked in his tent for three days, then relented.
recogAlexander set up 12 giant altars (one per Olympian god) marking the furthest extent of the empire.
powerThis is the only time Alexander's will was overridden; the mutiny ended his eastern ambitions. He returned via the Gedrosian Desert march, a catastrophic retreat that killed thousands.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key numbers & dates

  • 356 BCE — born at Pella, Macedon
  • 336 BCE — becomes king (age 20)
  • 334 BCE — crosses into Asia with ~47,000 troops
  • 333, 331 BCE — Issus, Gaugamela (the two great battles vs. Darius)
  • 326 BCE — Hydaspes; mutiny at Hyphasis (easternmost point)
  • 323 BCE — dies Babylon, June 10/11; age 32
  • 12 altars at Hyphasis; 20+ cities named Alexandria

People & relationships

  • Philip II — father; created the Macedonian phalanx
  • Olympias — mother; claimed descent from Achilles
  • Aristotle — tutor (age 13–16) at Mieza
  • Hephaestion — closest friend / possible lover; died 324 BCE
  • Parmenion — senior general; later executed (330 BCE)
  • Cleitus the Black — saved Alexander at Granicus; killed by him at Samarkand
  • Roxane — Bactrian wife; Stateira II — Persian wife (daughter of Darius III)
  • Bucephalus — war horse; died at Hydaspes

Places & geography

  • Pella — Macedonian capital
  • Granicus → Issus → Gaugamela — the three Persian-war battles (west to east)
  • Siwa Oasis — oracle of Ammon; divine sonship proclaimed
  • Persepolis — burned 330 BCE
  • Samarkand (Maracanda) — where he killed Cleitus
  • Hydaspes (Jhelum) — last major battle; India
  • Babylon — where he died; was planning Arabian campaign
  • Gedrosia — desert march home; catastrophic losses

Quotes, terms & concepts

  • "If I were not Alexander, I would be Diogenes" — to Diogenes the Cynic at Corinth
  • "So would I, if I were Parmenion" — refusing Darius's peace offer
  • Proskynesis — Persian prostration Alexander tried to introduce; Macedonians resisted
  • Hellenization — spread of Greek language and culture across the East
  • Diadochi ("successors") — generals who divided empire after his death
  • Anabasis of Arrian — best ancient source on Alexander
  • Alexander Romance — legendary / fictional biography, widely translated in the Middle Ages
  • Krateros — general; led veterans home from India; rival of Hephaestion

Comparison Traps

Adjacent events and figures on the historical clock most likely to cause mis-buzzes.

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Philip II of MacedonAlexander's father; created the sarissa-phalanx; defeated Greece at Chaeronea (338 BCE); assassinated before he could invade Persia. Clue that says "father of Alexander" or "creator of the Macedonian phalanx" → Philip, not Alexander.
Darius III of PersiaThe Persian king Alexander defeated at Issus and Gaugamela; fled both times; killed by Bessus (his own satrap). Clues about a Persian king who loses → Darius III, not Darius I (of Marathon fame) or Xerxes.
Darius I / XerxesPersian kings of the earlier Greco-Persian Wars: Darius I sent the Marathon expedition (490 BCE); Xerxes invaded Greece (480 BCE, burned Athens). Both predate Alexander by 150 years.
Julius CaesarBoth admired Achilles and wept near statues of Alexander. But Caesar is Roman, 1st century BCE; Roman context, Senate, assassination on Ides of March → Caesar. Ancient Greek/Macedonian context → Alexander.
Hannibal BarcaAnother undefeated general; Carthaginian; known for crossing the Alps with elephants and winning at Cannae (216 BCE). Elephants crossing mountains → Hannibal. War elephants in India or Persia → Alexander (or Porus).
Ptolemy I vs. AlexanderPtolemy was Alexander's general and bodyguard who seized Egypt after Alexander's death and stole his corpse. Questions about a Macedonian ruling Egypt → Ptolemy I, not Alexander (Alexander merely founded Alexandria and was named pharaoh).
Battle of Marathon vs. GaugamelaMarathon (490 BCE) — Athenians vs. Darius I; no Alexander involved. Gaugamela (331 BCE) — Alexander vs. Darius III, scythed chariots, decisive end of Achaemenid Persia.

Cross-discipline big-picture connections
  • Literature: Alexander's admiration for Achilles links to Homer's Iliad; his tutor Aristotle links to Nicomachean Ethics, Politics, and natural history. John Dryden's Alexander's Feast (1697) is a canonical English Lit poem.
  • Philosophy: Famous encounter with Diogenes the Cynic at Corinth ("stand out of my sunlight"); campaign of Aristotle's pupil created a channel for Greek philosophy to spread to Persia, Bactria, and India.
  • Geography / Science: Alexander's campaigns generated the first systematic Greek knowledge of Central Asia and India; his admiral Nearchus charted the Persian Gulf coast.
  • Art: The Alexander Mosaic (House of the Faun, Pompeii, ~100 BCE) depicting Issus is a standard Art History answer; the Alexander Sarcophagus (Istanbul) shows hunting and battle scenes.
  • Religion/Mythology: Alexander's claimed divine sonship (Zeus-Ammon) links to Egyptian pharaonic tradition and to Heracles myths. His emulation of Dionysus in India is a classic mythology cross-reference.
