Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Kite-and-key lightning experiment (1752) — single most-cited Franklin clue.
  • Bifocals and the Franklin stove (called the "Pennsylvania Fireplace").
  • Poor Richard's Almanack and the pen name Richard Saunders.
  • Only Founding Father to sign all four major founding documents.
  • Join, or Die — his 1754 snake-cartoon, first American political cartoon.

Defining traits (buzzer personality)

  • Autobiography is frequently quoted — first major American autobiography.

High-Yield Events & Achievements

The 6 topics most likely to anchor a tossup. power = early/hard clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

Kite-and-Key Lightning Experiment
1752 · Philadelphia · proves lightning is electrical
recogFlew a kite with a metal key tied to the string during a thunderstorm; a Leyden jar collected charge — proved lightning is electrical in nature.
recogLed directly to his invention of the lightning rod (grounded conductor on buildings).
powerFranklin's full account was first published in Experiments and Observations on Electricity (1751); French scientists performed the Marly experiment confirming his theory before the kite — so his own kite experiment may have been performed after European confirmation. He introduced the terms positive and negative for electrical charge.
Diplomatic Mission to France (Minister Plenipotentiary)
1778–1785 · Paris · securing the French Alliance
recogNegotiated the 1778 Treaty of Alliance with France — the decisive foreign alliance of the Revolutionary War.
recogCo-negotiated the 1783 Treaty of Paris ending the Revolutionary War (with John Adams and John Jay), over French objections, securing favorable U.S. boundaries.
powerPresented himself as a Quaker-simple frontier sage wearing a fur cap at Versailles — a deliberate image crafted for French philosophe admiration. His portrait was printed on snuffboxes, rings, and medallions ("Franklin fever"). Minister Vergennes was his key French counterpart.
Poor Richard's Almanack
1732–1758 · Philadelphia · pen name Richard Saunders
recogPublished annually under pseudonym Richard Saunders; sold ~10,000 copies/year, one of the most widely read colonial publications.
recogSource of aphorisms: "Early to bed and early to rise…", "A penny saved is a penny earned", "Fish and visitors stink after three days."
powerFranklin compiled the best sayings into Father Abraham's Speech, later reprinted as The Way to Wealth (1758) — one of the most reprinted texts of the 18th century. The fictitious narrator Richard Saunders pretended to predict his own wife's death to sell copies.
Albany Plan of Union
1754 · Albany Congress · first colonial union proposal
recogFranklin's plan proposed a Grand Council of colonial delegates with a President-General appointed by the Crown — rejected by both the colonies and Britain.
recogAccompanied by his famous "Join, or Die" snake cartoon — first American political cartoon, the snake's segments = colonies.
powerProposed at the Albany Congress to coordinate colonial defense during the French and Indian War; historians call it a precursor to the Articles of Confederation. Franklin later revived the cartoon during the Revolution, now with a rattlesnake.
Signing the Four Founding Documents
1754–1787 · Philadelphia
recogOnly Founding Father to sign the Declaration of Independence, Treaty of Alliance with France, Treaty of Paris (1783), and U.S. Constitution — all four major documents.
recogDelegate to the Constitutional Convention (1787) at age 81 — oldest delegate; reportedly moved to approve the final document despite reservations.
powerAt the Convention's close, Franklin pointed to the sun carved on Washington's chair and said he had wondered whether it was a rising or setting sun — now he knew it was rising. Famous closing remark delivered via James Wilson (Franklin was too weak to read it himself).
The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin
Written 1771–1790 · published posthumously 1791
recogBegun as a letter to his son William during a visit to Twyford, England (1771); written in four parts over 19 years.
recogFamous for his 13 Virtues self-improvement program (temperance, silence, order, resolution, frugality, industry, sincerity, justice, moderation, cleanliness, tranquility, chastity, humility).
powerFirst major American autobiography; covers only through 1758 (never finished). Describes his arrival in Philadelphia eating a bread roll — an iconic scene of American self-made myth. Son William was a Loyalist; the letter frame adds irony Franklin never directly resolved.

Deeper Cuts — Life Timeline

Harder early-clue facts: lesser-known roles, inventions, and turning points.

1706
Born in Boston, 15th of 17 children
Father Josiah Franklin, a tallow chandler & soap maker. Named after uncle Benjamin.
1718
Apprenticed to brother James's print shop
Wrote anonymous "Silence Dogood" letters published in James's New-England Courant; 14 letters, often satirical.
1723
Fled Boston for Philadelphia
Broke apprenticeship contract; arrived penniless — the "bread roll" scene in the Autobiography.
1727
Founded the Junto (Leather Apron Club)
Mutual improvement society in Philadelphia; forerunner of the American Philosophical Society.
1731
Founded the first American subscription library
Library Company of Philadelphia — members pooled money to buy books from London.
1736
Founded Philadelphia's first volunteer fire company
The Union Fire Company; modeled on Boston's system.
1743
Founded the American Philosophical Society
First learned society in the colonies; Franklin served as president 1769–1790.
1749
Founded what became the University of Pennsylvania
Proposed the Academy of Philadelphia; opened 1751, became Penn.
1751
Experiments and Observations on Electricity published
Letters to Peter Collinson (Royal Society, London) collected; earned him the Copley Medal (1753).
1757–1762 / 1764–1775
Colonial agent in London
Represented Pennsylvania, then also Georgia, New Jersey, Massachusetts; lobbied against the Stamp Act.
1766
Testified before Parliament against the Stamp Act
Key testimony helped secure repeal; made him a colonial hero. Distinguished "internal" vs. "external" taxes.
1772
Hutchinson Letters affair
Leaked private letters of Governor Thomas Hutchinson to Boston radicals; publicly humiliated before the Privy Council by solicitor Alexander Wedderburn (Jan 1774).
1775
First U.S. Postmaster General
Appointed by Continental Congress; had earlier been colonial deputy postmaster general (1753–1774).
1784
Invented bifocals
Combined distance and reading lenses in one frame to avoid switching glasses.
1784
Served on royal commission investigating Mesmerism
Commission (with Lavoisier and others) debunked Franz Mesmer's "animal magnetism" — early controlled experiment.
1790
Death & final petition
Last public act: signed petition to Congress to abolish slavery (Feb 1790). Died April 17; ~20,000 attended funeral in Philadelphia.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Inventions & Science

  • Lightning rod — grounded conductor; derived from kite experiment
  • Franklin stove ("Pennsylvania Fireplace") — more efficient iron stove, 1741
  • Bifocals — split lens for near & far vision, ~1784
  • Armonica (glass harmonica) — musical instrument using spinning glass bowls, 1761
  • Flexible urinary catheter — designed for his brother John
  • Positive & negative charge — his terminology, still used
  • Leyden jar — used in experiments; did not invent, but key tool
  • Gulf Stream — charted and named it for postal/navigation purposes
  • Copley Medal — won 1753 for electrical research (Royal Society's top prize)

Writings & Publications

  • Poor Richard's Almanack — 1732–1758, pen name Richard Saunders
  • The Way to Wealth (1758) — compilation of Poor Richard maxims
  • Autobiography — begun 1771, first addressed to son William
  • "Silence Dogood" letters — 1722, anonymous; 14 satirical essays
  • "Bagatelles" — witty essays written in Paris (e.g., The Morals of Chess, The Flies)
  • Experiments and Observations on Electricity — 1751
  • "Fart Proudly" — humorous essay to the Royal Academy of Brussels (~1781)

Political & Diplomatic

  • Only Founder to sign all four: Declaration, Treaty of Alliance, Treaty of Paris, Constitution
  • Albany Plan of Union (1754) — "Join, or Die" cartoon
  • Stamp Act testimony (1766) — helped secure repeal
  • Hutchinson Letters — leaked to radicals; Wedderburn humiliation
  • Treaty of Alliance with France (1778)
  • Treaty of Paris (1783) — with Adams & Jay
  • Postmaster General — first U.S. holder
  • Abolitionism — president, Pennsylvania Abolition Society; last act 1790 petition

Key Quotes & Numbers

  • "In this world nothing can be said to be certain, except death and taxes."
  • "A penny saved is a penny earned." (actually "a penny saved is twopence dear")
  • "Early to bed and early to rise, makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise."
  • "We must all hang together, or most assuredly we shall all hang separately." (apocryphal, attributed)
  • 13 Virtues (Autobiography self-improvement system)
  • $100 bill — Franklin's face on the U.S. $100 note
  • Fur cap — worn at Versailles, symbol of frontier/philosophe image

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Thomas JeffersonJefferson drafted the Declaration; Franklin signed it but was not the primary drafter. Jefferson was in France 1784–1789 as Minister; Franklin left France in 1785. Jefferson wrote the Notes on the State of Virginia; Franklin wrote the Autobiography.
John AdamsAdams also negotiated the Treaty of Paris — but Adams did not secure the French Alliance (Franklin did). Adams was openly hostile to France; Franklin cultivated French goodwill. Adams signed the Declaration; did not sign the Constitution.
Alexander HamiltonHamilton was on the $10 bill (Treasury secretary, died in duel with Burr); Franklin is on the $100 bill. Hamilton was a Founder but focused on finance/Constitution, not electricity or diplomacy.
John JayJay co-negotiated the Treaty of Paris (1783) with Franklin and Adams, but Jay is best known for the Jay Treaty (1794) — a different treaty with Britain, signed when Franklin had been dead 4 years.
George WashingtonWashington commanded the army and became president; Franklin never held executive office. The "rising sun" chair speech was Franklin's at the Constitutional Convention — not Washington's, though it was Washington's chair.
Joseph PriestleyBoth worked on electricity/chemistry in the 1700s; Priestley discovered oxygen. If clue mentions phlogiston or oxygen — Priestley. If lightning, lightning rod, positive/negative charge — Franklin.
Continental Congress diplomatsSilas Deane was the first American envoy to France (sent 1776); Franklin arrived Dec 1776 and quickly eclipsed him. Arthur Lee was the third commissioner — often in conflict with Franklin. The trio ≠ Franklin alone.

🔗 Cross-discipline big picture Franklin sits at the intersection of American history, science history, and literature. Science questions may ask about electrical theory (positive/negative charge, Leyden jar, Copley Medal) before pivoting to politics — buzz early if you hear those. Literature tossups may open with Poor Richard aphorisms or the Autobiography's 13 Virtues. Diplomatic questions often pair Franklin with the French Enlightenment (Voltaire, philosophes) and the Treaty of Paris (Adams + Jay). The Albany Plan of Union bridges early colonial politics to constitutional history. Franklin's final antislavery petition connects to antebellum reform and Quaker abolitionism (though Franklin himself was not Quaker, he presided over the Pennsylvania Abolition Society).
