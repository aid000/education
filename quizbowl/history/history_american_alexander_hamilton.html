Quick Overview

Born / Diedc. 1755–1757 (disputed), Nevis, British West Indies — July 12, 1804, New York City
RoleFounding Father; first U.S. Secretary of the Treasury (1789–1795)
PartyFounded and led the Federalist Party
MilitaryWashington's aide-de-camp; led charge at Yorktown (1781)
Key textPrincipal author of The Federalist Papers (51 of 85 essays)
DeathKilled in duel with Aaron Burr, July 11, 1804, Weehawken, New Jersey

Defining roles in quizbowl

  • Almost always the answer when a question mentions "first Secretary of the Treasury" or the Federalist Papers.
  • Frequent answer for the duel with Burr, the Reynolds Affair, the Report on Manufactures, or the National Bank debate.
  • Power clues involve his Caribbean origin, his King's College (Columbia) education, or his Continentalist newspaper essays.

Classic giveaway clues

  • Shot by Aaron Burr in Weehawken — single most common giveaway.
  • His face on the $10 bill.
  • "Publius" pen name for the Federalist Papers (shared with Madison and Jay).

High-Yield Events & Actions

The 6 topics most likely to be tossup answers or anchor a tossup on Hamilton. power = hard early clue; recog = recognition / middle clue.

The Federalist Papers
1787–1788 · New York newspapers · co-authored with James Madison and John Jay
recog85 essays under pen name "Publius" advocating ratification of the Constitution.
recogHamilton wrote Federalist No. 70 ("energy in the Executive") and No. 78 (judicial review / life tenure for judges).
powerFederalist No. 51 (Madison) is the "ambition must be made to counteract ambition" essay — distinguish from Hamilton's; Hamilton wrote No. 1 (introduction) and most of Nos. 65–85. The essays were collected as The Federalist in 1788; Hamilton arranged and contributed to that collection.

First Secretary of the Treasury & Financial Program
1789–1795 · Washington Administration
recogProposed assumption of state debts from the Revolution, funding them at face value.
recogFounded the First Bank of the United States (1791); argued for it via the "necessary and proper" / implied powers doctrine — opposed by Jefferson and Madison.
powerThe Compromise of 1790 (dinner-table deal with Jefferson and Madison): Hamilton got assumption and the national bank; in exchange, the permanent capital moved to the Potomac (Washington, D.C.). His Report on the Subject of Manufactures (1791) proposed tariffs and subsidies to industrialize — rejected by Congress but influential in the 19th century.
wiki ↗
Duel with Aaron Burr
July 11, 1804 · Weehawken, New Jersey · Hamilton died July 12
recogBurr challenged Hamilton after Hamilton's derogatory remarks reportedly influenced Burr's loss in the 1804 New York gubernatorial race.
recogHamilton famously threw away his shot (fired first but into the air or wide); Burr shot and mortally wounded him.
powerHamilton had previously intervened against Burr in the 1800 presidential election, helping Jefferson defeat Burr in the House runoff after their Electoral College tie — long-running personal animosity. Hamilton also killed Philip, his son, in a duel in 1801.
wiki ↗
Reynolds Affair (Reynolds Pamphlet)
Affair 1791–1792 · Pamphlet published 1797
recogHamilton's extramarital affair with Maria Reynolds; her husband James Reynolds extorted money from Hamilton.
recogWhen accused of financial corruption, Hamilton published the Reynolds Pamphlet (1797) admitting the affair to clear himself of financial misconduct.
powerThe pamphlet is considered America's first major political sex scandal. James Monroe, John Muhlenberg, and Frederick Venable originally confronted Hamilton in 1792 — Hamilton always blamed Monroe for later leaking the evidence to the press; this was a source of lasting enmity with Monroe.
wiki ↗
Constitutional Convention & Ratification
1787 · Philadelphia
recogHamilton proposed a strongly centralized government — at the extreme end of the Convention; favored an elected executive for life (critics called it "monarchy").
recogHe was the sole New York delegate to sign the Constitution (Lansing and Yates walked out).
powerHamilton's June 18, 1787 speech ran five to six hours and outlined a "British model" plan — widely noted but not adopted. He later led the campaign to ratify in New York, a hostile state where the Federalist Papers were published specifically to sway delegates.
wiki ↗
Whiskey Rebellion Response & Federalist Leadership
1794 · Western Pennsylvania
recogHamilton's excise tax on whiskey (1791) triggered the Whiskey Rebellion; Hamilton personally accompanied Washington's 13,000-man army to suppress it.
recogHe co-founded the Federalist Party in opposition to Jefferson's Democratic-Republican Party — defining the first American party system.
powerAfter leaving Treasury (1795) Hamilton remained the inspector general / de facto commander of the U.S. Army during the Quasi-War with France (1798–1800), ranking above General Washington. He clashed with Adams over expanding the military — a major source of the 1800 Federalist split.

Deeper Cuts — Timeline & Lesser Events

Chronological sweep of harder clues. ● = very high frequency; ● = medium.

c. 1755–1757 — Nevis, British West Indies
Caribbean Birth & Illegitimacy
Born out of wedlock to Rachel Faucette and James Hamilton; orphaned by ~12. His Caribbean, illegitimate origins are frequent power clues ("this man, born on Nevis…").
1773–1774 — New York
King's College (now Columbia)
Enrolled at King's College; wrote early pamphlets defending colonial rights (A Full Vindication of the Measures of Congress).
1777–1781 — Revolutionary War
Washington's Aide-de-Camp
Served as Washington's aide-de-camp and chief staff officer for four years; handled crucial correspondence. Commanded a battalion at the Siege of Yorktown (1781), leading a bayonet charge on Redoubt No. 10.
1781–1783
The Continentalist Essays
Six essays published in the New York Packet arguing for a stronger central government — early statement of Federalist principles before the Federalist Papers.
1784 — New York
Founded the Bank of New York
Co-founded the Bank of New York (now BNY Mellon); one of the earliest chartered banks in the U.S.
1786 — Annapolis, Maryland
Annapolis Convention
Only five states attended; Hamilton drafted the report calling for a broader convention — the direct precursor to the 1787 Constitutional Convention in Philadelphia.
1790 — Washington Administration
Report on Public Credit
First of three great Treasury reports; proposed assumption of $21.5 million in state war debts and funding the national debt at par. Jefferson and Madison opposed assumption as benefiting Northern speculators.
1791
Report on a National Bank
Proposed the First Bank of the United States (20-year charter). Jefferson argued it was unconstitutional; Hamilton's broad / implied powers argument (McCulloch v. Maryland precedent) prevailed with Washington.
1791
Report on Manufactures
Advocated protective tariffs, government subsidies, and immigration of skilled workers to build American manufacturing; Congress rejected it but it influenced 19th-century American System (Clay, Lincoln).
1794
Jay Treaty Support
Wrote the "Camillus" essays defending the Jay Treaty with Britain — opposed by Jefferson and Madison ("Helvidius" papers). Illustrates the Hamilton–Jefferson foreign-policy divide: pro-Britain vs. pro-France.
1795
Leaves Treasury; Returns to Law
Resumed law practice in New York; defended Croswell v. People (1804), pioneering the defense that truth is a defense to libel.
1800
Letter Against Adams / Election of 1800
Published a pamphlet attacking President John Adams, splitting the Federalist Party; helped elect Jefferson. Hamilton then supported Jefferson over Burr in the House runoff — cementing Burr's hatred.
1801
Son Philip Hamilton Killed in Duel
Hamilton's eldest son Philip was killed at age 19 in a duel with George Eacker — same Weehawken location, same pistols used later in Hamilton's own duel.

Lesser-known works & writings

Pacificus / Helvidius Debate (1793)
1793
Hamilton ("Pacificus") vs. Madison ("Helvidius") on executive power during the French Revolutionary Wars; Hamilton argued Washington could declare neutrality without Congress.
The Examination (1801–1802)
1801–1802
18-essay series attacking Jefferson's first presidential address; last major journalistic effort.
New York Evening Post
Founded 1801
Hamilton founded this newspaper (still publishing as New York Post) to counter Jeffersonian press.
Society for Establishing Useful Manufactures (SUM)
1791
Industrial planned city at Paterson, New Jersey; early attempt to implement Report on Manufactures vision.
"The Farmer Refuted" (1775)
1775
Early pamphlet rebutting Loyalist Samuel Seabury; demonstrates natural-law philosophy at age ~20.
Washington's Farewell Address
1796
Hamilton substantially drafted and revised Washington's Farewell Address from Madison's 1792 draft; often asked as a "who really wrote it" power clue.

  • 1791 — First Bank charter; excise tax; Report on Manufactures; Bank of New York co-founded

Quotes & Slogans

  • "I never expect to see a perfect work from imperfect man." — Federalist No. 85
  • "Energy in the Executive is a leading character in the definition of good government." — Federalist No. 70
  • "In framing a government… you must first enable the government to control the governed; and in the next place, oblige it to control itself." — often misattributed to Hamilton but written by Madison (No. 51)
  • "Publius" — pen name shared with Madison and Jay
  • "Implied powers" — his constitutional doctrine

  • Eliza (Elizabeth Schuyler) — wife; daughter of General Philip Schuyler
  • Maria Reynolds — affair partner; Reynolds Pamphlet
  • Robert Morris — mentor in finance during the Revolution
  • John Jay — third Federalist Papers author

Comparison Traps

Adjacent figures and events most often confused with Hamilton in tossups.

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Aaron BurrBurr shot Hamilton; Burr was Vice President under Jefferson, later tried for treason (Burr Conspiracy). If the question is about the Treasury, Federalist Papers, or national bank — it's Hamilton; if it's about the 1804 duel trigger or the Burr Conspiracy — Burr is the answer.
James MadisonMadison co-wrote the Federalist Papers but wrote No. 10, 14, and 51 (the famous ones on factions and ambition). Madison broke with Hamilton after 1790 and led the Democratic-Republicans. Hamilton ≠ Father of the Constitution (Madison's title).
Thomas JeffersonJefferson was Secretary of State, not Treasury. Jefferson opposed the National Bank, assumption, and protective tariffs — the exact opposite of Hamilton's program. Both are in Washington's cabinet but on opposite sides of every major policy.
John AdamsAdams (2nd President) and Hamilton were both Federalists but bitter rivals. Hamilton published the pamphlet attacking Adams in 1800 that split the Federalists. Adams ≠ the duel — Adams outlived Hamilton and died in 1826.
Robert MorrisMorris was Superintendent of Finance under the Articles of Confederation (1781–1784) — the pre-Hamilton treasury figure. Morris mentored Hamilton. If the question is about the Revolutionary era central finance, Morris may be the answer, not Hamilton.
Benjamin FranklinFranklin is the other non-president on U.S. currency ($100 bill). Both are Founding Fathers on bills — Franklin is on $100, Hamilton on $10.
Election of 1800 (Jefferson vs. Adams vs. Burr)Hamilton is a major actor but not a candidate. The election produced a Jefferson–Burr Electoral College tie resolved in the House; Hamilton's support for Jefferson over Burr was decisive. Don't confuse Hamilton's role with his being a presidential candidate — he was constitutionally ineligible (foreign-born).

Big-Picture Cross-Discipline Link Hamilton's financial architecture — national debt assumption, a central bank, implied-powers doctrine, protective tariffs — is the direct ancestor of Henry Clay's American System, Lincoln's Whig/Republican economic program, and modern arguments for federal regulatory power (every McCulloch v. Maryland / Necessary and Proper Clause question in Constitutional Law traces back to Hamilton's 1791 bank argument). On the political-science side, Federalist Nos. 70 and 78 are cited in virtually every Supreme Court confirmation debate about executive energy and judicial independence — a clean bridge from history tossups into law and government ones.
