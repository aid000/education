Quick Overview

Full NameFranklin Delano Roosevelt (FDR)
Dates1882–1945; born Hyde Park, New York
Presidency32nd president; 1933–1945 (four terms)
PartyDemocratic; Vice Presidents: Garner, Wallace, Truman
Key contextGreat Depression + World War II; longest-serving US president
DisabilityContracted polio in 1921; paralyzed from waist down

Defining traits (classic quizbowl giveaways)

  • "The only thing we have to fear is fear itself" — First Inaugural Address, 4 March 1933.
  • Fireside chats — radio broadcasts directly to the American public, first used March 1933.
  • New Deal — sweeping domestic programs to combat the Great Depression (1933–1939).
  • Court-packing plan (1937) — proposed adding up to 6 extra Supreme Court justices; failed in Congress.
  • Died in office at Warm Springs, Georgia, 12 April 1945, while having his portrait painted.
  • Cousin of Theodore Roosevelt (26th president); wife Eleanor Roosevelt was TR's niece.

Key terms to know

  • Brain Trust — FDR's informal advisory group of academics (Moley, Tugwell, Berle).
  • "Happy Warrior" — nickname, borrowed from a phrase he used about Al Smith.
  • Four Freedoms — speech, worship, want, fear (January 1941 State of the Union).
  • Arsenal of Democracy — FDR's December 1940 radio address framing US industrial role.

High-Yield Events (Timeline)

Key sub-events most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = middle / recognition clue.

March 4, 1933
First Inaugural Address & Bank Holiday
recog"The only thing we have to fear is fear itself" — opening line of first inaugural address.
recogDeclared a national bank holiday (March 6–10) to halt bank runs; Emergency Banking Act passed within days.
powerThe speech was drafted largely by Raymond Moley (Brain Trust); FDR also quoted Dante: "the money changers have fled from their high seats in the temple." Full phrase: "…nameless, unreasoning, unjustified terror."
1933–1935 (First New Deal)
The New Deal — First Hundred Days
recogCivilian Conservation Corps (CCC) — hired unemployed young men; planted 3 billion trees.
recogAgricultural Adjustment Act (AAA) — paid farmers to reduce crop production; ruled unconstitutional 1936.
recogTennessee Valley Authority (TVA) — federal dam/power network in rural South; still operates.
recogNational Industrial Recovery Act (NIRA) / NRA codes — struck down by Schechter Poultry v. US (1935).
powerThe "Hundred Days" term coined from this era: March 9 – June 16, 1933, 15 major bills passed. FDR sent 15 messages and made 10 speeches in 100 days.
1935–1938 (Second New Deal)
The Second New Deal
recogSocial Security Act (1935) — old-age pensions, unemployment insurance; signed August 14, 1935.
recogWagner Act / NLRA (1935) — guaranteed workers' right to organize unions; created the NLRB.
recogWorks Progress Administration (WPA) — employed 8.5 million; built roads, bridges, public art (Federal Arts Project).
powerThe Second New Deal was partly a response to pressure from the left: Huey Long's "Share Our Wealth," Father Coughlin's radio attacks, and Francis Townsend's pension plan.
1937
Court-Packing Plan
recogProposed adding one justice for each sitting justice over 70 (up to 15 total); seen as attack on judicial independence.
recogCongress rejected the plan; FDR's chief failure — even Democrats opposed it.
powerMotivated by Supreme Court striking down NIRA and AAA; ironically, the "switch in time that saved nine" (Owen Roberts changing his vote in West Coast Hotel v. Parrish) made the plan moot before the Senate killed it.
December 7, 1941
Pearl Harbor & Declaration of War
recog"A date which will live in infamy" — FDR's address to Congress, December 8, 1941; asked for declaration of war on Japan.
recogCongress declared war on Japan; Germany and Italy declared war on US December 11.
powerFDR's original draft said "a date which will live in world history"; speechwriter Sherwood or FDR himself changed it to "infamy." The only dissenting vote in Congress was Jeannette Rankin.
February 1942
Executive Order 9066 — Japanese American Internment
recogAuthorized forcible relocation of ~120,000 Japanese Americans from the West Coast into internment camps.
recogSupreme Court upheld it in Korematsu v. United States (1944).
powerRecommended by General John DeWitt; War Relocation Authority ran the camps. Congress formally apologized and paid reparations ($20,000/person) under the Civil Liberties Act of 1988.
1941–1944 (WWII Strategy)
Allied Conferences — Wartime Diplomacy
recogAtlantic Charter (August 1941, off Newfoundland) — FDR & Churchill; declared war aims, self-determination, free seas.
recogCasablanca Conference (January 1943) — FDR & Churchill demanded unconditional surrender of Axis powers.
recogTehran Conference (November–December 1943) — FDR, Churchill, Stalin; first Big Three meeting; agreed on Operation Overlord (D-Day).
powerYalta Conference (February 1945) — FDR, Churchill, Stalin; agreed on UN structure, Soviet entry into Pacific war, postwar Europe zones. FDR's declining health visible; died 2 months later.
April 12, 1945
Death at Warm Springs
recogDied of a cerebral hemorrhage at Warm Springs, Georgia ("Little White House") while sitting for a portrait.
recogArtist Elizabeth Shoumatoff was painting the portrait; companion Lucy Mercer Rutherfurd was present (his former mistress).
powerFDR had been going to Warm Springs since 1924 to treat his polio in the therapeutic pools; he founded the Georgia Warm Springs Foundation. Succeeded by Harry S. Truman.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-asked events and acts that appear as early/hard clues. One-line ID each.

1920
VP Nomination (Lost)
Democratic VP nominee on James Cox's losing ticket; early national profile.
1921
Polio Diagnosis
Struck at Campobello Island, New Brunswick; paralyzed below waist permanently.
1928–1932
Governor of New York
Won governorship over Al Smith's vacancy; established relief programs used as New Deal models.
1933
Glass-Steagall Act
Separated commercial and investment banking; created FDIC deposit insurance.
1933
Gold Standard Abandoned
Executive Order 6102 prohibited gold hoarding; dollar devalued via Gold Reserve Act (1934).
1933
Recognition of USSR
First US diplomatic recognition of Soviet Union; hoped for trade and counterweight to Japan.
1933
Good Neighbor Policy
Renounced unilateral US military intervention in Latin America; Platt Amendment abrogated (Cuba).
1935
Neutrality Acts (1935–37)
Congress barred arms sales to belligerents; FDR signed reluctantly, preferred more executive discretion.
1936
Landslide Reelection
Won 46 of 48 states vs. Alf Landon; largest Electoral College margin since 1820.
1937–38
Roosevelt Recession
Premature budget-cutting caused sharp economic relapse; reversed by renewed spending.
1939
Einstein-Szilard Letter
Einstein's letter warned FDR of German atomic research; led to Advisory Committee on Uranium, precursor to Manhattan Project.
1940
Destroyers-for-Bases Deal
Transferred 50 US destroyers to Britain in exchange for 99-year leases on naval bases; bypassed Congress.
1941
Lend-Lease Act
Authorized lending or leasing war supplies to Allied nations; FDR compared it to lending a garden hose to a neighbor.
1941
Atlantic Charter
Eight-point declaration with Churchill; included self-determination and freedom of the seas.
1944
GI Bill (Servicemen's Readjustment Act)
Provided college tuition, low-cost mortgages, and unemployment pay for returning veterans.
1944
Second Bill of Rights (Economic Bill of Rights)
January 1944 State of the Union proposed rights to employment, education, healthcare; never legislated.

Famous quotes & speeches

  • "A date which will live in infamy" — Pearl Harbor address, 1941
  • Four Freedoms: speech, worship, want, fear — State of the Union, Jan. 1941

New Deal agencies (alphabet soup)

  • CCC — Civilian Conservation Corps (young men, reforestation)
  • TVA — Tennessee Valley Authority (dams, electricity)
  • AAA — Agricultural Adjustment Administration (crop subsidies)
  • NRA / NIRA — industry codes (struck down 1935)
  • WPA — Works Progress Administration (public works, arts)
  • PWA — Public Works Administration (Harold Ickes)
  • NLRB — National Labor Relations Board (Wagner Act)
  • SEC — Securities and Exchange Commission (1934)
  • FDIC — Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation
  • FHA — Federal Housing Administration (mortgage insurance)

Key people around FDR

  • Eleanor Roosevelt — wife; UN Human Rights advocate; "his eyes and ears"
  • Harry Hopkins — chief aide; ran WPA and Lend-Lease
  • Harold Ickes — Interior Secretary; ran PWA; "Honest Harold"
  • Frances Perkins — Labor Secretary; first woman Cabinet member; architect of Social Security
  • Henry Morgenthau Jr. — Treasury Secretary (long-serving)
  • Cordell Hull — Secretary of State; won 1945 Nobel Peace Prize for UN role
  • Brain Trust: Moley, Tugwell, Berle (Columbia academics)
  • Louis Howe — FDR's personal political manager, helped him through polio years

Numbers & landmarks

  • 4 terms — only president elected 4 times (1932, 36, 40, 44)
  • 22nd Amendment (1951) — ratified in reaction to FDR's 4 terms; two-term limit
  • $5 billion — approximate annual WPA spending at peak
  • 1933–1945 — 12 years, 39 days in office
  • Warm Springs, GA — "Little White House"; death site
  • Hyde Park, NY — birthplace and burial site; Springwood estate
  • FDR Memorial — Washington DC, dedicated 1997 (shows him in wheelchair)
  • March of Dimes — FDR's polio fundraising campaign (originally National Foundation for Infantile Paralysis)

Comparison Traps

Adjacent figures and events that trip up buzzers. Know the distinguishing clue fast.

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Theodore Roosevelt (TR)TR = 26th president (1901–09), "Bully pulpit," Square Deal, trust-busting, Panama Canal, Rough Riders — Republican. FDR = 32nd, Democrat, New Deal. TR was FDR's fifth cousin. If the question says "Bull Moose" or "Rough Rider," it's TR.
Herbert HooverHoover = 31st president (1929–1933), presided over crash's onset, preferred voluntarism over federal action, "Hoovervilles." FDR defeated him 1932. If the question blames inaction after 1929, it's Hoover.
Woodrow WilsonWilson = 28th president (1913–1921), WWI, League of Nations, 14 Points, Federal Reserve Act. FDR served as Assistant Secretary of the Navy under Wilson. If the question mentions the League or 14 Points, it's Wilson.
Harry S. TrumanTruman = 33rd; succeeded FDR April 12, 1945; atomic bombs, Marshall Plan, Korean War, NATO. FDR signed no atomic order — that was Truman. If the question mentions Hiroshima or Cold War doctrine, it's Truman.
Huey LongLong = Democratic Senator from Louisiana, FDR opponent from the left; "Share Our Wealth" program; assassinated 1935. Often confused as part of FDR's coalition — he was a rival who pushed FDR leftward.
The New Deal vs. the Fair DealNew Deal = FDR's Depression-era programs (1933–39). Fair Deal = Truman's domestic agenda (1949). If the question says "Fair Deal," it's Truman.
Yalta vs. PotsdamYalta (Feb. 1945) = FDR, Churchill, Stalin — UN, postwar zones, Soviet Pacific entry. Potsdam (July–Aug. 1945) = Truman, Churchill/Attlee, Stalin — ultimatum to Japan, Germany occupation. FDR died before Potsdam.
WWII Firsts: FDR's roleFDR authorized the Manhattan Project (1942) but never knew of the atomic bomb's success — Truman was told at Potsdam. FDR signed the internment order; Truman dropped the bombs.
