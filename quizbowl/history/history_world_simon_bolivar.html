Quick Overview

Born / DiedCaracas, 1783 – Santa Marta, 1830
Title"El Libertador" — the universal nickname
Liberated nationsVenezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia
Dream projectGran Colombia (1819–1831); collapsed before his death
Class / originCreole aristocracy (criollo); educated in Europe
Key mentorSimón Rodríguez (tutor); inspired by Rousseau

Defining clue cluster

  • The only person to have a country named after him during his lifetime — Bolivia (1825).
  • Crossed the Andes in the winter of 1819 to surprise Royalists — compared to Napoleon and Hannibal.
  • Dictated the "Letter from Jamaica" (1815) predicting Latin American independence from exile in Kingston.
  • Died of tuberculosis at age 47; was considered a failed statesman by many at the time of his death.
  • Close associate: Francisco de Paula Santander (vice-president of Gran Colombia; later rival).
  • Military right hand: Antonio José de Sucre, victor at Ayacucho.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "El Libertador" — the single most given-away descriptor.
  • "This man liberated Bolivia, which is named after him."
  • "The Battle of Boyacá" or "Battle of Carabobo" → Bolívar.
  • "Gran Colombia" as a fragile union he created and watched disintegrate.

High-Yield Events (Very Common)

Key episodes most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard/early clue. recog = mid-question recognition clue.

Oath on Monte Sacro (1805)
Rome, 1805 · Sworn on Aventine Hill with Simón Rodríguez
powerBolívar vowed to free South America from Spanish rule while standing on the Aventine Hill in Rome, with his tutor Rodríguez as witness. The vow is frequently quoted as early-question biography.
recogInspired by his European travels and observation of Napoleon's coronation in Paris the same year.
recogOften cited as the founding mythological moment of his independence mission.
Letter from Jamaica (1815)
Kingston, Jamaica · September 6, 1815
powerWritten in exile after Royalists retook Venezuela; addressed to an unnamed British merchant (Henry Cullen). Predicted independence for all of Spanish America and outlined potential republican governments.
recogOften called the "Jamaica Letter" — one of Latin America's founding political documents.
recogBolívar argued Spanish Americans were neither fully European nor fully indigenous — a key statement of Creole identity.

Crossing of the Andes & Battle of Boyacá (1819)
July–August 1819 · New Granada (modern Colombia)
powerLed ~2,500 troops including British legionnaires across the páramo of Pisba in July 1819 — high-altitude crossing in winter conditions; hundreds died from cold and altitude sickness before battle.
recogDecisive victory at Boyacá Bridge (August 7, 1819) — Spanish viceroy fled Bogotá; effectively liberated New Granada in one campaign.
recogDirectly led to the founding of Gran Colombia at the Congress of Angostura (December 1819).
wiki ↗
Congress of Angostura & Gran Colombia (1819–1821)
Angostura (Ciudad Bolívar), Venezuela · Feb 1819 / Congress of Cúcuta 1821
powerBolívar delivered the Angostura Address (February 15, 1819) — a constitutional speech proposing a republic with a hereditary senate and strong executive, drawing on British constitutional models.
recogGran Colombia officially unified Venezuela, New Granada, and Quito (Ecuador). Bolívar = President; Santander = Vice-President.
recogThe union collapsed by 1830 — Venezuela and Ecuador seceded before Bolívar's death.
wiki ↗
Battle of Carabobo (1821)
June 24, 1821 · Near Valencia, Venezuela
powerThe British Legion (Irish and British volunteers) played a decisive flanking role, breaking Spanish lines. Its commander, Thomas Ilderton Ferriar (often cited as the British Legion acting as a whole), took severe casualties.
recogEffectively ended Spanish rule in Venezuela — Bolívar proclaimed Venezuelan independence on the field.
recogJune 24 is now Venezuelan Armed Forces Day and Bolívar's greatest Venezuelan military victory.
wiki ↗
Guayaquil Conference & Liberation of Peru (1822–1824)
Guayaquil, July 26–27, 1822 · Battles of Junín & Ayacucho 1824
powerThe Guayaquil Conference between Bolívar and José de San Martín — their only meeting — is historically mysterious; San Martín left immediately afterward and withdrew from South American politics entirely.
recogBolívar then led the campaign into Peru; his general Antonio José de Sucre won the Battle of Ayacucho (December 9, 1824) — the final major battle of Spanish American independence.
recogThe Battle of Junín (August 6, 1824) was Bolívar's personal last major battle command — a cavalry engagement.
wiki ↗
Bolivia Founded & Bolivian Constitution (1825–1826)
Upper Peru → Bolivia, August 6, 1825
powerBolívar drafted the Bolivian Constitution of 1826 — his most controversial constitutional document, proposing a life-term president who could appoint his successor (an elected monarchy in practice). Rejected in Venezuela and Colombia.
recogUpper Peru renamed itself Bolivia in his honor on August 6, 1825 — the only country ever named after a living person during their own lifetime.
recogSucre became Bolivia's first president; Bolívar was named lifetime protector.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known events and facts that open hard tossups. One-line ID each.

1783
Birth in Caracas
Born July 24 to wealthy criollo family; both parents died by age 9; raised by slaves and uncle.
1799–1802
European education & marriage
Studied in Madrid; married María Teresa Rodríguez del Toro (1802); she died of yellow fever within a year — Bolívar vowed never to remarry.
1810–1812
First Venezuelan Republic
Venezuela declared independence July 5, 1811; republic collapsed after Royalist counter-offensive and the catastrophic 1812 Caracas earthquake (interpreted as divine punishment by clergy).
1813
Admirable Campaign & "War to the Death"
Bolívar reconquered Venezuela in 90 days; issued the brutal Decree of War to the Death (June 1813): all Spaniards who did not actively support independence would be executed. Earned him the title "El Libertador."
1814–1815
Llanero uprising & second exile
José Tomás Boves led loyalist Llanero cavalry that devastated Bolívar's forces; Bolívar fled to Cartagena, then Jamaica, then Haiti.
1816
Haitian support from Pétion
President Alexandre Pétion of Haiti gave Bolívar ships, men, and supplies in exchange for a promise to abolish slavery in liberated territories.
1817–1818
Orinoco base & alliance with Páez
Established base at Angostura on the Orinoco; allied with Llanero caudillo José Antonio Páez, switching the plains cavalry to the patriot side.
1822
Liberation of Ecuador & Guayaquil annexation
Sucre won the Battle of Pichincha (May 24, 1822); Bolívar then annexed Guayaquil to Gran Colombia over San Martín's objections — precipitating their famous conference.
1826
Congress of Panama (1826)
Bolívar's vision of a Latin American confederation; delegates from Gran Colombia, Peru, Mexico, and Central America attended. The resulting treaty was not ratified by most states — the confederation dream failed.
1828
September Conspiracy (Septembrina)
Assassination attempt in Bogotá (September 25, 1828); his mistress Manuela Sáenz — nicknamed "La Libertadora del Libertador" — warned him and helped him escape through a window.
1829–1830
Dissolution of Gran Colombia & death
Venezuela and Ecuador seceded; Bolívar resigned the presidency (April 1830); died of tuberculosis at Quinta de San Pedro Alejandrino estate, Santa Marta, December 17, 1830. Famous last words reportedly: "America is ungovernable."

Recognition / Clue Bank

Titles & epithets

  • "El Libertador" — universal title
  • "The George Washington of South America" — common comparison in questions
  • "Father of the Nation" in Venezuela, Colombia, Bolivia, Ecuador, Peru
  • Born July 24 → Simón Bolívar Day in many countries

Key battles & dates

  • Boyacá, August 7, 1819 — liberates New Granada
  • Carabobo, June 24, 1821 — liberates Venezuela
  • Pichincha, May 24, 1822 — liberates Ecuador (Sucre)
  • Junín, August 6, 1824 — last Bolívar-commanded battle
  • Ayacucho, December 9, 1824 — final independence battle (Sucre)

Key documents & quotes

  • Letter from Jamaica (1815) — predicts independence, defines Creole identity
  • Angostura Address (1819) — constitutional speech, hereditary senate proposal
  • Bolivian Constitution (1826) — life-term president; rejected everywhere
  • Decree of War to the Death (1813) — all Spaniards who do not side with patriots face execution
  • "America is ungovernable… He who serves a revolution plows the sea." — attributed deathbed quote

People around him

  • Antonio José de Sucre — top general; won Ayacucho; Bolivia's first president
  • Francisco de Paula Santander — VP of Gran Colombia; later rival
  • José Antonio Páez — Venezuelan caudillo; key cavalry ally; later led Venezuela's secession
  • Manuela Sáenz — lover; saved his life in 1828 Septembrina; called "La Libertadora del Libertador"
  • Simón Rodríguez — tutor/mentor; inspired him with Rousseau
  • Alexandre Pétion — Haitian president; supplied resources in 1816
  • José de San Martín — rival liberator of southern South America; met only once (Guayaquil)

Numbers & geography

  • 5 countries liberated: Venezuela, Colombia, Ecuador, Peru, Bolivia
  • Bolivia named for him — area: Upper Peru → Bolivia, August 6, 1825
  • Gran Colombia covered roughly modern Venezuela + Colombia + Ecuador + Panama
  • British Legion — ~5,000 European volunteers who fought for him
  • Andes crossing: Pisba páramo, ~13,000 ft elevation, mid-winter 1819

"Also known for" & legacy

  • The Bolivarian Revolution — Hugo Chávez's ideology named after him
  • Appears on the currency of Venezuela (bolívar) and coins/notes across the region
  • Gabriel García Márquez novel: The General in His Labyrinth (1989) — fictionalized last journey
  • His remains repatriated to Venezuela 1842; moved to the National Pantheon, Caracas
  • The Bolivarian Alliance for the Peoples (ALBA) — modern political bloc invoking his name

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
José de San MartínSan Martín liberated Argentina, Chile, and southern Peru from the south; Bolívar came from the north. They met once at Guayaquil (1822) — San Martín then retired. San Martín = no country named after him; never became a dictator. Bolívar = Gran Colombia, Bolivia, life-president aspirations.
Francisco de MirandaMiranda was Bolívar's predecessor as Venezuelan revolutionary leader; led the First Republic (1812); surrendered to Royalists; died in a Spanish prison. Bolívar actually handed him over to the Spanish — a controversial act. Miranda ≠ "El Libertador."
Antonio José de SucreSucre was Bolívar's most trusted general, not the overall leader. Sucre won Ayacucho and became Bolivia's first president. He was assassinated in 1830. Bolivia's capital is named Sucre after him — not after Bolívar directly.
Bernardo O'HigginsO'Higgins = "Supreme Director" of Chile after 1817; fought alongside San Martín at Chacabuco. Not connected to Bolívar's northern campaigns. Distinct Irish-Chilean background.
Miguel HidalgoHidalgo rang the bell in Mexico (Grito de Dolores, 1810) — entirely separate northern independence movement; died 1811. Bolívar never operated in Mexico; different era, different geography.
Napoleon BonaparteQuestions often compare them directly — both crossed mountains, both attempted continental political unification, both ended as failed dictators/exiles. Key difference: Bolívar never crowned himself; he repeatedly resigned power. Napoleon = French emperor; Bolívar = elected republican who admired and ultimately rejected Napoleonic autocracy.

Big-Picture Cross-Discipline Links Bolívar bridges several tossup categories: his campaigns connect to Enlightenment political philosophy (Rousseau, Montesquieu — which he read and cited); the Congress of Panama (1826) anticipates 20th-century Pan-Americanism and the OAS; his failure to hold Gran Colombia together mirrors the tensions between federalism and centralism debated in the U.S. during the same period. García Márquez's The General in His Labyrinth makes him a literature answer too. Questions comparing him to Napoleon are almost always about both figures' ambiguous relationship with republican versus authoritarian governance.
