Quick Overview

Dates1564–1593 (b. Canterbury; died Deptford, age 29)
NationalityEnglish; Elizabethan era
FormBlank verse drama; narrative poetry; translations
Signature meter"Mighty line" — iambic pentameter, unrhymed
EducationCorpus Christi College, Cambridge (MA 1587, rumored spy)
DeathStabbed at Deptford tavern — Ingram Frizer; disputed spy/brawl

Defining traits

  • Inventor of English blank verse drama — iambic pentameter on stage before Shakespeare mastered it.
  • Overreacher protagonists: each hero strains beyond human limits (knowledge, power, beauty, conquest) and is destroyed.
  • Marlowian hero: solitary, transgressive, contemptuous of convention — Tamburlaine, Faustus, Barabas, Edward II.
  • Secularism and ambivalence: orthodox Christian endings paired with deeply sympathetic portrayals of damnation.
  • Probable government spy for Walsingham's intelligence network — absences from Cambridge explained by Privy Council letter.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The "mighty line" — Ben Jonson's phrase for Marlowe's meter.
  • "Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships" — Doctor Faustus, Helen of Troy scene.
  • Stabbed at Deptford tavern in 1593 — specific location is a giveaway for biographical tossups.
  • The Baines Note — informant's report listing Marlowe's alleged atheist/blasphemous opinions.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 5 works most likely to be the answer or anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Doctor Faustus (The Tragical History of the Life and Death of Doctor Faustus)
c. 1592 (pub. 1604 A-text / 1616 B-text) · play in blank verse and prose
powerTwo competing texts — A-text (1604) and B-text (1616); scholars debate which is closer to Marlowe's original. Source is the Historia von D. Johann Fausten (German Faustbuch, 1587), translated into English by P.F.
recogFaustus sells his soul to the devil Mephistopheles (Mephostophilis) for 24 years of power and knowledge.
recogFamous last soliloquy begins: "Ah, Faustus, now hast thou but one bare hour to live." The clock strikes 11 PM.
recogFaustus conjures the shade of Helen of Troy: "Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships, / And burnt the topless towers of Ilium?"
recogComic subplot with the clown Wagner and the horse-courser; the Good Angel and Evil Angel appear on stage.
"Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it." — Mephistopheles on being asked if hell is a place

Tamburlaine the Great (Parts I and II)
c. 1587–1588 · two-part play; Part I performed ~1587
powerBased on the life of the Central Asian conqueror Timur (Timur-e Lang / Timur the Lame). Marlowe's source was Pedro Mexía's Silva de Varia Lección and George Whetstone's The English Myrror.
recogTamburlaine begins as a Scythian shepherd, rises to conquer Persia, defeating King Mycetes and King Cosroe.
recogHe captures the Turkish emperor Bajazeth, who is kept in a cage and used as a footstool; Bajazeth and his wife Zabina dash their brains out against the bars.
recogTamburlaine's beloved wife is Zenocrate; her death drives Part II. He burns the Quran, claiming to be greater than Muhammad.
recogThree-color tent sequence: white tent (mercy first day), red tent (no mercy second day), black tent (no prisoners third day).
"Is it not passing brave to be a king, / And ride in triumph through Persepolis?" — Tamburlaine I, II.v
wiki ↗
The Jew of Malta
c. 1589–1590 · pub. 1633 · five-act play
powerPrologue delivered by Machiavel (personified Machiavellianism), who introduces the play as a tribute to his "disciple." The prologue's tone — amoral, satiric — colors the entire work.
recogProtagonist: Barabas, a wealthy Jewish merchant in Malta, whose fortune is seized by the Governor Ferneze to pay Turkish tribute.
recogBarabas poisons his own daughter Abigail and her lover; schemes with the slave Ithamore; eventually boils to death in a cauldron of his own devising.
recogConsidered the prototype for Shakespeare's Shylock in The Merchant of Venice.
"I count religion but a childish toy, / And hold there is no sin but ignorance." — Machiavel, Prologue
wiki ↗
Edward II
c. 1592 · pub. 1594 · five-act history play
powerSource: Holinshed's Chronicles. Notable for sympathetic portrayal of a same-sex relationship between Edward and his favorite Piers Gaveston — groundbreaking for Elizabethan drama.
recogThe barons, led by the Earl of Mortimer, force Edward to exile Gaveston, who is captured and executed. Edward's queen, Isabella, becomes Mortimer's lover and conspirator.
recogEdward is imprisoned in Berkeley Castle and murdered — traditionally with a red-hot poker inserted through a horn, though the play leaves the method ambiguous.
recogConsidered the first English history play with a fully tragic, psychologically complex protagonist; influenced Shakespeare's Richard II.
wiki ↗
Hero and Leander
written c. 1593; pub. 1598 (completed by George Chapman) · narrative poem (epyllion)
powerAn epyllion ("little epic") in heroic couplets; Marlowe left it unfinished at his death — Chapman wrote Sestiads III–VI. Source: the Greek poet Musaeus Grammaticus (5th–6th c. CE), not Ovid directly.
recogHero, priestess of Venus at Sestos; Leander of Abydos swims the Hellespont nightly to visit her. He drowns in a storm; Hero dies of grief.
recogFamous erotic digression: the sea-god Neptune mistakes Leander for Ganymede and embraces him while he swims.
"Who ever loved that loved not at first sight?" — Hero and Leander I.176 (later quoted by Shakespeare in As You Like It)

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Minor works and lesser-known facts that surface as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

Dido, Queen of Carthage
c. 1585–1586 (co-authored with Thomas Nashe) · pub. 1594
Earliest known play; Aeneas abandons Dido, who immolates herself; likely written for boy actors.
The Massacre at Paris
c. 1593 · survives only in corrupt octavo
Dramatizes the 1572 St. Bartholomew's Day Massacre; the Duke of Guise is the Marlovian overreacher.
"The Passionate Shepherd to His Love"
pub. 1599 in The Passionate Pilgrim
Pastoral lyric — "Come live with me and be my love"; answered by Raleigh's "The Nymph's Reply to the Shepherd."
Lucan's Pharsalia (translation, Book I)
pub. 1600
Marlowe's only surviving classical translation; Latin epic of Caesar vs. Pompey.
Ovid's Amores (translation, All Ovid's Elegies)
c. 1580s; pub. c. 1595–1600
Early erotic translations; copies burned by order of the Archbishop of Canterbury in 1599 along with Hall's Satires.
Baines Note (1593)
informant document, not a literary work
Richard Baines's report to the Privy Council alleging Marlowe's atheism, blasphemy, and forgery of coin; submitted days before his death.
The Authorship Question
19th c.–present
"Marlovian theory": some claim Marlowe faked his death and wrote Shakespeare's works; consensus rejects this; Oxford UP's New Oxford Shakespeare (2016) lists him as co-author of Henry VI Parts 1–3.
Corpus Christi Portrait (1585)
possibly Marlowe; motto "Quod me nutrit me destruit"
"What nourishes me destroys me" — frequently cited as Marlovian self-description.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Characters (by play)

  • Mephistopheles / Mephostophilis — Faustus's devil
  • Wagner — Faustus's servant/clown
  • Barabas — the Jew of Malta; dies in boiling cauldron
  • Ithamore — Barabas's Turkish slave
  • Abigail — Barabas's daughter, poisoned by him
  • Zenocrate — Tamburlaine's wife
  • Bajazeth — Turkish emperor kept in cage
  • Piers Gaveston — Edward II's favorite
  • Mortimer — Edward II's nemesis
  • Isabella — Edward's queen, Mortimer's accomplice

Motifs & style markers

  • "Mighty line" — Ben Jonson's label for Marlowe's unrhymed iambic pentameter
  • Overreacher — hero who transgresses human limits, suffers for it
  • Machiavellian villain — amoral schemer (Barabas, Mortimer)
  • Hyperbolic rhetoric — characters catalogue power in vast geographic terms
  • In medias res plotting — action begins already at crisis
  • Recurring motif: the bargain or contract (Faustus's deed of gift in blood)

Famous lines (source ID)

  • "Was this the face that launch'd a thousand ships" — Faustus
  • "Why, this is hell, nor am I out of it" — Faustus (Mephisto)
  • "Cut is the branch that might have grown full straight" — Faustus Chorus
  • "Who ever loved that loved not at first sight?" — Hero and Leander
  • "Come live with me and be my love" — "The Passionate Shepherd"
  • "I count religion but a childish toy" — Jew of Malta, Prologue
  • "Infinite riches in a little room" — Jew of Malta, Barabas

Biographical hard clues

  • Born Canterbury, 1564 — same year as Shakespeare
  • Cambridge degree withheld until Privy Council intervened — evidence of spy work for Walsingham
  • Killed at Eleanor Bull's house in Deptford, 30 May 1593; official verdict: quarrel over the "reckoning" (the bill)
  • Killers: Ingram Frizer (stabbed him); witnesses Robert Poley and Nicholas Skeres
  • Died 10 days after being called before the Privy Council on Baines's charges
  • Shares birth year with Galileo (1564)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
ShakespeareContemporary — both born 1564, both wrote Elizabethan history plays. Key differentiators: Marlowe's heroes are singular, solitary overreachers; Shakespeare's tragic figures are embedded in complex social worlds. Marlowe = blank verse before Shakespeare fully developed it. Faustus ≠ any Shakespeare play. "Mighty line" = Marlowe.
Ben JonsonJonson coined "mighty line" as a tribute to Marlowe. Jonson's Volpone and The Alchemist are also anti-hero satires, but Jonson is Jacobean, his tone is satiric-comic, his verse more compressed. Jonson ≠ Faustus ≠ Tamburlaine.
Thomas KydKyd's Spanish Tragedy (c. 1587) is the other founding Elizabethan tragedy, featuring the first revenge tragedy and proto-Hamlet figure Hieronimo. Kyd = revenge tragedy; Marlowe = overreacher tragedy. Kyd may have roomed with Marlowe.
John Ford / John WebsterJacobean/Caroline dramatists known for dark tragedy ('Tis Pity She's a Whore, The Duchess of Malfi). Marlowe is Elizabethan and pre-dates them by a generation.
Goethe's FaustGoethe's two-part Faust (1808/1832) is German Romantic — Faust is ultimately saved through striving. Marlowe's Faustus is damned, dragged to hell at the end. Marlowe = damnation; Goethe = redemption.
Ovid (on Hero & Leander)The Hero and Leander myth is in Ovid's Heroides (XVIII–XIX), but Marlowe's direct source is the Greek poet Musaeus, not Ovid. Don't say Ovid as the source for Marlowe's poem.

Big Picture — Cross-Discipline Links Marlowe sits at the intersection of multiple quizbowl categories: his Faustus connects to the Faust myth (compare Goethe, Gounod's opera, Mann's Doctor Faustus); Tamburlaine ties to Central Asian history (Timur, the Timurid dynasty); Edward II to English medieval history (barons' wars, the Despensers) and to LGBTQ literary history; Hero and Leander to Greek mythology (Leander, Hellespont, Musaeus) and to Ovid. The 1564 birth-year links Marlowe to both Shakespeare and Galileo — a common tossup year. His suspected espionage connects to Elizabethan intelligence history (Walsingham, the spy network that brought down Mary Queen of Scots).
