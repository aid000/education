Quick Overview

Full namePublius Vergilius Maro
Dates70–19 BCE; born near Mantua, died Brundisium
PatronMaecenas (early works); Emperor Augustus (Aeneid)
Three major worksEclogues · Georgics · Aeneid
MeterDactylic hexameter throughout
Later influenceDante names him as guide in Divine Comedy

Defining traits (buzz-worthy style markers)

  • Dactylic hexameter — the meter of Homer, used for all major works.
  • Spondaic effects — slow, heavy feet to convey weight and grief ("sunt lacrimae rerum").
  • Epic similes drawn from nature (bees, storms, fire) echoing Homer but surpassing in pathos.
  • Imitative harmony — sound mimics sense (hissing snakes, galloping hooves).
  • Unfinished deathbed wish: asked that the Aeneid be burned; Augustus overruled him.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Arma virumque cano" — opening of the Aeneid, most often-quoted Latin line.
  • "Sunt lacrimae rerum" — "there are tears for things," Aeneid I.
  • The Mantuan poet or "the poet of Mantua."
  • Dante's guide through Hell and Purgatory in the Divine Comedy.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The three canonical works. power = hard, early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Aeneid
c. 29–19 BCE · epic poem · 12 books · dactylic hexameter · left unfinished at Virgil's death
powerOpens in medias res — Aeneas's fleet is driven off course by Juno's wrath and Aeolus's winds; the hero washes up at Carthage. Book structure mirrors Homer: Books 1–6 = Odyssey; Books 7–12 = Iliad.
recogBooks 1–6: sea-storm → Carthage → Dido (queen who falls in love with Aeneas and kills herself on a pyre when he leaves) → Sicily → descent to the Underworld (Book 6), guided by the Sibyl of Cumae.
recogIn the Underworld, his father Anchises reveals the future glory of Rome; the parade of Roman heroes (including Augustus) justifies Rome's destiny.
recogBooks 7–12: war in Latium against Turnus (king of the Rutulians); key allies: Evander (Arcadian king) and his son Pallas. Aeneas kills Turnus in the final line.
recogFamous characters: Dido, Turnus, Lavinia (Aeneas's destined wife), Camilla (warrior maiden), Mezentius (impious Etruscan), Nisus and Euryalus (loyal pair killed in night raid).
recogFamous lines: "Arma virumque cano" (opening); "Sunt lacrimae rerum" (I.462); "Forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit" (I.203 — "perhaps one day it will help to remember these things"); "Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes" — said by Laocoon about the Trojan Horse (II.49).
recogThe Gates of Sleep: Aeneas and the Sibyl exit the Underworld through the Gate of Ivory (false dreams), not the Gate of Horn — a controversial interpretive crux.

Eclogues (Bucolics)
c. 42–39 BCE · 10 pastoral poems · modeled on Theocritus's Idylls
powerEclogue 4 ("the Messianic Eclogue") — predicts birth of a golden-age child who will usher in a new age of peace; later read by Christians as a prophecy of Jesus, earning Virgil his medieval reputation as a prophet/magician.
recogSetting is an idealized Arcadia; shepherds Tityrus and Meliboeus open Eclogue 1 — Tityrus keeps his farm, Meliboeus is dispossessed (allusion to Mantuan land confiscations after Philippi).
recogEclogue 5: lament and deification of the shepherd Daphnis (widely read as Julius Caesar). Eclogue 10: mourns the poet Gallus's unhappy love.
recogInvented the singing contest (amoebean verse) — alternating couplets between two shepherds; Eclogues 3 and 7.
wiki ↗
Georgics
c. 36–29 BCE · 4 books · didactic poem on farming · commissioned by Maecenas
powerBook 4 contains the embedded myth of Orpheus and Eurydice — Orpheus looks back, loses Eurydice a second time, is torn apart by Maenads; his severed head floats down the Hebrus still calling "Eurydice." Also includes the Aristaeus epyllion: beekeeper Aristaeus causes Orpheus's grief (his bees chasing Eurydice to her death); learns from Proteus how to regenerate bees from a bull's carcass (bugonia).
recogBook 1: farming / weather signs. Book 2: vines and trees; the "O fortunatos agricolas" passage praising farmers. Book 3: cattle and horses; ends with a devastating cattle plague in Noricum. Book 4: bees — the hive as a model society.
recogModeled on Hesiod's Works and Days and Lucretius's De Rerum Natura; surpasses both in emotional range.

Minor works, internal episodes, and hard tossup clues — one-line IDs.

Camilla (Books 7, 11) Warrior maiden of the Volsci; killed by Arruns while distracted by golden armor.

Aristaeus Epyllion (Georgics 4) Beekeeper consults Proteus; learns bugonia (new bees from sacrificed bull).
Georgics Book 2 praise of Italy "Salve, magna parens frugum, Saturnia tellus" — Italy as Saturn's land.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Characters (Aeneid)

  • Aeneas — Trojan hero, son of Venus and Anchises; epithet "pious" (pius Aeneas)
  • Dido — Queen of Carthage; suicide on a pyre using Aeneas's sword
  • Turnus — Rutulian king; Aeneas's final opponent
  • Juno — chief divine antagonist; protects Carthage and Turnus
  • Ascanius / Iulus — Aeneas's son; ancestor of the Julian gens (Augustus)
  • Sibyl of Cumae — prophetess who guides Aeneas in Book 6
  • Anchises — Aeneas's father; rescued from Troy; dies in Sicily; meets Aeneas in Elysium
  • Lavinia — daughter of King Latinus; Aeneas's destined Latin bride
  • Evander — Arcadian king on the future site of Rome; father of Pallas
  • Pallas — Evander's son; killed by Turnus; drives Aeneas's final fury

Key Latin phrases

  • "Arma virumque cano" — "I sing of arms and the man" (Aeneid opening)
  • "Sunt lacrimae rerum" — "there are tears for things" (I.462)
  • "Timeo Danaos et dona ferentes" — Laocoon warning about the Trojan Horse
  • "Forsan et haec olim meminisse iuvabit" — Aeneas comforting his men at sea
  • "Furor impius" — "impious rage"; the force Augustus will chain (I.295)
  • "Tu regere imperio populos, Romane, memento" — Anchises' charge to Rome (VI.851)
  • "Omnia vincit amor" — "Love conquers all" (Eclogue 10)
  • "Dum Domus Aeneae..." — eternity of Rome tied to Aeneas's line

Style / form markers

  • Dactylic hexameter — one heavy dactyl (— ˘ ˘) per foot; 6 feet per line
  • Epic simile — extended comparisons (queen bee simile; fire simile for Dido's grief)
  • Golden line — two adjectives, two nouns, verb in center: ABVBA word order
  • Ecphrasis — vivid description of objects (shield of Aeneas, Book 8)
  • In medias res — begins at sea-storm, not at the fall of Troy
  • Pastoral Arcadia as idealized retreat (Eclogues)

Context & influence

  • Patron: Maecenas (Eclogues, Georgics); Augustus directly for the Aeneid
  • Models: Homer (Iliad + Odyssey), Hesiod, Theocritus, Apollonius of Rhodes, Lucretius
  • Dante names him guide; calls him "my master and my author" (Inferno I)
  • Sortes Vergilianae — divination by opening the Aeneid at random; practiced through the Middle Ages
  • Birth near Mantua; estate near Naples (Parthenope); tomb on the road to Pozzuoli
  • Died 19 BCE returning from Greece; asked that the Aeneid be destroyed
  • Contemporary poets: Horace, Ovid, Propertius (Augustan Age)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
HomerHomer's Iliad is the Trojan War itself; Homer's Odyssey is Odysseus's return. Virgil's Aeneid follows the Trojan survivor to Italy. If the clue says Dido, Turnus, Lavinia, or Roman destiny — it's Virgil. If it says Achilles, Penelope, or the suitors — it's Homer.
OvidOvid's Metamorphoses also retells myths in Latin hexameter but focuses on transformation. Ovid's tone is witty and ironic; Virgil's is solemn and patriotic. Orpheus/Eurydice appears in both — Virgil's version is in Georgics 4; Ovid's is in Metamorphoses 10–11.
HoraceFellow Augustan; calls Virgil "animae dimidium meae" (half of my soul). Horace wrote Odes and Satires, not epic. If the clue says Odes, Ars Poetica, or Sapphic/Alcaic meter — it's Horace.
Apollonius of RhodesGreek epic on the Argonauts (Argonautica); the Medea/Jason love story is Virgil's model for Dido/Aeneas. If the answer is Greek and about Jason — it's Apollonius.
LucretiusLucretius also wrote Latin didactic hexameter (De Rerum Natura), but his subject is Epicurean atomic philosophy, not farming or epic myth.
TheocritusThe Greek model for pastoral poetry; wrote Idylls. Virgil's Eclogues imitate him but are in Latin and add political allegory. If the setting is Sicily and the language is Greek — it's Theocritus.
StatiusLater Latin epic poet (Thebaid, Achilleid); Dante also uses Statius in Purgatorio. If the subject is the Seven Against Thebes or Achilles' youth, it's Statius, not Virgil.

Big Picture — Cross-Discipline Web
  • Politics: the Aeneid is Augustan propaganda — Aeneas's son Iulus legitimizes the Julian gens; Anchises' parade culminates with Augustus as a second founder.
  • Mythology: Dido ↔ Medea (both foreign queens destroyed by a hero's departure); Orpheus myth in Georgics 4 ↔ Ovid Metamorphoses 10; Shield of Aeneas ↔ Shield of Achilles (Iliad 18).
  • Later literature: Dante (Divine Comedy); Spenser (Faerie Queene); Milton (Paradise Lost — Satan's rhetoric echoes Juno's); Tennyson's "Tithonus"; Ursula K. Le Guin's Lavinia.
  • Cross-subject trap: "Orpheus" in a tossup can lead to Virgil (Georgics), Ovid (Met.), Monteverdi's opera L'Orfeo, or the Orphic mystery cult — read the context carefully.
