Quick Overview

Marble bust, British Museum
TraditionAncient Greek oral epic poetry; ~8th c. BCE
LanguageArchaic Greek (Ionic with Aeolic elements)
MeterDactylic hexameter ("heroic meter")
Two epicsIliad (war at Troy) · Odyssey (voyage home)
"The Homeric Question"Did one author write both? Was "Homer" even a person?
Epithets"The blind bard," "the bard of Chios," "the divine poet"

Defining traits

  • Oral-formulaic composition: repeated epithets ("wine-dark sea," "rosy-fingered Dawn," "swift-footed Achilles") fill the meter — key quizbowl clue type.
  • In medias res: both epics begin mid-action; the Iliad covers only ~51 days of the 10-year war.
  • Epic similes (Homeric similes): extended nature comparisons injected mid-battle.
  • Invocation of the Muse opens each epic — the Iliad invokes the Muse of the wrath of Achilles; the Odyssey invokes the Muse for "the man of many ways."
  • Transmitted via rhapsodes (professional reciters); written down in Athens under Peisistratos, c. 6th c. BCE.

Classic quizbowl giveaway lines

  • "Sing, O goddess, the anger of Achilles" — Iliad first line.
  • "Tell me, O Muse, of that ingenious hero who traveled far and wide" — Odyssey first line.
  • "Wine-dark sea," "rosy-fingered Dawn," "grey-eyed Athena" — formulaic epithets.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The two Homeric epics — learn both inside-out. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / mid-clue.

The Iliad
~8th c. BCE · 24 books · ~15,700 lines · dactylic hexameter

Setting, plot, structure

  • Covers 51 days in the final year of the 10-year Trojan War — not the whole war.
  • Opens with the wrath of Achilles after King Agamemnon seizes his prize Briseis.
  • Achilles withdraws from battle → Greeks suffer → his companion Patroclus dons Achilles' armor, is killed by Hector (Troy's greatest warrior).
  • Achilles returns, kills Hector, drags his body; the epic ends with Hector's funeral (not the fall of Troy).
  • King Priam of Troy ransoms Hector's body from Achilles in the final book — a scene of pathos central to tossups.

Key characters

  • Achilles — near-invincible Greek hero; son of sea-nymph Thetis; heel is his only vulnerability (not in the Iliad itself).
  • Hector — Trojan prince, son of Priam, husband of Andromache; the most sympathetic warrior.
  • Agamemnon — commander of the Greek forces; brother of Menelaus (Helen's husband).
  • Helen — whose elopement with Paris (aka Alexander) caused the war.
  • Ajax (Telamonian Ajax / "Greater Ajax") — huge Greek warrior; fights Hector to a draw.
  • Diomedes — Greek hero who wounds gods (Ares and Aphrodite) in Book 5.
  • Nestor — old, verbose Greek counselor famous for lengthy speeches.
  • Odysseus — wily Greek; co-stars in the Odyssey.
  • Sarpedon — Trojan ally, son of Zeus; Zeus debates saving him but lets him die.

Divine intervention ("theomachy")

  • Pro-Greek gods: Athena, Hera, Poseidon.
  • Pro-Trojan gods: Apollo, Aphrodite, Ares, Artemis.
  • Zeus aims to honor Thetis's request to let Trojans prevail temporarily (the "plan of Zeus").
  • Book 20 features actual gods fighting each other — the Theomachy.
powerAchilles' shield is described in a 130-line ekphrasis in Book 18 — made by Hephaestus, depicts the cosmos, cities at war and peace, a vineyard, and dancing; frequent standalone tossup clue.
powerThe quarrel over Chryseis (Agamemnon returns her to Apollo's priest Chryses only after a plague) triggers the whole plot before Briseis appears.
recog"Wrath of Achilles" = menis (first word of the Greek text — a rare, solemn word for divine rage).
recogHector bids farewell to wife Andromache and infant son Astyanax (Book 6) — the scene frightens the child when Hector removes his helmet.
The Odyssey
~8th c. BCE · 24 books · ~12,100 lines · dactylic hexameter

Setting, plot, structure

  • Covers the 10-year voyage of Odysseus (Latin: Ulysses) from Troy back to Ithaca.
  • Opens with the Telemachy (Books 1–4): Odysseus's son Telemachus seeks news of his father while suitors overrun the palace.
  • Odysseus tells his own story at Books 9–12 — the "embedded narrative" (Apologoi) to King Alcinous of the Phaeacians.
  • Ends with Odysseus disguised as a beggar, the contest of the bow (shooting an arrow through 12 axe-heads), and slaughter of the suitors.
  • Penelope (wife) — weaves and unravels a shroud to delay suitors; symbol of fidelity.
  • Argos — Odysseus's dog; recognizes him after 20 years, then dies.
  • Eurycleia — old nurse who recognizes Odysseus by his scar.

Famous episodes (quizbowl "episode bank")

  • Cyclops (Polyphemus) — blinded by a sharpened stake; Odysseus says his name is "Nobody" (Outis) so no one helps Polyphemus. Son of Poseidon → Poseidon curses the voyage.
  • Circe — sorceress on island Aeaea; turns men into pigs with her wand; Odysseus protected by the herb moly given by Hermes.
  • Sirens — Odysseus lashed to the mast; crew's ears stopped with beeswax.
  • Scylla and Charybdis — six-headed monster and a whirlpool; "between Scylla and Charybdis" = a dilemma.
  • Lotus-Eaters — eat lotus; sailors forget home.
  • Cattle of the Sun (Helios) — crew eats them; all killed by Zeus's thunderbolt; only Odysseus survives.
  • Calypso — nymph on island Ogygia; keeps Odysseus 7 years; Hermes orders her to release him (Book 5, where the Odyssey actually begins).
  • Nausicaa — Phaeacian princess who finds washed-up Odysseus.
  • Tiresias — blind prophet in the Underworld (Book 11, Nekyia); also meets the shades of Ajax, Agamemnon, Achilles, and his own mother Anticlea.
powerPenelope's web — she weaves a burial shroud for Laertes by day and unravels it by night for three years to delay choosing a suitor.
powerThe Phaeacians (King Alcinous, Queen Arete) provide the ship that finally brings Odysseus home; Poseidon petrifies that ship as punishment.
recog"The man of many ways" / "much-enduring Odysseus" / "polytropos" (many-turning) — the opening epithets of the Odyssey.
recogContest of the bow: only Odysseus can string his own great bow and shoot through 12 axe-handle holes — the suitors cannot.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Minor works, attributed texts, and hard sub-details that appear as early tossup clues or in bonus parts.

Homeric Hymns
Collection of 33 hexameter hymns attributed to Homer (not actually his); address individual gods — key ones: Hymn to Demeter (Persephone myth), Hymn to Hermes (cattle theft, lyre invention).
Batrachomyomachia ("Battle of Frogs and Mice")
Comic epic parody of the Iliad; wrongly attributed to Homer in antiquity; frogs vs. mice in mock heroic battle.
The Epic Cycle
Lost epics filling the Trojan War gaps: Cypria, Little Iliad, Iliupersis, Nostoi, Telegony — attributed to various poets, not Homer.
Book of the Dead (Nekyia) — Odyssey Book 11
Odysseus digs a pit, pours blood offerings; shades of Tiresias, Achilles, Agamemnon, Ajax, and his mother Anticlea speak; source of countless later literary "descents to the underworld."
Shield of Achilles — Iliad Book 18
130-line ekphrasis; Hephaestus forges it; depicts the cosmos, two cities (war/peace), harvest, vintage, cattle, dancing — a microcosm of human life.
Catalog of Ships — Iliad Book 2
Long enumeration of Greek forces and contingents from across Greece; the "Boiotian Catalog"; a famous test of patience and a source for historical geography.
Doloneia — Iliad Book 10
Night raid by Odysseus and Diomedes; they kill the Trojan spy Dolon and capture the horses of King Rhesus; sometimes thought a later interpolation.
Funeral Games for Patroclus — Iliad Book 23
Chariot race, boxing, wrestling, foot race; Achilles organizes them; the race features a famous dispute involving Antilochus and Menelaus.
The Teichoscopia — Iliad Book 3
"Viewing from the wall" — Helen identifies Greek heroes for King Priam from the walls of Troy; introduces characters via ekphrasis.
Aristarchus of Samothrace
Alexandrian scholar (~200 BCE) who produced the definitive edition of Homer; coined the 24-book division with Greek letters; his "athetized" lines are still debated.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Epithets (Homeric formulas)

  • Wine-dark sea (oinops pontos)
  • Rosy-fingered Dawn (rhododaktylos Eos)
  • Swift-footed Achilles (podas okys Achilleus)
  • Grey-eyed Athena / "bright-eyed Athena"
  • Ox-eyed Hera
  • Cloud-gathering Zeus
  • Many-wiled / wily Odysseus (polytropos)
  • Man-slaughtering Hector

Named characters (Iliad)

  • Patroclus — Achilles' companion/lover (in later tradition); killed by Hector
  • Thetis — sea-nymph, Achilles' mother; petitions Zeus
  • Priam — king of Troy; father of 50 sons incl. Hector & Paris
  • Hecuba — queen of Troy; wife of Priam
  • Andromache — Hector's wife; pleads with him not to fight
  • Chryses — Apollo's priest; Agamemnon refuses to return his daughter Chryseis
  • Calchas — Greek prophet; tells Agamemnon to give Chryseis back
  • Thersites — ugly, common soldier; insults Agamemnon; beaten by Odysseus

Named characters (Odyssey)

  • Penelope — weaves/unravels; faithful wife
  • Telemachus — Odysseus's son; his coming-of-age = Telemachy
  • Antinous — leading suitor; first killed by Odysseus
  • Eurymachus — another major suitor
  • Eumaeus — loyal swineherd; Odysseus confides in him first
  • Laertes — Odysseus's aged father
  • Melanthius — treacherous goatherd
  • Alcinous / Arete — Phaeacian king and queen

Style markers / literary terms

  • Dactylic hexameter — the meter (— ᴗ ᴗ or — —, 6 feet per line)
  • Homeric / epic simile — extended comparison, often natural imagery
  • Ekphrasis — verbal description of a visual work (Shield of Achilles)
  • In medias res — begins mid-action
  • Oral-formulaic theory — Milman Parry / Albert Lord; formulaic phrases fit the meter
  • Invocation of the Muse — standard epic opening
  • Nekyia — descent/consultation of the dead (Od. 11)
  • Xenia — guest-friendship (sacred host-guest bond); its violation causes the Trojan War

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Virgil's AeneidRoman epic (~19 BCE); follows Aeneas from Troy to found Rome; consciously imitates both Homer but is written (not oral), in Latin, with a teleological/Roman destiny theme. Aeneas = Trojan refugee; Achilles = Greek warrior. Underworld descent in Book 6 (Aeneas, guided by Sibyl) ≠ Odysseus in Od. 11.
HesiodContemporary Greek poet; wrote Theogony (genealogy of gods) and Works and Days (farmer's almanac / five ages of man). Not epics about heroes — cosmogony and didactic verse. Often paired with Homer in quizbowl "Greek poets" questions.
Ovid's MetamorphosesRoman, Latin, not Greek; mini-narratives of transformation (15 books); not in dactylic hexameter alone (elegiac couplets in other works). Ovid covers many of the same myths but is clearly later, literary, witty.
Sophocles / Aeschylus / EuripidesThese are tragedians (drama, not epic); they draw on Homeric material (Agamemnon, Ajax, Electra) but their medium is stage performance. Ajax in Sophocles kills himself; in the Iliad he merely fights on.
Achilles vs. Ajax (tossup traps)Achilles: heel, wrath, Patroclus, Thetis, dies after the Iliad (not in it). Ajax: huge shield (like a tower), contests armor of dead Achilles, later goes mad and kills himself (Sophocles). They are not the same character.
Iliad vs. Odyssey endingsIliad ends with Hector's funeral — Troy has NOT fallen yet. Troy's fall (wooden horse, etc.) is in the Iliupersis (lost) and later accounts (Virgil). The Odyssey ends with Odysseus's reunion and a brief truce among Ithacans.

Cross-discipline "big picture" Homer anchors an enormous tossup web: classical mythology (Achilles → Thetis → sea nymphs; Odysseus → Circe → Hermes), Greek history (Trojan War debates, Mycenaean archaeology under Heinrich Schliemann at Hisarlik), later literature (Virgil's Aeneid, Dante's Inferno where Ulysses speaks in Canto 26, Keats's "On First Looking into Chapman's Homer," Joyce's Ulysses, Tennyson's "Ulysses"), and oral tradition studies (Milman Parry, Albert Lord, The Singer of Tales). Any of these can cross-reference a Homer tossup.
