Quick Overview

Chandos portrait (c. 1600–1610)
NationalityEnglish (born Stratford-upon-Avon, 1564–1616)
PeriodElizabethan / Jacobean Renaissance (active c. 1590–1613)
Form masteryTragedy, comedy, history, romance + 154 sonnets + narrative poems
TheatreCo-owner/playwright of the Globe Theatre, Bankside; also Blackfriars
CompanyLord Chamberlain's Men (later King's Men under James I)
First FolioPublished 1623 by Heminges & Condell; 36 plays collected

Defining traits

  • Primary metre: iambic pentameter (unrhymed = blank verse; rhymed = heroic couplets at scene ends).
  • Mixes prose and verse: lower-status characters often speak prose; verse marks elevation or formality.
  • Soliloquy as interior monologue — Hamlet, Macbeth, Iago, Richard III all use it for dramatic irony.
  • Recurring tropes: disguise/cross-dressing (comedies), play-within-a-play, tragic flaw (hamartia), redemptive "romances" ending in reunion.
  • Major sources: Holinshed's Chronicles (histories), Plutarch's Lives (Roman plays), Ovid's Metamorphoses, Italian novellas.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "To be, or not to be, that is the question" — Hamlet III.i.
  • "All the world's a stage" — Jaques in As You Like It.
  • Globe Theatre, Stratford-upon-Avon, Anne Hathaway (wife).
  • "The Bard of Avon" — standard epithet in quizbowl lead-ins.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 8 works most likely to be a tossup answer or to anchor a clue pyramid. power = hard early clue; recog = mid-question recognition clue.

Hamlet
c. 1600–01 · tragedy · source: Saxo Grammaticus / Belleforest
power"The Murder of Gonzago" (play-within-a-play) staged by Hamlet to catch Claudius; Hamlet also feigns madness and kills Polonius behind an arras.
recogPrince of Denmark, son of the Ghost of King Hamlet, nephew of Claudius; loves Ophelia (drowns); friends Horatio, Rosencrantz & Guildenstern.
recog"To be, or not to be" soliloquy (III.i); "The lady doth protest too much"; ends in poison — Gertrude drinks from the poisoned cup, Laertes and Hamlet die by the poisoned blade.

Macbeth
c. 1606 · tragedy · source: Holinshed's Chronicles
powerThe witches' prophecy "none of woman born shall harm Macbeth" — undone by Macduff, who was "untimely ripped" (caesarean); Birnam Wood moves to Dunsinane when soldiers carry branches.
recogThree Weird Sisters (witches) hail Macbeth as future king; he and Lady Macbeth murder King Duncan; Lady Macbeth's sleepwalking scene ("Out, damned spot!").
recogBanquo's ghost at the feast; Hecate governs the witches; famous "Tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow" speech by Macbeth; setting: Scotland.

Othello
c. 1603–04 · tragedy · source: Cinthio's Hecatommithi
powerIago engineers jealousy via a handkerchief (Othello's first gift to Desdemona, planted in Cassio's lodging); Iago's wife Emilia reveals the plot too late.
recogMoor of Venice, general; marries Desdemona (elopes); Iago (ensign, "I am not what I am") manipulates Roderigo; setting: Venice then Cyprus.
recogOthello smothers Desdemona, then kills himself; Brabantio's racist objections to the marriage; "the green-eyed monster" = jealousy (Iago's phrase).

King Lear
c. 1605–06 · tragedy · source: Holinshed; Historia Regum Britanniae
powerParallel subplot: Edmund (bastard) deceives Gloucester against Edgar; Gloucester is blinded by Cornwall ("Out, vile jelly!") — the play's most brutal scene.
recogKing divides kingdom among daughters Goneril, Regan, and Cordelia; only Cordelia honest; Lear goes mad on the heath; the Fool accompanies him.
recogCordelia is hanged; Lear dies of grief over her body; Edmund dies fighting Edgar; Goneril poisons Regan, then kills herself.

A Midsummer Night's Dream
c. 1595–96 · comedy
powerTitania (Fairy Queen) falls in love with Bottom (weaver with donkey's head) after Oberon applies love-in-idleness flower juice — the love potion from "a little western flower."
recogFairy king Oberon, trickster sprite Puck (Robin Goodfellow); four Athenian lovers (Hermia, Lysander, Helena, Demetrius) flee to the enchanted forest.
recogMechanicals' play-within-a-play: "Pyramus and Thisbe"; Bottom's transformation; closing couplet by Puck: "If we shadows have offended…"
wiki ↗
The Tempest
c. 1610–11 · romance (last solo play)
powerProspero's island was stolen from the witch Sycorax; spirit Ariel was imprisoned in a cloven pine by Sycorax before Prospero freed him; Caliban is Sycorax's son.
recogProspero (rightful Duke of Milan), exiled to island with daughter Miranda; raises a storm to shipwreck usurper brother Antonio and King Alonso of Naples.
recog"We are such stuff as dreams are made on"; Prospero breaks his staff and drowns his book (renunciation of magic); often read as Shakespeare's farewell to the stage.
wiki ↗
Romeo and Juliet
c. 1594–96 · tragedy · source: Arthur Brooke's Romeus and Juliet
powerThe friar's letter to Romeo fails to arrive because of a plague quarantine; Friar Lawrence gives Juliet the sleeping potion; Rosaline is Romeo's first infatuation (never appears speaking).
recogRival Veronese families Montague (Romeo) and Capulet (Juliet); Mercutio killed by Tybalt; Romeo kills Tybalt, is banished to Mantua.
recog"A rose by any other name would smell as sweet" (balcony scene); both die in the Capulet tomb; Prince Escalus ends the feud.
wiki ↗
Sonnets (154 poems)
written c. 1592–1598; published 1609
powerPublished by Thomas Thorpe; dedicated to "Mr. W. H." (identity debated — candidates: Henry Wriothesley Earl of Southampton; William Herbert Earl of Pembroke).
recogSonnets 1–126: addressed to the Fair Youth; 127–154: to the Dark Lady; Sonnet 18: "Shall I compare thee to a summer's day?"; Sonnet 130: "My mistress' eyes are nothing like the sun."
recogForm: three quatrains + closing couplet, rhyme scheme ABAB CDCD EFEF GG (Shakespearean sonnet); iambic pentameter throughout.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Recurring characters & figures

  • Falstaff — fat knight; in Henry IV pts 1 & 2, Merry Wives; dies offstage in Henry V
  • Puck / Robin Goodfellow — trickster fairy in Midsummer Night's Dream
  • Iago — archetype villain; "motiveless malignity" (Coleridge's phrase)
  • The Fool — truth-telling jester in King Lear; disappears after III.vi
  • Prospero — magician duke; associated with Shakespeare himself
  • Shylock — Jewish moneylender; debated sympathetic/villainous reading
  • Bottom — weaver; transformed; speaks "Bottom's Dream" prose

Style markers & technique

  • Iambic pentameter — 10-syllable line, da-DUM pattern
  • Blank verse — unrhymed iambic pentameter (noble characters)
  • Soliloquy — character alone on stage; key for characterization
  • Aside — character speaks to audience unheard by others
  • Tragic flaw / hamartia — Othello = jealousy; Lear = pride; Macbeth = ambition
  • Comic subplot — mirrors/contrasts main plot (gravediggers in Hamlet)
  • Malapropism — Dogberry, Mistress Quickly get words wrong for comic effect

Famous quotes / clue phrases

  • "To be, or not to be" — Hamlet III.i
  • "All the world's a stage" — Jaques, As You Like It
  • "Out, out, brief candle" — Macbeth V.v
  • "Et tu, Bruté?" — Julius Caesar III.i
  • "The quality of mercy is not strained" — Portia, Merchant of Venice
  • "Cry 'Havoc!' and let slip the dogs of war" — Antony, Julius Caesar
  • "What's in a name?" — Juliet, Romeo and Juliet
  • "We know what we are, but know not what we may be" — Ophelia, Hamlet

Biographical / context clues

  • Born April 23, 1564, Stratford-upon-Avon; died same date 1616
  • Wife: Anne Hathaway; children: Susanna, twins Hamnet & Judith (Hamnet died 1596, age 11)
  • Shareholder in the Globe Theatre (built 1599; burned 1613 during Henry VIII)
  • Patron: Henry Wriothesley, Earl of Southampton (poems dedicated to him)
  • First Folio (1623) collected 36 plays; Pericles and others excluded
  • Lost plays: Cardenio (with Fletcher), Love's Labour's Won
  • Authorship debate: Baconian, Oxfordian theories — quizbowl uses canonical attribution

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Christopher MarloweShakespeare's great contemporary rival. Marlowe wrote Doctor Faustus, Tamburlaine, Edward II — his tragic heroes are overreachers driven by one grand desire. Marlowe died 1593; no comedies, no histories in his canon. Quizbowl clue: "mighty line" = Marlowe's blank verse style.
Ben JonsonFellow Elizabethan/Jacobean playwright — wrote Volpone, The Alchemist, Bartholomew Fair. Jonson's satirical "comedy of humours" (one dominant personality trait per character) differs from Shakespeare's psychologically complex figures. Jonson penned the commendatory poem in the First Folio.
John MiltonMilton is 17th-c. Puritan, not Elizabethan. Paradise Lost (epic, 1667) is blank verse but epic, not drama. Questions about "blank verse epic" = Milton; "blank verse drama" = Shakespeare.
Francis Beaumont & John FletcherJacobean collaborators who co-wrote tragi-comedies (e.g., The Maid's Tragedy, Philaster). Shakespeare collaborated with Fletcher on Henry VIII and likely Cardenio. When clue says "collaborated" with Fletcher, the play may be Shakespeare's late work.
Thomas KydAuthor of The Spanish Tragedy — the prototype revenge tragedy that precedes and influences Hamlet. Kyd, not Shakespeare, invented the Elizabethan revenge genre. Clue: "ur-Hamlet" refers to a lost pre-Shakespearean Hamlet text often attributed to Kyd.
Petrarchan vs. Shakespearean sonnetPetrarchan (Italian): octave (ABBAABBA) + sestet (variable) — turn after 8 lines. Shakespearean: three quatrains + couplet (ABAB CDCD EFEF GG) — turn at the couplet. Both use iambic pentameter.

Cross-discipline "big picture" Shakespeare anchors a dense quizbowl answer web: his plays draw from Plutarch (Roman plays — link to classical history), Ovid (mythology), and Holinshed (English history). His sonnets connect to the Petrarchan tradition (Wyatt, Surrey), while his influence radiates forward through Romantic poets (Keats's "negative capability," Coleridge on Iago), opera (Verdi's Otello, Falstaff, Macbeth; Gounod's Roméo et Juliette), psychology (Freud on the Oedipus reading of Hamlet), and postcolonial theory (Caliban-as-colonized in The Tempest). Almost any category can turn up a Shakespeare clue.
