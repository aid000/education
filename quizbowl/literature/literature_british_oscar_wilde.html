Quick Overview

Born / Died1854, Dublin — 1900, Paris
NationalityIrish (wrote in English; associated with London)
MovementAestheticism ("Art for Art's Sake"); Decadence
EducationTrinity College Dublin; Magdalen College, Oxford
Trial / FallConvicted of "gross indecency" 1895; 2 years hard labor in Reading Gaol
DeathDied penniless in Paris; buried Père Lachaise cemetery

Defining traits

  • Epigrams and paradox — Wilde's prose and speech constantly invert conventional morality ("The truth is rarely pure and never simple").
  • Aestheticism — beauty as the highest value; art need not serve a moral purpose; influenced by Walter Pater at Oxford.
  • Dandyism — the dandy as a cultural figure; flamboyant persona, sunflower/lily aesthetic.
  • Gothic doubling — the portrait / double self (Dorian Gray); the hidden, corrupted self beneath a beautiful surface.
  • Society comedies — drawing-room wit, mistaken identities, hypocritical Victorian propriety exposed through farce.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "The Importance of Being Earnest" and its punning title (earnest/Ernest).
  • "The Picture of Dorian Gray" — the portrait ages while the subject remains young.
  • The phrase "Art for Art's Sake" or mention of the Aesthetic Movement.
  • Conviction for gross indecency after suit against the Marquess of Queensberry.
  • The Ballad of Reading Gaol — written after prison.

The works most likely to be the answer or to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early, buzz-here clue. recog = recognition / middle-clue.

The Importance of Being Earnest
1895 · play (comedy of manners) · first performed St. James's Theatre, London
powerThe subtitle is "A Trivial Comedy for Serious People." Jack Worthing was found as a baby in a handbag at Victoria Station by Thomas Cardew.
recogTwo men — Jack Worthing (country) and Algernon Moncrieff (London) — both use the alias "Ernest" to escape social obligations ("Bunburying").
recogLady Bracknell interrogates Jack and delivers the line: "A handbag?!" — the most-quoted moment in the play.
recogHeroines: Gwendolen Fairfax (Lady Bracknell's daughter) and Cecily Cardew; both are in love with a man named Ernest. Resolution: Jack really is named Ernest — he discovers this in the Army Lists.
recogMiss Prism is the governess who left the baby Jack in the handbag; the Rector is Canon Chasuble.
"The truth is rarely pure and never simple."— Algernon, Act I

The Picture of Dorian Gray
1890 (magazine) / 1891 (novel) · Gothic novel
powerThe novel opens with painter Basil Hallward telling Lord Henry Wotton about his obsession with Dorian; the Preface declares "All art is quite useless" — a core Aestheticist manifesto statement.
recogDorian sells his soul (via a wish over the portrait) so that the painting ages and bears his sins while he remains young and beautiful.
recogDorian's corruption begins when Lord Henry gives him a yellow book (widely identified as Huysmans's À rebours). He falls in love with actress Sibyl Vane; when she stops acting well (out of real love) he abandons her and she kills herself.
recogDorian murders Basil when Basil confronts him about the portrait; he uses blackmail on chemist Alan Campbell to dispose of the body. Sibyl's brother James Vane hunts Dorian for revenge.
recogEnding: Dorian stabs the portrait; the servants find a hideous old man dead beside a beautiful portrait of Dorian as a young man.
"The only way to get rid of a temptation is to yield to it."— Lord Henry Wotton

The Ballad of Reading Gaol
1898 · poem (narrative ballad) · published under "C.3.3." (Wilde's prison cell number)
powerPublished pseudonymously as "C.3.3." — Wilde's cell designation (block C, landing 3, cell 3) — Wilde refused to put his name on it initially.
recogInspired by the execution of Charles Thomas Wooldridge, a Royal Horse Guards trooper who murdered his wife; Wilde witnessed his fate in Reading Gaol.
recogRefrain: "Each man kills the thing he loves" — the most-quoted line from the poem.
recogWritten after Wilde's release; combines ballad meter with prison imagery; themes of justice, shame, and compassion for the condemned.
"Yet each man kills the thing he loves / By each let this be heard."— The Ballad of Reading Gaol

An Ideal Husband
1895 · play (society comedy)
powerSir Robert Chiltern's past secret: he sold a Cabinet secret (the Suez Canal purchase) to Baron Arnheim for £110,000 — his entire fortune is founded on corruption.
recogThe villain is Mrs. Cheveley, who blackmails Chiltern with a letter; foiled partly by Lord Goring (the play's witty dandy figure).
recogTheme: the gap between public reputation and private morality; Lady Chiltern must accept her husband is not perfectly ideal.

Lady Windermere's Fan
1892 · play (well-made play / society comedy) · first of the "society comedies"
powerThe fan is a birthday gift from Lord Windermere; Mrs. Erlynne is secretly Lady Windermere's long-lost mother — this is withheld from Lady Windermere throughout the play.
recogLord Darlington tempts Lady Windermere to elope; Mrs. Erlynne sacrifices her own reputation by being found in Darlington's rooms, saving her daughter.
recogFamous epigram: "We are all in the gutter, but some of us are looking at the stars" (Lord Darlington, Act III).
"We are all in the gutter, but some of us are looking at the stars."— Lord Darlington, Lady Windermere's Fan, Act III

Salomé
1891 (written in French) · 1893 (published) · 1896 (first performed, Paris) · symbolist one-act play
powerWritten in French for Sarah Bernhardt (though she never performed it); banned in England until 1931 under the Lord Chamberlain's prohibition on depicting biblical characters on stage.
recogSalome dances the "Dance of the Seven Veils" for Herod in exchange for the head of Jokanaan (John the Baptist).
recogThe illustrations by Aubrey Beardsley for the English translation are nearly as famous as the text; basis for Richard Strauss's opera (1905).

De Profundis
1897 (written in prison) · 1905 (partial publication) · 1962 (full publication) · prose letter / confessional essay
powerWritten as a long letter to Lord Alfred Douglas ("Bosie") in Reading Gaol; originally titled "Epistola: In Carcere et Vinculis" (Latin: "Letter: In Prison and in Chains").
recogRecounts the spiritual and emotional journey through suffering; Wilde reframes Christ as the supreme artist and individualist.
recogTitle (Latin: "From the depths") alludes to Psalm 130. Full text suppressed by Douglas until after his death.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Recurring characters

  • Lord Henry Wotton — Dorian Gray's corrupting mentor; Wildean epigram machine
  • Basil Hallward — artist who paints Dorian's portrait; murdered by Dorian
  • Lady Bracknell — imperious aristocrat; "A handbag?!"
  • Algernon Moncrieff ("Algy") — invents "Bunburying"
  • Jack Worthing / Ernest — found in a handbag at Victoria Station
  • Miss Prism — governess who lost baby Jack; left him in a handbag
  • Lord Goring — dandy hero of An Ideal Husband
  • Jokanaan — John the Baptist in Salomé

Key motifs / style markers

  • Epigrams — witty paradoxical one-liners inverting moral convention
  • The double / doppelgänger — hidden corrupted self (Dorian/portrait)
  • Art for Art's Sake / Aestheticism — beauty over utility
  • Dandyism — elegance, idleness, wit as lifestyle
  • Bunburying — maintaining a fictional person to escape obligations
  • The fallen woman — redeemed or sacrificed maternal figure
  • Gothic / grotesque — aging portrait, corrupted soul
  • The yellow book — symbol of Decadent influence / corruption

Famous lines (quizbowl clues)

  • "A handbag?!" — Lady Bracknell
  • "The truth is rarely pure and never simple." — Algernon
  • "We are all in the gutter, but some of us are looking at the stars." — Lord Darlington
  • "Each man kills the thing he loves." — Ballad of Reading Gaol
  • "All art is quite useless." — Preface, Dorian Gray
  • "I can resist everything except temptation." — Lord Darlington
  • "To lose one parent may be regarded as a misfortune; to lose both looks like carelessness." — Lady Bracknell

Biographical clues

  • Born Dublin, 1854; full name Oscar Fingal O'Flahertie Wills Wilde
  • Mother: Lady Jane Wilde ("Speranza") — Irish nationalist poet
  • Married Constance Lloyd 1884; two sons (Cyril, Vyvyan)
  • Relationship with Lord Alfred Douglas ("Bosie"); Marquess of Queensberry left calling card "posing sodomite"
  • Wilde sued Queensberry for libel — lost; prosecuted and convicted 1895
  • Served 2 years hard labor at Reading Gaol (cell C.3.3)
  • Exiled to France; died Paris, 1900 — cerebral meningitis (possibly syphilis)
  • Influenced by: Walter Pater (Aestheticism), John Ruskin (Oxford), French Symbolists
  • Won the Newdigate Prize for poetry at Oxford (1878, Ravenna)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
George Bernard ShawShaw's plays are explicitly political/ideological (socialism, feminism, Fabianism); his wit is argumentative, not purely decorative. Shaw did not do Gothic doubles or Aestheticism. Key works: Pygmalion, Major Barbara, Saint Joan.
W. S. Gilbert (Gilbert & Sullivan)Also Victorian satirical wit and comedy of manners, but in operetta form; mockery is topical/political (H.M.S. Pinafore, The Mikado). No Gothic novel, no Aestheticist manifesto, no prison ballad.
Algernon SwinburneFellow Decadent / Aesthete, but primarily a poet — lush, pagan, rhythmically complex verse (Poems and Ballads); no society comedies, no novel. Both associated with "Art for Art's Sake."
Walter PaterPater is Wilde's Oxford mentor / influence, not Wilde himself. Pater wrote Studies in the History of the Renaissance; coined "art for art's sake." If the clue says "influenced Wilde," answer is Pater.
Henry JamesJames also writes about society, hypocrisy, and Americans in Europe, but his style is dense, psychological, late realist — no epigrams, no farce, no aesthetic manifesto. Clues: "the governess," "turn of the screw," "Isabel Archer."
Aubrey BeardsleyBeardsley illustrated Salomé — he is a visual artist (decadent black-and-white drawings), not the author. If the answer is about the illustrator of Salomé, it is Beardsley, not Wilde.
Robert Louis StevensonAlso uses Gothic doubling (Dr. Jekyll and Mr. Hyde, 1886) — a common trap with Dorian Gray. Tell apart: Jekyll = scientific/chemical; Dorian = portrait/aesthetic/vanity. Both are late-Victorian Gothic doubles.

Cross-discipline big picture Wilde sits at the intersection of several quizbowl categories: Victorian aesthetics (links to Pater, Ruskin, the Pre-Raphaelites), LGBT history (the 1895 trial is a landmark; links to Section 11 of the Criminal Law Amendment Act 1885), mythology (Salomé from Mark 6 / Matthew 14; the Sphinx; Narcissus), European literature (French Symbolists, Huysmans's À rebours as the "yellow book"), and opera (Richard Strauss's Salome, 1905). His Gothic double in Dorian Gray is directly comparable to Stevenson's Jekyll and Hyde (1886) — both signal late-Victorian anxieties about hidden identity.
