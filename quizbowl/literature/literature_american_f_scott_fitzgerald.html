Quick Overview

Born / Died1896 (St. Paul, MN) — 1940 (Hollywood, CA)
Era / MovementModernism; chronicler of the Jazz Age / Lost Generation
Signature themesThe American Dream, wealth/class, nostalgia, moral decay
Key relationshipWife Zelda Sayre Fitzgerald — muse, collaborator, rival
Reputation arcCelebrated in 1920s, forgotten by death; Gatsby canonized by WWII paperback editions
EducationPrinceton (left without degree, 1917); also see This Side of Paradise's thinly veiled Princeton

Defining style markers

  • Lyrical, image-dense prose — long, subordinate-heavy sentences; romantic cadences.
  • First-person observer narrators who stand slightly outside the action (Nick Carraway is the model).
  • Outsider-gazing-at-the-rich structural conceit — protagonists desire entry into a glittering world that is revealed as hollow.
  • Recurring motif: the green light / unreachable past — striving toward an idealized vision that recedes.
  • Short stories as financial engine — published hundreds in The Saturday Evening Post and Scribner's Magazine.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "the green light at the end of Daisy's dock" — instant Fitzgerald / Gatsby buzz.
  • "So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past." — famous final line.
  • "The Valley of Ashes" and the "eyes of Doctor T. J. Eckleburg" billboard — Gatsby middle-tier clues.
  • "Dick Diver" or "Nicole Warren" — immediate Tender Is the Night signal.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The works most likely to be the tossup answer. power = hard early clue; recog = recognition / mid-clue.

The Great Gatsby
1925 · novel · Scribner's · Fitzgerald's masterpiece
powerNarrator Nick Carraway, cousin to Daisy Buchanan, rents a house next to the mansion of mysterious millionaire Jay Gatsby in West Egg, Long Island, 1922. Gatsby's mentor was the gambler Dan Cody; his associate is the fixer Meyer Wolfsheim (based on Arnold Rothstein).
recogGreen light at the end of Daisy's dock across the bay; parties at Gatsby's West Egg mansion every Saturday night.
recogDaisy's husband Tom Buchanan is having an affair with Myrtle Wilson; Daisy kills Myrtle with Gatsby's car; Myrtle's husband George Wilson kills Gatsby and himself.
recogThe Valley of Ashes — industrial wasteland between West Egg and NYC; the billboard of Dr. T. J. Eckleburg (giant eyes, faded spectacles) watches over it as a symbol of moral blindness.
"So we beat on, boats against the current, borne back ceaselessly into the past."— final line
"Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!"— Gatsby to Nick
recogOriginal cover art by Francis Cugat — disembodied eyes over lights of Coney Island; painted before the novel was finished.

Tender Is the Night
1934 · novel · Scribner's · Fitzgerald's most autobiographical major novel
powerPsychiatrist Dick Diver marries his wealthy patient Nicole Warren (who suffered a breakdown after incest with her father). Set on the French Riviera and in Switzerland/Paris. Dick's foil is Tommy Barban, who eventually takes Nicole.
recogYoung actress Rosemary Hoyt falls for Dick at the beach in Gausse's Hôtel des Étrangers (modeled on the Hôtel du Cap d'Antibes).
recogDick's decline into alcoholism mirrors Fitzgerald's own; Nicole's mental illness mirrors Zelda's.
recogTitle from Keats's Ode to a Nightingale: "Already with thee! tender is the night…"
wiki ↗
This Side of Paradise
1920 · novel · Scribner's · Fitzgerald's debut; instant bestseller at age 23
powerProtagonist Amory Blaine — a Midwestern youth whose ambitions are formed at St. Regis prep school and Princeton, then shattered by WWI and failed romances.
recogLove interests include Isabelle Borge, Clara Page (a widow he idealizes), and Rosalind Connage, who rejects him for a wealthier man — an early template for Daisy/Gatsby.
recogEnds with Amory crying "I know myself… but that is all." — theme of self-knowledge amid disillusionment.
recogTitle from Rupert Brooke's poem "Tiare Tahiti": "…well this side of Paradise."
wiki ↗
The Beautiful and Damned
1922 · novel · Scribner's
powerProtagonist Anthony Patch expects to inherit his grandfather Adam Patch's millions; marries the beautiful Gloria Gilbert. Both descend into alcoholism and decadence while waiting for the inheritance.
recogAdam Patch disinherits Anthony after witnessing a drunken party; Anthony eventually wins the lawsuit but is mentally destroyed.
recogModeled heavily on Fitzgerald and Zelda; Gloria is Zelda; Anthony is Scott. Theme: wealth as corrosive rather than redemptive.
wiki ↗
The Last Tycoon (The Love of the Last Tycoon)
1941 · novel (unfinished) · published posthumously, ed. Edmund Wilson
powerBased on Hollywood producer Irving Thalberg; protagonist Monroe Stahr is a brilliant, dying film mogul. Narrator is Cecelia Brady, daughter of Stahr's rival Pat Brady.
recogFitzgerald died of a heart attack in December 1940 before completing it; Edmund Wilson edited and published the fragments.
recogStahr falls for Kathleen Moore, who resembles his dead wife Minna Davis.

Key Short Fiction & Essays
1920–1940 · magazines, then collections
power"The Crack-Up" (1936, Esquire) — autobiographical essays about Fitzgerald's mental breakdown; candid; influenced confessional writing. Also published posthumously as a book (ed. Wilson, 1945).
recog"The Curious Case of Benjamin Button" (1922) — man born old, grows younger; collected in Tales of the Jazz Age.
recog"Bernice Bobs Her Hair" (1920) — shy Bernice learns social manipulation; her hair bobbing as revenge plot.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Minor works and biographical facts — appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line each.

Flappers and Philosophers 1920 First story collection; includes "Bernice Bobs Her Hair" and "The Ice Palace."
Tales of the Jazz Age 1922 Second collection; includes "Benjamin Button" and "May Day."
All the Sad Young Men 1926 Third collection; contains "Winter Dreams" and "Absolution" (Gatsby prequel fragment).
"Absolution" 1924 Short story originally drafted as Gatsby's backstory; priest and young Rudolph Miller.
"The Ice Palace" 1920 Southern girl Sally Carrol Harper trapped inside an ice palace in a Northern city.
"May Day" 1920 Panoramic NYC novella; May 1919 socialist riots, parallel plotlines.
"The Rich Boy" 1926 Anson Hunter, a rich Yalie incapable of love; opens "Let me tell you about the very rich…"
"The Offshore Pirate" 1920 Ardita Farnam captured by a jazz-playing pirate who is revealed as her suitor.
Taps at Reveille 1935 Last story collection published in his lifetime; includes "Babylon Revisited."
The Vegetable 1923 Failed satirical play; Jerry Frost dreams he becomes President; flopped in tryout.
"One Trip Abroad" 1930 American couple Nelson and Nicole Kelly deteriorate morally while traveling Europe.
"Financing Finnegan" 1938 Comic meta-story about a charming writer who perpetually borrows from his editor and agent.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Recurring characters

  • Nick Carraway — observer narrator, Yale man, bond trader; cousin to Daisy
  • Jay Gatsby (born James Gatz) — nouveau riche Long Island host; idealizes Daisy
  • Daisy Buchanan (née Fay) — golden girl; voice "full of money"
  • Tom Buchanan — brutal old-money Yale athlete
  • Jordan Baker — golfer, Nick's love interest; "incurably dishonest"
  • Dick Diver — psychiatrist protagonist of Tender Is the Night
  • Monroe Stahr — Hollywood tycoon in The Last Tycoon
  • Amory Blaine — self-conscious Princeton hero of debut novel

Motifs & symbols

  • Green light — unreachable ideal; Daisy's dock; American Dream
  • Valley of Ashes — industrial wasteland; moral corruption
  • Eyes of Dr. T. J. Eckleburg — billboard; faded God; moral blindness
  • East Egg vs. West Egg — old money vs. new money
  • The clock Gatsby nearly knocks off the mantel — attempting to stop/reverse time
  • White dresses worn by Daisy and Jordan — false purity
  • Gold/yellow vs. white — corrupt wealth vs. false innocence

Famous lines / quotes

  • "So we beat on, boats against the current…" — Gatsby closing
  • "Can't repeat the past? Why of course you can!" — Gatsby
  • "Her voice is full of money." — Nick about Daisy
  • "Let me tell you about the very rich. They are different from you and me." — "The Rich Boy" (Hemingway's rejoinder: "Yes, they have more money.")
  • "In the real dark night of the soul it is always three o'clock in the morning." — "The Crack-Up"
  • "The test of a first-rate intelligence is the ability to hold two opposed ideas in mind at the same time…" — "The Crack-Up"

Biographical hard clues

  • Born St. Paul, Minnesota, 1896; full name Francis Scott Key Fitzgerald (named after the anthem's author)
  • Married Zelda Sayre (1920); she was institutionalized (schizophrenia) 1930–1940
  • Editor at Scribner's: Maxwell Perkins — also edited Hemingway and Wolfe
  • Close friend and rival: Ernest Hemingway (ambivalent relationship documented in Hemingway's A Moveable Feast)
  • Died December 21, 1940, Hollywood; heart attack; working on The Last Tycoon
  • Buried initially in Rockville, MD; later reinterred at St. Mary's Church Cemetery, Rockville, with Zelda (1948 plane crash)
  • Zelda's Save Me the Waltz (1932) used many of the same autobiographical events as Tender Is the Night — source of marital tension

Style markers

  • Lyrical, subordinate-clause-heavy sentences; romantic imagery
  • First-person peripheral narrator (Nick, Cecelia) who observes a central figure
  • Nostalgia and lost time — the past as irrecoverable ideal
  • Critique of the American Dream as materialist illusion
  • Jazz Age / Roaring Twenties setting; parties, bootleg liquor, new wealth
  • Heavy use of color symbolism (gold, green, white, grey)

Editors, publishers, connections

  • Maxwell Perkins — Scribner's editor, lifelong champion
  • H. L. Mencken — early champion of Fitzgerald's work
  • Edmund Wilson ("Bunny") — Princeton friend; edited The Last Tycoon and The Crack-Up posthumously
  • Ring Lardner — friend; Fitzgerald helped advance his career
  • Expatriate Paris scene: Gertrude Stein's salon; Sylvia Beach's Shakespeare and Company

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Ernest HemingwayHemingway = iceberg theory, spare declarative prose, masculinity, war/hunting. Fitzgerald = lush, lyrical, parties, wealth obsession. Both Jazz Age/Lost Generation expatriates — read for sentence style and subject. Hemingway's famous putdown: "Yes, they have more money." (re: Fitzgerald's "very rich" line).
Sinclair LewisLewis = satirical social realism, Midwestern conformity (Main Street, Babbitt, Elmer Gantry). First American Nobel laureate (1930). No lyrical dream-chasing — Lewis is blunter, satirical, sociological.
John Dos PassosU.S.A. Trilogy — modernist collage (newsreels, biographies, stream of consciousness). Radical politics, working-class focus. No Gatsby-style romantic idealism.
Theodore DreiserNaturalism, not modernism. Sister Carrie, An American Tragedy — deterministic, dense, unglamorous. Clyde Griffiths (not Gatsby) is the parallel to young men seduced by wealth.
Edith WhartonOld New York aristocracy, Gilded Age; The Age of Innocence, The House of Mirth. Both critique American wealth/society, but Wharton = 1880s–1910s, female protagonists, Old Money insider. Fitzgerald = 1920s, new money, male narrators.
Zelda FitzgeraldZelda wrote Save Me the Waltz (1932) — same Riviera setting, same events as Tender Is the Night but from the wife's perspective. If a question mentions Alabama Beggs (protagonist's maiden name/home state), that's Zelda's novel.

Big-Picture Cross-Discipline Links
  • American Dream mythology — Fitzgerald interrogates the same optimistic founding myth as Walden (Thoreau), Leaves of Grass (Whitman), and Death of a Salesman (Miller). "Boats against the current" is a clean Fitzgerald-to-American-Studies bridge.
  • Jazz Age / 1920s history — Prohibition (bootlegging is Gatsby's source of wealth), the 1929 crash (hits Fitzgerald's career and subject matter hard), the Harlem Renaissance (overlapping cultural moment).
  • Literary modernism — alongside Hemingway, Dos Passos, and Faulkner in the Lost Generation. Key contrast: Fitzgerald's romanticism vs. Hemingway's minimalism.
  • Zelda Fitzgerald is her own quizbowl answer: Save Me the Waltz, pioneer of 1920s "flapper" journalism, dancer who studied with Egorova in Paris.
