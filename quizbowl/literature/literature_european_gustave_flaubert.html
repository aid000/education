Quick Overview

Gustave Flaubert (1821–1880)
NationalityFrench; born Rouen, Normandy, 1821–1880
PeriodRealism / late Romanticism; mid-to-late 19th century
MovementLiterary Realism; pioneer of the modern novel
Signature stylele mot juste — obsessive search for the exact right word
Prose techniqueFree indirect discourse (style indirect libre) — merging narrator + character voice
Notable trialProsecuted 1857 for Madame Bovary's alleged immorality; acquitted

Defining traits

  • Obsessive revision — rewrote endlessly; spent 5 years on Madame Bovary; "I am a man of the pen."
  • Impersonal narration — "The author in his work must be like God in the universe, present everywhere and visible nowhere."
  • Le mot juste — every word weighed; rhythm and sound as important as meaning.
  • Free indirect style — narrator slides invisibly into character's thoughts without quotation marks; revolutionary technique.
  • Irony and bêtise — deep contempt for bourgeois stupidity (bêtise); characters delude themselves through received ideas.
  • Oriental / exotic subjects alongside provincial Normandy — vast range of settings.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "A woman who reads romance novels and dreams of escape" → Emma Bovary.
  • "Le mot juste" or "free indirect discourse" → Flaubert.
  • "Tried for obscenity in 1857" and acquitted → Madame Bovary.
  • "This author's collected clichés and platitudes" → Dictionary of Received Ideas / Bouvard et Pécuchet.

High-Yield Works (Very Common)

The 5 works most likely to be a tossup answer or anchor hard early clues. power = hard/early buzz clue. recog = recognition / mid-clue.

Madame Bovary
1856 (serialized) / 1857 (book) · novel · narrator: Charles Bovary via omniscient
powerFlaubert reportedly said "Madame Bovary, c'est moi" — he identified with Emma's romantic frustration. The novel began from a real case: Delphine Delamare, a Norman doctor's wife who died in debt.
recogEmma Rouault marries country doctor Charles Bovary in Normandy; bored, she pursues affairs with Rodolphe Boulanger (a wealthy landowner) and Léon Dupuis (a law clerk).
recogEmma accumulates crushing debts through the merchant Lheureux; when creditors seize her household, she swallows arsenic and dies in agony.
recogThe novel opens through Charles's perspective — a structural joke: the "hero" is the least perceptive character. The agricultural fair (comices agricoles) scene intercuts a seduction with a cattle prize ceremony — a famous ironic juxtaposition.
recogProsecuted (alongside publisher) for "offenses against public morals and religion" in January 1857; both acquitted. The trial made the novel a bestseller.

Sentimental Education (L'Éducation sentimentale)
1869 · novel
powerProtagonist Frédéric Moreau, a young man from Nogent-sur-Seine, arrives in Paris and falls hopelessly in love with Madame Arnoux — a married woman he idealizes but never possesses. The novel spans 1840–1867, ending with the Revolution of 1848 as backdrop.
recogFrédéric also pursues Rosanette (a courtesan) and Madame Dambreuse (an aristocrat); his divided desires and inaction are the point — the novel is an anatomy of failed ambition and romantic illusion.
recogFinal scene: Frédéric and his friend Deslauriers reminisce that their happiest memory was a botched visit to a brothel in their youth — the novel's ironic anti-climax.
recogOften called Flaubert's most autobiographical novel; Madame Arnoux is based on Élisa Schlésinger, a woman Flaubert loved since age 14.
wiki ↗
Salammbô
1862 · novel · set in ancient Carthage
powerSet during the Mercenary War (241–238 BC) — the revolt of Carthaginian mercenaries after the First Punic War. Flaubert spent years researching ancient sources, visiting Tunisia in 1858.
recogSalammbô is the daughter of Carthaginian general Hamilcar Barca; she is a priestess devoted to the goddess Tanit. She falls into contact with the mercenary leader Mathô, who steals the sacred veil of Tanit (zaïmph).
recogThe zaïmph (sacred veil/mantle of Tanit) is the novel's central symbolic object; whoever holds it gains power but is also doomed.
recogFlaubert's most "exotic" and spectacularly violent novel — mass crucifixions, siege warfare, a scene where mercenaries sacrifice children to Moloch.
wiki ↗
Three Tales (Trois Contes)
1877 · short fiction collection
powerThree stories spanning radically different eras: "A Simple Heart" (Un Coeur simple) — contemporary Normandy; "The Legend of Saint Julian Hospitator" — medieval; "Hérodias" — biblical (the beheading of John the Baptist).
recog"A Simple Heart": servant woman Félicité spends her life in selfless devotion; her beloved nephew Victor dies at sea; her only companion becomes a stuffed parrot named Loulou; as she dies, she hallucinates Loulou as the Holy Spirit.
recog"Hérodias": retells the story of Salome's dance and the decapitation of John the Baptist (Iaokanann) — head carried in on a platter; source for Wilde's Salome and Strauss's opera.
recogWritten near the end of Flaubert's life; often considered his most perfectly achieved short prose. "A Simple Heart" was written in response to a dare from George Sand to write something "tender."
wiki ↗
Bouvard and Pécuchet (Bouvard et Pécuchet)
1881 (posthumous, unfinished) · novel
powerTwo copy-clerks, Bouvard and Pécuchet, retire to Normandy and systematically attempt to master every field of human knowledge — agriculture, chemistry, medicine, history, philosophy, religion — and fail disastrously at each.
recogThe novel was to end with the two men abandoning all intellectual effort and returning to copying — specifically compiling the Dictionary of Received Ideas (Dictionnaire des idées reçues), a satirical catalog of bourgeois clichés.
recogLeft unfinished at Flaubert's death in 1880; a monument to bêtise (human stupidity) — Flaubert's lifelong obsession. The Dictionnaire is sometimes published separately.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Early Clues)

Minor works and specific details that appear as hard opening clues in tossups. One-line ID each.

The Temptation of Saint Anthony 1874 (3rd ver.) Dramatic prose poem; Saint Anthony beset by sins, heresies, monsters, gods — Flaubert's lifelong "drawer-piece," revised 1849–1874.
November (Novembre) 1842 (posth.) Semi-autobiographical prose; young narrator obsessed with a courtesan named Marie — early Romantic exercise, never published in his lifetime.
A Sentimental Education (first version) 1845 (posth.) Earlier, shorter draft of the novel; unrelated plot but same thematic territory — distinct from the canonical 1869 version.
"The Legend of Saint Julian Hospitator" 1877 Medieval tale in Three Tales; Julian kills his parents by mistake; becomes a leper-ferryman; Christ appears and embraces him.
Dictionary of Received Ideas (Dictionnaire des idées reçues) posth. Alphabetical satire of bourgeois clichés, e.g. "Artists — all feckless"; originally an appendix to Bouvard et Pécuchet.
Correspondance (Letters) ongoing Massive correspondence with George Sand, Louise Colet, and others — chief source for his aesthetic philosophy (le mot juste, impersonality).
Memoirs of a Madman (Mémoires d'un fou) 1838 (posth.) Adolescent autobiographical sketch; records his first sight of Élisa Schlésinger on a Trouville beach — source for Madame Arnoux.
Hérodias 1877 Third tale in Trois Contes; Herod, Herodias, Salome, and the Baptist's head on a platter — later source for Wilde and Richard Strauss.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key characters

  • Emma Bovary — doctor's wife, romantic dreamer, dies of arsenic
  • Charles Bovary — bumbling country doctor, Emma's husband
  • Rodolphe Boulanger — Emma's first lover, abandons her
  • Léon Dupuis — Emma's second lover, a law clerk
  • Lheureux — merchant who entangles Emma in debt
  • Frédéric Moreau — protagonist of Sentimental Education
  • Madame Arnoux — Frédéric's idealized, unattainable love
  • Félicité — servant in "A Simple Heart"; parrot Loulou
  • Salammbô — Carthaginian priestess of Tanit; Hamilcar's daughter
  • Mathô — mercenary leader who steals the zaïmph
  • Bouvard & Pécuchet — copy-clerk duo who fail at all knowledge

Style markers & techniques

  • Le mot juste — the exact right word; extreme care in prose
  • Free indirect discourse (style indirect libre) — narrative merged with character's thought
  • Impersonal narrator — "author nowhere visible"
  • Bêtise — human stupidity; recurring satirical target
  • Received ideas (idées reçues) — clichés Flaubert catalogued and mocked
  • Ironic juxtaposition — agricultural fair / seduction scene in Bovary
  • Agonizing revision — famously slow writer; "a good sentence ... is good prose — it must first of all be good prose"

Biographical hard clues

  • Born in Rouen, Normandy, 1821; father was chief surgeon at Hôtel-Dieu hospital
  • Epilepsy (or nervous disorder) struck him at ~22; retreated to Croisset family estate, rarely left
  • Lifelong love for Élisa Schlésinger (met age 14) → Madame Arnoux
  • Long correspondence / affair with poet Louise Colet
  • Close friendship with George Sand and Ivan Turgenev
  • Visited the Levant and Egypt 1849–51; notes fed Salammbô and Temptation
  • Died of a cerebral hemorrhage (apoplexy) at Croisset, 1880
  • "Hermit of Croisset" — his nickname

Famous quotes & motifs

  • "Madame Bovary, c'est moi."
  • "The author in his work must be like God in the universe, present everywhere and visible nowhere."
  • "I am a man of the pen."
  • Arsenic poisoning — Emma Bovary's death method
  • Parrot Loulou — stuffed parrot hallucinated as Holy Spirit (Simple Heart)
  • Zaïmph — sacred veil of Tanit in Salammbô
  • The 1848 Revolution as backdrop to Sentimental Education
  • Rouen Cathedral — setting of the famous carriage scene in Bovary

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Émile ZolaZola = Naturalism, working-class/industrial subjects (Germinal, Nana), the Rougon-Macquart series. Flaubert = Realism, bourgeois Normandy + antiquity, obsessive prose style. Zola is more political and systemic; Flaubert more aestheticist and ironic.
StendhalStendhal = Realism's earlier wave (The Red and the Black, The Charterhouse of Parma), psychological "crystallization" of love, Napoleonic ambition. Flaubert is the next generation, more technically precise, less Romantic hero-worship.
Guy de MaupassantMaupassant was Flaubert's student and protégé. Maupassant = short stories (The Necklace, Boule de Suif). If the clue mentions a short story, lean Maupassant; if a novel, lean Flaubert. Both Norman, both Realist.
BalzacBalzac = La Comédie humaine (90+ linked novels), social panorama of post-Napoleonic France, recurring characters. Flaubert wrote fewer, more polished works. Balzac is prolific and sprawling; Flaubert is sparse and lapidary.
TurgenevTurgenev (Fathers and Sons, On the Eve) was Flaubert's close friend and shared his circle in Paris. Turgenev = Russian; Flaubert = French. Both Realists, but Turgenev has the "superfluous man" and Russian nihilism; Flaubert has bêtise and bourgeois Normandy.
Gustave MoreauSame first name! Moreau = French Symbolist painter, not a novelist. Frequently painted Salome — a trap when the question mentions Salome, Herodias, or Hérodias. If it's a painting: Moreau. If it's prose fiction: Flaubert's Hérodias.
Flaubert vs. the Bovaryisme conceptBovaryisme (coined by philosopher Jules de Gaultier) = the capacity to conceive of oneself as other than one is — Emma's condition generalized. The term itself can be a tossup; its source is Flaubert's novel.

Big Picture — Why Flaubert Is a Quizbowl Cornerstone Flaubert sits at the hinge between the 19th-century novel and literary Modernism. His two chief legacies — free indirect discourse and the cult of le mot juste — flow directly into Joyce, Woolf, and Nabokov. His Madame Bovary trial connects to the obscenity trials of Baudelaire (Les Fleurs du mal, same year 1857), Zola, and later Joyce. His satirical bêtise — catalogued in the unfinished Dictionary of Received Ideas — anticipates Dadaism and conceptual art's war on cliché. For tournaments, Flaubert crosses into: French history (1848 Revolution in Sentimental Education), religion/mythology (Salome, Saint Anthony, Tanit/Moloch), and art history (Gustave Moreau's Salome paintings, Renoir's portrait of Flaubert).
