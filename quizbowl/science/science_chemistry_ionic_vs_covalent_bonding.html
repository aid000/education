Quick Overview

Core distinctionIonic: electron transfer → ions; Covalent: electron sharing between atoms
Who forms ionicMetal + nonmetal (e.g., NaCl, Na gives e⁻ to Cl)
Who forms covalentNonmetal + nonmetal (e.g., H₂O, CO₂, N₂)
Governing ruleElectronegativity difference ≥ 1.7 → ionic; < 1.7 → covalent (polar if 0.4–1.7)
Linus Pauling's roleDevised the electronegativity scale (1932) used to predict bond type
Third bond typeMetallic bonding — delocalized "sea of electrons" in metals

Electronegativity and the Pauling scale

  • Electronegativity = tendency of an atom to attract bonding electrons.
  • Fluorine is the most electronegative element (Pauling value: 4.0).
  • Cesium / Francium are the least electronegative.
  • Increases across a period (left → right) and up a group on the periodic table.
Electronegativity difference rule (Pauling)
ΔEN = |EN(A) − EN(B)|
ΔEN ≥ 1.7 → ionic  |  0.4 ≤ ΔEN < 1.7 → polar covalent  |  ΔEN < 0.4 → nonpolar covalent

Classic quizbowl giveaway framings

  • "Involves transfer of electrons" → ionic bond.
  • "Sharing of electrons" → covalent bond.
  • "Sea of electrons" → metallic bond (the trap!).
  • Any clue about Lewis structures, VSEPR, or molecular geometry → covalent molecule.

High-Yield (Very Common)

Principal statements, properties, and applications most likely to appear. power = hard early clue. recog = middle clue. giveaway = FTP-level.

Ionic Bonds — Formation and Properties
Electron transfer · lattice formation · NaCl prototype
powerFormed when an atom with low ionization energy (metal) transfers an electron to an atom with high electron affinity (nonmetal), creating oppositely charged ions held by electrostatic attraction.
recogIons arrange into a crystal lattice — in NaCl, each Na⁺ is surrounded by 6 Cl⁻ ions (face-centered cubic structure).
recogHigh melting points (e.g., NaCl melts at 801 °C); brittle — planes of like charges repel when shifted.
giveawayConduct electricity only when dissolved in water or melted (ions must be mobile); solid ionic compounds do not conduct.
Covalent Bonds — Sharing and Molecular Properties
Electron sharing · Lewis structures · VSEPR
powerDescribed quantum-mechanically by valence bond theory (overlapping atomic orbitals) and molecular orbital (MO) theory (linear combination of atomic orbitals, LCAO); MO theory explains O₂'s paramagnetism.
recogLewis structures (dot diagrams) show shared electron pairs; the octet rule drives most bonding (8 electrons in outer shell).
recogVSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion) predicts molecular geometry: H₂O is bent (104.5°), NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal, CH₄ is tetrahedral (109.5°).
giveawayGenerally lower melting points than ionic compounds; molecular covalent compounds (e.g., CO₂, H₂O) do not conduct electricity.
Polar vs Nonpolar Covalent
Partial charges · dipole moments · molecular symmetry
powerA molecule can have polar bonds yet be nonpolar overall if it is perfectly symmetrical — e.g., CO₂ (linear) and CCl₄ (tetrahedral) have bond dipoles that cancel.
recogH₂O is polar because its bent geometry leaves a net dipole; the partial negative charge (δ⁻) sits on oxygen.
giveawayPolar molecules experience dipole–dipole forces; water's special case is hydrogen bonding (H bonded to F, O, or N).
Bond Order, Length, and Strength
Single / double / triple bonds · bond energy
powerBond order = (bonding e⁻ − antibonding e⁻) / 2 in MO theory. O₂ has bond order 2 (double bond) with two unpaired electrons in π* antibonding orbitals → paramagnetism. N₂ has bond order 3 — the strongest common diatomic bond (945 kJ/mol).
recogHigher bond order → shorter bond length and higher bond energy: C≡C (121 pm, 839 kJ/mol) < C=C (134 pm, 614 kJ/mol) < C–C (154 pm, 347 kJ/mol).
giveawaySingle bonds are sigma (σ) bonds; double bonds add one pi (π) bond; triple bonds add two pi bonds.
Lattice Energy and the Born–Haber Cycle
Ionic stability · thermodynamic measurement
powerLattice energy cannot be measured directly; it is calculated via the Born–Haber cycle (Hess's law applied to ionic formation: sublimation + ionization + dissociation + electron affinity + lattice energy = ΔH°f).
recogLattice energy increases with higher ionic charge and smaller ionic radius — MgO has a much higher lattice energy than NaCl (both factors: Mg²⁺/O²⁻ vs Na⁺/Cl⁻).
giveawayHigher lattice energy → harder, higher-melting ionic solid.
Hybridization in Covalent Molecules
sp, sp², sp³ orbitals · geometry prediction
powerConcept developed by Linus Pauling (1931) to explain observed bond angles. In sp³ hybridization (CH₄), one s + three p orbitals mix to form four equivalent orbitals at 109.5°.
recogsp² (trigonal planar, 120°): ethylene (C₂H₄), benzene (C₆H₆). sp (linear, 180°): acetylene (C₂H₂), CO₂.
giveawayLone pairs count as electron groups for electron geometry but are invisible in molecular geometry — water is sp³ but bent, not tetrahedral.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results and subtler concepts that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line each.

Resonance structures
Multiple valid Lewis structures for one molecule (e.g., O₃, NO₃⁻, benzene); real structure is a hybrid with delocalized electrons.
Formal charge
Assigns electron ownership to atoms in Lewis structures; minimizing formal charge selects the best resonance form.
Network covalent solids
Covalent bonds extending in 3-D lattice: diamond (sp³ C), graphite (sp² C layers), SiO₂ (quartz) — extremely hard, very high melting points.
Graphite's conductivity
Network covalent but conducts electricity because sp² carbon leaves one unhybridized p orbital per atom → delocalized π electrons between layers.
London dispersion forces
Weakest intermolecular force; temporary induced dipoles; only force between nonpolar molecules like noble gases or CH₄.
Hydrogen bonding (special case)
Exceptionally strong dipole–dipole force (H bonded to F, O, N); responsible for water's anomalously high boiling point (100 °C) and ice's lower density than liquid water.
Fajans' rules
Predict when a "nominally ionic" bond gains significant covalent character: small, highly charged cation + large, polarizable anion → more covalent character.
Coordinate (dative) covalent bond
Both electrons in the shared pair come from one atom (Lewis base donates to Lewis acid); seen in NH₄⁺ formation and metal–ligand bonds.
Molecular orbital theory — bonding in H₂
Two 1s atomic orbitals combine to form σ bonding (lower energy) and σ* antibonding (higher energy) MOs; electrons fill bonding MO → H₂ is stable.
O₂ paramagnetism (MO theory proof)
Lewis structure predicts all electrons paired (wrong); MO theory correctly shows two unpaired electrons in degenerate π* MOs — confirmed experimentally by liquid O₂ attracted to a magnet.
Ionic character percentage
No bond is 100% ionic; HF (~43% ionic character despite large ΔEN) is partially covalent — continuum between pure covalent and pure ionic.
VSEPR: five + electron domains
Phosphorus pentachloride (PCl₅) is trigonal bipyramidal; SF₆ is octahedral — these expand the octet using d orbitals (or, in modern view, hypervalency).

Recognition / Clue Bank

Ionic — key terms

  • Lattice energy — energy to separate ionic lattice to gas-phase ions
  • Born–Haber cycle — indirect thermodynamic calculation
  • Madelung constant — geometry factor in lattice energy formula
  • Crystal lattice / unit cell — repeating structural unit
  • Coulomb's law — force ∝ q₁q₂/r² governs ionic attraction
  • Electrolyte — ionic compound that conducts when dissolved

Covalent — key terms

  • Lewis structure / electron-dot diagram
  • Octet rule (exceptions: H duet; B/Al sextet; expanded octets P, S, Cl, etc.)
  • VSEPR theory — electron domains repel to maximize separation
  • Hybridization — sp, sp², sp³, sp³d, sp³d²
  • Sigma (σ) and pi (π) bonds
  • Resonance / delocalization

People and history

  • Linus Pauling — electronegativity scale (1932), hybridization, The Nature of the Chemical Bond (1939)
  • G. N. Lewis — Lewis structures, Lewis acid/base theory, electron-pair sharing model (1916)
  • Walter Kossel — ionic bond theory (1916), simultaneous with Lewis
  • Fritz Haber / Max Born — Born–Haber cycle
  • Nevil Sidgwick / Herbert Powell — VSEPR precursor (1940)
  • Ronald Gillespie — VSEPR formalized (1957)

Canonical examples

  • NaCl — prototype ionic (table salt); ΔEN = 2.1
  • MgO — high lattice energy ionic solid
  • H₂O — polar covalent, bent, hydrogen bonding
  • CO₂ — polar bonds, nonpolar molecule (linear)
  • N₂ — triple bond, bond order 3, very stable
  • O₂ — paramagnetic, bond order 2, MO theory showpiece
  • C₆H₆ (benzene) — resonance, sp² C, aromatic
  • Diamond / graphite — network covalent allotropes of carbon

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Metallic bondingA third type: metal atoms share electrons in a delocalized sea, not discrete bonds. Metals are malleable (layers slide), conduct in solid state, and show lustrous appearance. Clue: "sea of electrons," "electron cloud," "ductile and malleable."
Ionic vs covalent by conductivityBoth solid NaCl and solid covalent compounds are nonconductors. The key is when dissolved: ionic solution conducts; covalent solution (e.g., sugar water) does not. Molten NaCl conducts; molten sugar does not.
Polar bond vs polar moleculeCO₂ has polar C=O bonds but is a nonpolar molecule (linear, dipoles cancel). A molecule is polar only if bond dipoles do not cancel — depends on geometry (VSEPR).
Network covalent vs molecular covalentBoth involve electron sharing. Network covalent (diamond, SiO₂) has very high melting points and is very hard — often confused with ionic. Molecular covalent (H₂O, CO₂) has low melting points.
Ionic vs polar covalent (continuum)The ΔEN = 1.7 cutoff is approximate, not absolute. HF (ΔEN ≈ 1.9) is classified as covalent despite large difference — the rule is a guideline. Always check context (is it a discrete molecule or a lattice?).
Sigma vs pi bondEvery single bond is one sigma; a double bond = 1σ + 1π; a triple bond = 1σ + 2π. Sigma bonds allow free rotation; pi bonds restrict rotation (key for cis/trans isomerism). Don't confuse bond order with the number of sigma bonds.
Lewis acid/base vs Brønsted acid/baseBrønsted: proton (H⁺) donor/acceptor. Lewis: electron-pair acceptor/donor — broader category; all Brønsted acids are Lewis acids but not vice versa. Relevant in coordinate bond / covalent chemistry.

🔗 Cross-discipline big picture Ionic vs covalent bonding is chemistry's master framework: it feeds into materials science (ceramics = ionic/network-covalent; polymers = covalent), biochemistry (all biomolecules — DNA, proteins, lipids — are covalent; ATP hydrolysis is driven by resonance stabilization of phosphate ions), electrochemistry (electrolytes must be ionic or dissociate), and quantum mechanics (MO theory is directly derived from Schrödinger wave equations applied to multi-electron systems). Questions about Linus Pauling (Chemistry Nobel 1954, Peace Nobel 1962) often hinge on the electronegativity scale and hybridization — bridging chemistry tossups to physics and to peace/politics categories.
