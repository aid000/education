Notation warsNewton's dot notation (ẋ) vs. Leibniz's dy/dx — Leibniz notation won in practice

Governing formulas

Derivative (limit definition) f'(x) = lim[h→0] (f(x+h) − f(x)) / h
FTC Part 1 — differentiation of an accumulation function d/dx ∫[a to x] f(t) dt = f(x)
FTC Part 2 — evaluation theorem ∫[a to b] f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a), where F' = f

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

The principal statements and theorems most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard, early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

Fundamental Theorem of Calculus (FTC)
Formalized by Newton & Leibniz, 17th c. · Two parts
recogPart 1: if F(x) = ∫[a to x] f(t) dt, then F'(x) = f(x) — differentiating an integral recovers the integrand.
recogPart 2: ∫[a to b] f(x) dx = F(b) − F(a) — a definite integral equals the antiderivative evaluated at the endpoints.
powerThe FTC formally shows that differentiation and integration are inverse operations; Barrow's geometric proof (~1670) predates Newton's algebraic formulation. A key early clue is "this theorem, whose two parts are sometimes credited to Barrow."

Derivative Rules: Chain, Product, Quotient
Core differentiation rules · Single-variable calculus
recogChain Rule: d/dx[f(g(x))] = f'(g(x)) · g'(x) — "derivative of the outside times derivative of the inside."
recogProduct Rule: (uv)' = u'v + uv' — Leibniz rule; also written d(uv) = v du + u dv.
recogQuotient Rule: (u/v)' = (u'v − uv') / v² — "low d-high minus high d-low, over low squared."
powerThe Product Rule is Leibniz's rule; the Chain Rule is sometimes called the composite function rule. Tossups may specify "this rule, which can be iterated to differentiate compositions of three or more functions."
wiki ↗
Mean Value Theorem (MVT) & Rolle's Theorem
Mean Value Theorem · Rolle 1691, Lagrange generalized 1797
recogMVT: if f is continuous on [a,b] and differentiable on (a,b), there exists c such that f'(c) = (f(b)−f(a))/(b−a).
recogRolle's Theorem is the special case where f(a) = f(b) → ∃ c with f'(c) = 0.
powerThe MVT is also called the Lagrange mean value theorem; Cauchy's generalization gives (f(b)−f(a))/(g(b)−g(a)) = f'(c)/g'(c), enabling L'Hôpital's Rule proofs. Tossups may open with "a theorem named for the French mathematician who generalized Rolle's result."
wiki ↗
Riemann Integral & Riemann Sums
Bernhard Riemann, 1854 · Foundations of integration
recogThe Riemann integral is defined as the limit of Riemann sums — partitions of [a,b] into n subintervals, summing f(x*ᵢ) Δxᵢ as n→∞.
recogA function is Riemann-integrable iff it is bounded and its set of discontinuities has measure zero.
powerRiemann's 1854 Habilitation thesis defined the integral using upper and lower Darboux sums; he used it to study Fourier series. A hard tossup clue: "defined using tagged partitions and the mesh (norm) of a partition going to zero."
wiki ↗
L'Hôpital's Rule
Guillaume de l'Hôpital, 1696 · Analyse des Infiniment Petits
recogIf lim f(x)/g(x) gives 0/0 or ∞/∞, then lim f(x)/g(x) = lim f'(x)/g'(x) (under appropriate conditions).
recogPublished in the first differential calculus textbook (1696), though the result was largely due to Johann Bernoulli.
powerL'Hôpital paid Johann Bernoulli for calculus lessons and rights to publish Bernoulli's discoveries — the authorship controversy is a common hard clue. The rule also applies to indeterminate forms 0·∞, 1^∞, ∞^0 via algebraic manipulation.
wiki ↗
Newton–Leibniz Priority Dispute
Royal Society investigation, 1712–1716 · Decades of acrimony
recogNewton developed calculus (~1666, "method of fluxions") but did not publish until 1693/1704; Leibniz published in 1684 (Nova Methodus).
recogA Royal Society committee (dominated by Newton) ruled for Newton in 1712, but historians now credit independent simultaneous invention.
powerThe Leibniz camp included Jacob and Johann Bernoulli; Leibniz's notation (dy/dx, ∫) is standard today. Key tossup term: Newton's term for the derivative was "fluxion"; the variable was called a "fluent."

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related theorems and results that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

Intermediate Value Theorem (IVT)
Cauchy, Bolzano · ~1817
f continuous, f(a)≠f(b) → f hits every value in between
Used to prove roots exist; basis of bisection method.
Taylor & Maclaurin Series
Brook Taylor 1715, Maclaurin 1742
f(x) = Σ f⁽ⁿ⁾(a)/n! · (x−a)ⁿ
Maclaurin = Taylor at a=0; used to approximate e^x, sin x, ln(1+x).
Integration by Parts
Leibniz rule · integral analog of Product Rule
∫ u dv = uv − ∫ v du
Acronym LIATE guides choice of u; derived directly from Product Rule.
Cauchy Mean Value Theorem
Augustin-Louis Cauchy · 1821
[f(b)−f(a)]/[g(b)−g(a)] = f'(c)/g'(c)
Generalized MVT; key step in rigorous proof of L'Hôpital's Rule.
Squeeze Theorem (Sandwich Theorem)
Standard single-variable calculus
g(x) ≤ f(x) ≤ h(x), lim g = lim h = L → lim f = L
Classic application: lim(sin x)/x = 1 as x→0.
Lebesgue Integral
Henri Lebesgue · 1902 thesis
∫ f dμ (measure-theoretic)
Integrates a broader class than Riemann; integrates indicator of rationals.
Extreme Value Theorem (EVT)
Bolzano, Weierstrass · 19th c.
f continuous on [a,b] → f attains max and min
Basis for optimization; fails on open intervals.
Fundamental Theorem of Line Integrals
Multivariable calculus
∫_C ∇f · dr = f(B) − f(A)
Vector calculus analog of FTC; gradient fields are path-independent.
Green's Theorem
George Green · 1828
∮_C P dx + Q dy = ∬_D (∂Q/∂x − ∂P/∂y) dA
Relates a line integral around a closed curve to a double integral; special case of Stokes' theorem.
Differential Equations (ODEs)
Core application of derivatives & integrals
dy/dx = ky → y = Ce^(kx)
Separation of variables; exponential growth/decay; Newton's law of cooling.
Partial Derivatives & Gradient
Multivariable calculus
∇f = (∂f/∂x, ∂f/∂y, ∂f/∂z)
Gradient points in direction of steepest ascent; used in Lagrange multipliers.
Arc Length Formula
Single-variable application of integration
L = ∫[a to b] √(1 + [f'(x)]²) dx
Parametric form: ∫ √((dx/dt)² + (dy/dt)²) dt.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & notation

  • Fluxion / fluent — Newton's terms for derivative / variable
  • dy/dx — Leibniz notation; f'(x) — Lagrange notation; Df — Euler/operator notation
  • Antiderivative / indefinite integral — F such that F' = f
  • ∫ (integral sign) — elongated "S" for "summa" (Leibniz, 1675)
  • Differential — infinitesimal increment dx, df
  • Gradient, divergence, curl — vector-calc extensions

Named people & their contributions

  • Newton — fluxions, dot notation, Principia (1687)
  • Leibniz — published first (1684), standard notation
  • Barrow — Isaac Barrow, geometric precursor to FTC
  • Cauchy — rigorous epsilon-delta limits (1821)
  • Riemann — Riemann integral, Riemann sums
  • Lebesgue — measure-theoretic integration (1902)
  • Rolle — Rolle's theorem (1691)
  • L'Hôpital / Johann Bernoulli — L'Hôpital's Rule dispute
  • Weierstrass — formal epsilon-delta; nowhere-differentiable function

Key numbers & special values

  • e ≈ 2.71828 — base of natural log; d(eˣ)/dx = eˣ
  • ∫[0 to ∞] e^(−x²) dx = √π/2 — Gaussian integral
  • ∫[−1 to 1] 1/(1+x²) dx = π/2 — arctan integral
  • d(sin x)/dx = cos x; d(cos x)/dx = −sin x
  • Power rule: d(xⁿ)/dx = nxⁿ⁻¹; ∫xⁿ dx = xⁿ⁺¹/(n+1) + C
  • ∫ 1/x dx = ln|x| + C

Cross-discipline links

  • Physics — velocity = dx/dt; acceleration = d²x/dt²; position = ∫v dt
  • Thermodynamics — exact vs. inexact differentials (dU vs. δQ)
  • Maxwell's equations — divergence and curl of E, B fields
  • Economics — marginal cost/revenue = derivative of total cost/revenue
  • Fourier analysis — Riemann / Lebesgue integrals of trig series
  • Probability — CDF is integral of PDF; F'(x) = f(x)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Derivative vs. DifferentialDerivative f'(x) is a function (limit of a ratio). A differential df = f'(x) dx is an infinitesimal quantity. Leibniz treated them separately; Newton had "fluxions" and "moments."
Indefinite vs. Definite IntegralIndefinite ∫f dx = F(x)+C is a family of antiderivatives (no bounds). Definite ∫[a to b] f dx is a number (area). FTC Part 2 links them.
Riemann integral vs. Lebesgue integralRiemann partitions the domain (x-axis). Lebesgue partitions the range (y-axis) using measure theory — integrates more functions (e.g., the Dirichlet function).
MVT vs. IVT vs. EVTMVT (Mean Value): a tangent slope equals a secant slope — requires differentiability. IVT (Intermediate Value): a continuous function hits every intermediate value — no derivative needed. EVT (Extreme Value): a continuous function on a closed interval attains its max and min.
Rolle's Theorem vs. MVTRolle's is a special case of the MVT: requires f(a)=f(b), guarantees f'(c)=0. The MVT generalizes to any secant slope.
L'Hôpital's Rule vs. Taylor seriesBoth evaluate indeterminate limits. L'Hôpital differentiates numerator and denominator. Taylor expansion substitutes series — sometimes faster and needed when L'Hôpital cycles.
Newton (calculus) vs. Newton (physics)In calculus tossups, clues like "fluxions," "fluents," "method of inverse tangents," or "1666 plague years" point to Newton-as-mathematician. "F=ma," "law of gravitation," or "Principia Mathematica" point to Newton-as-physicist — often the same answer, but clue framing differs.
Green's Theorem vs. Stokes' Theorem vs. Divergence TheoremAll are generalizations of the FTC to higher dimensions. Green's = 2-D (line integral ↔ double integral). Stokes' = surface integral ↔ line integral (3-D). Divergence (Gauss') = volume integral ↔ surface integral.

Cross-discipline big picture The FTC is the spine of all applied mathematics: in physics, position = ∫velocity dt and force = d(momentum)/dt; in electromagnetism, Maxwell's equations are expressed as derivatives and integrals (and more compactly via the divergence theorem and Stokes' theorem); in probability, the PDF and CDF are linked by the FTC (F'(x) = f(x)); in economics, the derivative is marginal cost and the integral is total cost. Any tossup on related theorems — Green's, Stokes', Divergence — is a higher-dimensional echo of the same fundamental idea.
