Quick Overview

DefinitionArrangement of elements by increasing atomic number; properties recur periodically
Credited toDmitri Mendeleev (1869) — left gaps for undiscovered elements
Also proposedLothar Meyer (independent, same year); John Newlands "law of octaves" (1865)
Modern basisMoseley (1913) — ordered by atomic number (proton count), not atomic mass
Structure18 groups (columns), 7 periods (rows); 118 confirmed elements (as of 2016)
Scale for electronegativityPauling scale — F = 4.0 (highest), Cs ≈ 0.79 (lowest)

The core trends — summary arrows

PropertyAcross a period (left → right)Down a group (top → bottom)
Atomic radius ↓ decreases — more protons, stronger pull on same shell ↑ increases — added shells, more shielding
Ionization energy (IE) ↑ increases — harder to remove e⁻ from smaller, more-charged nucleus ↓ decreases — outer e⁻ farther away, more shielded
Electronegativity (EN) ↑ increases — same trend as IE; nucleus pulls bond e⁻ more ↓ decreases — larger radius, weaker pull
Electron affinity (EA) Generally ↑ increases (more negative); halogens highest Generally ↓ decreases (less negative)
Metallic character ↓ decreases — metals left, nonmetals right ↑ increases — easier to lose e⁻

Governing concept: effective nuclear charge (Zeff)

Effective nuclear charge
Zeff = Z − σ
Z = atomic number (protons); σ = shielding constant (inner electrons). Higher Zeff → smaller radius, higher IE and EN. This single concept drives almost all periodic trends.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Fluorine — highest electronegativity (4.0) and highest electron affinity on the Pauling scale.
  • Cesium / Francium — largest atomic radius / lowest electronegativity (Fr rarely used due to instability; Cs = practical answer).
  • Mendeleev predicted gallium ("eka-aluminum") and germanium ("eka-silicon") — the classic "left gaps" clue.
  • Moseley fixed element ordering by atomic number; he died at age 27 in WWI (Gallipoli).

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

The principal topics most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = mid-clue giveaway.

Electronegativity
Pauling scale · Linus Pauling (1932) · bond polarity driver
powerPauling defined it using bond dissociation energies: EN difference between two atoms equals the square root of the excess bond energy (in eV) relative to a purely covalent bond. Also quantified by Mulliken (average of IE + EA) and Allred-Rochow (based on Zeff/r²).
recogFluorine = 4.0 (highest); oxygen = 3.5; nitrogen = 3.0; hydrogen = 2.2; cesium ≈ 0.79 (lowest among stable).
recogEN difference > 1.7 → ionic bond; < 0.5 → nonpolar covalent; in between → polar covalent. These thresholds are frequent clue anchors.
recogIncreases across a period (left to right), decreases down a group — noble gases typically excluded from the scale.
Atomic Radius
Covalent / van der Waals / metallic radius · picometers (pm) or angstroms (Å)
powerThree distinct measurement types: covalent radius (half the distance between bonded atoms of same element), van der Waals radius (half distance between nonbonded atoms in contact), metallic radius (for metals in crystal lattice). Covalent radius < metallic radius < van der Waals radius for same element.
recogDecreases left → right across a period: more protons pull the same-shell electrons inward; shielding constant barely changes within a period.
recogIncreases top → bottom in a group: each new period adds a principal quantum shell, and added inner electrons increase shielding.
recogLargest neutral atom: Cesium (Cs) (or francium by some measures). Smallest neutral atom: Helium; smallest nonnoble-gas: Fluorine.
Ionization Energy (IE)
Energy to remove one electron from gaseous atom · kJ/mol · first vs. successive
powerSuccessive ionization energies show a dramatic jump when an electron must be removed from the next inner shell (full subshell), revealing group number. A large jump after removing 2 electrons → element is in Group 2 (e.g., Mg). This data-table question type is very common at nationals level.
recogTwo exceptions to the clean left-to-right increase across Period 3: IE1 of Al < Mg (Al's 3p is easier to remove than Mg's 3s); IE1 of S < P (S's paired 3p electron repels, easier to remove). These specific exceptions appear constantly.
recogHighest first IE: Helium (noble gases) among all elements; highest among metals: Mercury (relativistic effect). Lowest first IE: Cesium (practical) or francium (unstable).
Electron Affinity (EA)
Energy released when neutral gaseous atom gains one electron · kJ/mol (sign conventions vary)
powerChlorine (not fluorine!) has the highest electron affinity (most negative ΔH) among all elements: Cl = −349 kJ/mol vs. F = −328 kJ/mol. Fluorine's smaller size means more electron–electron repulsion in the compact 2p shell, reducing its EA despite higher EN.
recogHalogens (Group 17) have highest electron affinities — one electron away from a noble-gas configuration. Noble gases and alkaline earths have near-zero or positive (unfavorable) EA.
recogNitrogen has a lower (less negative) EA than oxygen and carbon due to its half-filled 2p subshell — extra stability makes it resist gaining an electron. Same idea as why IE(P) > IE(S).
Key Groups & Periods
Structural landmarks most likely to appear in clues
powerLanthanide contraction: f-orbital filling across lanthanides (La→Lu) produces minimal increase in ionic radius, so 5d transition metals (Hf–Au) are nearly the same size as their 4d counterparts above — explains why Zr≈Hf, Nb≈Ta, Mo≈W in pairs. A frequent hard-clue anomaly.
recogGroup 1 (alkali metals): largest radii in each period; lowest IE; react vigorously with water, producing H₂ and metal hydroxide.
recogGroup 17 (halogens): highest EN and EA in each period; F > Cl > Br > I for both EN and oxidizing power.
recogGroup 18 (noble gases): smallest radii (van der Waals) in each period, highest IE — except He which has no defined covalent radius; first noble-gas compound was XePtF₆, made by Neil Bartlett in 1962.
recogPeriod 2 anomalies: Li, Be, B, N, O all have properties atypical of their group because of the absence of d orbitals and small size; F and O have weaker-than-expected single bonds due to lone-pair repulsion.
History of the Periodic Table
Mendeleev · Moseley · Newlands · Meyer — frequently tested biographical/history clues
powerMendeleev presented his table on March 6, 1869 to the Russian Chemical Society (he was on a dairy-farm tour when it was read for him). He reportedly dreamed the table's arrangement. His 1869 table ordered by atomic mass, not atomic number — leading to transpositions of Ar/K, Co/Ni, Te/I that Moseley later corrected.
recogMendeleev predicted eka-boron (= Sc), eka-aluminum (= Ga), eka-silicon (= Ge) before they were discovered; all matched his predictions remarkably well. "Eka" = Sanskrit for "one beyond."
recogHenry Moseley (1913): used X-ray spectroscopy to determine atomic numbers; killed at Gallipoli age 27 (1915). His work showed element identity = proton count, resolving anomalies in Mendeleev's mass-based table.
recogJohn Newlands "law of octaves" (1865): every 8th element repeats properties — mocked by contemporaries (J.A.R. Newlands denied a Chemical Society reading), but awarded Davy Medal retroactively.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results and fine-grained facts that appear as early/hard tossup clues. 5–10 words each.

Diagonal relationship
Li≈Mg, Be≈Al, B≈Si in properties; similar charge-to-radius ratio.
Slater's rules
Empirical rules for calculating shielding constant σ; basis of Zeff estimates.
Isoelectronic species
Same electron count, same radius trend (e.g., O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺).
Relativistic effects
Explains Au's gold color, Hg's liquid state, Pb²⁺ stability over Pb⁴⁺; inner s-orbitals contract at high Z.
Covalent vs. ionic radius distinction
Cations smaller than neutral atom; anions larger than neutral atom.
Madelung / Aufbau filling order
4s fills before 3d (Aufbau); energy crossover explains transition-metal configurations.
Half-filled subshell stability
Cr = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹, Cu = [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ — classic exceptions to Aufbau.
Lanthanide contraction detail
Poor f-orbital shielding causes Zeff spike across lanthanides; 6s contracts.
Fluorine anomalies
No d-orbital; F–F bond unusually weak (lone-pair repulsion in small atom).
Allred-Rochow electronegativity
EN ∝ Zeff/r²; better for transition metals than Pauling scale.
Neil Bartlett's XePtF₆
First noble-gas compound (1962); showed noble gases can react.
Mendeleev Nobel controversy
Denied Nobel Prize in 1906 by one vote; Moissan (F isolation) won instead.
Period 2 / Period 3 IE anomalies
B < Be and O < N in first IE; P < S ditto — exam-critical exceptions.
Moseley's law
√f = a(Z − b); X-ray Kα frequency scales with (Z − 1)²; defines atomic number.
Seaborg / actinide concept
Glenn Seaborg proposed actinides as separate row; revised the table's shape (1944).
Newlands' Davy Medal
Awarded 1887, retroactively; denied reading at Chemical Society in 1866.

  • Moseley's law — √f ∝ (Z − b)
  • Law of octaves — Newlands, 1865
  • Slater's rules — shielding constants

Key people

  • Dmitri Mendeleev — 1869 table; predicted eka-elements
  • Lothar Meyer — independent 1869 table (atomic volume graph)
  • John Newlands — law of octaves, 1865
  • Henry Moseley — atomic number via X-rays; died Gallipoli 1915
  • Linus Pauling — electronegativity scale
  • Glenn Seaborg — actinide series; element 106 named seaborgium
  • Neil Bartlett — first noble-gas compound (XePtF₆, 1962)

Extreme / record elements

  • Highest EN: Fluorine (F) — 4.0
  • Highest EA: Chlorine (Cl) — −349 kJ/mol
  • Highest 1st IE: Helium — 2372 kJ/mol
  • Lowest 1st IE: Cesium — 376 kJ/mol
  • Largest radius: Cesium (stable) / Francium (unstable)
  • Smallest radius: Helium (nonbonding); F (bonding/covalent)

Group nicknames & key behaviors

  • Group 1 (alkali metals) — 1 valence e⁻; explode in water; Fr most reactive
  • Group 2 (alkaline earths) — 2 valence e⁻; Be anomalously small/covalent
  • Group 17 (halogens) — 7 valence e⁻; highest EN; diatomic X₂
  • Group 18 (noble gases) — full octets; highest IE; Rn most reactive noble gas
  • d-block (transition metals) — variable oxidation states; colored ions
  • Metalloids (semimetals) — B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te; staircase on table

Comparison Traps

Common confusions in quizbowl and on exams.

Concept pair / trapHow to distinguish
Electronegativity vs. electron affinity EN measures pull on shared bond electrons (dimensionless Pauling number, no units). EA measures energy released when a gaseous atom gains an electron (kJ/mol, measurable). You can have high EN with modest EA (F: EN 4.0 but EA less than Cl's).
Cl highest EA, F highest EN The classic F vs. Cl trap. F wins EN (4.0 vs. 3.2); Cl wins EA (−349 vs. −328 kJ/mol) because F's compact 2p shell has more electron-electron repulsion.
Atomic radius vs. ionic radius Cations are smaller than parent atom (lost e⁻ → fewer shells or higher Zeff). Anions are larger (gained e⁻ → more repulsion, same Z). Na < Na⁺ is wrong; Na⁺ < Na is correct.
Ionization energy exceptions (B<Be, O<N) Be: full 2s² subshell — harder to remove than B's 2p¹. N: half-filled 2p³ — extra stability; O's paired 2p electron repels, easier to remove. Memorize both exception pairs.
Mendeleev vs. Moseley ordering Mendeleev used atomic mass (1869) — works mostly, but flips Ar/K, Co/Ni, Te/I. Moseley used atomic number/proton count (1913) — resolves all anomalies. Modern table = Moseley order.
Mendeleev vs. Lothar Meyer Both published in 1869. Mendeleev gets credit because he predicted missing elements and published first (February vs. December). Meyer's atomic-volume curve is his distinctive contribution.
First IE vs. second IE jumps A large jump after the Nth IE = element has N valence electrons = belongs to Group N. E.g., large jump after 3rd IE → Group 3 (aluminum). Don't confuse with period position.
Shielding vs. penetration Shielding — inner electrons reduce Zeff seen by outer electrons. Penetration — how close an orbital comes to the nucleus (s > p > d > f). Higher penetration → lower shielding of that electron by others → lower energy (more stable).

Cross-discipline big picture Periodic trends are the backbone of all quantitative chemistry: electronegativity differences drive bond polarity and molecular geometry (VSEPR), which leads to dipole moments and intermolecular forces; ionization energies determine oxidation-state patterns in transition-metal chemistry and redox; atomic radius governs crystal-structure prediction (ionic radius ratios for lattice types), nuclear magnetic resonance chemical shifts, and solid-state conductivity. In physics, relativistic effects at high Z — a direct consequence of electrons orbiting a nucleus with Z > 70 protons — explain why gold is yellow, mercury is a liquid at room temperature, and lead prefers +2 over +4. These connections frequently bridge chemistry tossups into physics bonus parts.
