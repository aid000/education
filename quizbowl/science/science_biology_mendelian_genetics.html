Quick Overview

OriginatorGregor Mendel — Augustinian friar, Brünn (Brno), 1856–1863
PublishedVersuche über Pflanzenhybriden (1866); ignored until 1900 rediscovery
Model organismPisum sativum (garden pea); ~29,000 plants over 8 years
Core conceptHereditary traits controlled by discrete particulate factors (now called genes/alleles)
Governing framework
Genotype → Phenotype via dominance:
AA or Aa → dominant phenotype  |  aa → recessive phenotype
Monohybrid F2 ratio: 3 dominant : 1 recessive (phenotype)  |  1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa (genotype)

Key vocabulary (must know)

  • Allele — one of two (or more) variant forms of a gene at a locus.
  • Homozygous — two identical alleles (AA or aa); heterozygous — two different alleles (Aa).
  • Dominant allele — expressed whenever present (even one copy); conventionally uppercase.
  • Recessive allele — expressed only in homozygous recessive (aa); conventionally lowercase.
  • Phenotype — observable trait; genotype — genetic constitution.
  • F1 generation — first filial (offspring of P cross); F2 — offspring of F1 × F1.
  • Test cross — crossing unknown dominant phenotype with homozygous recessive to determine genotype.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The 3:1 phenotypic ratio in the F2 generation of a monohybrid cross.
  • Garden peas, Brünn monastery, Augustinian monk → answer is Mendel or Mendelian genetics.
  • Law of Segregation and Law of Independent Assortment named together → Mendel's two laws.

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

The core statements most likely to be answer or anchor. power = hard/early clue. recog = recognition / mid-clue.

Law of Segregation (Mendel's First Law)
Derived from monohybrid crosses · 1866
recogEach organism carries two alleles for each trait; alleles separate during gamete formation so each gamete receives only one.
recogExplains why the 3:1 ratio appears in F2 — recessive allele is "hidden" in Aa but not lost.
powerMendel's particulate model refuted blending inheritance (the then-dominant view). The cellular basis — meiosis — was not identified until after Mendel's death; the connection made by Boveri and Sutton (~1902) in the chromosome theory of heredity.
Law of Independent Assortment (Mendel's Second Law)
Derived from dihybrid crosses · 1866
recogAlleles of different genes sort into gametes independently of one another (assuming genes on different chromosomes).
recogDihybrid F2 ratio: 9:3:3:1 (9 dominant both : 3 dominant A only : 3 dominant B only : 1 recessive both).
powerBreaks down when genes are linked (on the same chromosome) — discovered by Morgan and Sturtevant via Drosophila crosses. This exception is itself a high-frequency quizbowl clue.
Punnett Square
Method introduced by Reginald Crundall Punnett · 1905
recogGrid where parental gametes are arrayed on each axis; cells show all possible offspring genotypes.
recogMonohybrid Aa × Aa → 4 cells: AA, Aa, Aa, aa (1:2:1 genotype; 3:1 phenotype).
powerNamed for R. C. Punnett, co-founder (with William Bateson) of the Journal of Genetics; Bateson coined the term "genetics" in 1905. A Punnett-square setup in a tossup is a near-certain Mendel/genetics clue.
Example: Monohybrid cross Aa × Aa (e.g., purple × purple pea flowers)

Mendel's Seven Pea Traits
Pisum sativum · all on separate chromosomes (near-ideal independent assortment)
recogSeed shape (round vs. wrinkled), seed color (yellow vs. green), pod shape (inflated vs. constricted), pod color (green vs. yellow), flower color (purple vs. white), flower position (axial vs. terminal), stem height (tall vs. short).
recogIn each pair, the first-listed trait is dominant; Mendel's crosses always recovered 3:1 in F2.
powerChromosome mapping later showed all 7 traits lie on different chromosomes (or far-apart loci) — fortuitously allowing independent assortment to hold perfectly. Modern geneticists note this was unusually lucky.
Test Cross
Fundamental diagnostic procedure · used throughout genetics
recogCross an organism with dominant phenotype but unknown genotype to a homozygous recessive (aa) individual.
recogIf all offspring show dominant phenotype → parent is AA. If ~half recessive → parent is Aa.
powerThe test cross is the definitional way to distinguish homozygous from heterozygous dominant — used extensively in animal and plant breeding; the logic underpins modern carrier testing in human genetics.
Dominance Relationships: Complete, Incomplete, Codominance
Extensions of Mendelian principles · post-Mendel refinements
recogComplete dominance: one allele fully masks the other (classical Mendelian). Incomplete dominance: heterozygote shows an intermediate phenotype (e.g., red × white → pink in snapdragons — Antirrhinum).
recogCodominance: both alleles expressed simultaneously in heterozygote (e.g., AB blood type: both A and B antigens present; also roan cattle).
powerSnapdragon incomplete dominance (1:2:1 phenotypic ratio in F2, not 3:1) is the most-tested exception in quizbowl. Codominant ABO system: alleles IA, IB are codominant; i is recessive — one of three alleles at a single locus (multiple alleles).

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Extensions and related results that appear as early/hard clues. One-line ID each.

Epistasis
One gene masks expression of a different gene's alleles; produces modified F2 ratios (9:7, 12:3:1, etc.).
Pleiotropy
Single gene affects multiple phenotypic traits; classic example: sickle-cell anemia (HBB gene).
Polygenic inheritance
Multiple gene loci each contribute to a single trait; produces bell-curve continuous variation (e.g., human height, skin color).
Sex-linked (X-linked) traits
Alleles on the X chromosome; recessive traits expressed more often in males (XY); classic examples: color blindness, hemophilia A.
Chromosome theory of heredity
Proposed by Boveri and Sutton (~1902): chromosomes carry Mendelian factors; segregation = meiosis I separation.
Morgan's Drosophila linkage
Thomas Hunt Morgan (Nobel 1933) — white-eyed male fly; showed genes on same chromosome do not assort independently (gene linkage).
Genetic linkage maps (Sturtevant)
Alfred Sturtevant, 1913 — first chromosome map using recombination frequency; 1 centimorgan = 1% recombination.
Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium
Population allele frequencies stay constant absent evolution; equations: p² + 2pq + q² = 1, p + q = 1.
ABO blood group system
Three alleles (IA, IB, i) at single locus; codominance + recessiveness; used to illustrate multiple alleles and blood compatibility.
Lethal alleles
Homozygous dominant lethal → 2:1 ratio instead of 3:1; classic example: yellow coat color in mice (agouti locus).
Incomplete penetrance / variable expressivity
Genotype does not always produce expected phenotype (penetrance) or produces it to varying degree (expressivity); explains discordant twins.
Chi-square test in genetics
Statistical test used to determine if observed offspring ratios fit expected Mendelian ratios; χ² = Σ(O−E)²/E.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key ratios

  • 3:1 — monohybrid F2 phenotypic ratio
  • 1:2:1 — monohybrid F2 genotypic ratio (AA:Aa:aa)
  • 9:3:3:1 — dihybrid F2 phenotypic ratio
  • 1:2:1 — incomplete dominance F2 phenotypic ratio
  • 1:1 — test cross outcome for Aa parent
  • 2:1 — Aa × Aa with homozygous dominant lethal

Named people

  • Gregor Mendel — Augustinian friar, Brünn (Brno, Czech Republic)
  • R. C. Punnett — Punnett square; co-founded Journal of Genetics
  • William Bateson — coined "genetics" (1905); introduced "allele," "homozygous," "heterozygous"
  • Thomas Hunt Morgan — Drosophila linkage; Nobel Prize 1933
  • Alfred Sturtevant — first genetic linkage map (1913)
  • Boveri & Sutton — chromosome theory of heredity (~1902)

Terminology

  • Locus — chromosomal location of a gene
  • Wild type — standard phenotype in a natural population
  • Carrier — heterozygous for a recessive allele; does not express it
  • Backcross — crossing offspring back to a parent genotype
  • Reciprocal cross — reversing the sexes in a cross to test maternal effects
  • Centimorgan (cM) — map unit; 1% recombination frequency

Classic model organisms

  • Pisum sativum — garden pea; Mendel's organism
  • Drosophila melanogaster — fruit fly; Morgan's organism (4 chromosomes, fast generation)
  • Antirrhinum majus — snapdragon; incomplete dominance (pink flowers)
  • Mus musculus — mouse; yellow coat / lethal allele studies
  • Neurospora crassa — bread mold; Beadle & Tatum one-gene one-enzyme

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Incomplete dominance vs. codominanceIncomplete dominance: heterozygote shows intermediate phenotype (pink flower — neither parent's color). Codominance: both parental phenotypes expressed simultaneously (AB blood type shows both A and B antigens, not a "blend").
Epistasis vs. pleiotropyEpistasis: one gene masks another gene's expression (gene-gene interaction). Pleiotropy: one gene has multiple phenotypic effects (e.g., sickle-cell allele affects red cell shape, oxygen transport, and organ damage).
Genotype vs. phenotype ratioMonohybrid Aa × Aa: genotype 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa (1:2:1), but phenotype 3:1 (three dominant : one recessive) — they differ because AA and Aa look the same when dominant is complete.
Linked genes vs. independent assortmentGenes on different chromosomes (or far apart) → 9:3:3:1 in F2. Genes linked (same chromosome, close) → offspring ratios deviate; parental combinations far more frequent than recombinant ones.
Sex-linked vs. autosomal recessiveX-linked recessive: males (XY) only need one copy to express; females need two → affected males >> females; fathers cannot pass to sons (give X to daughters only). Autosomal recessive: equal sex ratio among affected; carrier parents show no phenotype.
Mendel's laws vs. Hardy-WeinbergMendel's laws describe individual crosses (family-level). Hardy-Weinberg describes allele frequencies in an entire population at equilibrium (no selection, mutation, drift, migration, non-random mating).
Mitosis vs. meiosis (as it relates to Mendel)Segregation = meiosis I (homologs separate). Independent assortment = meiosis I orientation of bivalents is random. Mitosis does NOT produce genetic diversity — it makes identical copies; confusing the two is a classic trap.

🔬 Big-picture cross-discipline links Mendelian genetics is the conceptual foundation for all of modern biology: the chromosome theory (Boveri-Sutton) anchors it to cell biology; Hardy-Weinberg extends it to evolution; DNA structure (Watson-Crick 1953) explains the molecular basis of alleles; linkage mapping prefigures the entire Human Genome Project. In quizbowl, a tossup mentioning garden peas, 3:1 ratios, Augustinian monks, or Brünn almost certainly answers Gregor Mendel or Mendelian genetics; one that mentions Drosophila white-eye, centimorgan, or Journal of Genetics pivots toward Morgan / Punnett / Bateson.
