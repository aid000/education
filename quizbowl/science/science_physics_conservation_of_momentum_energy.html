Quick Overview

Core ideaCertain physical quantities remain constant when no external influence acts
Two lawsConservation of momentum + conservation of energy (both in classical and relativistic mechanics)
Deeper foundationNoether's theorem — every continuous symmetry yields a conserved quantity
Key namesNewton, Leibniz, Huygens, Helmholtz, Emmy Noether, Lorentz, Einstein

Governing equations

Conservation of linear momentum (isolated system) p_total = Σ m_i v_i = constant Equivalently: ΔP = 0 when F_net = 0
Conservation of energy (isolated system) E_total = KE + PE = constant (no non-conservative forces) KE = ½mv²; PE (gravity) = mgh; PE (spring) = ½kx²
Relativistic energy-momentum relation (Einstein) E² = (pc)² + (m₀c²)² For photons (m=0): E = pc; For rest: E = m₀c²

Noether's theorem — the deep reason

Emmy Noether proved (1915, published 1918) that every differentiable symmetry of the action of a physical system corresponds to a conserved quantity:
  • Time-translation symmetry → conservation of energy
  • Space-translation symmetry → conservation of linear momentum
  • Rotational symmetry → conservation of angular momentum
Frequently asked as: "This theorem by a German mathematician links symmetries to conserved quantities."

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Elastic vs. inelastic collisions — the most common applied context for both laws together.
  • "Vis viva" controversy — Descartes (mv) vs. Leibniz (mv²) — recurrent historical clue.
  • The phrase "conserved in all inertial reference frames" triggers momentum.
  • Impulse-momentum theorem — J = Δp — links force×time to momentum change.

High-Yield Statements & Applications

Principal forms and applications most likely to anchor a tossup or bonus. power = hard early clue. recog = gettable recognition clue.

Elastic Collisions
Both momentum AND kinetic energy conserved
powerCoefficient of restitution = 1; relative speed of approach equals relative speed of separation. For two equal masses, velocities exchange (head-on: m₁ stops, m₂ moves at m₁'s initial speed).
recogBoth p_total and KE_total are the same before and after — use two simultaneous equations to solve.
recogCommon examples: billiard balls, atomic/subatomic particle scattering (neutron–proton), ideal gas molecular collisions.
Inelastic & Perfectly Inelastic Collisions
Momentum conserved; kinetic energy NOT conserved
powerIn a perfectly inelastic collision objects stick together; KE lost is maximum possible. Formula: v_f = (m₁v₁ + m₂v₂)/(m₁+m₂).
recogKE is converted to heat, sound, or deformation — energy is still conserved overall but not as kinetic energy.
recogClassic example: ballistic pendulum — bullet embeds in block, momentum gives velocity, then energy gives height.
Rocket Propulsion / Tsiolkovsky Rocket Equation
Momentum conservation in variable-mass systems
powerTsiolkovsky rocket equation: Δv = v_e · ln(m_i / m_f), where v_e is exhaust speed. Derived purely from conservation of momentum.
recogA rocket accelerates by ejecting mass; no external force needed — momentum of rocket+exhaust system is conserved.
recogNamed for Russian scientist Konstantin Tsiolkovsky; foundational to spaceflight.
Conservation of Angular Momentum
L = Iω = r × p = constant (no external torque)
powerFollows from rotational symmetry via Noether's theorem. For a point mass: L = mvr sin θ. Kepler's second law ("equal areas in equal times") is a direct consequence.
recogFigure skater pulling arms in → smaller I → larger ω (spins faster); total L unchanged.
recogGyroscopes, planetary orbits, collapsing stellar cores (neutron star spin-up) all invoke this law.
Work-Energy Theorem
W_net = ΔKE
powerStates that the net work done on an object equals its change in kinetic energy, independent of path. Derived from Newton's second law; does NOT require a conservative force.
recogDistinct from conservation of energy: WET applies to single objects; energy conservation applies to isolated systems.
recogUsed to find speed after work against friction, gravity, spring forces, etc.
Mass-Energy Equivalence (E = mc²)
Energy and mass are interconvertible — Einstein, 1905
powerDerived in Einstein's "Does the Inertia of a Body Depend upon Its Energy Content?" (1905, Annalen der Physik). Mass is a form of rest energy; total relativistic energy is E = γm₀c².
recogNuclear fission and fusion: small mass defect (binding energy) releases enormous energy per E = mc².
recog4-momentum (E/c, p) is the relativistic generalization; its magnitude m₀c is a Lorentz invariant.
The "Vis Viva" Controversy
Descartes vs. Leibniz — what is conserved?
powerDescartes claimed mv (momentum) is the conserved "quantity of motion." Leibniz countered that mv² (vis viva, "living force") is conserved — the ancestor of kinetic energy. The dispute lasted decades.
recogÉmilie du Châtelet (1706–1749) resolved it experimentally, showing both can be conserved depending on the system; she also translated Newton's Principia into French.
recogThe modern answer: both are conserved — in different contexts (momentum for all collisions; KE only for elastic).

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results that appear as hard early tossup clues — one-line ID each.

Noether's Theorem (1918)
Emmy Noether; every symmetry of the action yields a conserved quantity.
Impulse-Momentum Theorem
J = FΔt = Δp; area under force-time graph equals momentum change.
Center-of-Mass Frame
Reference frame where total momentum is zero; simplifies collision analysis.
Coefficient of Restitution (e)
e = relative speed after / relative speed before; e=1 elastic, e=0 perfectly inelastic.
Ballistic Pendulum
Uses momentum conservation then energy conservation in two separate steps.
Virial Theorem
Time-averaged KE = −½ time-averaged potential energy; applies to gravitationally bound systems and ideal gases.
Hamiltonian Mechanics
Reformulates mechanics via total energy H = T + V; conservation laws emerge from Hamilton's equations.
Lagrangian / Principle of Least Action
L = T − V; equations of motion minimize action S = ∫L dt; basis for Noether's theorem.
Poynting's Theorem
Energy conservation for electromagnetic fields; Poynting vector S = E × H gives EM energy flux.
Compton Scattering
X-ray photon–electron collision; both momentum and energy conserved relativistically; proves photon has momentum p = h/λ.
Pair Production / Annihilation
E = mc² in action: photon → electron + positron (production); e⁺e⁻ → 2γ (annihilation); energy and momentum both conserved.
Nuclear Binding Energy / Mass Defect
Nucleus lighter than constituent nucleons; difference released as energy per E = mc²; explains fission and fusion yields.
Neutrino Discovery (Pauli / Beta Decay)
Missing energy in beta decay led Pauli to postulate the neutrino in 1930; conservation of energy demanded it.
Helmholtz / First Law of Thermodynamics
Hermann von Helmholtz (1847) formalized energy conservation as a universal law: ΔU = Q − W.

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Elastic vs. Inelastic collisionElastic: both p and KE conserved, objects bounce, coefficient of restitution = 1. Inelastic: only p conserved; KE lost. Perfectly inelastic: objects stick; maximum KE loss.
Momentum vs. Kinetic EnergyMomentum p = mv is a vector; KE = ½mv² is a scalar. Doubling speed doubles momentum but quadruples KE. In a collision, momentum is always conserved; KE is conserved only if elastic.
Conservation of Energy vs. Work-Energy TheoremWET (W_net = ΔKE) applies to one object, requires knowing net work, does NOT require conservative forces. Energy conservation applies to isolated systems and requires no non-conservative forces doing net work.
Angular momentum vs. Linear momentumL = Iω (or r × p) depends on the axis of rotation; conserved when net torque = 0. Linear momentum p = mv conserved when net force = 0. Both can be conserved simultaneously.
Noether's theorem (Noether) vs. Newton's lawsNewton's laws give equations of motion. Noether's theorem gives the deeper reason conservation laws exist — via symmetry. Noether was a mathematician, not a physicist; her theorem is in the context of the Lagrangian / action formalism.
First Law of Thermodynamics vs. Conservation of Mechanical Energy1st law (Helmholtz/Clausius): ΔU = Q − W — includes heat and internal energy. Conservation of mechanical energy: KE + PE = const — only for conservative forces, no heat. The 1st law is the more general statement.
Impulse vs. MomentumImpulse J = FΔt = Δp is the change in momentum (a transfer), not the momentum itself. A large force for a short time can equal a small force for a long time.
Rest energy (E = mc²) vs. Total energy (E = γmc²)E = mc² is rest energy only (v = 0). Moving object has total energy E = γm₀c² which includes kinetic energy. The invariant relation is E² = (pc)² + (m₀c²)².

Cross-Discipline Big Picture Conservation laws underpin every branch of physics and extend far beyond mechanics:
  • Thermodynamics: 1st law is energy conservation; entropy (2nd law) is NOT a conservation law — a key distinction.
  • Electromagnetism: Poynting's theorem is EM energy conservation; photons carry momentum (Compton, radiation pressure).
  • Particle physics: every Feynman diagram enforces 4-momentum conservation at each vertex; new particles (neutrinos, positrons) were predicted by requiring conservation.
  • General relativity: in curved spacetime, global energy conservation is subtle (Noether's theorem still holds locally); dark energy complicates cosmological energy accounting.
  • Mathematics link: Noether's theorem lives in the calculus of variations / symplectic geometry — a bridge from physics to pure math tossups about Noether or Lagrangians.
