Quick Overview

Lavoisier & wife Marie-Anne
(Jacques-Louis David, 1788)
NationalityFrench; born Paris, 1743 — guillotined 1794
Title"Father of modern chemistry"
Key roleTax farmer (fermier général); member Académie des Sciences
ExecutedGuillotined 8 May 1794 during the Reign of Terror
CollaboratorWife Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier — translated, drew apparatus, assisted experiments
InstrumentCalorimeter (ice-calorimeter, built with Laplace)

Defining contributions

  • Overturned the phlogiston theory — replaced it with oxygen-based combustion.
  • Established the law of conservation of mass: matter is neither created nor destroyed.
  • Named the elements oxygen (oxygène) and hydrogen (hydrogène), 1787.
  • Co-authored the Méthode de nomenclature chimique (1787) — systematic chemical naming.
  • Wrote Traité élémentaire de chimie (1789), the first modern chemistry textbook.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Father of modern chemistry" — the single most common descriptor.
  • Refuted phlogiston; discovered / named oxygen and hydrogen.
  • Executed by guillotine; Judge Coffinhal reportedly said "The Republic has no need of scientists."

High-Yield Discoveries & Works

The 6 topics most likely to anchor a tossup. power = early/hard clue. recog = recognition clue.

Discovery and Naming of Oxygen
1774–1778 · concurrent with Priestley and Scheele
recogNamed the gas oxygène (Greek: "acid-former") — argued it was essential to combustion and respiration.
recogDemonstrated oxygen is the component of air consumed during burning, not a hypothetical "phlogiston."
powerJoseph Priestley visited Lavoisier in October 1774 and described his "dephlogisticated air"; Carl Wilhelm Scheele discovered oxygen independently but published later. Lavoisier received credit for recognizing and naming the substance — a priority dispute central to many tossups.

Refutation of Phlogiston Theory
1770s–1780s
recogPhlogiston — the supposed substance released during burning — was proposed by Georg Ernst Stahl; Lavoisier demolished it with careful mass measurements.
recogShowed metals gain mass when calcined (oxidized), contradicting phlogiston's requirement that mass be lost.
powerUsed a sealed glass vessel to demonstrate that the total mass of mercury + air = mass of mercury calx + residual air, prefiguring the conservation of mass. The adversaries here are Stahl and Johann Joachim Becher (phlogiston's originators) — named-adversary clues appear early in tossups.
wiki ↗
Law of Conservation of Mass
~1774; stated explicitly in Traité, 1789
recog"Nothing is created, nothing is destroyed, everything is transformed." — paraphrase of his principle.
recogIn any chemical reaction the total mass of reactants = total mass of products.
powerLavoisier used an extremely precise balance scale — weighing before and after reactions — as the central experimental tool. His balances were accurate to ~0.0005 g, far beyond contemporaries. Tossups often open on the precision-balance detail.
mass(reactants) = mass(products)
Traité élémentaire de chimie
1789 (published just before the Revolution)
recogFirst modern chemistry textbook; rejected ancient four-element theory (earth, water, fire, air).
recogListed 33 chemical elements — the first operational definition of "element" as a substance that cannot be further broken down.
powerMarie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier engraved the 13 plates illustrating the apparatus; the book was illustrated by Marie-Anne and translated by Robert Kerr into English (1790). The list included some errors (light and caloric listed as elements), but established the operational definition that drove 19th-century chemistry.
wiki ↗
Méthode de nomenclature chimique
1787 · with Guyton de Morveau, Berthollet, Fourcroy
recogReplaced alchemical names (e.g. "oil of vitriol," "flowers of zinc") with systematic names — e.g. sulfuric acid, zinc oxide.
recogThe system is the direct ancestor of modern IUPAC nomenclature.
powerNamed hydrogen (hydrogène, "water-former") in 1787 based on its role in forming water. Co-authors Guyton de Morveau, Berthollet, and Fourcroy — named-collaborator clues appear early.
wiki ↗
Ice Calorimeter & Animal Respiration
1780–1783 · with Pierre-Simon Laplace
recogBuilt the ice calorimeter with Laplace — measured heat released by chemical reactions and by a guinea pig's respiration.
recogConcluded that animal respiration is a form of slow combustion — combining oxygen with carbon to produce CO₂ and heat.
powerThe guinea-pig experiment: placed animal inside ice calorimeter and measured the melt-water produced by its body heat, then compared to burning charcoal producing the same CO₂ amount — first quantitative link between metabolism and combustion. Lavoisier coined the term "caloric" (heat substance) though he later abandoned it.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known results and roles that show up as early / power-mark clues. One-line ID each.

Diamond combustion experiment 1772 Focused sunlight burned a diamond; showed it is pure carbon (same combustion product as charcoal).
Synthesis of water 1783 Burned hydrogen in oxygen; proved water is H₂O, not a basic element.
Role in Ferme Générale 1768–1791 Tax-farming partnership that funded his research — and ultimately led to his execution.
Paris gunpowder reform 1775–1792 Appointed director of the Régie des Poudres; dramatically improved French gunpowder quality and supply.
Caloric theory 1783 Proposed heat as a weightless fluid ("caloric"); later disproved by Rumford's cannon-boring experiment.
Metric system commission 1790–1793 Member of the commission that established the metric system; also involved in land surveys.
Sulfur / phosphorus combustion 1772 Showed sulfur and phosphorus gain mass on combustion — early evidence against phlogiston.
Académie des Sciences secretary 1785 Elected director (1785) and treasurer; held powerful institutional position in French science.
Nitrogen / azote naming 1787 Named the non-reactive component of air azote ("without life"); modern French still uses azote for nitrogen.
Opium / drug analysis 1790 Early quantitative organic analysis; determined elemental composition of organic compounds.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & concepts

  • Phlogiston — disproved by Lavoisier
  • Caloric — Lavoisier's heat-fluid theory (later abandoned)
  • Oxygène / hydrogène / azote — his naming
  • Conservation of mass — his central law
  • Element — operational definition (simplest substance)
  • Calorimeter — instrument for measuring heat

Collaborators & rivals

  • Marie-Anne Paulze Lavoisier — wife, translator, illustrator, lab partner
  • Pierre-Simon Laplace — ice-calorimeter co-builder
  • Joseph Priestley — discovered "dephlogisticated air" (O₂) but kept phlogiston
  • Carl Wilhelm Scheele — independently discovered oxygen
  • Georg Ernst Stahl — phlogiston theory author (adversary)
  • Guyton de Morveau, Berthollet, Fourcroy — nomenclature co-authors

Key works & dates

  • Méthode de nomenclature chimique — 1787
  • Traité élémentaire de chimie — 1789
  • Ice calorimeter paper (with Laplace) — 1783
  • Memoir on the nature of water — 1783
  • Opuscules physiques et chymiques — 1774

Biography & death

  • Born 26 Aug 1743, Paris
  • Guillotined 8 May 1794 — executed as fermier général
  • Judge Coffinhal: "The Republic has no need of scientists"
  • Lagrange: "It took only a moment to cut off his head, but a hundred years may not produce another like it."
  • Posthumously exonerated 1795 (18 months after death)

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Joseph PriestleyPriestley discovered oxygen ("dephlogisticated air") in 1774 but kept believing phlogiston theory. Lavoisier took Priestley's discovery and correctly interpreted it. Priestley = discovery, Lavoisier = interpretation and naming.
Carl Wilhelm ScheeleSwedish apothecary who discovered oxygen and many other elements (chlorine, manganese, barium). Scheele found O₂ slightly before Priestley but published late. Tossups distinguish: "Swedish" or "apothecary" = Scheele; "French" = Lavoisier.
Georg Ernst StahlGerman chemist who systematized phlogiston theory — he's the adversary, not the hero. If the question mentions "phlogiston as a positive achievement," it's Stahl. If it mentions phlogiston being overthrown, it's Lavoisier.
Henry CavendishEnglish chemist who first isolated hydrogen ("inflammable air") and showed water is formed when it burns. Lavoisier named hydrogen; Cavendish isolated and described it. "Weighing the Earth" tossup = Cavendish (Cavendish experiment, 1798).
Count Rumford (Benjamin Thompson)Disproved Lavoisier's caloric theory with cannon-boring experiments (~1798). Rumford = heat as motion, not fluid. "Cannon boring / caloric disproved" → Rumford; "caloric named/proposed" → Lavoisier.
Antoine Béchamp / other 18th-c. French chemistsLavoisier is uniquely identified by: fermier général + guillotine + "father of modern chemistry" + oxygen naming. No other French chemist of the era has all four markers.

⚛️ Physics / math through-line Lavoisier's work is a landmark in the mathematization of chemistry: his conservation of mass law is the direct precursor to energy-mass conservation in physics; the ice calorimeter bridges thermodynamics and chemistry (prefiguring the first law of thermodynamics); and his quantitative balance-based method turned chemistry from a qualitative to a quantitative science — the same rigor that defined Newtonian mechanics. On a tossup about thermodynamics history or conservation laws, Lavoisier is a frequent wrong-answer trap for answers like Rumford, Joule, or Carnot — knowing where each figure fits on the conservation-of-energy timeline prevents those misbuzzes.
