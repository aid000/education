Quick Overview

Two of the most foundational results in elementary mathematics — each appears as a tossup answer, a bonus part, or embedded as a clue in science/physics questions. Both have ancient roots and dozens of proofs.

DomainEuclidean geometry (Pythagorean) · algebra / polynomial roots (Quadratic)
Ancient originPythagorean: Babylonian/Greek, ~6th c. BCE; Quadratic: Babylonian/Indian, ~2000 BCE–600 CE
Quizbowl frequency⭐⭐⭐ Core — appear directly and as embedded clues in physics, history of math tossups
Named forPythagoras of Samos (Pythagorean); "quadratic" from Latin quadratus = square

Governing formulas

a² + b² = c² Pythagorean Theorem — for a right triangle with legs a, b and hypotenuse c
x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / 2a Quadratic Formula — roots of ax² + bx + c = 0, where a ≠ 0

Key definitions

  • Hypotenuse — side opposite the right angle; always the longest side; equals c in the theorem.
  • Pythagorean triple — integer triple satisfying the theorem: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (7,24,25).
  • Discriminant — the expression b²−4ac inside the radical of the quadratic formula; determines number/type of roots.
  • Completing the square — algebraic technique used to derive the quadratic formula from ax²+bx+c=0.

High-Yield Statements & Applications

The principal facts, corollaries, and applications most likely to be quizzed. power = hard early clue. recog = common mid/late clue.

Pythagorean Theorem
a² + b² = c² · right triangles · Euclidean geometry
power Over 370 distinct proofs documented — by Euclid (Book I, Prop. 47), by James Garfield (20th U.S. President, 1876, using a trapezoid), and by a 12-year-old Einstein (apocryphal but commonly cited).
recogEuclid's proof in Elements Book I, Proposition 47 — constructs squares on each side; area of two leg-squares equals area of hypotenuse-square.
recogConverse is also true: if a²+b²=c² then the triangle is right-angled — used in Euclid I.48.
recogFails in non-Euclidean geometry: on a sphere or hyperbolic plane the law is modified (spherical/hyperbolic law of cosines).
recogGeneralizes to any number of dimensions: distance formula d = √(x₁²+x₂²+…+xₙ²).
recogThe 3-4-5 triangle is the canonical Pythagorean triple; rope-stretchers in ancient Egypt used it to construct right angles.
Quadratic Formula
x = (−b ± √(b²−4ac)) / 2a · roots of ax²+bx+c = 0
power Derived by completing the square — add (b/2a)² to both sides to make the left side a perfect square; result is (x + b/2a)² = (b²−4ac)/(4a²), then take the square root.
recogThe discriminant Δ = b²−4ac: if Δ > 0 → two distinct real roots; Δ = 0 → one repeated real root; Δ < 0 → two complex conjugate roots.
recogVieta's formulas: sum of roots = −b/a; product of roots = c/a — useful shortcut, do not require the full formula.
recogEarliest general solution attributed to Brahmagupta (~628 CE) and earlier Babylonian geometric methods (~1800 BCE).
recogAl-Khwarizmi's Kitab al-mukhtasar fi hisab al-jabr wal-muqabala (~820 CE) — the book that gave us the word "algebra" — gave geometric solutions to quadratics.
recogThe vertex of the parabola y = ax²+bx+c lies at x = −b/2a — the axis of symmetry, which is the average of the two roots.
Answer-web note: These results anchor questions about Euclid's Elements, Pythagoras of Samos, Brahmagupta, Al-Khwarizmi, and François Viète. A clue ending "this theorem, also generalized by the law of cosines" points here.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results and extensions that appear as early/hard tossup clues or bonus parts. One-line ID each.

Result / Term One-Line ID
Law of cosinesGeneralization: c² = a²+b²−2ab·cos(C); reduces to Pythagorean theorem when C = 90°.
Pythagorean triple generationEuclid's formula: (m²−n², 2mn, m²+n²) for integers m > n > 0 generates all primitive triples.
Fermat's Last TheoremNo integer solution to aⁿ+bⁿ=cⁿ for n > 2; directly extends the Pythagorean form; proved by Andrew Wiles (1995).
Garfield's proof (1876)Rep. James Garfield used a trapezoid area argument; published in New England Journal of Education.
Bhaskara's proofIndian mathematician Bhaskara II (~12th c.) gave an elegant dissection proof with the word "Behold!"
Distance formulad = √((x₂−x₁)²+(y₂−y₁)²) — direct Cartesian application of the Pythagorean theorem.
Hypotenuse of unit right isosceles triangleLegs = 1, hypotenuse = √2; proof that √2 is irrational is attributed to the Pythagoreans (Hippasus).
Completing the square — geometricAl-Khwarizmi's original method: literally completing a square diagram to solve x²+10x = 39.
Discriminant sign rulesb²−4ac determines real vs. complex roots; if negative, roots are complex conjugates a ± bi.
Vieta's formulasSum of roots = −b/a; product = c/a; named for François Viète (16th c.); generalize to higher-degree polynomials.
Quadratic and conic sectionsThe parabola y = ax²+bx+c is the graph of a quadratic; arises as a conic section (intersection of cone and plane parallel to slant).
Cardano's cubic formulaAnalogue for cubics; Gerolamo Cardano published in Ars Magna (1545); more complex analog of quadratic formula.
Quadratic formula in modular arithmeticIn finite fields, the formula still applies when 2a has an inverse mod p; relevant in number theory/cryptography tossups.
Sridharacharya's ruleEarly Indian quadratic formula (c. 1025 CE) credited to Sridhara; an alternate historical attribution often clued.

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Law of cosinesc²=a²+b²−2ab·cos(C) — works for ANY triangle; the Pythagorean theorem is the special case cos(90°)=0. Clue says "any triangle" or "non-right" → law of cosines.
Law of sinesRelates a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C — no squares, no addition; used when you have an angle-side pair. Pythagorean → squares of sides; law of sines → ratios.
Distance formulaThe distance formula is the Pythagorean theorem in Cartesian coordinates — but quizbowl clues about "squaring differences of coordinates" may be angling for "distance formula" as the answer rather than "Pythagorean theorem."
Completing the square (as an answer)"Technique for deriving the quadratic formula" → the method answer is "completing the square"; the formula answer is "the quadratic formula." Read whether they want the method or the result.
Vieta's formulasGive sum/product of roots without solving — the discriminant and ± are absent. Clue "sum of roots equals −b/a" → Vieta's, not the quadratic formula.
Fermat's Last TheoremLooks like a²+b²=c² but with exponent n>2 and no solutions — the answer is Fermat's Last Theorem, not the Pythagorean theorem. Clue "no integer solutions," "Andrew Wiles," "Wiles proved in 1995" → Fermat's.
Quadratic vs. cubic formulaQuadratic formula solves degree-2 polynomials; cubic formula (Cardano, Ars Magna 1545) solves degree-3. If "Cardano" or "Tartaglia" or "Ars Magna" appear → cubic/quartic, not quadratic.
Pythagorean theorem vs. Pythagorean theorem's converseTheorem: right angle → a²+b²=c²; Converse (I.48): a²+b²=c² → right angle. Both are Euclid results — clue about "verifying a right angle exists" → converse.

⚛️ Cross-discipline big picture Both results are structural pillars across quizbowl categories: the Pythagorean theorem underlies the metric tensor in special relativity (Minkowski spacetime uses a "spacetime interval" ds² = c²dt²−dx²−dy²−dz²), vector magnitudes in physics, and the definition of a Euclidean norm in linear algebra. The quadratic formula appears in projectile motion (solving for time when y=0 in kinematics), in particle physics energy-momentum relations (E²=(pc)²+(mc²)²), in the orbital mechanics of conic sections, and as the foundation for the quadratic sieve in number theory / cryptography. Knowing which branch of mathematics they belong to ("Euclidean geometry" vs. "algebra / polynomial roots") is itself a frequent early clue.
