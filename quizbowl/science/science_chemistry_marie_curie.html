Quick Overview

Full nameMaria Sklodowska-Curie (born Warsaw, 1867; died Sancellemoz, 1934)
NationalityPolish-French
FieldsPhysics & Chemistry
Nobel PrizesTwo — Physics 1903 (with Pierre & Becquerel); Chemistry 1911 (solo)
Key term (her own)"Radioactivity" — coined by Curie
Lab / institutionÉcole Normale Supérieure / Sorbonne; later Institut Curie, Paris

Defining firsts & records

  • First woman to win a Nobel Prize (1903).
  • First person — and still only woman — to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
  • First female professor at the Sorbonne (1906, after Pierre's death).
  • Discovered two elements: polonium (named for her homeland Poland) and radium.
  • Coined the term "radioactivity" to describe spontaneous emission from uranium and thorium.

Classic Quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "She and her husband Pierre discovered polonium and radium in 1898."
  • "The only person to win Nobel Prizes in two different scientific fields."

Coining "Radioactivity" & Uranium Investigations (1897–1898)
1897–1898 · ESPCI / Sorbonne, Paris
powerUsed a piezoelectric quartz electrometer (built by Pierre) to precisely measure the ionizing current produced by uranium rays — proving the emission is an atomic property, not a chemical reaction.
recogFound that thorium also emits the same rays — generalizing Becquerel's 1896 uranium observation.
recogCoined the word "radioactivité" (radioactivity) in her 1898 papers.

Discovery of Polonium (Po, element 84)
July 1898 · Paris
powerIsolated from pitchblende (uraninite ore) after noting pitchblende was more radioactive than pure uranium — implying an unknown element; processed hundreds of kilograms of ore to extract a trace.
recogNamed polonium in honor of her occupied homeland Poland — the first element named for a political cause.
recogAnnounced jointly with Pierre on 18 July 1898 in Comptes Rendus.
wiki ↗
Discovery of Radium (Ra, element 88)
December 1898 · Paris
powerSecond unknown element found in pitchblende; announced 26 December 1898. Required processing ~1 tonne of pitchblende to yield ~0.1 g of radium chloride — accomplished in a leaky shed (former dissection room at ESPCI).
recogRadium (Latin radius, ray) — the ore glows blue-green in the dark from its own radiation.
recogMarie isolated pure radium metal in 1910 (solo work), confirming its atomic weight — key to the 1911 Chemistry Nobel.

Nobel Prize in Physics, 1903
Awarded 10 December 1903 · Stockholm
powerInitially the Nobel Committee planned to award only Pierre and Becquerel; Pierre insisted Marie be included. She became the first woman Nobel laureate.
recogShared with Pierre Curie and Henri Becquerel for research on spontaneous radiation.
recogThe Curies were too ill (from radiation effects) to attend; Pierre gave the lecture in 1905.

Nobel Prize in Chemistry, 1911
Awarded 10 December 1911 · Stockholm
powerAwarded for the isolation of pure radium metal and the study of its properties — despite a French press scandal (affair with physicist Paul Langevin) attempting to block her attendance. She attended anyway.
recogMade her the first person ever to win Nobel Prizes in two different sciences.
recogHer Nobel lecture explicitly established radium and polonium as new chemical elements, not compounds.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known contributions that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

Petites Curies (mobile X-ray units)
1914–1918
Curie developed 18 mobile radiology vehicles ("petites Curies") to bring X-ray diagnosis to WWI front lines; personally drove one.
Doctoral thesis (1903)
1903, Sorbonne
First woman in France to earn a physics doctorate; thesis "Recherches sur les substances radioactives" — considered among the greatest doctoral theses in science history.
Atomic weight determination of radium
1907
Precisely established radium's atomic weight as ~226 g/mol, confirming its position in Mendeleev's periodic table.
First woman on Sorbonne faculty
1906
Appointed to Pierre's professorship after his death (struck by horse-drawn wagon, 1906) — first female professor at the Sorbonne.
Institut Curie, Paris
Founded 1920
Co-founded with the Pasteur Institute as a radioactivity research center; still a leading cancer research hospital today.
International Radium Standard (1911)
1911
Curie prepared the international standard sample of radium used to calibrate all other measurements worldwide.
Isolation of radioactive isotopes for medicine
1920s
Supplied radium samples to hospitals for cancer treatment — early foundation of radiation therapy (radiotherapy).
Solvay Conferences participation
1911, 1913, 1921
One of only two women (with Hertha Ayrton) invited to the 1911 Solvay Conference alongside Einstein, Planck, Lorentz, Rutherford.
Death from aplastic anemia
4 July 1934
Died of aplastic anemia almost certainly caused by decades of radiation exposure; her papers and cookbooks remain radioactive today.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & units

  • Radioactivity — her coined term
  • Curie (Ci) — unit of radioactivity (3.7 × 10¹⁰ decays/sec), named after both Curies
  • Pitchblende / uraninite — the ore she processed
  • Polonium (Po, 84) and Radium (Ra, 88) — her two elements
  • Aplastic anemia — her cause of death

People & collaborators

  • Pierre Curie — husband, co-discoverer; killed 1906
  • Henri Becquerel — discovered uranium rays 1896; shared 1903 Nobel
  • Irène Joliot-Curie — daughter; won 1935 Nobel Chemistry with Frédéric Joliot-Curie
  • Paul Langevin — physicist, affair rumored; caused 1911 scandal
  • Władysław Skłodowski — father; physics teacher in Warsaw

Places & institutions

  • Born Warsaw (then Russian Empire), 1867
  • Emigrated to Paris, 1891
  • Sorbonne (University of Paris) — doctoral degree & professorship
  • ESPCI — lab where polonium & radium isolated in a shed
  • Institut Curie, Paris — her cancer-research legacy
  • Buried in the Panthéon, Paris (1995 — first woman interred by merit)

Instruments & methods

  • Piezoelectric electrometer — Pierre's invention; Marie used it to measure ionizing current
  • Crystallography fractionation — purification technique to isolate radium
  • X-ray fluoroscopy — deployed in WWI mobile units
  • Spectroscopy — used to confirm new elements via characteristic emission lines

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Henri BecquerelBecquerel discovered uranium rays in 1896 (accidentally, via photographic plates). Curie built on his work to coin "radioactivity" and isolate two elements. Both are in the 1903 Nobel.
Ernest RutherfordRutherford named the alpha and beta rays (1899) and proposed the nuclear model (1911). He worked on radioactivity but did not discover polonium or radium. Key: Rutherford → nucleus; Curie → new elements.
Irène Joliot-CurieMarie's daughter; won 1935 Nobel Chemistry with husband Frédéric for artificial radioactivity (not natural radioactivity). Questions about "artificial radioactivity" → Irène; "radioactivity" coined / polonium / radium → Marie.
Lise MeitnerMeitner co-discovered nuclear fission (1938, with Hahn & Strassmann) and element meitnerium is named for her. Both are pioneering women in nuclear physics — but fission questions → Meitner; radioactivity/polonium/radium → Curie.
Otto HahnHahn won the 1944 Nobel Chemistry for fission. He and Meitner collaborated; Curie has no fission connection.
Wilhelm RöntgenRöntgen discovered X-rays in 1895 (won 1901 Nobel Physics). Curie used X-ray technology but did not discover it. Clue "discovered a penetrating radiation in 1895" → Röntgen, not Curie.

⚛️ Physics / chemistry through-line Curie's work sits at the crossroads of atomic theory and nuclear physics. By proving radioactivity is an intrinsic atomic property (not a chemical reaction between molecules), she laid the empirical groundwork for Rutherford's nuclear model and Bohr's atom. The unit curie (Ci) appears in nuclear physics, medical imaging, and radiotherapy. Her isolation of radium and polonium anchored the lower rows of the periodic table. Tossup tip: questions about the discovery of radioactivity often cross-link to Becquerel (precursor) and Rutherford (alpha/beta naming), while questions about the first Nobel woman or two-science Nobel are straight Curie giveaways.
