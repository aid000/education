Quick Overview

Core definitionDifferential reproductive success due to heritable variation; allele frequencies shift across generations
Proposed byCharles Darwin & Alfred Russel Wallace, 1858 (Linnean Society); Darwin's On the Origin of Species 1859
Key synthesisModern Evolutionary Synthesis (~1930s–1940s): Darwinian selection + Mendelian genetics
Mathematical engineHardy–Weinberg equilibrium (null model); population genetics
Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (null / no-evolution baseline) p² + 2pq + q² = 1     p + q = 1
p = freq. dominant allele; q = freq. recessive allele; 2pq = heterozygote frequency

Darwin's four postulates (the logical core)

  1. Variation — individuals in a population differ in heritable traits.
  2. Heritability — offspring resemble parents (traits are inherited).
  3. Differential fitness — some variants survive/reproduce better.
  4. Selection — beneficial variants increase in frequency over generations.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Survival of the fittest" — phrase coined by Herbert Spencer, adopted by Darwin in later editions.
  • Galapagos finch beaks — adaptive radiation; different islands, different diets.
  • Peppered moth (Biston betularia) — industrial melanism, textbook selection example.
  • HMS Beagle voyage (1831–1836) — where Darwin collected evidence.

High-Yield Concepts (Most Tested)

Principal forms, mechanisms, and applications most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / giveaway.

Natural Selection — Modes
Directional · Stabilizing · Disruptive · Sexual
recogDirectional selection: one extreme phenotype favored — bell curve shifts. Example: antibiotic resistance, industrial melanism.
recogStabilizing selection: intermediate phenotype favored; variance reduced. Classic example: human birth weight.
recogDisruptive selection: both extremes favored over intermediates; can drive speciation. Example: African seedcracker finch bill sizes.
powerSexual selection (Darwin's second mechanism, 1871 The Descent of Man): intersexual (mate choice — peacock tail) vs. intrasexual (male–male combat — elk antlers). Zahavian handicap principle (costly signals must be honest) by Amotz Zahavi.
Hardy–Weinberg Equilibrium
Godfrey Hardy & Wilhelm Weinberg, 1908 — null model for population genetics
recogFive conditions for equilibrium (no evolution): no mutation, no migration, no selection, random mating, large population.
recogEquation: p² + 2pq + q² = 1; deviations signal evolutionary forces at work.
powerHardy was a mathematician who published in Science (1908); Weinberg published independently in German the same year. Frequently quizzed as "this 1908 principle states that allele frequencies remain constant absent ___."
Genetic Drift
Stochastic allele frequency change — strongest in small populations
recogFounder effect: small colonizing group carries subset of original gene pool — e.g., Amish high rates of Ellis–van Creveld syndrome.
recogBottleneck effect: population crashes, survivors' alleles dominate — e.g., cheetah near-total genetic uniformity.
powerNeutral theory of molecular evolution (Motoo Kimura, 1968): most mutations are selectively neutral; drift drives molecular evolution more than selection does at the molecular level. Kimura used diffusion equations; his "nearly neutral" theory extended by Tomoko Ohta.
Speciation
Allopatric · Sympatric · Parapatric · Peripatric
recogAllopatric speciation: geographic barrier → reproductive isolation → divergence. The dominant model; Galapagos finches, Darwin's finches, Hawaiian honeycreepers.
recogSympatric speciation: divergence within the same geographic range — requires strong disruptive selection or polyploidy. Polyploidy is common in plants (e.g., wheat = hexaploid).
powerBiological species concept (Ernst Mayr, 1942): species = groups that can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. Mayr formalized allopatric speciation; alternative concepts — phylogenetic species concept (monophyletic clade) and morphological species concept (older, pre-genetic).
Punctuated Equilibrium vs. Gradualism
Gould & Eldredge 1972 vs. Darwin's gradualism
recogPunctuated equilibrium: long stasis in the fossil record punctuated by rapid speciation bursts — proposed by Stephen Jay Gould and Niles Eldredge (1972).
recogPhyletic gradualism: Darwin's model — slow, steady change over time.
powerGould also developed the concept of spandrels (with Richard Lewontin, 1979 "San Marco" paper) — architectural byproducts not directly selected for; used to critique pan-adaptationism. Gould's other key term: exaptation (trait repurposed from original function — feathers for flight).
Kin Selection & Inclusive Fitness
W.D. Hamilton 1964 — explains altruism via shared alleles
recogHamilton's rule: altruism evolves when rB > C — where r = genetic relatedness, B = benefit to recipient, C = cost to actor.
recogExplains eusocial insects (bees, ants): workers share ~75% of genes with sisters (haplodiploidy) — extreme kin selection.
powerJ.B.S. Haldane reportedly quipped he would give his life for "two brothers or eight cousins" — matching r = 0.5 and r = 0.125 respectively. Reciprocal altruism (Robert Trivers, 1971): non-kin altruism when future reciprocation likely — vampire bat food sharing.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results, mechanisms, and historical figures — common as early/hard tossup clues.

Concept / PersonOne-line ID
LamarckismInheritance of acquired characteristics — Jean-Baptiste Lamarck; disproven (pre-Darwin); giraffes stretching necks.
Sexual conflictOpposing selection pressures on males vs. females; Trivers–Willard hypothesis — sex ratio bias based on maternal condition.
Red Queen hypothesisLeigh Van Valen (1973): species must keep evolving to maintain fitness against co-evolving competitors/parasites.
Müller's ratchetH.J. Muller: asexual populations accumulate deleterious mutations irreversibly — favors sexual reproduction.
Frequency-dependent selectionFitness of phenotype depends on its frequency — e.g., rare-male advantage in Drosophila, mimicry systems.
Coevolution / arms raceReciprocal evolutionary change — classic: Acacia and ant mutualism; toxin vs. resistance (newts vs. garter snakes).
Adaptive radiationRapid diversification from a common ancestor into many niches — Hawaiian honeycreepers, cichlid fish of African Rift Lakes.
ExaptationGould & Vrba (1982): trait serving a new function different from original — bird feathers (insulation → flight).
Neutral theory (Kimura)Most molecular variation is selectively neutral; molecular evolution rate ≈ mutation rate.
Evo-devo (evolutionary developmental biology)Hox genes conserved across phyla; small regulatory changes → large morphological shifts (Sean Carroll).
Group selectionV.C. Wynne-Edwards proposed; largely rejected; resurrected as multilevel selection by D.S. Wilson.
Bet-hedgingEvolutionary strategy spreading risk across time/environments — e.g., variable germination timing in seeds.
Epigenetic inheritanceNon-sequence heritable changes (methylation, histone modification); challenged strict genetic determinism.
Macroevolution vs. microevolutionMacro = above species level (new body plans, mass extinctions); micro = allele frequency change within a species.
Heterozygote advantageOverdominance; sickle cell trait — HbAS heterozygotes resist malaria better than HbAA; maintains both alleles.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key people

  • Charles Darwin — Origin of Species (1859); HMS Beagle; Galapagos finches
  • Alfred Russel Wallace — independent codiscoverer; sent Darwin the essay that spurred publication; biogeography (Wallace Line)
  • Gregor Mendel — particulate inheritance (genes); rediscovered 1900 by de Vries, Correns, Tschermak
  • Ronald Fisher — The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection (1930); Fisher's fundamental theorem; modern synthesis architect
  • J.B.S. Haldane — mathematical population genetics; "two brothers or eight cousins"
  • Sewall Wright — adaptive landscapes; genetic drift; F-statistics
  • Ernst Mayr — biological species concept; allopatric speciation; Systematics and the Origin of Species (1942)
  • W.D. Hamilton — inclusive fitness, kin selection, Hamilton's rule
  • Robert Trivers — reciprocal altruism; parental investment theory; parent–offspring conflict
  • Stephen Jay Gould — punctuated equilibrium; spandrels; exaptation
  • Richard Dawkins — The Selfish Gene (1976); "meme"; extended phenotype
  • Motoo Kimura — neutral theory of molecular evolution (1968)

Classic examples / organisms

  • Peppered moth (Biston betularia) — industrial melanism; H.B.D. Kettlewell's experiments
  • Galapagos finches — beak shape tracks food; Peter & Rosemary Grant studied for decades
  • Sickle cell anemia — heterozygote advantage; balancing selection; malaria belt
  • Antibiotic resistance — directional selection on bacteria; MRSA
  • Horseshoe crab / coelacanth — "living fossils" (slow evolutionary rate)
  • Cichlids of Lake Victoria/Malawi — adaptive radiation in hundreds of species
  • Darwin's finches ≠ Hawaiian honeycreepers — both adaptive radiations, different islands
  • Peacock tail — sexual selection; Zahavi handicap principle

Terms & mechanisms

  • Fitness — reproductive success relative to other genotypes; not strength
  • Adaptation — heritable trait increasing fitness in a given environment
  • Gene flow — migration of alleles between populations; prevents divergence
  • Reproductive isolation — prezygotic (barriers before fertilization) vs. postzygotic (hybrid inviability/sterility)
  • Selective sweep — beneficial allele rises quickly, reducing nearby diversity
  • Balancing selection — maintains multiple alleles (overdominance, frequency-dependent)
  • Clade / monophyletic group — ancestor + ALL descendants; cladistics
  • Phylogenetics — reconstructing evolutionary trees; parsimony, maximum likelihood, Bayesian methods

Key texts & equations

  • On the Origin of Species (Darwin, 1859) — natural selection by common descent
  • The Descent of Man (Darwin, 1871) — sexual selection; human evolution
  • The Selfish Gene (Dawkins, 1976) — gene-centric view; meme concept
  • Ever Since Darwin / The Panda's Thumb (Gould) — popular essays
  • Hamilton's rule: rB > C
  • Hardy–Weinberg: p² + 2pq + q² = 1
  • Price equation — general equation for selection and transmission; George Price
  • Fisher's fundamental theorem — rate of increase in mean fitness = additive genetic variance in fitness

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
LamarckismAcquired traits inherited — giraffe stretches its neck, offspring born with longer neck. Wrong mechanism. Darwin's selection operates on existing heritable variation, not use/disuse. Often asked as "this rejected precursor."
Genetic drift vs. Natural selectionDrift = random change (especially in small pops); selection = non-random differential reproduction. Bottleneck/founder effects = drift, not selection.
Phyletic gradualism vs. Punctuated equilibriumGradualism = slow, steady change (Darwin). Punctuated equilibrium = long stasis + rapid bursts (Gould & Eldredge). Fossil record shows more punctuation than gradualism.
Kin selection vs. Group selectionKin selection = altruism toward relatives sharing alleles (Hamilton); mathematically rigorous. Group selection = altruism for the good of the group; rejected in 1960s (Williams), partially rehabilitated as multilevel selection.
Sympatric vs. Allopatric speciationAllopatric = geographic separation first (most common). Sympatric = divergence with no geographic barrier — requires strong disruptive selection or instant isolation (polyploidy).
Adaptation vs. ExaptationAdaptation = trait evolved for its current function. Exaptation (Gould & Vrba) = trait evolved for one function, co-opted for another (ossicles: jaw bones → ear bones).
Darwin vs. WallaceBoth proposed natural selection independently. Wallace is often the answer to "who sent Darwin a letter that prompted publication." Wallace also named for the Wallace Line (biogeographic boundary in Indonesia).
Natural selection vs. Artificial selectionNatural selection = environment is the agent. Artificial selection (selective breeding) = humans are the agent; Darwin used it as an analogy in Origin (dog breeds, pigeon fancying).

Cross-discipline big picture Natural selection sits at the intersection of multiple quizbowl categories:
  • Mathematics: Hardy–Weinberg uses algebra; Fisher's fundamental theorem and the Price equation use statistics; Kimura applied stochastic differential equations to drift.
  • Chemistry/Biochemistry: Sickle cell — a single amino acid substitution (valine for glutamate at position 6 of hemoglobin beta chain) — demonstrates how molecular-level changes connect to selection.
  • History/Philosophy: Darwin's ideas were shaped by Thomas Malthus's Essay on Population (competition for limited resources) and Adam Smith's economics; Darwinism intersects Social Darwinism (a misapplication).
  • Literature: Alfred Lord Tennyson's "Nature, red in tooth and claw" (In Memoriam, 1850) predates Darwin's publication; T.H. Huxley ("Darwin's Bulldog") debated Bishop Samuel Wilberforce in 1860.
