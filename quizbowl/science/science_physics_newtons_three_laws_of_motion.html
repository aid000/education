Quick Overview

PublishedPrincipia Mathematica (1687) by Isaac Newton
Latin titlePhilosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica
Core domainClassical mechanics — macroscopic, non-relativistic motion
Superseded byRelativity (Einstein, 1905/1915) at high speed/mass; QM at atomic scale
Latin namesLex Prima, Lex Secunda, Lex Tertia
Reference frameLaws hold in inertial (non-accelerating) reference frames only

The three laws — one-line each

  • First Law (Inertia): An object at rest or in uniform motion stays that way unless acted on by a net external force.
  • Second Law (F = ma): Net force equals mass times acceleration; force and acceleration are in the same direction.
  • Third Law (Action–Reaction): For every action force there is an equal and opposite reaction force acting on the other object.

Governing formulas

First Law If Fnet = 0, then a = 0 (constant velocity or rest)
Second Law Fnet = ma  |  equivalently: F = dp/dt  (rate of change of momentum)
Third Law FA on B = −FB on A  (equal magnitude, opposite direction)

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • Any tossup mentioning "inertia" as a property of matter almost always ends here or at Newton's First Law.
  • "F equals ma" or "net force equals mass times acceleration" — instant Second Law buzz.
  • "equal and opposite" — instant Third Law buzz.
  • Published in the Principia (1687) — anchor date for every Newton laws tossup.

High-Yield Laws & Forms (Very Common)

The three laws and their principal forms most likely to be tested. power = hard/early clue; recog = recognition/giveaway clue.

First Law — Law of Inertia
Lex Prima · also called the "law of inertia"
recogAn object at rest stays at rest; an object in motion stays in motion at constant velocity — unless a net external force acts on it.
recogDefines inertia: the tendency of matter to resist changes in its state of motion.
recogMass is the measure of inertia — heavier objects are harder to accelerate or decelerate.
powerNewton generalized Galileo's law of inertia (Galileo showed horizontal motion persists without a push — Newton formalized it). Aristotelian physics had wrongly claimed a constant force was needed to maintain motion.
powerEquivalent formulation: in the absence of a net force, the momentum (p = mv) of a system is conserved. The First Law is a special case of the Second where F = 0.
Second Law — Law of Acceleration
Lex Secunda · F = ma · the cornerstone of classical mechanics
recogFnet = ma: net force equals mass times acceleration; both are vectors in the same direction.
recogMore general form: F = dp/dt (net force equals the rate of change of momentum). This covers variable-mass systems (rockets).
recogSI unit of force named after Newton: 1 N = 1 kg·m/s².
powerNewton originally stated it as: "The change of motion is proportional to the motive force impressed, and is made in the direction of the right line in which that force is impressed." The compact "F = ma" notation came later.
powerThe Second Law implies superposition of forces: multiple forces add as vectors to produce the net force that determines acceleration.
Third Law — Law of Action and Reaction
Lex Tertia · "action and reaction"
recog"For every action, there is an equal and opposite reaction." — the most-quoted formulation.
recogThe two forces always act on different objects (an action-reaction pair can never cancel each other on the same body).
recogClassic examples: rocket thrust (exhaust pushed backward → rocket pushed forward); recoil of a gun; a person pushing off a wall; Earth and a falling apple pull each other equally.
powerUnderlies the principle of conservation of momentum: if two objects interact with no external forces, the total momentum is unchanged because their mutual forces are equal and opposite, integrated over the same time.
powerFailure cases: the Third Law breaks down for electromagnetic forces between moving charges (radiation reaction, the Abraham–Lorentz force) and in special relativity — a common hard tossup angle.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results and extensions that appear as early/hard tossup clues. One-line ID each.

Principia Mathematica (1687)
Newton's three-volume work; laws appear in "Axiomata sive Leges Motus" section; also introduces universal gravitation.
Momentum (p = mv)
The "quantity of motion" in Newton's own terms; his Second Law is precisely F = dp/dt, not merely ma.
Impulse–Momentum Theorem
J = F·Δt = Δp; follows directly from Second Law; explains why airbags and padding work.
Conservation of Momentum
Derived from Third Law: internal forces cancel in pairs, so total momentum of an isolated system is constant.
Inertial vs. Non-inertial Reference Frames
Laws hold only in inertial frames; in rotating/accelerating frames, fictitious forces (Coriolis, centrifugal) must be added.
Weight vs. Mass distinction
Weight (W = mg) is a force (Newtons); mass is a scalar (kg) — a direct application of the Second Law.
Free-body diagrams (FBDs)
Standard tool for applying Second Law; identify all forces on one object, find net force, compute acceleration.
Normal force and apparent weight
Normal force is the Third-Law reaction to a surface; apparent weight changes in elevators (Fnet = ma on occupant).
Galileo's Inclined Plane Experiments
Pre-Newtonian experiments that showed acceleration is uniform under gravity; Newton's First Law generalized Galileo's finding.
Lagrangian and Hamiltonian Mechanics
18th–19th century reformulations of Newton's laws using energy minimization; equivalent results for most problems but extensible to QM.
Atwood Machine
Classic textbook device — two masses over a pulley — used to verify Second Law by measuring acceleration vs. net force.
Newton's Cannon thought experiment
Cannonball fired horizontally from a mountain; at sufficient speed, curvature of fall matches Earth's curvature — orbital motion as continuous free-fall, connecting laws to gravitation.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & quantities

  • Inertia — resistance to change in motion (First Law)
  • Momentum (p = mv) — "quantity of motion"
  • Net force / resultant force — vector sum of all forces
  • Newton (N) — SI unit of force = kg·m/s²
  • Impulse (J = FΔt) — change in momentum
  • Action-reaction pair — Third Law force pair

Classic examples (quizbowl-frequent)

  • Falling apple → Earth and apple attract equally (Third Law)
  • Rocket propulsion → exhaust/thrust pair (Third Law)
  • Seat belt / car crash → inertia (First Law)
  • Atwood machine → Second Law demonstration
  • Recoil of a gun → equal/opposite reaction
  • Walking → foot pushes Earth back; Earth pushes foot forward

Historical / bibliographic clues

  • Principia Mathematica (1687) — source text
  • Published with support of Edmond Halley (funded printing)
  • Newton's own Latin: "Lex Prima, Secunda, Tertia"
  • Preceded by Galileo's law of inertia (horizontal motion)
  • Gottfried Leibniz — calculus dispute contemporary
  • Introduced concept of absolute space and time (later challenged by Einstein)

Scope & limits

  • Valid only in inertial reference frames
  • Breaks down near speed of light → special relativity
  • Breaks down at atomic scale → quantum mechanics
  • Third Law breaks down for electromagnetic forces between moving charges (advanced)
  • Embedded in Lagrangian/Hamiltonian reformulations

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Newton's Law of Universal GravitationA separate law (F = Gm₁m₂/r²) also in the Principia — not one of the three laws of motion. Questions about gravity and the inverse-square law go here, not to F = ma.
Hooke's Law (F = –kx)Governs spring restoring force, not inertia or momentum. Hooke's Law is used with Newton's Second Law, not instead of it.
Kepler's Laws of Planetary MotionDescribe the shape and timing of orbits (ellipses, equal areas, period² ∝ a³); Newton's laws explain why those laws hold — do not conflate the two sets.
Conservation of Energy (Work-Energy Theorem)W = ΔKE follows from the Second Law but is a distinct principle. Energy conservation is not one of the three laws of motion.
Conservation of MomentumDerived from the Third Law; it is a consequence, not the law itself. Tossups on momentum conservation are related but distinct from the laws-of-motion category.
Einstein's Special Relativity / E = mc²Supersedes Newton at high speeds (v → c). F = ma is only the low-velocity limit; the correct relativistic form uses relativistic momentum p = γmv.
First Law vs. Second Law (F = 0 case)The First Law is not a special case of the Second in Newton's framing — it asserts the existence of inertial frames, while the Second quantifies force within them. A common conceptual trap in physics questions.
Action-reaction forces and equilibriumThird Law pairs act on different objects and never balance each other on a single body. Equilibrium (ΣF = 0 on one object) involves different forces, not action-reaction pairs.

Cross-discipline big picture Newton's three laws sit at the intersection of physics, math, history of science, and philosophy. In physics tossups they link to: universal gravitation (Principia), orbital mechanics (Kepler), and thermodynamics (momentum/energy). In history-of-science tossups they appear with Galileo (inertia precursor), Halley (Principia funding), and Leibniz (calculus dispute). In philosophy/epistemology they anchor debates about absolute vs. relative space and time — challenged first by Leibniz and Mach, then demolished by Einstein. Modern physics traces its entire mathematical structure (Lagrangian, Hamiltonian, quantum mechanics, field theory) as generalizations of Newton's Second Law, making F = ma arguably the single most generative equation in science.
