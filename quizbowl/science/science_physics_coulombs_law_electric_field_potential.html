Quick Overview

The cluster of classical electrostatics anchored by Charles-Augustin de Coulomb's 1785 inverse-square force law; extends into the electric field, potential, and flux concepts that underpin all of electromagnetism.

OriginCoulomb's torsion-balance experiments, 1785
BranchClassical electrostatics (electromagnetism)
Key figureCharles-Augustin de Coulomb (France, 1736–1806)
SI unit of chargeCoulomb (C); named after him
AnalogyStructurally identical to Newton's law of gravitation
UnificationSubsumed by Maxwell's equations (Gauss's law)

Governing formulas at a glance

F = k · |q₁ q₂| / r² Coulomb's law — force between two point charges; k ≈ 8.99 × 10⁹ N·m²/C² (= 1/4πε₀)
E = F / q = k · Q / r² Electric field magnitude due to point charge Q; direction away from + charge
V = k · Q / r Electric potential (scalar) from point charge Q; V = 0 at r → ∞
E = −∇V  (E = −dV/dx in 1-D) Field is the negative gradient of potential; field lines perpendicular to equipotentials

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "inverse-square law for electrostatics" — almost always Coulomb's law.
  • "torsion balance" — the instrument Coulomb used to measure the force.
  • "permittivity of free space (ε₀)" — always surfaces in E&M questions.
  • "equipotential surfaces perpendicular to field lines" — potential clue.

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

The principal laws/forms/applications most likely to anchor a tossup. power = early, hard clue. recog = middle/giveaway clue.

Coulomb's Law
1785 · torsion-balance derivation · F ∝ q₁q₂/r²
powerCoulomb measured force between charged spheres using a torsion balance (a fiber whose twist angle gives force) — the same apparatus Henry Cavendish later used for gravity. The constant k equals 1/(4πε₀) where ε₀ = 8.854 × 10⁻¹² C²/(N·m²) is the permittivity of free space.
recogForce is proportional to the product of the charges and inversely proportional to the square of the distance; like gravity but charges can attract or repel.
recogThe SI unit of charge, the coulomb (C), is named after him; one electron carries −1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.
wiki ↗
Electric Field (E)
Force-per-unit-charge field introduced by Faraday · units N/C = V/m
powerThe field concept was introduced by Michael Faraday (via "lines of force") and mathematized by Maxwell. The field at a point is the force per unit positive test charge placed there: E = F/q₀. For a point charge Q: E = kQ/r², directed radially outward (inward if Q is negative).
recogField lines start on positive charges, end on negative charges; density of lines indicates field strength.
recogInside a conductor in electrostatic equilibrium, E = 0; all excess charge resides on the surface.
wiki ↗
Electric Potential (V)
Work per unit charge to move from ∞ to a point · units volts (V) = J/C
powerPotential is a scalar; field is a vector. V = kQ/r for a point charge. Moving a charge q through a potential difference ΔV changes its potential energy by ΔU = qΔV. The electron-volt (eV) = energy gained by e⁻ through 1 V.
recogEquipotential surfaces are always perpendicular to field lines; no work is done moving along an equipotential.
recogA conductor in electrostatic equilibrium is an equipotential surface — its entire surface is at the same potential.
wiki ↗
Gauss's Law
∮ E · dA = Q_enc / ε₀ · Carl Friedrich Gauss, 1835 (pub. 1867)
powerGauss's law is the integral form of Coulomb's law generalized to any charge distribution. Choose a Gaussian surface — a closed imaginary surface — and the total electric flux through it equals enclosed charge divided by ε₀. Gauss is the first of Maxwell's four equations.
recogUsed to derive the field of a sphere, infinite line, or infinite plane — the standard three Gaussian-surface examples.
recogElectric flux Φ = E · A · cos θ; for a closed surface, net outward flux = Q_enc / ε₀.
wiki ↗
Superposition Principle
Net force / field / potential = vector (or scalar) sum of individual contributions
powerBecause Coulomb's law is linear, the force on charge q from N other charges is the vector sum of each pairwise force. The same linearity holds for the field (vector sum) and for potential (scalar sum — no angle needed).
recogThe scalar nature of potential makes superposition of V easier than superposition of E; compute V first, then take its gradient to get E if needed.
wiki ↗
Capacitance and Electrostatic Energy
C = Q/V · energy U = ½CV² = ½QV = Q²/2C
powerA parallel-plate capacitor has C = ε₀A/d. The uniform field inside is E = σ/ε₀ = V/d. Inserting a dielectric of constant κ multiplies C by κ because κ = ε/ε₀. The Leyden jar (1745, van Musschenbroek / Kleist) was the first capacitor.
recogEnergy stored in an electric field: u = ½ε₀E² (energy density, J/m³).
recogFor a sphere of radius R: C = 4πε₀R — the self-capacitance of an isolated sphere.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results that appear as early/hard clues or as standalone hard tossup answers. One-line each.

  • Poisson's Equation∇²V = −ρ/ε₀; relates potential to charge density; reduces to Laplace's equation (∇²V = 0) in charge-free regions.
  • Laplace's Equation∇²V = 0; solutions are harmonic functions; governs potential in free space; named for Pierre-Simon Laplace.
  • Electric DipoleTwo equal and opposite charges ±q separated by d; dipole moment p = qd; field falls off as 1/r³, not 1/r².
  • Multipole ExpansionPotential far from charge distribution: monopole (1/r) + dipole (1/r²) + quadrupole (1/r³) + …; monopole dominant if net charge ≠ 0.
  • Method of ImagesReplaces a conductor with fictitious image charges to satisfy boundary conditions; classic problem: charge above a grounded plane.
  • Gauss's Law for Magnetism∮ B · dA = 0; no magnetic monopoles; the "other" Gauss's law (Maxwell's second equation).
  • Earnshaw's TheoremA charged particle cannot be held in stable equilibrium by electrostatic forces alone; V obeys Laplace's equation, so no stable interior extremum.
  • Shell Theorem (electric)Uniformly charged spherical shell: E = 0 inside; outside, acts as point charge. Exact analog of Newton's shell theorem for gravity.
  • Leyden JarFirst practical capacitor (1745); developed independently by Pieter van Musschenbroek and Ewald Georg von Kleist; used by Franklin and early electricians.
  • Franklin's Kite ExperimentBenjamin Franklin (1752) showed lightning is electrical; proposed single-fluid theory of charge; invented the lightning rod.
  • Faraday CageConducting enclosure: interior field = 0; external fields cannot penetrate; named for Michael Faraday who demonstrated it 1836.
  • Point Discharge / Corona EffectVery strong field at sharp conductor tips causes ionization of surrounding air; basis for lightning rods and Van de Graaff generators.
  • Van de Graaff GeneratorElectrostatic machine (Robert Van de Graaff, 1929) uses a belt to accumulate charge; can reach MV potentials; used in early particle accelerators.
  • Millikan Oil-Drop ExperimentRobert Millikan (1909–1913) balanced gravity against electric force on tiny oil drops to measure the elementary charge e = 1.602 × 10⁻¹⁹ C.

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Newton's Law of GravitationSame 1/r² form, but gravity is always attractive; Coulomb force can attract or repel. Coulomb constant k ≈ 10⁹ vs. G ≈ 6.67 × 10⁻¹¹ — electrostatics is ~10³⁶× stronger. Clue: "torsion balance" belongs to Coulomb (and Cavendish for gravity).
Ohm's Law (V = IR)Ohm's law governs current flow through a resistor; Coulomb's law is about static forces. Both use voltage, but V = IR is circuit/dynamics; Coulomb/potential is statics. Do not confuse V (potential) with V (volts unit) in context.
Biot–Savart LawBiot–Savart gives the magnetic field from a current element (∝ 1/r²); Coulomb's law gives the electric field from a charge. Both are inverse-square but for different fields.
Gauss's Law for Magnetism∮ B·dA = 0 (no monopoles) vs. ∮ E·dA = Q/ε₀. The electric version has a source term; the magnetic version does not.
Electric Potential vs. Electric Potential EnergyPotential V is a property of a point in space (J/C = V). Potential energy U = qV is a property of a charge q at that point (J). Common mistake: equating the two directly.
Electric Field vs. Electric ForceField E = F/q is defined per unit charge and exists independently of a test charge. Force F = qE requires an actual charge q. Questions about the field at a point do not require specifying q.
Capacitance vs. ChargeC = Q/V is a geometric property of the capacitor (depends on area, separation, dielectric) — it does not change when connected to a different voltage unless the geometry changes. Charge Q = CV changes with V.

🌐 Big Picture: Electrostatics → All of E&M Coulomb's law is the seed of all classical electromagnetism. Gauss's law (Maxwell eq. 1) is its generalization to flux; Faraday's induction law (Maxwell eq. 3) extends fields to changing cases; Ampère–Maxwell (eq. 4) links changing E to B. The inverse-square form mirrors Newtonian gravity (both 1/r²), and the same Gaussian-surface technique works for both. In quantum field theory, the Coulomb interaction is mediated by virtual photons; in atomic physics, the proton–electron Coulomb attraction is what holds the hydrogen atom together, making Coulomb's law the foundation of atomic structure and chemistry as well.
