Quick Overview

Full nameDmitri Ivanovich Mendeleev
Born / died1834 Tobolsk, Siberia — 1907 St. Petersburg
NationalityRussian
FieldChemistry — periodic law, physical chemistry
Key institutionSt. Petersburg University (professor 1867–1890)
Famous firstPublished first widely accepted periodic table, 1869

Defining contributions

  • Periodic Law: properties of elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights.
  • Periodic Table (1869): arranged 63 known elements by atomic weight and valence; left gaps for undiscovered elements.
  • Predicted three elements: eka-boron, eka-aluminium, eka-silicon — confirmed as scandium, gallium, germanium.
  • Textbook: The Principles of Chemistry (1868–1870) — the organizing project that led him to the table.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • The periodic table of elements — single most given-away clue for Mendeleev.
  • He left blank spaces for undiscovered elements and accurately predicted their properties.
  • Reportedly saw the arrangement in a dream after exhausting conscious effort.
  • The element mendelevium (Md, 101) is named for him.

High-Yield Discoveries & Results

The discoveries most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard early clue. recog = recognition / middle clue.

The Periodic Table of Elements
1869 · published in Zeitschrift für Chemie and the Russian Chemical Society journal
recogArranged 63 known elements by increasing atomic weight; grouped by similar chemical properties in columns.
recogLeft deliberate gaps for three unknown elements, predicting their atomic weights and properties with remarkable accuracy.
powerThe 1869 paper was read to the Russian Chemical Society on March 6 by Nikolai Menshutkin because Mendeleev was traveling. He simultaneously sent a German version to Lothar Meyer (an independent discoverer); Mendeleev published first and predicted unknown elements — Meyer did not. His 1871 revised table switched to vertical groups of similar elements (the modern layout).

The Periodic Law
1869 · formalized in print 1871
Mendeleev's Periodic Law "The properties of the elements are a periodic function of their atomic weights."
recogStated that when elements are ordered by atomic weight, chemical and physical properties recur at regular intervals.
recogModern version (after Moseley, 1913) replaces atomic weight with atomic number — but Mendeleev's ordering held for all but a few elements.
powerMendeleev was bold enough to swap the order of pairs like tellurium and iodine (where weight order disagreed with chemical properties) — a move vindicated only when atomic number replaced weight. This "inversion" is a frequent hard clue.

Three Predicted Elements: Eka-Boron, Eka-Aluminium, Eka-Silicon
Predicted 1871 · confirmed 1875–1886
recogEka-aluminium → gallium (discovered by Lecoq de Boisbaudran, France, 1875). Predicted density ~5.9; actual 5.91.
recogEka-boron → scandium (Nilson, Sweden, 1879). Eka-silicon → germanium (Winkler, Germany, 1886).
powerThe prefix "eka-" (Sanskrit for "one") means "one step below" in the same group. Gallium's near-exact match to Mendeleev's prediction — density, melting point, formula of oxide — was the moment the chemical world accepted his table. Gallium melts in the palm of your hand (~29.8 °C), a trivia anchor.

The Principles of Chemistry (Osnovy khimii)
1868–1870 · two volumes · revised through 8 editions
recogMendeleev wrote it to serve as a university textbook while teaching general chemistry at St. Petersburg; organizing the material forced him to seek a rational system for the elements.
recogWent through eight Russian editions and was translated into English, German, and French; the most influential chemistry textbook of 19th-century Russia.
powerMendeleev later recalled that the impossibility of arranging elements arbitrarily in the textbook — he couldn't decide whether to introduce halogens or alkali metals first — was the direct intellectual trigger for seeking a unified ordering principle.
Legacy: Mendelevium & the Nobel Near-Miss
Nobel 1905/1906 · Mendelevium named 1955
recogElement 101, mendelevium (Md), named in his honor — synthesized 1955 by Ghiorso, Harvey, Choppin, Thompson, and Seaborg at Berkeley.
recogNominated for the Nobel Prize in Chemistry 1905 and 1906; did not receive it — reportedly because Henri Moissan's fluorine isolation work was favored and because Swedish chemist Peter Klason opposed him.
powerMendeleev died in 1907 — just two years after his final nomination — making him one of the most famous figures to narrowly miss the Nobel. This biographical detail is a common hard clue in science tossups.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Lesser-known results, writings, and biographical facts that appear as early power clues. One-line each.

Lothar Meyer (rival)
Independently created a periodic table in 1869–1870; did not predict gaps; Mendeleev published first and is credited with the law.
John Newlands — Law of Octaves (1865)
Earlier attempt: elements repeat properties every 8; ridiculed at the time; precursor Mendeleev built on.
1871 revised table
Switched layout so similar elements form vertical groups (modern orientation); corrected early 1869 version.
Tellurium/iodine inversion
Mendeleev placed iodine after tellurium despite lower atomic weight, trusting chemical properties over mass — vindicated by atomic number.
Aether / ether (1904 paper)
Proposed a group-zero column lighter than hydrogen for the hypothetical element "ether" — a pre-noble-gas attempt he later abandoned.
Hydration of alcohol (1865 doctoral thesis)
Dissertation On the Combinations of Water with Alcohol — his formal PhD work, not the table.
Russian Bureau of Weights and Measures
Director 1893–1907; standardized Russian metrology and defined a new national standard for the meter and kilogram.
Resignation from St. Petersburg (1890)
Resigned after the Ministry of Education ignored a student petition he had personally delivered — a politicized exit.
Dream legend
Claimed the final arrangement came to him in a dream; likely a romanticized retelling — he had worked the problem consciously for years.
Investigation of oil fields (1876)
Traveled to Pennsylvania to study U.S. oil industry; recommended Russia develop its own Baku petroleum resources.
Long hair / distinctive appearance
Famously cut his hair only once a year; his flowing beard and hair were recognized across Russia — biographical flavor clue.
Smoless pyrokollodium (smokeless powder)
Developed a smokeless gunpowder formulation for the Russian navy in 1890s; believed the U.S. stole the formula.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & units

  • Periodic Law — properties = periodic function of atomic weight
  • Eka- prefix — Sanskrit "one," means one below in the group
  • Atomic weight (not number) — Mendeleev's ordering metric
  • Groups / periods — his vertical groups hold similar elements
  • Mendelevium (Md, 101) — element named for him
  • Valence — he used this alongside weight to place elements

Collaborators & rivals

  • Lothar Meyer — simultaneous German periodic table; no predictions
  • John Newlands — Law of Octaves precursor (1865)
  • Alexandre-Émile Béguyer de Chancourtois — telluric screw (1862, earlier but ignored)
  • Nikolai Menshutkin — read Mendeleev's 1869 paper to the society in his absence
  • Lecoq de Boisbaudran — discovered gallium (eka-aluminium) 1875
  • Henry Moseley — replaced atomic weight with atomic number (1913)

Key numbers & dates

  • 1834 — born, Tobolsk, Siberia (14th and last child)
  • 1869 — first periodic table published
  • 1871 — revised, modern-layout table with predictions
  • 63 — elements known when he built the table
  • 1875, 1879, 1886 — gallium, scandium, germanium confirmed
  • 1893 — appointed head of Bureau of Weights and Measures
  • 1907 — died; narrowly missed Nobel Prize

Biographical / cultural

  • Born in Tobolsk, Siberia; 14th of 14 or 17 children
  • Mother walked him to St. Petersburg for university admission
  • Studied under Henri Regnault in Paris and Robert Bunsen in Heidelberg
  • The dream story: arrangement came in a dream (apocryphal but canonical)
  • Strongly opposed radioactivity and transmutation late in life
  • Published widely on agriculture, economics, and tariffs for Russia

Comparison Traps

Figures and concepts most often confused with Mendeleev in quizbowl.

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
Lothar MeyerAlso made a periodic table in 1869–70 but did not predict undiscovered elements — only Mendeleev left gaps with specific predictions. Meyer focused on atomic volumes, not the law. If the question mentions predictions, the answer is Mendeleev.
John NewlandsHis Law of Octaves (1865) proposed elements repeat every 8 — ridiculed at the Chemical Society. Earlier and less accurate than Mendeleev; used in questions as a precursor, not the inventor of the table.
Henry MoseleyMoseley replaced atomic weight with atomic number (1913) using X-ray spectroscopy, giving the modern periodic law. If the question says "atomic number," the answer is Moseley, not Mendeleev.
Antoine LavoisierEarlier French chemist; compiled the first modern list of elements (1789) and named oxygen. No periodic table — confusion arises in "history of element classification" tossups.
Béguyer de ChancourtoisProposed the telluric screw (vis tellurique) in 1862 — elements on a helix arranged by weight. Published in a geology journal; largely ignored. An obscure power clue, not the main answer.
Glenn SeaborgSeaborg extended the periodic table with actinides / transuranic elements; element 106 seaborgium named for him. The naming parallel to mendelevium often causes confusion — check the century (20th vs 19th).

The math/physics through-line Mendeleev's periodic law is fundamentally a statement about mathematical periodicity: properties repeat with a fixed period when elements are ordered by a discrete variable (atomic weight, then atomic number). The modern explanation — electron shell configuration (Bohr/quantum mechanics, 1913–1926) — shows that period lengths (2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32…) follow from solutions to the Schrödinger equation for hydrogen-like atoms. The noble gases discovered by Ramsay and Rayleigh in the 1890s required adding an entirely new Group 0 (now Group 18) — Mendeleev initially resisted this, then accepted it. His table thus bridges 19th-century empirical chemistry tossups and 20th-century quantum mechanics and nuclear physics tossups: element 101 (mendelevium) was made by bombarding einsteinium-253 with alpha particles — a nuclear physics answer in a chemistry costume.
