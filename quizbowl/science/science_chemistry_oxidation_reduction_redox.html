Quick Overview

Core definitionRedox = simultaneous oxidation (loss of e⁻) + reduction (gain of e⁻)
MnemonicOIL RIG — Oxidation Is Loss, Reduction Is Gain (of electrons)
Galvanic cellSpontaneous redox → electrical work; ΔG < 0; E°cell > 0
Electrolytic cellElectrical work → non-spontaneous redox; ΔG > 0; E°cell < 0
Key equationΔG° = −nFE° (Gibbs / Faraday / Nernst connection)
Quizbowl frequencyCore AP Chem; common as a concept-tossup answer and as clue context in electrochemistry questions

Governing formulas at a glance

Cell potential (standard conditions)
E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode
Cathode = reduction (higher potential); anode = oxidation (lower potential).
Gibbs–Faraday link
ΔG° = −nFE°cell
n = moles of electrons transferred; F = Faraday constant = 96,485 C/mol.
Nernst equation (non-standard)
E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q ≈ E° − (0.0592/n) log Q (at 25 °C)
Corrects E°cell for actual concentrations; at equilibrium E = 0, Q = K.
Faraday's law of electrolysis
m = (M · I · t) / (n · F)
m = mass deposited; M = molar mass; I = current (A); t = time (s).

Oxidation states — core rules

  • Pure element: O.S. = 0 (e.g., Cu, O₂, Fe).
  • Monatomic ion: O.S. = charge (Fe²⁺ → +2).
  • Oxygen in compounds: usually −2 (except peroxides: −1; OF₂: +2).
  • Hydrogen: +1 with nonmetals; −1 in metal hydrides (NaH).
  • Fluorine: always −1.
  • Sum of O.S. in neutral molecule = 0; in polyatomic ion = charge.

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

Principal statements, applications, and structures most likely to anchor a tossup. power = hard/early clue. recog = recognition/middle clue.

Galvanic (Voltaic) Cell — Anatomy
Core structure of every electrochemical cell question
Standard cell notation (shorthand):
Zn(s) | Zn²⁺(aq) || Cu²⁺(aq) | Cu(s)
Left = anode (oxidation); || = salt bridge; Right = cathode (reduction)
recogAnode = oxidation, negative terminal in galvanic cell; cathode = reduction, positive terminal.
recogSalt bridge maintains electrical neutrality by allowing ion flow (typically KCl or KNO₃).
recogElectrons flow from anode → cathode through external circuit; conventional current flows opposite.
powerThe classic Daniell cell uses a Zn anode / Cu cathode in sulfate solutions; E°cell = +1.10 V. Invented by John Frederic Daniell, 1836.
Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)
Reference point for all reduction potentials
recogSHE is defined as E° = 0.000 V exactly; all other potentials are measured relative to it.
recogConditions: H⁺ at 1 M, H₂ at 1 atm, 25 °C, over a platinum electrode.
powerThe platinum serves as an inert conductor / catalyst; this makes it a canonical "inert electrode" example in tossups about electrolysis or half-cell setups.
Nernst Equation
Derived by Walther Nernst (~1889)
recogRelates cell potential to non-standard concentrations; at equilibrium, E = 0 and Q = K.
recogAt 25 °C: E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q. The 0.0592 V factor is the "Nernst factor" (RT ln10/F).
powerWalther Nernst also formulated the Third Law of Thermodynamics (entropy → 0 at absolute zero) and won the 1920 Nobel Prize in Chemistry.
Faraday's Laws of Electrolysis
Michael Faraday, 1833–1834
recogFirst law: mass of substance deposited/liberated ∝ quantity of charge (Q = I·t).
recogSecond law: same charge liberates proportional amounts of different substances; equivalents are equal.
recogFaraday constant F ≈ 96,485 C/mol — charge of one mole of electrons.
powerFaraday's electrochemical work led him to coin the terms electrode, electrolyte, anode, cathode, ion, anion, cation (with William Whewell's help naming them).
The ΔG–E°cell–Keq Triangle
Core thermodynamic relationships
recogΔG° = −nFE°cell = −RT ln Keq — three quantities all encode spontaneity of the same reaction.
recogSpontaneous redox (galvanic): E°cell > 0, ΔG° < 0, Keq > 1.
powerSubstituting n and E° into ΔG° = −RT ln K gives: ln K = nFE° / RT; at 25 °C, ln K = nE° / 0.02569. A common source of tricky calculation questions.
Standard Reduction Potential Table & Activity Series
Electrochemical series
recogMore negative E°red = stronger reducing agent (readily oxidized); more positive E°red = stronger oxidizing agent.
recogKey potentials: F₂ → F⁻ = +2.87 V (strongest oxidizer); Li⁺ → Li = −3.04 V (strongest reducer).
recogA metal higher in the activity series (more negative E°) displaces ions of a metal lower in the series from solution.
powerGold's very positive reduction potential (+1.50 V for Au³⁺/Au) explains its corrosion resistance — it resists oxidation by O₂ or most acids. Only aqua regia (HNO₃ + HCl, 1:3) dissolves it.

Deeper Cuts (Harder Clues)

Related results, specific applications, and named concepts that appear as early/power clues in tossups. One-line ID each.

Daniell Cell Zn/Cu sulfate galvanic cell; E° = +1.10 V; invented 1836 by John Frederic Daniell.
Lead–Acid Battery Pb anode, PbO₂ cathode, H₂SO₄ electrolyte; rechargeable; common car battery.
Hydrogen Fuel Cell Combines H₂ + O₂ → H₂O; produces electricity; product is only water.
Disproportionation One species simultaneously oxidized and reduced; e.g., 2 H₂O₂ → 2 H₂O + O₂.
Comproportionation Two forms of same element combine to give intermediate oxidation state; reverse of disproportionation.

Electroplating Electrolytic cell deposits metal ions onto a cathode; object to plate is the cathode.

Chlor-Alkali Process Electrolysis of brine (NaCl + H₂O) → Cl₂ at anode, NaOH + H₂ at cathode; industrial staple.
Concentration Cell Both electrodes same metal; voltage driven by concentration difference; E = 0 at equilibrium.
Half-Reaction Method Balance redox in acidic/basic solution by splitting into oxidation + reduction half-reactions, balancing charge with H⁺/OH⁻.
Overpotential (Overvoltage) Extra voltage needed beyond E°cell for electrolysis to proceed; especially large for O₂ evolution.

Recognition / Clue Bank

Key terms & definitions

  • Oxidizing agent — gets reduced; causes others to be oxidized (e.g., MnO₄⁻, Cr₂O₇²⁻, F₂)
  • Reducing agent — gets oxidized; causes others to be reduced (e.g., H₂, Na, Fe)
  • OIL RIG mnemonic — universal giveaway in tossup clues
  • Electron donor = reducing agent; electron acceptor = oxidizing agent
  • Half-reaction — either the oxidation or reduction portion alone
  • Redox couple — an oxidized/reduced pair (e.g., Fe²⁺/Fe³⁺)

Electrochemical cell parts

  • Anode — oxidation, negative terminal (galvanic), positive terminal (electrolytic)
  • Cathode — reduction, positive terminal (galvanic), negative terminal (electrolytic)
  • Salt bridge — maintains neutrality; often KCl or KNO₃ in agar
  • Porous disk — alternative to salt bridge in some cells
  • External circuit — carries electrons; internal circuit carries ions
  • Cell notation: anode | anode ion || cathode ion | cathode

Named scientists

  • Michael Faraday — laws of electrolysis; F constant; coined electrode/anode/cathode/ion
  • Walther Nernst — Nernst equation; Third Law; 1920 Nobel Chemistry
  • John Frederic Daniell — Daniell cell (1836)
  • Charles Hall & Paul Héroult — aluminum smelting electrolysis (1886)
  • Luigi Galvani & Alessandro Volta — galvanic cell origins; Volta's crown of cups (1800)
  • Humphry Davy — electrolytic isolation of Na, K, Ca, Mg, Ba

Numbers & constants

  • F = 96,485 C/mol (Faraday constant)
  • Daniell cell E° = 1.10 V
  • SHE E° = 0.000 V (by definition)
  • F₂/F⁻ +2.87 V — strongest common oxidizer
  • Li⁺/Li −3.04 V — strongest common reducer
  • Nernst factor at 25 °C: 0.0592 V (per log unit per electron)

Common oxidation states

  • Permanganate MnO₄⁻: Mn = +7 (turns colorless in acidic solution)
  • Dichromate Cr₂O₇²⁻: Cr = +6 (orange → green Cr³⁺)
  • Hydrogen peroxide H₂O₂: O = −1 (can oxidize or reduce)
  • Iron: Fe²⁺ (ferrous) / Fe³⁺ (ferric)
  • Copper: Cu⁺ (cuprous) / Cu²⁺ (cupric)
  • Chromate CrO₄²⁻: Cr = +6 (yellow, basic)

Cross-discipline links

  • Redox ↔ acid–base: proton transfer vs. electron transfer (Brønsted vs. Lewis)
  • Redox ↔ thermodynamics: ΔG°, Keq, spontaneity all linked via E°
  • Biological redox: cellular respiration (NADH → NAD⁺); photosynthesis (H₂O oxidized)
  • Corrosion = accidental galvanic cell; cathodic protection = engineering fix
  • Redox titration: permanganate or dichromate as self-indicating oxidizer

Comparison Traps

Trap / ConfusionHow to distinguish
Galvanic vs. Electrolytic cellGalvanic: spontaneous, produces electricity, E°cell > 0, ΔG < 0. Electrolytic: requires electricity input, non-spontaneous, E°cell < 0. Anode/cathode labels swap in charge (anode is + in electrolytic vs. − in galvanic).
Oxidizing agent vs. reduced speciesThe oxidizing agent is itself reduced (gains electrons); it is NOT the species whose oxidation number goes up. E.g., in Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu: Cu²⁺ is the oxidizing agent and is reduced to Cu.
Anode sign (galvanic vs. electrolytic)In a galvanic cell the anode is negative (electrons flow out). In an electrolytic cell the anode is connected to the positive terminal of the power supply. Oxidation always at the anode regardless.
Reduction potential vs. oxidation potentialStandard tables list reduction potentials. To get oxidation potential, flip the sign. E°cell = E°red(cathode) − E°red(anode), NOT E°red + E°ox (a common error).
Faraday's Laws vs. Nernst EquationFaraday: how much substance is deposited/dissolved (moles ↔ charge). Nernst: how potential changes with concentration. Different questions — mass vs. voltage.
Disproportionation vs. comproportionationDisproportionation: one oxidation state splits into two (higher + lower). Comproportionation: two oxidation states converge to one intermediate. H₂O₂ → H₂O + O₂ is disproportionation (O: −1 → −2 and 0).
Activity series vs. reduction potential tableBoth rank metals by reducibility. Activity series = qualitative; lists which metals displace others from solution. Standard reduction potential table = quantitative; gives exact E° values.
Daniell cell vs. lead–acid batteryDaniell cell (Zn/Cu, not rechargeable, E° = 1.10 V) vs. Lead–acid (Pb/PbO₂, rechargeable, E° ≈ 2 V per cell; 12 V car battery = 6 cells in series).

Cross-discipline big picture Redox chemistry is the engine of energy conversion across all of science: cellular respiration (NADH donates electrons through the electron transport chain, driving ATP synthesis) and photosynthesis (light oxidizes water, reducing CO₂) are both redox processes. The same ΔG–E° thermodynamics governs both laboratory galvanic cells and the biological "electron transport chain" — a molecular wire running in the inner mitochondrial membrane. In materials science, corrosion is simply an uncontrolled galvanic cell, and the engineering fix (cathodic protection) is a controlled one. Every rechargeable battery, from lead–acid to lithium-ion, is an electrolytic/galvanic cell pair. Electrochemistry also links to analytical chemistry (potentiometry, pH meters use concentration cells) and coordination chemistry (oxidation state bookkeeping is essential for metal-complex reactivity).
