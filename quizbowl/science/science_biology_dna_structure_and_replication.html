Quick Overview

DefinitionDouble-stranded antiparallel helix of nucleotides; stores genetic information
MonomerNucleotide = phosphate + deoxyribose sugar + nitrogenous base
Bonds holding strandsHydrogen bonds between complementary bases (A–T, G–C)
Backbone bondsPhosphodiester bonds (covalent); 5'→3' direction per strand
Helix dimensions~2 nm wide; base pairs repeat every 3.4 nm (10 bp/turn)
Replication modeSemiconservative — each daughter molecule keeps one original strand
Chargaff's Rules (base-pairing ratios) [A] = [T]   ·   [G] = [C]   ·   ([A]+[G]) = ([T]+[C])   ·   %GC varies by species

Structural hierarchy

  • Nucleotide backbone: alternating deoxyribose–phosphate; bases project inward.
  • B-form DNA (standard, right-handed) — the Watson–Crick model; A-form and Z-form (left-handed) are alternate conformations.
  • Major groove / minor groove: proteins read base sequence via major groove.
  • Supercoiling: DNA wraps around histone octamers to form nucleosomes; condensed further into chromosomes.

Classic quizbowl giveaway clues

  • "Double helix" almost always signals Watson & Crick 1953 discovery.
  • Semiconservative replication — the Meselson–Stahl experiment is the #1 confirmatory clue.
  • Okazaki fragments on the lagging strand — single most asked replication detail.

High-Yield Concepts (Very Common)

Core statements, discoveries, and mechanisms most likely to be the answer. power = hard early clue. recog = middle/giveaway clue.

Watson & Crick Double-Helix Model (1953)
Proposed 1953 · Nature · Cambridge, England
recogTwo antiparallel polynucleotide strands coiled around a common axis; complementary base pairing (A–T, G–C).
recogModel built with Rosalind Franklin's X-ray diffraction data (Photo 51) and Chargaff's base-ratio rules.
powerFranklin's Photo 51 was shared with Watson via Maurice Wilkins without her knowledge; Nobel Prize (1962) went to Watson, Crick, and Wilkins — Franklin had died in 1958 and was ineligible.

Semiconservative Replication — Meselson–Stahl Experiment (1958)
Confirmed 1958 · E. coli · Caltech
recogEach new DNA molecule contains one original strand and one newly synthesized strand.
recogProved using heavy nitrogen (¹⁵N) / light nitrogen (¹⁴N) density-gradient centrifugation in E. coli.
powerAfter one generation, all DNA was intermediate density (hybrid ¹⁵N/¹⁴N). After two generations, hybrid and light bands appeared — ruling out conservative and dispersive models. Often called "the most beautiful experiment in biology" (John Cairns).
wiki ↗
DNA Polymerase & the 5'→3' Rule
Key enzyme in replication; discovered in E. coli by Arthur Kornberg, 1956
recogDNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to the 3'-OH end; synthesizes only in the 5'→3' direction.
recogRequires a short RNA primer (laid by primase) to begin; cannot start de novo.
powerArthur Kornberg isolated DNA Pol I in E. coli and won the 1959 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine (shared with Severo Ochoa). DNA Pol III is the main replicative polymerase in prokaryotes; DNA Pol δ / ε in eukaryotes. Pol I fills primer gaps and has 5'→3' exonuclease activity.
wiki ↗
Okazaki Fragments & the Lagging Strand
Discovered by Reiji & Tsuneko Okazaki, 1968
recogThe lagging strand is synthesized discontinuously as short Okazaki fragments (100–200 nt in eukaryotes, ~1000–2000 nt in prokaryotes).
recogEach Okazaki fragment begins with a RNA primer; primers later replaced by DNA and joined by DNA ligase.
powerNamed for Reiji Okazaki, who died of radiation-linked leukemia (Hiroshima survivor). His wife Tsuneko Okazaki continued the work. The leading strand is synthesized continuously toward the replication fork; the lagging strand is synthesized away from the fork in fragments. Often clued as "short, discontinuous segments on the lagging strand."
wiki ↗
Origin of Replication, Helicase & the Replication Fork
Core replication machinery — prokaryotes: one oriC; eukaryotes: thousands of origins
recogReplication begins at origins of replication (oriC in E. coli); proceeds bidirectionally, forming two replication forks.
recogHelicase unwinds the double helix; single-strand binding proteins (SSBPs) stabilize unpaired strands; topoisomerase relieves torsional stress ahead of the fork.
powerIn prokaryotes, the replisome assembles at a single oriC and produces a single circular theta structure. Eukaryotic replication bubbles fire at multiple origins simultaneously, enabling faster replication of larger genomes. Topoisomerase II (gyrase in bacteria) is the target of fluoroquinolone antibiotics.
wiki ↗
Telomeres & Telomerase
Telomerase discovered by Elizabeth Blackburn, Carol Greider & Jack Szostak; Nobel 2009
recogTelomeres are repetitive sequences (TTAGGG in humans) at chromosome ends; shorten with each replication cycle due to the end-replication problem.
recogTelomerase is an RNA-dependent DNA polymerase (reverse transcriptase) that extends telomeres using its own built-in RNA template; active in germ cells and cancer cells.
powerBlackburn & Greider discovered telomerase in Tetrahymena (1984). The end-replication problem arises because DNA Pol cannot copy the very 5' end of the lagging strand after primer removal — each cycle shortens telomeres by ~50–200 bp. Telomere length is associated with cellular aging and the Hayflick limit.

Deeper Cuts (Hard Clues)

Related results, named experiments, and harder details — each in one line.

Chargaff's Rules
Erwin Chargaff, 1950 — %A=%T, %G=%C in any DNA; ratio varies by species, not tissue.
Rosalind Franklin & Photo 51
X-ray diffraction image of DNA B-form (1952); cross pattern revealed helical structure and 3.4 nm repeat.
Avery–MacLeod–McCarty Experiment (1944)
Showed DNA (not protein) is the transforming principle in Griffith's pneumococcus transformation.
Hershey–Chase Experiment (1952)
Radioactive ³²P/³⁵S labeling of T2 phage confirmed DNA, not protein, carries genetic information.
A-form & Z-form DNA
A-form: right-handed, shorter/wider; Z-form: left-handed zigzag, high GC content; B-form is physiological norm.
Nucleosome Structure
147 bp of DNA wound 1.65 turns around histone octamer (H2A, H2B, H3, H4 ×2); H1 linker histone.
Sliding Clamp / Processivity
PCNA (eukaryotes) / beta clamp (prokaryotes) encircles DNA to keep polymerase attached during synthesis.
Proofreading (3'→5' Exonuclease)
DNA Pol III (prokaryote) and Pol ε/δ (eukaryote) remove mismatched bases via 3'→5' exonuclease activity; error rate ~10⁻⁹.
Mismatch Repair (MMR)
MutS recognizes mismatch; MutL recruits MutH (prokaryote) to nick and excise error; HNPCC linked to MMR defects.
Topoisomerase I vs. II
Topo I cuts one strand (no ATP); Topo II cuts both strands (requires ATP); gyrase (type II) targeted by quinolones.
Griffith's Transformation (1928)
Heat-killed virulent S-strain + live R-strain pneumococcus → live mice die; "transforming principle" concept born.
Cairns Autoradiography (1963)
John Cairns visualized circular E. coli chromosome by ³H-thymidine autoradiography, confirming theta-form replication.
DnaA / Initiator Protein (prokaryotes)
DnaA binds AT-rich oriC repeats, recruits DnaB helicase (DnaC loader) to initiate strand unwinding.
Purine vs. Pyrimidine Bases
Purines (A, G) — double ring; Pyrimidines (T, C, U) — single ring. A–T: 2 H-bonds; G–C: 3 H-bonds.

  • Hayflick limit — ~50 divisions before telomere-driven senescence
  • Theta structure — circular DNA mid-replication appearance

Comparison Traps

Don't confuse withHow to tell them apart
DNA vs. RNADNA has deoxyribose (no 2'-OH), thymine (T); RNA has ribose, uracil (U). DNA is double-stranded (usually); RNA is single-stranded. "Deoxyribonucleic" = DNA; "ribonucleic" = RNA.
Semiconservative vs. Conservative vs. DispersiveSemiconservative = one old + one new strand per molecule (proven by Meselson–Stahl). Conservative = both old strands stay together + both new strands together. Dispersive = old and new intermixed throughout both strands. Only semiconservative is correct.
Leading vs. Lagging StrandLeading strand synthesized continuously toward the fork (3'→5' template read, 5'→3' synthesis). Lagging strand synthesized away from fork in Okazaki fragments. Both strands run antiparallel — key: which way the fork moves relative to synthesis.
DNA Pol I vs. DNA Pol III (prokaryotes)Pol III is the main replicative polymerase (high processivity). Pol I is the repair/primer-removal polymerase (5'→3' exonuclease removes RNA primer). Kornberg's original enzyme is Pol I — do not say he discovered the "main" polymerase.
Purines vs. PyrimidinesPurines (A, G) — two rings, larger. Pyrimidines (C, T, U) — one ring, smaller. Mnemonic: PURe As Gold (Purines = A, G). A always pairs with T/U (2 H-bonds); G always pairs with C (3 H-bonds).
Topoisomerase I vs. IITopo I cuts one strand — relaxes supercoils, no ATP. Topo II (gyrase) cuts both strands — introduces negative supercoils ahead of the fork, requires ATP. Fluoroquinolone antibiotics (e.g. ciprofloxacin) target gyrase (bacterial Topo II), not Topo I.
Telomerase vs. Other DNA PolymerasesTelomerase is a reverse transcriptase — uses its own RNA component (TERC) as template to synthesize DNA. Other DNA polymerases read a DNA template. Telomerase adds 5'-TTAGGG-3' repeats to chromosome 3' ends.
Replication vs. Transcription vs. TranslationReplication: DNA → DNA (whole genome, once per cell cycle). Transcription: DNA → RNA (gene-by-gene, RNA Pol). Translation: mRNA → protein (ribosome). Helicase, polymerase, and primer are involved in replication; RNA Pol needs no primer.
